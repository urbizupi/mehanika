Chapter 11 The boundary value problem of linear elasticity

11.3 Beltrami-Michell equations (Beltrami-Michellove enačbe)

In section 11.2 we collected the equations of the boundary value problem (definition 11.1) into three equations for the displacement. The displacement is the only unknown there, and the strains and stresses are computed from it. Can it also be done the other way, with the stresses as the only unknown? This route is natural when forces are prescribed on the surface, because the stress gives them directly (Cauchy's formula, theorem 2.24). A question appears at once that did not arise with displacements: which equations must a stress field satisfy for it to be possible at all?

Let us look at an example. Take a bar of rectangular cross section lying along the \(x\) axis, with depth \(2c = 40\) mm, the \(y\) axis pointing up along the depth and the \(z\) axis perpendicular to the plane of the drawing. The material is the steel of the book, \(E = 196\) GPa and \(\nu = 0{,}3\). Let the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = k\,y^2\) be the only nonzero component, with the constant \(k = 2{,}5 \cdot 10^{10}\ \text{N/m}^4\), so that at the top and bottom edges \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(\pm c) = k\,c^2 = 10\) MPa. The equilibrium equations (theorem 2.28) hold without body force, because the field does not depend on \(x\), so \(\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/\partial x = 0\), and the other components are zero. For comparison take the field \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = k\,y\), which equals the pure bending of chapter 5. This field is in equilibrium too. We believe pure bending, because a bar really does bend. Do we believe the parabola as well?

We look for the answer through the strains. By Hooke's law (theorem 3.11) \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/E\), so the fibre at height \(y\) stretches or shortens in proportion to \(k\,y\) in the first field and in proportion to \(k\,y^2\) in the second. We imagine the bar as a bundle of fibres along the \(x\) axis and let each fibre lengthen by its own amount (figure 11.6). In the first field the elongations are linear in \(y\). The upper fibres are longer, the lower ones shorter, and a surface that was flat stays flat and only turns. The fibres fit against each other if we bend them about a common centre, which is bending. In the second field the upper and the lower fibre are both longer than the middle one. The two outer fibres would both have to lie outside the curved middle fibre, which is impossible for any bending with one centre. The gap between fibres would be too narrow everywhere or too wide everywhere after loading. It seems that no displacement field gives such strains. We know the exact condition for this from chapter 1: strains that come from a displacement field satisfy the compatibility equations (1.68) (theorem 1.127), and in a simply connected body these equations are also sufficient (theorem 4.119). In a simply connected body, stresses are therefore possible exactly when the strains we get from them by Hooke's law are possible. The compatibility equations for these strains are the condition we are looking for on the stresses.

This section answers the question what compatibility demands of the stresses. First we transform the compatibility equations into a form that fits the equilibrium equations, then we insert Hooke's law and equilibrium.

11.3.1 Contracted compatibility equations (Skrčene enačbe kompatibilnosti)

The compatibility equations (1.68) contain the second derivative of the shear strain, for example \(\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}/\partial x\,\partial y\). If we insert Hooke's law in it, we get the second derivative of the shear stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\), which the equilibrium equations do not know: they contain only first derivatives, in the sum \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{ik}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{k}}\). Such sums arise in expressions where one index is summed. Lemma 4.105 showed that the compatibility equations are the same as the vanishing of all numbers \(Z_{\mathrm{ijkl}}\). Take the numbers \(Z_{\mathrm{ikjk}}\), where the second and fourth index are equal, and for fixed \(i\), \(j\) sum them over \(k\). We get nine numbers, of which by symmetry only six are different. Did we lose information by summing? The lemma says we did not.

Lemma 11.8 Contracted compatibility equations (skrčene enačbe kompatibilnosti)

Let \(D\) be an open set of points and let \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{ji}}\), where the indices run over \(x\), \(y\), \(z\), be functions whose partial derivatives of the first and second order exist and are continuous at every point of \(D\). For the indices \(i\), \(j\) let

\[\begin{aligned} &R_{\mathrm{ij}} = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} Z_{\mathrm{ikjk}} \\ &\quad = \nabla^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} \\ &\quad + \frac{\partial^2(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}})}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} \\ &\quad - \sum_{\mathrm{k}} \frac{\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ik}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{k}}} \\ &\quad - \sum_{\mathrm{k}} \frac{\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{jk}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{k}}} , \end{aligned} \tag{11.6}\]

where \(Z_{\mathrm{ikjk}}\) is the expression (4.71) with the indices \(i\), \(k\), \(j\), \(k\) and \(\nabla^2\) is the operator of definition 11.5. At a point \(P\) of \(D\) the compatibility equations (1.68) hold exactly when the number \(R_{\mathrm{ij}}\) is zero at \(P\) for all nine choices of the indices \(i\), \(j\).

Proof

We must show that compatibility implies \(R_{\mathrm{ij}} = 0\) and that \(R_{\mathrm{ij}} = 0\) implies compatibility. We express the six expressions whose zeros are the compatibility equations by the \(R_{\mathrm{ij}}\) and the other way round.

  1. The expression (4.71), in which \(i\), \(k\), \(j\), \(k\) stand in place of \(i\), \(j\), \(k\), \(l\), is
    \[Z_{\mathrm{ikjk}} = \frac{\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{k}}^2} + \frac{\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{kk}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} - \frac{\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ik}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{k}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} - \frac{\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{kj}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{k}}} .\]
    The sum over \(k\) of the first term is \(\nabla^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\), of the second \(\partial^2(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}})/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}\). In the third and fourth term we exchange the order of differentiation (theorem 1.125) and use \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{kj}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{jk}}\). This is (11.6).
  2. Let \(S_1, \dots, S_6\) be the difference of the left and right side of the equations (1.68) in order. By steps 7 and 8 of the proof of lemma 4.105, \(S_1 = Z_{\mathrm{xyxy}}\), \(S_2 = Z_{\mathrm{yzyz}}\), \(S_3 = Z_{\mathrm{zxzx}}\), \(S_4 = Z_{\mathrm{xyxz}}\), \(S_5 = Z_{\mathrm{yzyx}}\), \(S_6 = Z_{\mathrm{zxzy}}\).
  3. In the sum \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} Z_{\mathrm{ikjk}}\) the term with \(k = i\) is zero, because the first two indices are equal, and the term with \(k = j\) is zero, because the last two indices are equal (step 4 of the proof of lemma 4.105). The terms with \(k\) different from \(i\) and \(j\) remain. For \(R_{\mathrm{xx}}\) these are \(k = y\) and \(k = z\):
    \[R_{\mathrm{xx}} = Z_{\mathrm{xyxy}} + Z_{\mathrm{xzxz}} = S_1 + S_3 .\]
    Here \(Z_{\mathrm{xzxz}} = \partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}/\partial z^2 + \partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}/\partial x^2 - 2\,\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}/\partial x\,\partial z\) is the difference of the left and right side of the third equation (1.68), so \(S_3\), and \(Z_{\mathrm{xyxy}} = S_1\) by step 2.
  4. In the same way, with the exchange of pairs, \(Z_{\mathrm{ijkl}} = Z_{\mathrm{klij}}\), and of both orders, \(Z_{\mathrm{jikl}} = -Z_{\mathrm{ijkl}}\) and \(Z_{\mathrm{ijlk}} = -Z_{\mathrm{ijkl}}\) (steps 1 to 3 of the proof of lemma 4.105), we get
    \[R_{\mathrm{yy}} = Z_{\mathrm{yxyx}} + Z_{\mathrm{yzyz}} = S_1 + S_2 , \qquad R_{\mathrm{zz}} = Z_{\mathrm{zxzx}} + Z_{\mathrm{zyzy}} = S_3 + S_2 ,\]
    \[R_{\mathrm{yz}} = Z_{\mathrm{yxzx}} = -Z_{\mathrm{xyzx}} = Z_{\mathrm{xyxz}} = S_4 ,\]
    \[R_{\mathrm{xz}} = Z_{\mathrm{xyzy}} = Z_{\mathrm{zyxy}} = -Z_{\mathrm{yzxy}} = Z_{\mathrm{yzyx}} = S_5 ,\]
    \[R_{\mathrm{xy}} = Z_{\mathrm{xzyz}} = Z_{\mathrm{yzxz}} = -Z_{\mathrm{zyxz}} = Z_{\mathrm{zyzx}} = Z_{\mathrm{zxzy}} = S_6 .\]
    By the same steps \(R_{\mathrm{ji}} = R_{\mathrm{ij}}\), so the nine numbers \(R_{\mathrm{ij}}\) are only six different ones.
  5. If the compatibility equations hold, then \(S_1 = \dots = S_6 = 0\) and by step 4 \(R_{\mathrm{ij}} = 0\) for all \(i\), \(j\).
  6. Conversely, if \(R_{\mathrm{ij}} = 0\) for all \(i\), \(j\), then by step 4 \(S_4 = S_5 = S_6 = 0\), and
    \[\begin{aligned} S_1 &= \tfrac12\,(R_{\mathrm{xx}} + R_{\mathrm{yy}} - R_{\mathrm{zz}}) , \\ S_2 &= \tfrac12\,(R_{\mathrm{yy}} + R_{\mathrm{zz}} - R_{\mathrm{xx}}) , \\ S_3 &= \tfrac12\,(R_{\mathrm{xx}} + R_{\mathrm{zz}} - R_{\mathrm{yy}}) , \end{aligned}\]
    which on insertion from step 4 indeed gives \(S_1\), \(S_2\), \(S_3\) (for example \(R_{\mathrm{xx}} + R_{\mathrm{yy}} - R_{\mathrm{zz}} = 2S_1 + S_2 + S_3 - S_2 - S_3 = 2S_1\)). So \(S_1\), \(S_2\), \(S_3\) are zero as well and all compatibility equations hold.

Source: SADD §5.3 (PDF p. 109): the six meaningful equations are obtained with \(k = l\) in the general form (5.1.2), so by contraction over one index. The converse direction, the solution in step 6, is ours. The proof by inversion is not from the sources. SymPy checked all six relations of step 4 for general functions.

The lemma showed that the nine contracted numbers carry the same content as the six compatibility equations, only in a form where the sum \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} \partial\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ik}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{k}}\) appears. Let us test it on the bar of the introduction. The strains of the field \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = k\,y^2\) are by Hooke's law \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = k\,y^2/E\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = -\nu\,k\,y^2/E\), the other components are zero. The derivatives are \(\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}/\partial y^2 = 2k/E\), \(\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}/\partial y^2 = -2\nu k/E\), all other second derivatives are zero. The first compatibility equation gives \(S_1 = \partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}/\partial y^2 + \partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}/\partial x^2 - 2\,\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}/\partial x\,\partial y = 2k/E = 0{,}2551\ \text{m}^{-2}\), the second \(S_2 = \partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}/\partial z^2 + \partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}/\partial y^2 - 2\,\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}/\partial y\,\partial z = -2\nu k/E = -0{,}0765\ \text{m}^{-2}\), the third \(S_3 = 0\), the fourth to sixth are zero. By the lemma the contracted values are \(R_{\mathrm{xx}} = S_1 + S_3 = 0{,}2551\ \text{m}^{-2}\), \(R_{\mathrm{yy}} = S_1 + S_2 = 0{,}1786\ \text{m}^{-2}\) and \(R_{\mathrm{zz}} = S_2 + S_3 = -0{,}0765\ \text{m}^{-2}\). They are not zero, so the parabola is not a possible stress field. For the field \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = k\,y\) every second derivative of the strains is zero and compatibility holds, as figure 11.6 shows. SymPy checked the computation.

11.3.2 Stress formulation (Napetostna formulacija)

Now we insert Hooke's law and equilibrium into \(R_{\mathrm{ij}} = 0\), so that only the stresses remain as unknowns. The sum \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\), that is the trace (sled) of the stress tensor, we will write \(\Theta\). The equations also contain first derivatives of the body force, which the equilibrium equations connect with the stresses.

Theorem 11.9 Beltrami-Michell equations (Beltrami-Michellove enačbe)

Let the material be isotropic and homogeneous (definitions 3.4 and 3.5) with modulus of elasticity \(E\) and Poisson's ratio \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\) (theorem 3.48), with Hooke's law (3.12). Let \(D\) be an open set of points, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{ji}}\) functions with continuous partial derivatives up to the second order on \(D\) and \(\Theta = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\). Let the equilibrium equations (2.31) \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}} + b_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\) hold at every point of \(D\) with a body force \(\vek b = (b_{\mathrm{x}}, b_{\mathrm{y}}, b_{\mathrm{z}})\) whose components have continuous first partial derivatives. Let \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) be the strains that the stresses determine by Hooke's law. Then at a point \(P\) of \(D\) the compatibility equations (1.68) hold exactly when at \(P\) for all nine choices of the indices \(i\), \(j\)

\[\nabla^2\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}} + \frac{1}{1 + \nu}\,\frac{\partial^2\Theta}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} = -\frac{\nu}{1 - \nu}\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\mathrm{div}\,\vek b - \Big(\frac{\partial b_{\mathrm{i}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} + \frac{\partial b_{\mathrm{j}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}}\Big) . \tag{11.7}\]

Here \(\delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\) equals \(1\) for \(\ix{i} = \ix{j}\) and \(0\) otherwise. From these equations follows, by summing over \(i = j\) (step 6 of the proof),

\[\nabla^2\Theta = -\frac{1 + \nu}{1 - \nu}\,\mathrm{div}\,\vek b . \tag{11.8}\]
Proof

We must show that \(R_{\mathrm{ij}}\) for stresses that satisfy equilibrium equals, up to a constant factor, the difference of the left and right side of (11.7), if (11.8) holds. First we express \(R_{\mathrm{ij}}\) by the stresses, then we show that (11.8) follows from each of the two sides of the claim.

  1. Hooke's law (3.12) can be written for all six components as \(E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} = (1 + \nu)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}} - \nu\,\Theta\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\). For \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) this is \(\big((1 + \nu)\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} - \nu(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}})\big)/E = (\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} - \nu(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}))/E\), for the shear components \(\delta_{\mathrm{ij}} = 0\) and we get \((1 + \nu)\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}/E\). The sum of the diagonal components is \(E\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}) = (1 + \nu)\,\Theta - 3\nu\,\Theta = (1 - 2\nu)\,\Theta\).
  2. Because \(E\) and \(\nu\) are the same at every point (homogeneity), the derivatives of the strains are the derivatives of the stresses divided by \(E\). The first term of \(R_{\mathrm{ij}}\) from (11.6) is by step 1
    \[E\,\nabla^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} = (1 + \nu)\,\nabla^2\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}} - \nu\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\nabla^2\Theta ,\]
    the second is \(E\,\partial^2(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}})/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{j}} = (1 - 2\nu)\,\partial^2\Theta/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}\).
  3. The third term contains the sum \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{ik}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{k}}\). By the equilibrium equations \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{ik}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{k}} = -b_{\mathrm{i}}\) (the stress is symmetric, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ik}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{ki}}\), so the order of the indices in the equation does not matter). We exchange the derivatives, because the second derivatives of the stresses are continuous (theorem 1.125), and get
    \[\begin{aligned} E\sum_{\mathrm{k}} \frac{\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ik}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{k}}} &= (1 + \nu)\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}}\sum_{\mathrm{k}} \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{ik}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{k}}} - \nu\,\frac{\partial^2\Theta}{\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}} \\ &= -(1 + \nu)\,\frac{\partial b_{\mathrm{i}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} - \nu\,\frac{\partial^2\Theta}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} . \end{aligned}\]
    The fourth term is the same with \(i\) and \(j\) exchanged.
  4. We assemble \(E\,R_{\mathrm{ij}}\) from the terms of steps 2 and 3, the third and fourth term with the sign reversed:
    \[\begin{aligned} &E\,R_{\mathrm{ij}} = (1 + \nu)\,\nabla^2\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}} \\ &\quad + \frac{\partial^2\Theta}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} - \nu\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\nabla^2\Theta \\ &\quad + (1 + \nu)\Big(\frac{\partial b_{\mathrm{i}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} \\ &\quad + \frac{\partial b_{\mathrm{j}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}}\Big) . \end{aligned} \tag{11.9}\]
    For the second term we have \((1 - 2\nu) + \nu + \nu = 1\): the first term gives \(1 - 2\nu\), and the third and fourth have a minus in front and contain \(-\nu\,\Theta\), so each contributes \(+\nu\).
  5. We sum (11.9) over \(i = j\). The sum \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \nabla^2\sigma_{\mathrm{ii}}\) is \(\nabla^2\Theta\), the sum \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \partial^2\Theta/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}^2\) is \(\nabla^2\Theta\), \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \delta_{\mathrm{ii}} = 3\), the sum \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} 2\,\partial b_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\) is \(2\,\mathrm{div}\,\vek b\). Therefore
    \[E\sum_{\mathrm{i}} R_{\mathrm{ii}} = 2\,(1 - \nu)\,\nabla^2\Theta + 2\,(1 + \nu)\,\mathrm{div}\,\vek b ,\]
    because \((1 + \nu) + 1 - 3\nu = 2\,(1 - \nu)\). If all \(R_{\mathrm{ij}}\) are zero, the sum is zero, and since \(1 - \nu > 0\), (11.8) follows.
  6. If the equations (11.7) hold for all \(i\), \(j\), then (11.8) follows from them as well: their sum over \(i = j\) is \(\nabla^2\Theta + \nabla^2\Theta/(1 + \nu) = -3\nu\,\mathrm{div}\,\vek b/(1 - \nu) - 2\,\mathrm{div}\,\vek b\). The left side is \(\frac{2 + \nu}{1 + \nu}\,\nabla^2\Theta\), the right side \(-\frac{2 + \nu}{1 - \nu}\,\mathrm{div}\,\vek b\), and since \(2 + \nu > 0\), this gives (11.8).
  7. So let (11.8) hold, either from step 5 or from step 6. The term \(-\nu\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\nabla^2\Theta\) in (11.9) is then \(+\nu\,(1 + \nu)\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\mathrm{div}\,\vek b/(1 - \nu)\) and
    \[E\,R_{\mathrm{ij}} = (1 + \nu)\Big[\nabla^2\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}} + \frac{1}{1 + \nu}\,\frac{\partial^2\Theta}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} + \frac{\nu}{1 - \nu}\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\mathrm{div}\,\vek b + \frac{\partial b_{\mathrm{i}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} + \frac{\partial b_{\mathrm{j}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}}\Big] ,\]
    which is \((1 + \nu)\) times the left side of (11.7) from which we subtracted the right side. Therefore \(R_{\mathrm{ij}} = 0\) exactly when (11.7) holds.
  8. Let the compatibility equations hold. By lemma 11.8 \(R_{\mathrm{ij}} = 0\), by step 5 (11.8) holds, and by step 7 (11.7). Conversely, let the equations (11.7) hold. By step 6 (11.8) holds, by step 7 \(R_{\mathrm{ij}} = 0\) for all \(i\), \(j\), and by lemma 11.8 the compatibility equations hold.

Source: SADD (5.3.3), (5.3.4) (PDF pp. 109 to 110), CM (7.2.26) (in vector form, with \(\rho_0\,\mathbf f = \vek b\)) and (7.2.27) (constant body force) (PDF p. 300). The sources write the equations with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij},\mathrm{kk}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{kk},\mathrm{ij}}/(1 + \nu)\) on the left, as (11.7). The converse direction and step 6 are ours, not from the sources.

The proof showed that for stresses in equilibrium the compatibility equations are the same as the six equations (11.7). Equilibrium is not just an assumption, the computation needs it: it removed the derivatives of the stresses that cannot be expressed in any other way, and the body force remained only in first derivatives.

Let us test the theorem on examples. For \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = k\,y\) we have \(\nabla^2\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0\), \(\Theta = k\,y\) and its second derivatives are zero, \(\vek b = \vek 0\). All six equations are satisfied, as we saw with the strains. For \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = k\,y^2\) we have \(\Theta = k\,y^2\) and \(\vek b = \vek 0\), and

\[\text{equation } xx:\ \nabla^2\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \frac{1}{1 + \nu}\,\frac{\partial^2\Theta}{\partial x^2} = 2k = 5{,}0 \cdot 10^{10}\ \text{N/m}^4 \ne 0 , \qquad \text{equation } yy:\ 0 + \frac{1}{1 + \nu}\,\frac{\partial^2\Theta}{\partial y^2} = \frac{2k}{1 + \nu} = 3{,}85 \cdot 10^{10}\ \text{N/m}^4 \ne 0 .\]

The equations \(zz\), \(xy\), \(xz\) and \(yz\) are satisfied. Two equations fail, so the parabola is not a possible stress field, and the trace of the stress is not harmonic, \(\nabla^2\Theta = 2k \ne 0\), which contradicts (11.8) at \(\vek b = \vek 0\). The answer is the same as from the strains, as it must be. A body force would not give a solution: from the equilibrium equation for \(x\), \(b_{\mathrm{x}} = -\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/\partial x = 0\), and the other two components are also zero by equilibrium.

For the hanging bar of chapter 1 \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \rho g\,(L - x)\), the other components are zero, and the body force \(\vek b = \rho g\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) is constant, so all its derivatives and \(\mathrm{div}\,\vek b\) are zero. The stress is linear in \(x\), so all second derivatives are zero and both sides of (11.7) are zero for all nine index pairs. The strains are compatible, which chapter 1 already checked, and here we obtained this without strains. For a bar of length \(L = 10\) m the stress at the top is \(\rho g L = 0{,}7652\) MPa.

Chapter 5, in the elastic solution of a simply supported beam, required \(\nabla^2(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}) = 0\) and called it the compatibility condition of the plane problem. Now we see where it comes from: for a field that does not depend on \(z\) and has \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) we have \(\Theta = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) and \(\partial^2\Theta/\partial x^2 + \partial^2\Theta/\partial y^2 = \nabla^2\Theta\). The sum of the equations \(xx\) and \(yy\) from (11.7) is therefore \(\frac{2 + \nu}{1 + \nu}\,\nabla^2\Theta = 0\) at \(\vek b = \vek 0\), and since \(2 + \nu > 0\), this gives the condition of chapter 5. The condition is thus only one consequence, the sum of two of the six equations. That it does not suffice by itself is shown by the cantilever of chapter 5 with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = F\,(L - x)\,y/I\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = -F\,(c^2 - y^2)/(2I)\) and the other components zero. For it \(\Theta = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) is a linear function, so \(\nabla^2\Theta = 0\) and the condition of chapter 5 holds. The equation \(xy\) from (11.7), however, is \(\nabla^2\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} + \frac{1}{1 + \nu}\,\partial^2\Theta/\partial x\,\partial y = F/I - F/\big((1 + \nu)I\big) = \nu F/\big((1 + \nu)I\big)\) and is not zero. For \(F = 500\) N and \(I = 1{,}0667 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^4\) (cross section \(20 \times 40\) mm) this is \(1{,}082 \cdot 10^9\ \text{N/m}^4\). By step 7 of the proof \(R_{\mathrm{xy}} = \nu F/(EI) = 7{,}175 \cdot 10^{-3}\ \text{m}^{-2}\), which is exactly \(\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}/\partial x\,\partial y\) for \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = -\nu\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/E\). The cantilever field with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\) is therefore not an exact solution of the three-dimensional problem. In chapter 5 we used it as a model. In what sense it is a good model we say later in this chapter, with plane problems.

For a computation with stresses we now have nine equations: three equilibrium equations and the six equations (11.7), for six unknowns \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\). The number of equations exceeds the number of unknowns. This is not a contradiction, because the compatibility equations are not expressed independently: SADD (PDF p. 110) says that the six relations give only three independent results. We do not prove this here and nothing that follows relies on it. Figure 11.7 compares the two formulations: with displacements one connection leads us forward, with stresses compatibility has to be required separately, because there are no displacements.

11.3.3 From stresses to displacements (Od napetosti do pomikov)

The theorem gave a condition on the stresses inside the body. Do stresses that satisfy equilibrium, the Beltrami-Michell equations and the prescribed forces on the surface really make up a solution of the boundary value problem? For this we must find the displacement. The strains are compatible, and in a simply connected body compatibility suffices for a displacement (theorem 4.119). What remains is the question of what happens up to the surface of the body, because the theorem gives the displacement only in the interior. Here lemma 4.127 helps, which extends the displacement and its first derivatives to every cell of the body.

Corollary 11.10 Stress formulation (napetostna formulacija)

Let \(V\) be a body made of boxes (definition 4.11) whose interior points make up a connected (definition 1.130) and simply connected (definition 1.136) open set, of an isotropic homogeneous material with \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\). Let \(D\) be an open set that contains \(V\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{ji}}\) functions with continuous partial derivatives up to the second order on \(D\) and \(\vek b\) a body force with continuous first partial derivatives on \(D\). Let the equilibrium equations (2.31) and the equations (11.7) hold at every interior point of \(V\), and at every point of the surface Cauchy's formula \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \bar{\vek t}\) with a prescribed stress vector \(\bar{\vek t}\). Then:

  1. At the interior points of \(V\) there is a displacement field \(\vek u\) with continuous partial derivatives of the first and second order whose small strain tensor has, by Hooke's law, the stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\).
  2. The displacement \(\vek u\) and its partial derivatives of the first order extend to continuous functions on every cell of the body, which agree on the common faces of the cells.
  3. If the extended displacement can be extended to a continuously differentiable field on an open set \(W \subset D\) that contains the body together with its surface, and this field has the small strain tensor with components \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) on \(W\), then \(\vek u\) is a solution of the boundary value problem with body force \(\vek b\) and prescribed stress vector \(\bar{\vek t}\) on the whole surface, without prescribed displacement, and by theorem 11.4 it is unique up to a small rigid displacement.
Proof

We must show that the stresses give a displacement and that it satisfies the conditions of the boundary value problem. We get the displacement from compatibility and its stresses from Hooke's law.

  1. Let \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) be the strains that we get from the stresses by Hooke's law (3.12). They have continuous first and second partial derivatives on \(D\), because the stresses have them. By theorem 11.9, applied at every interior point, they satisfy the compatibility equations.
  2. The interior points of \(V\) make up a connected and simply connected open set, so by theorem 4.119 there is a displacement field \(\vek u\), continuously differentiable, with continuous second derivatives and with a small strain tensor with components \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\). This is point 1, except for the stresses.
  3. Hooke's law in Lamé's form (3.13), which is the relation (3.12) solved for the stresses (theorem 3.11), determines the stresses from the strains. Because the \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) are the strains that belong to the stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\), the stresses of the field \(\vek u\), that is \(\lambda\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}})\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}} + 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\), equal \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\). This completes point 1.
  4. The functions \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) have continuous first and second derivatives on \(D \supset V\), and \(\vek u\) has the strains \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) at interior points. By lemma 4.127 point 2 holds.
  5. The equilibrium equations and \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \bar{\vek t}\) hold for the stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\) by assumption, so by step 3 they hold for the stresses of the field \(\vek u\). The continuous differentiability of the extension on \(W\) is requirement 1 of definition 11.1. On \(W\) the stresses of the extension are equal to \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\) by step 3, so continuously differentiable, as point 2 of the definition requires. The prescribed stress vector \(\bar{\vek t}\) is continuous on every exterior face, because the \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\) are continuous there and the normal is constant. All requirements of a solution (definition 11.1) are therefore satisfied, and by theorem 11.4 this is the only solution up to a small rigid displacement. This is point 3.

Source: SADD §5.3 (PDF p. 110): the strains are obtained from the stresses by Hooke's law, the displacements by integration, which determines the displacements only up to a rigid displacement, and the displacements are single-valued only if the region is simply connected. The connection with the theorems of chapter 4 and the proof of this theorem are not from the sources.

The proof showed that in a body made of boxes that is simply connected, stresses with equilibrium and the Beltrami-Michell equations suffice for a displacement, and that it is a solution, if it extends continuously differentiably beyond the surface, and then the only one. The requirement of an extension of the displacement to the surroundings of the body did not follow from the lemma, which gives only an extension by cells. For every solution of this chapter that we assemble in closed form we therefore check it directly. We have not proved the corollary for bodies made of curved boxes, because there we have no extension to the cells, and there we will always assemble the solutions directly. For the hanging bar with a rectangular cross section (a box) the stresses are linear in \(x\), the Beltrami-Michell equations hold (above), the cross section is simply connected and the force at the top is uniform, \(\rho g L = 0{,}7652\) MPa upwards, so the corollary gives a displacement field. Later in this chapter we find it by integration and show that it is the field of chapter 1.

When does the stress formulation suit? For a boundary value problem with prescribed forces on the whole surface, because then there is no condition on the displacement. When the displacement is prescribed on a part of the surface, one more computation remains after the stresses are found: we must find the displacement and check that on the prescribed part it really equals the prescribed one, which the condition of theorem 4.130 expresses with virtual forces. The stress formulation therefore suits problems with prescribed forces best, and the formulation with displacements problems with prescribed displacements.

The condition that the interior points of the body are simply connected is essential. In a body with a hole, for example the ring of chapter 4, the strains can be compatible at every point and still not give a single displacement field across a cut (corollary 4.113). We will meet such a case again with polar coordinates later in this chapter.
