Chapter 11 The boundary value problem of linear elasticity

11.3 Beltrami-Michell equations (Beltrami-Michellove enačbe)

In section 11.2 we collected the equations of the boundary value problem (definition 11.1) into three equations for the displacement. The displacement is the only unknown there, and the strains and stresses are computed from it. Can it also be done the other way, with the stresses as the only unknown? This route is natural when forces are prescribed on the surface, because the stress gives them directly (Cauchy's formula, theorem 2.26). A question appears at once that did not arise with displacements: which equations must a stress field satisfy for it to be possible at all?

Let us look at an example. Take a bar of rectangular cross section lying along the \(x\) axis, with depth \(2c = 40\) mm, the \(y\) axis pointing up along the depth and the \(z\) axis perpendicular to the plane of the drawing. The material is the steel of the book, \(E = 196\) GPa and \(\nu = 0{,}3\). Let the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = k\,y^2\) be the only nonzero component, with the constant \(k = 2{,}5 \cdot 10^{10}\ \text{N/m}^4\), so that at the top and bottom edges \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(\pm c) = k\,c^2 = 10\) MPa. The equilibrium equations (theorem 2.31) hold without body force, because the field does not depend on \(x\), so \(\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/\partial x = 0\), and the other components are zero. For comparison take the field \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = k\,c\,y\), which equals the pure bending of chapter 5. The factor \(c\) is there so that \(k\,c\,y\) has the unit of a stress, and this field too has \(\pm 10\) MPa at the edges. This field is in equilibrium too. We believe pure bending, because a bar really does bend. Do we believe the parabola as well?

We look for the answer through the strains. By Hooke's law (theorem 3.19) \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/E\), so the fibre at height \(y\) stretches or shortens in proportion to \(k\,c\,y\) in the first field and in proportion to \(k\,y^2\) in the second. We imagine the bar as a bundle of fibres along the \(x\) axis and let each fibre lengthen by its own amount (figure 11.28). At the top is the stress over the depth. In the middle all fibres start at a common left face and before loading are of equal length \(\ell\). The top fibre lengthens by \(\ell\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}(c)\), where \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}(c) = 10\ \text{MPa}/196\ \text{GPa} = 5{,}10 \cdot 10^{-5}\), magnified 4000 times in the figure. In the first field the elongations are linear in \(y\). The upper fibres are longer, the lower ones shorter, and a surface that was flat stays flat and only turns: the right ends of the fibres lie on a straight line.

Why do such fibres fit against each other? We bend them about a common centre \(O\), all through the same angle \(\theta\), and let them all start at a common left face. An arc of radius \(r\) and angle \(\theta\) in radians has length \(r\,\theta\), since the angle in radians is exactly the length of the arc divided by the radius. The middle fibre is at the distance \(\rho\) from the centre and has length \(\rho\,\theta\), the fibre at height \(y\) above it \((\rho + y)\,\theta = \rho\,\theta + y\,\theta\). So the length grows linearly with \(y\): the outer fibre is longer, the inner one shorter, by the same amount on each side. These are exactly the elongations of the first field, and the right ends lie on a radius through \(O\), that is on a straight line. Such bending is what bending of a beam is. In the second field, however, the upper and the lower fibre are both longer than the middle one, and the right ends lie on a parabola. In a bending with one centre one of the two outer fibres is always on the inner side and shorter than the middle one, so the fibres of the right field do not fit in this way. It seems that no displacement field gives such strains. We know the exact condition for this from chapter 1: strains that come from a displacement field satisfy the compatibility equations (1.76) (theorem 1.133), and in a simply connected body these equations are also sufficient (theorem 4.120). In a simply connected body, stresses are therefore possible exactly when the strains we get from them by Hooke's law are possible. The compatibility equations for these strains are the condition we are looking for on the stresses.

This section answers the question what compatibility demands of the stresses. Figure 11.29 shows the route.

The top row is the route of the previous section: from the displacement through the strains to the stresses, which equilibrium closes into the three Navier-Lamé equations. The bottom row is the route of this section and runs the other way. We start at the six unknown stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\). Hooke's law gives the strains from them, for which we require compatibility. First we transform the compatibility equations into the form \(R_{\mathrm{ij}} = 0\), which fits the equilibrium equations, then we insert Hooke's law and equilibrium, which both rows share. The result will be the Beltrami-Michell equations.

11.3.1 Contracted compatibility equations (Skrčene enačbe kompatibilnosti)

The compatibility equations (1.76) contain the second derivative of the shear strain, for example \(\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}/\partial x\,\partial y\). If we insert Hooke's law in it, we get the second derivative of the shear stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\), which the equilibrium equations do not know: they contain only first derivatives, in the sum \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{ik}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{k}}\). Such sums arise in expressions where one index is summed. Lemma 4.106 showed that the compatibility equations are the same as the vanishing of all numbers \(Z_{\mathrm{ijkl}}\). Take the numbers \(Z_{\mathrm{ikjk}}\), where the second and fourth index are equal, and for fixed \(i\), \(j\) sum them over \(k\). We get nine numbers \(R_{\mathrm{ij}}\) of the node \(R_{\mathrm{ij}} = 0\), of which by symmetry only six are different. Did we lose information by summing? The lemma says we did not.

On the left is the table of figure 4.344: the rows are the pairs \(ij\), the columns the pairs \(kl\). Grey lines divide it into nine blocks of \(3 \times 3\) cells, in each the first indices of row and column are fixed, \(i\) and \(j\). In \(Z_{\mathrm{ikjk}}\) the second index of the row equals the second index of the column, both are \(k\), so these numbers lie on the diagonals of the blocks. The sum does not use the other 54 cells. The number \(R_{\mathrm{ij}}\) is the sum of the three numbers on the diagonal of block \(i\), \(j\), for block \(xx\) this is \(0 + 1 + 3\), so the sum of the differences of the sides of the first and third equation (1.76). The nine sums make up the table \(R_{\mathrm{ij}}\) on the right.

Lemma 11.8 Contracted compatibility equations (skrčene enačbe kompatibilnosti)

Let \(D\) be an open set of points and let \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{ji}}\), where the indices run over \(x\), \(y\), \(z\), be functions whose partial derivatives of the first and second order exist and are continuous at every point of \(D\). For the indices \(i\), \(j\) let

\[\begin{aligned} &R_{\mathrm{ij}} = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} Z_{\mathrm{ikjk}} \\ &\quad = \nabla^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} \\ &\quad + \frac{\partial^2(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}})}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} \\ &\quad - \sum_{\mathrm{k}} \frac{\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ik}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{k}}} \\ &\quad - \sum_{\mathrm{k}} \frac{\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{jk}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{k}}} , \end{aligned} \tag{11.6}\]

where \(Z_{\mathrm{ikjk}}\) is the expression (4.77) with the indices \(i\), \(k\), \(j\), \(k\) and \(\nabla^2\) is the operator of definition 11.5. At a point \(P\) of \(D\) the compatibility equations (1.76) hold exactly when the number \(R_{\mathrm{ij}}\) is zero at \(P\) for all nine choices of the indices \(i\), \(j\).

Proof

We must show that compatibility implies \(R_{\mathrm{ij}} = 0\) and that \(R_{\mathrm{ij}} = 0\) implies compatibility. We express the six expressions whose zeros are the compatibility equations by the \(R_{\mathrm{ij}}\) and the other way round.

  1. Expression (4.77) with \(i\), \(k\), \(j\), \(k\) for \(i\), \(j\), \(k\), \(l\) is
    \[Z_{\mathrm{ikjk}} = \frac{\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{k}}^2} + \frac{\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{kk}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} - \frac{\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ik}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{k}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} - \frac{\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{kj}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{k}}} .\]
    Summing the first term over \(k\) gives \(\nabla^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\), the second \(\partial^2(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}})/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}\). Swap the derivatives (theorem 1.131) in the rest. This is (11.6).
  2. Let \(S_1, \dots, S_6\) be the difference of the left and right side of the equations (1.76) in order. By steps 7 and 8 of the proof of lemma 4.106, \(S_1 = Z_{\mathrm{xyxy}}\), \(S_2 = Z_{\mathrm{yzyz}}\), \(S_3 = Z_{\mathrm{zxzx}}\), \(S_4 = Z_{\mathrm{xyxz}}\), \(S_5 = Z_{\mathrm{yzyx}}\), \(S_6 = Z_{\mathrm{zxzy}}\). In the table of the 81 numbers these are the cells with the numbers 1 to 6.
  3. In \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} Z_{\mathrm{ikjk}}\) the terms \(k = i\) and \(k = j\) are zero, since for \(k = i\) the first two indices are equal, \(Z_{\mathrm{iiji}}\), and for \(k = j\) the last two, \(Z_{\mathrm{ijjj}}\). On the diagonal of each block these are the grey zeros (step 4 of lemma 4.106). For \(R_{\mathrm{xx}}\) the terms \(k = y\), \(z\) remain, the cells 1 and 3 on the diagonal of block \(xx\):
    \[R_{\mathrm{xx}} = Z_{\mathrm{xyxy}} + Z_{\mathrm{xzxz}} = S_1 + S_3 .\]
    Here \(Z_{\mathrm{xzxz}} = \partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}/\partial z^2 + \partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}/\partial x^2 - 2\,\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}/\partial x\,\partial z\) is the difference of the sides of the third equation (1.76), so \(S_3\), and \(Z_{\mathrm{xyxy}} = S_1\) by step 2.
  4. By \(Z_{\mathrm{ijkl}} = Z_{\mathrm{klij}}\), \(Z_{\mathrm{jikl}} = -Z_{\mathrm{ijkl}}\), \(Z_{\mathrm{ijlk}} = -Z_{\mathrm{ijkl}}\) (steps 1 to 3 of lemma 4.106),
    \[R_{\mathrm{yy}} = Z_{\mathrm{yxyx}} + Z_{\mathrm{yzyz}} = S_1 + S_2 , \qquad R_{\mathrm{zz}} = Z_{\mathrm{zxzx}} + Z_{\mathrm{zyzy}} = S_3 + S_2 ,\]
    \[R_{\mathrm{yz}} = Z_{\mathrm{yxzx}} = -Z_{\mathrm{xyzx}} = Z_{\mathrm{xyxz}} = S_4 ,\]
    \[R_{\mathrm{xz}} = Z_{\mathrm{xyzy}} = Z_{\mathrm{zyxy}} = -Z_{\mathrm{yzxy}} = Z_{\mathrm{yzyx}} = S_5 ,\]
    \[R_{\mathrm{xy}} = Z_{\mathrm{xzyz}} = Z_{\mathrm{yzxz}} = -Z_{\mathrm{zyxz}} = Z_{\mathrm{zyzx}} = Z_{\mathrm{zxzy}} = S_6 .\]
    By the same steps \(R_{\mathrm{ji}} = R_{\mathrm{ij}}\), in a table:
    \[\begin{pmatrix} R_{\mathrm{xx}} & R_{\mathrm{xy}} & R_{\mathrm{xz}} \\ R_{\mathrm{yx}} & R_{\mathrm{yy}} & R_{\mathrm{yz}} \\ R_{\mathrm{zx}} & R_{\mathrm{zy}} & R_{\mathrm{zz}} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} S_1 + S_3 & S_6 & S_5 \\ S_6 & S_1 + S_2 & S_4 \\ S_5 & S_4 & S_2 + S_3 \end{pmatrix} .\]
    Off the diagonal of the table \(R_{\mathrm{ij}}\) stand \(S_4\), \(S_5\), \(S_6\), on the diagonal sums of two of \(S_1\), \(S_2\), \(S_3\).
  5. If the compatibility equations hold, then \(S_1 = \dots = S_6 = 0\) and by step 4 \(R_{\mathrm{ij}} = 0\) for all \(i\), \(j\).
  6. Conversely, if \(R_{\mathrm{ij}} = 0\) for all \(i\), \(j\), then by step 4 \(S_4 = S_5 = S_6 = 0\), and
    \[\begin{aligned} S_1 &= \tfrac12\,(R_{\mathrm{xx}} + R_{\mathrm{yy}} - R_{\mathrm{zz}}) , \\ S_2 &= \tfrac12\,(R_{\mathrm{yy}} + R_{\mathrm{zz}} - R_{\mathrm{xx}}) , \\ S_3 &= \tfrac12\,(R_{\mathrm{xx}} + R_{\mathrm{zz}} - R_{\mathrm{yy}}) , \end{aligned}\]
    which on insertion from step 4 indeed gives \(S_1\), \(S_2\), \(S_3\) (for example \(R_{\mathrm{xx}} + R_{\mathrm{yy}} - R_{\mathrm{zz}} = 2S_1 + S_2 + S_3 - S_2 - S_3 = 2S_1\)). So \(S_1\), \(S_2\), \(S_3\) are zero as well and all compatibility equations hold.

Source: SADD §5.3 (PDF p. 109): the six meaningful equations are obtained with \(k = l\) in the general form (5.1.2), so by contraction over one index. The converse direction, the solution in step 6, is ours. The proof by inversion is not from the sources. SymPy checked all six relations of step 4 for general functions.

The lemma showed that the nine contracted numbers carry the same content as the six compatibility equations, only in a form where the sum \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} \partial\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ik}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{k}}\) appears. Let us test it on the bar of the introduction. The strains of the field \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = k\,y^2\) are by Hooke's law \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = k\,y^2/E\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = -\nu\,k\,y^2/E\), the other components are zero. The only nonzero second derivatives are \(\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}/\partial y^2 = 2k/E\) and \(\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}/\partial y^2 = \partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}/\partial y^2 = -2\nu k/E\). We see the first of them in figure 11.28. The right end of the fibre at height \(y\) lies at the distance \(\ell\,(1 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}(y))\) from the common left face, so the second derivative of this distance with respect to \(y\) is \(\ell\,\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}/\partial y^2\). The number \(\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}/\partial y^2\) therefore measures how much the curve through the right ends of the fibres is bent. For the straight line of the left field it is zero, for the parabola \(2k/E\). We insert the derivatives into the six differences of step 2 of the proof and then into the table of step 4:

\[\begin{aligned} S_1 &= \frac{\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}}{\partial y^2} + \frac{\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}}{\partial x^2} - 2\,\frac{\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}}{\partial x\,\partial y} = \frac{2k}{E} + 0 - 0 = 0{,}2551\ \text{m}^{-2} , \\ S_2 &= \frac{\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}}{\partial z^2} + \frac{\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}}{\partial y^2} - 2\,\frac{\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}}{\partial y\,\partial z} = 0 - \frac{2\nu k}{E} - 0 = -0{,}0765\ \text{m}^{-2} , \\ S_3 &= S_4 = S_5 = S_6 = 0 , \end{aligned}\]
\[R_{\mathrm{xx}} = S_1 + S_3 = 0{,}2551\ \text{m}^{-2} , \qquad R_{\mathrm{yy}} = S_1 + S_2 = 0{,}1786\ \text{m}^{-2} , \qquad R_{\mathrm{zz}} = S_2 + S_3 = -0{,}0765\ \text{m}^{-2} .\]

The diagonal numbers are not zero, so the parabola is not a possible stress field. For the field \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = k\,c\,y\) every second derivative of the strains is zero and compatibility holds, as the straight line shows. SymPy checked the computation.

11.3.2 Stress formulation (Napetostna formulacija)

Now into \(R_{\mathrm{ij}} = 0\) we insert Hooke's law and equilibrium, so that only the stresses remain as unknowns. The sum \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\), that is the trace (sled) of the stress tensor, we will write \(\Theta\). The equations also contain first derivatives of the body force, which the equilibrium equations connect with the stresses.

Theorem 11.9 Beltrami-Michell equations (Beltrami-Michellove enačbe)

Let the material be isotropic and homogeneous (definitions 3.10 and 3.11) with modulus of elasticity \(E\) and Poisson's ratio \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\) (theorem 3.64), with Hooke's law (3.27). Let \(D\) be an open set of points, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{ji}}\) functions with continuous partial derivatives up to the second order on \(D\) and \(\Theta = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\). Let the equilibrium equations (2.38) \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}} + b_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\) hold at every point of \(D\) with a body force \(\vek b = (b_{\mathrm{x}}, b_{\mathrm{y}}, b_{\mathrm{z}})\) whose components have continuous first partial derivatives. Let \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) be the strains that the stresses determine by Hooke's law. Then at a point \(P\) of \(D\) the compatibility equations (1.76) hold exactly when at \(P\) for all nine choices of the indices \(i\), \(j\)

\[\nabla^2\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}} + \frac{1}{1 + \nu}\,\frac{\partial^2\Theta}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} = -\frac{\nu}{1 - \nu}\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\mathrm{div}\,\vek b - \Big(\frac{\partial b_{\mathrm{i}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} + \frac{\partial b_{\mathrm{j}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}}\Big) . \tag{11.7}\]
\[\sigma_{\mathrm{ij},\mathrm{kk}} + \frac{1}{1 + \nu}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{kk},\mathrm{ij}} = -\frac{\nu}{1 - \nu}\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\,b_{\mathrm{k},\mathrm{k}} - b_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j}} - b_{\mathrm{j},\mathrm{i}} \tag{11.7}\]

Here \(\delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\) equals \(1\) for \(\ix{i} = \ix{j}\) and \(0\) otherwise. From these six equations follows, by summing over \(i = j\) (step 6 of the proof),

\[\nabla^2\Theta = -\frac{1 + \nu}{1 - \nu}\,\mathrm{div}\,\vek b . \tag{11.8}\]
Proof

We must show that \(R_{\mathrm{ij}}\) for stresses that satisfy equilibrium equals, up to a constant factor, the difference of the left and right side of (11.7), if (11.8) holds. First we express \(R_{\mathrm{ij}}\) by the stresses, then we show that (11.8) follows from each of the two sides of the claim.

  1. Hooke's law (3.27), the arrow from the stresses to the strains, can be written for all six components as \(E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} = (1 + \nu)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}} - \nu\,\Theta\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\)\(E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} = (1 + \nu)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}} - \nu\,\sigma_{\mathrm{kk}}\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\). For \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) this is \(\big((1 + \nu)\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} - \nu(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}})\big)/E = (\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} - \nu(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}))/E\), for the shear components \(\delta_{\mathrm{ij}} = 0\) and we get \((1 + \nu)\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}/E\). The sum of the diagonal components is \(E\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}) = (1 + \nu)\,\Theta - 3\nu\,\Theta = (1 - 2\nu)\,\Theta\).
  2. Because \(E\) and \(\nu\) are the same at every point (homogeneity, which the bar of two materials lacks), they stay in front of the derivatives of the stresses as constants when we differentiate the relation of step 1. The first term of \(R_{\mathrm{ij}}\) from (11.6) is by step 1
    \[E\,\nabla^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} = (1 + \nu)\,\nabla^2\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}} - \nu\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\nabla^2\Theta ,\]
    the second is \(E\,\partial^2(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}})/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{j}} = (1 - 2\nu)\,\partial^2\Theta/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}\).
  3. The third term contains the sum \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{ik}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{k}}\). Here equilibrium enters. Because the stress is symmetric, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ik}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{ki}}\), the equilibrium equations give \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{ik}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{k}} = -b_{\mathrm{i}}\). We exchange the derivatives, because the second derivatives of the stresses are continuous (theorem 1.131), and get
    \[\begin{aligned} E\sum_{\mathrm{k}} \frac{\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ik}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{k}}} &= (1 + \nu)\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}}\sum_{\mathrm{k}} \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{ik}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{k}}} - \nu\,\frac{\partial^2\Theta}{\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}} \\ &= -(1 + \nu)\,\frac{\partial b_{\mathrm{i}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} - \nu\,\frac{\partial^2\Theta}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} . \end{aligned}\]
    The fourth term is the same with \(i\) and \(j\) exchanged.
  4. \(E\,R_{\mathrm{ij}}\) from steps 2 and 3, third and fourth term with reversed sign:
    \[\begin{aligned} &E\,R_{\mathrm{ij}} = (1 + \nu)\,\nabla^2\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}} \\ &\quad + \frac{\partial^2\Theta}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} - \nu\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\nabla^2\Theta \\ &\quad + (1 + \nu)\Big(\frac{\partial b_{\mathrm{i}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} \\ &\quad + \frac{\partial b_{\mathrm{j}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}}\Big) . \end{aligned} \tag{11.9}\]
    The coefficient of \(\partial^2\Theta/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}\) is \((1 - 2\nu) + \nu + \nu = 1\): the second term of \(R_{\mathrm{ij}}\) gives \(1 - 2\nu\) (step 2), the third and fourth, minus in front, contain \(-\nu\,\partial^2\Theta/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}\) (step 3) and give \(+\nu\).
  5. We sum (11.9) over \(i = j\). The sum \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \nabla^2\sigma_{\mathrm{ii}}\) is \(\nabla^2\Theta\), the sum \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \partial^2\Theta/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}^2\) is \(\nabla^2\Theta\), \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \delta_{\mathrm{ii}} = 3\), the sum \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} 2\,\partial b_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\) is \(2\,\mathrm{div}\,\vek b\). Therefore
    \[E\sum_{\mathrm{i}} R_{\mathrm{ii}} = 2\,(1 - \nu)\,\nabla^2\Theta + 2\,(1 + \nu)\,\mathrm{div}\,\vek b ,\]
    because \((1 + \nu) + 1 - 3\nu = 2\,(1 - \nu)\). If all \(R_{\mathrm{ij}}\) are zero, the sum is zero, and since \(1 - \nu > 0\), (11.8) follows.
  6. If the equations (11.7) hold for all \(i\), \(j\), then (11.8) follows from them as well: their sum over \(i = j\) is \(\nabla^2\Theta + \nabla^2\Theta/(1 + \nu) = -3\nu\,\mathrm{div}\,\vek b/(1 - \nu) - 2\,\mathrm{div}\,\vek b\). The left side is \(\frac{2 + \nu}{1 + \nu}\,\nabla^2\Theta\), the right side \(-\frac{2 + \nu}{1 - \nu}\,\mathrm{div}\,\vek b\), and since \(2 + \nu > 0\), this gives (11.8).
  7. So let (11.8) hold, either from step 5 or from step 6. The term \(-\nu\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\nabla^2\Theta\) in (11.9) is then \(+\nu\,(1 + \nu)\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\mathrm{div}\,\vek b/(1 - \nu)\) and
    \[E\,R_{\mathrm{ij}} = (1 + \nu)\Big[\nabla^2\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}} + \frac{1}{1 + \nu}\,\frac{\partial^2\Theta}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} + \frac{\nu}{1 - \nu}\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\mathrm{div}\,\vek b + \frac{\partial b_{\mathrm{i}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} + \frac{\partial b_{\mathrm{j}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}}\Big] ,\]
    which is \((1 + \nu)\) times the left side of (11.7) from which we subtracted the right side. Therefore \(R_{\mathrm{ij}} = 0\) exactly when (11.7) holds, which is the end of the bottom row.
  8. Let the compatibility equations hold. By lemma 11.8 \(R_{\mathrm{ij}} = 0\), by step 5 (11.8) holds, and by step 7 (11.7), the end of the bottom row. Conversely, let the equations (11.7) hold. By step 6 (11.8) holds, by step 7 \(R_{\mathrm{ij}} = 0\) for all \(i\), \(j\), and by lemma 11.8 the compatibility equations hold.

Source: SADD (5.3.3), (5.3.4) (PDF pp. 109 to 110), CM (7.2.26) (in vector form, with \(\rho_0\,\mathbf f = \vek b\)) and (7.2.27) (constant body force) (PDF p. 300). The sources write the equations with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij},\mathrm{kk}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{kk},\mathrm{ij}}/(1 + \nu)\) on the left, as (11.7). The converse direction and step 6 are ours, not from the sources.

The proof showed that for stresses in equilibrium the compatibility equations are the same as the six equations (11.7). Equilibrium is not just an assumption, the computation needs it: in step 3 it replaced the mixed second derivatives \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} \partial^2\sigma_{\mathrm{ik}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{k}}\), which cannot be expressed through \(\nabla^2\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\) and \(\Theta\), by first derivatives of the body force, \(-\partial b_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}\). That is why the body force enters the equations only through first derivatives.

The proof used four more assumptions. For each let us say in which step it enters, why it is right here, when it is not and what happens without it.

  1. Homogeneity (step 2). We obtained the derivatives of the strains by differentiating the relation of step 1 with \(E\) and \(\nu\) left in front of the derivatives as constants. This is right for a piece of one steel, where \(E\) and \(\nu\) are the same everywhere, but not for a body of two materials. Take a bar glued from steel with \(E = 196\) GPa and an aluminium alloy with \(E = 74\) GPa (figure 3.32), and put into it a uniform stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 10\) MPa. The equations (11.7) do not reject it, since all its derivatives are zero. The fibres that are equally long before loading and start at the common left face, however, stretch differently, the steel one by \(10/196\,000 = 5{,}10 \cdot 10^{-5}\), the aluminium one by \(10/74\,000 = 1{,}351 \cdot 10^{-4}\), and their right ends make a step at the joint of \(8{,}41 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\ell\). The glued faces must not separate, so the neighbouring fibres at the joint would have to have the same displacement, and the step says that they do not. A uniform stress in such a bar is therefore not possible, yet the equations (11.7) would accept it. So we write the equations for each material separately, and at the joint we require the same displacement of both sides and opposite forces with which one side acts on the other (the law of action and reaction).
  2. Isotropy (step 1). The form of Hooke's law with two numbers, \(E\) and \(\nu\), holds because the material responds equally in all directions (definition 3.10), in steel because its crystal grains are turned in all directions. It does not hold for wood and for fibre composites, where Hooke's law needs more numbers (section 3.4). The compatibility equations for the strains stay the same, but their form with stresses does not: the coefficients \(1/(1 + \nu)\) and \(\nu/(1 - \nu)\) in (11.7) come from step 1 and for an anisotropic material others replace them.
  3. Bounds of Poisson's ratio (steps 5 to 7). The proof divides by \(1 - \nu\), \(1 + \nu\) and \(2 + \nu\), which must not be zero. The bounds \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\) (theorem 3.64) guarantee this with a margin, for steel with \(\nu = 0{,}3\) the numbers are \(0{,}7\), \(1{,}3\) and \(2{,}3\).
  4. Continuous second derivatives of the stresses (step 3). Exchanging the order of differentiation needs them. At the joint of two materials they do not exist: the glued fibres at the joint stretch equally, and at equal strain steel carries a larger stress than aluminium (for uniaxial stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\)), so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) jumps across the joint. There the equations hold only on each side of the joint separately, as in point 1.

Let us test the theorem on examples. For \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = k\,c\,y\) we have \(\nabla^2\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0\), \(\Theta = k\,c\,y\) and its second derivatives are zero, \(\vek b = \vek 0\). All six equations are satisfied, as we saw with the strains on the straight line. For \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = k\,y^2\) we have \(\Theta = k\,y^2\) and \(\vek b = \vek 0\), and

\[\text{equation } xx:\ \nabla^2\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \frac{1}{1 + \nu}\,\frac{\partial^2\Theta}{\partial x^2} = 2k = 5{,}0 \cdot 10^{10}\ \text{N/m}^4 \ne 0 , \qquad \text{equation } yy:\ 0 + \frac{1}{1 + \nu}\,\frac{\partial^2\Theta}{\partial y^2} = \frac{2k}{1 + \nu} = 3{,}85 \cdot 10^{10}\ \text{N/m}^4 \ne 0 .\]

The equations \(zz\), \(xy\), \(xz\) and \(yz\) are satisfied. Two equations fail, so the parabola is not a possible stress field, and also \(\nabla^2\Theta = 2k\) is not zero, which contradicts (11.8) at \(\vek b = \vek 0\). The answer is the same as from the strains, as it must be. A body force would not give a solution: from the equilibrium equation for \(x\), \(b_{\mathrm{x}} = -\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/\partial x = 0\), and the other two components are also zero by equilibrium.

The equation (11.8) has a simple picture. For the bar \(\Theta = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), because it is the only nonzero component. On the stress graph of the right field let us compare the value of \(\Theta\) at the middle, \(y = 0\), with the average of the values at \(y = \pm c\). Both edge values are \(10\) MPa, their average is \(10\) MPa, and at the middle \(\Theta = 0\). The middle is below the average by \(k\,c^2 = 10\) MPa. For a quadratic function, such as \(\Theta = k\,y^2\), this difference at every distance \(h\) to the neighbouring points is exactly

\[\frac{\Theta(y - h) + \Theta(y + h)}{2} - \Theta(y) = \frac{k\,(y - h)^2 + k\,(y + h)^2}{2} - k\,y^2 = k\,h^2 = \frac{h^2}{2}\,\frac{\partial^2\Theta}{\partial y^2} ,\]

so at \(h = c\) it is \(k\,c^2\). For the left field the middle \(0\) is exactly the average of the values \(10\) and \(-10\) MPa, because the second derivative of a straight line is zero.

In space we do the same in the directions \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\). Around the point \(P\) we take six neighbouring points, two on each of the axes, all at distance \(h\) from \(P\). The sum of the three differences, one for each axis, is \(\tfrac12 h^2\,\nabla^2\Theta\). Without a body force (11.8) therefore says: the sum of the normal stresses at a point equals the average of its values at the six neighbouring points at distance \(h\) along the axes. For a quadratic \(\Theta\) this holds exactly, for a general one by Taylor's theorem 8.68 up to an error that goes to zero faster than \(h^2\).

Let us test this at \(h = c = 20\) mm. For the left field \(\Theta = k\,c\,y\) the values on the \(y\) axis are \(10\) and \(-10\) MPa, on the axes \(x\) and \(z\) they are \(0\). The average of the six is \(0\), the same as at \(P\). For the parabola \(\Theta = k\,y^2\) both values on the \(y\) axis are \(10\) MPa, and in the directions \(x\) and \(z\) the \(\Theta\) does not change. The average of the six is \(20/6 = 3{,}33\) MPa, while at \(P\) it is \(\Theta = 0\). The value at \(P\) is \(k\,c^2/3 = 3{,}33\) MPa below the average, which is exactly \(h^2\,\nabla^2\Theta/6\) for \(\nabla^2\Theta = 2k\): the average of the six differs from the value at \(P\) by a third of the sum of the three differences. The parabola therefore does not satisfy (11.8).

For the hanging bar of chapter 1 \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \rho g\,(L - x)\), the other components are zero, and the body force \(\vek b = \rho g\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) is constant, so all its derivatives and \(\mathrm{div}\,\vek b\) are zero. For a constant body force, such as weight, the right side of (11.7) is zero and the equations, multiplied by \(1 + \nu\), are

\[(1 + \nu)\,\nabla^2\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}} + \frac{\partial^2\Theta}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} = 0 . \tag{11.10}\]
\[(1 + \nu)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{ij},\mathrm{kk}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{kk},\mathrm{ij}} = 0 \tag{11.10}\]

The stress of the hanging bar is linear in \(x\), so all second derivatives are zero and the equations hold for all nine index pairs. The strains are compatible, which chapter 1 already checked, and here we obtained this without strains. For a bar of length \(L = 10\) m the stress at the top, where the ceiling pulls the bar upwards, is \(\rho g L = 0{,}7652\) MPa.

Chapter 5, in the elastic solution of a simply supported beam, required \(\nabla^2(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}) = 0\) and called it the compatibility condition of the plane problem. Now we see where it comes from: for a field that does not depend on \(z\) and has \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) we have \(\Theta = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) and \(\partial^2\Theta/\partial x^2 + \partial^2\Theta/\partial y^2 = \nabla^2\Theta\). The sum of the equations \(xx\) and \(yy\) from (11.7) is therefore \(\frac{2 + \nu}{1 + \nu}\,\nabla^2\Theta = 0\) at \(\vek b = \vek 0\), and since \(2 + \nu > 0\), this gives the condition of chapter 5. The condition is thus only one consequence, the sum of two of the six equations. That it does not suffice by itself is shown by the cantilever of chapter 5 with a force \(F\) at the free end, with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = F\,(L - x)\,y/I\), linear over the height, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = -F\,(c^2 - y^2)/(2I)\) and the other components zero. For it \(\Theta = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) is a linear function, so \(\nabla^2\Theta = 0\) and the condition of chapter 5 holds. The equation \(xy\) from (11.7), however, is not satisfied, for \(F = 500\) N and \(I = 1{,}0667 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^4\) (cross section \(20 \times 40\) mm):

\[\nabla^2\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} + \frac{1}{1 + \nu}\,\frac{\partial^2\Theta}{\partial x\,\partial y} = \frac{F}{I} - \frac{F}{(1 + \nu)\,I} = \frac{\nu F}{(1 + \nu)\,I} = 1{,}082 \cdot 10^9\ \text{N/m}^4 .\]

By step 7 of the proof \(R_{\mathrm{xy}} = \nu F/(EI) = 7{,}175 \cdot 10^{-3}\ \text{m}^{-2}\), which is exactly \(\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}/\partial x\,\partial y\) for \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = -\nu\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/E\). The cantilever field with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\) is therefore not an exact solution of the three-dimensional problem. In chapter 5 we used it as a model. In what sense it is a good model we say later in this chapter, with plane problems.

For a computation with stresses we now have nine equations: three equilibrium equations and the six equations (11.7), for six unknowns \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\). The number of equations exceeds the number of unknowns. This is not a contradiction, because the compatibility equations are not expressed independently: SADD (PDF p. 110) says that the six relations give only three independent results. We do not prove this here and nothing that follows relies on it. Why did compatibility with displacements not have to be required? There the strains are computed from the displacement, so the compatibility equations hold for them by themselves (theorem 1.133). With stresses there is no displacement yet, so we must require compatibility separately.

11.3.3 From stresses to displacements (Od napetosti do pomikov)

The theorem gave a condition on the stresses inside the body. Do stresses that satisfy equilibrium, the Beltrami-Michell equations and the prescribed forces on the surface really make up a solution of the boundary value problem? For this we must find the displacement, that is, walk the first arrow of the upper row in the opposite direction, from the strains to the displacement. The strains are compatible, and in a simply connected body compatibility suffices for a displacement (theorem 4.120). What remains is the question of what happens up to the surface of the body, because the theorem gives the displacement only in the interior. Here lemma 4.128 helps, which extends the displacement and its first derivatives to every cell of the body.

Corollary 11.10 Stress formulation (napetostna formulacija)

Let \(V\) be a body made of boxes (definition 4.11) whose interior points make up a connected (definition 1.136) and simply connected (definition 1.142) open set, of an isotropic homogeneous material with \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\). Let \(D\) be an open set that contains \(V\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{ji}}\) functions with continuous partial derivatives up to the second order on \(D\) and \(\vek b\) a body force with continuous first partial derivatives on \(D\). Let the equilibrium equations (2.38) and the equations (11.7) hold at every interior point of \(V\), and at every point of the surface Cauchy's formula \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \bar{\vek t}\) with a prescribed stress vector \(\bar{\vek t}\). Then:

  1. At the interior points of \(V\) there is a displacement field \(\vek u\) with continuous partial derivatives of the first and second order whose small strain tensor has, by Hooke's law, the stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\).
  2. The displacement \(\vek u\) and its partial derivatives of the first order extend to continuous functions on every cell of the body, which agree on the common faces of the cells.
  3. If the extended displacement can be extended to a continuously differentiable field on an open set \(W \subset D\) that contains the body together with its surface, and this field has the small strain tensor with components \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) on \(W\), then \(\vek u\) is a solution of the boundary value problem with body force \(\vek b\) and prescribed stress vector \(\bar{\vek t}\) on the whole surface, without prescribed displacement, and by theorem 11.4 it is unique up to a small rigid displacement.
Proof

We must show that the stresses give a displacement and that it satisfies the conditions of the boundary value problem. We get the displacement from compatibility and its stresses from Hooke's law.

  1. Let \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) be the strains that we get from the stresses by Hooke's law (3.27), along the arrow of the lower row. They have continuous first and second partial derivatives on \(D\), because the stresses have them. By theorem 11.9, applied at every interior point, they satisfy the compatibility equations.
  2. The interior points of \(V\) make up a connected and simply connected open set, so by theorem 4.120 there is a displacement field \(\vek u\), continuously differentiable, with continuous second derivatives and with a small strain tensor with components \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\). This is the first arrow of the upper row, walked backwards, and point 1, except for the stresses.
  3. Hooke's law in Lamé's form (3.28), which is the relation (3.27) solved for the stresses (theorem 3.19), determines the stresses from the strains, as the second arrow of the upper row. Because the \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) are the strains that belong to the stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\), the stresses of the field \(\vek u\), that is \(\lambda\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}})\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}} + 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\), equal \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\). This completes point 1.
  4. The functions \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) have continuous first and second derivatives on \(D \supset V\), and \(\vek u\) has the strains \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) at interior points. By lemma 4.128, which extends the displacement and its first derivatives up to the faces of every cell, point 2 holds.
  5. The equilibrium equations and \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \bar{\vek t}\) hold for the stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\) by assumption, so by step 3 they hold for the stresses of the field \(\vek u\). The continuous differentiability of the extension on \(W\) is requirement 1 of definition 11.1. On \(W\) the stresses of the extension are equal to \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\) by step 3, so continuously differentiable, as point 2 of the definition requires. The prescribed stress vector \(\bar{\vek t}\) is continuous on every exterior face, because the \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\) are continuous there and the normal is constant. All requirements of a solution (definition 11.1) are therefore satisfied, and by theorem 11.4 this is the only solution up to a small rigid displacement. This is point 3.

Source: SADD §5.3 (PDF p. 110): the strains are obtained from the stresses by Hooke's law, the displacements by integration, which determines the displacements only up to a rigid displacement, and the displacements are single-valued only if the region is simply connected. The connection with the theorems of chapter 4 and the proof of this theorem are not from the sources.

The proof showed that in a body made of boxes that is simply connected, stresses with equilibrium and the Beltrami-Michell equations suffice for a displacement, and that it is a solution, if it extends continuously differentiably beyond the surface, and then the only one. The requirement of an extension of the displacement to the surroundings of the body did not follow from the lemma, which gives only an extension by cells. For every solution of this chapter that we assemble in closed form we therefore check it directly. We have not proved the corollary for bodies made of curved boxes, because there we have no extension to the cells, and there we will always assemble the solutions directly. Let us replace the round cross section of diameter \(10\) mm of the hanging bar of figure 11.25 by a rectangular one, so that the body is a box. The stresses stay linear in \(x\), the Beltrami-Michell equations hold (above), the box is simply connected and the force per unit area at the top is uniform, \(\rho g L = 0{,}7652\) MPa upwards, so the corollary gives a displacement field. Later in this chapter we find it by integration and show that it is the field of chapter 1.

When does the stress formulation suit? For a boundary value problem with prescribed forces on the whole surface, because then there is no condition on the displacement. Such is the problem of the hanging bar, which the ceiling pulls with a known force while the rest of the surface is free. When the displacement is prescribed on a part of the surface, as at the clamped end of the cantilever, where it is zero, one more computation remains after the stresses are found: we must find the displacement and check that on the prescribed part it really equals the prescribed one, which the condition of theorem 4.131 expresses with virtual forces. The stress formulation therefore suits problems with prescribed forces best, and the formulation with displacements problems with prescribed displacements.

The condition that the interior points of the body are simply connected is essential. In a body with a hole, for example the ring of chapter 4, the strains can be compatible at every point and still not give a single displacement field across the cut (corollary 4.114). We will meet such a case again with polar coordinates later in this chapter.
