Chapter 16 Torsion

16.2 Warping function (funkcija zvitja)

The previous section showed that the field of the circle fails on the \(20 \times 40\) mm strip: on the free long face it leaves a stress of \(7{,}50\) MPa, and at the corner a stress of \(8{,}39\) MPa where it must be zero (lemma 16.14). The field assumed that the section stays plane, that is, that all its points move only in the plane of the section. If this is not true, then besides turning about the axis a point of the section must also move along the axis. How much, and how to compute it for every section, not only for a rectangle, is the question of this section.

From history

That the sections of a twisted bar do not stay plane was first noticed by Cauchy. Applying the general equations of elasticity to rectangular bars, he found a satisfactory solution for a narrow rectangle and showed that the sections of a twisted bar in general do not stay plane but bulge (TIMO, PDF p. 118). LOVE says that his result was not correct, but that it is historically important because he recognized that the normal sections do not remain plane (LOVE, PDF p. 36). No year for Cauchy's work is given on these pages. Attempts to use the assumption of plane sections for shafts of other shapes did not agree with experiments (TIMO, PDF p. 207). For this reason Saint-Venant assumed only a rotation of the section in its own plane and added the warping as an unknown that the equations of elasticity determine (TIMO, PDF p. 207). This is the semi-inverse method (definition 11.11), which chapter 11 presented together with Saint-Venant's treatise. This section carries it through to the end. The bulge he speaks of is shown by the figure in this section.

The semi-inverse method is a proposal, and the field equations tell whether the proposal holds. We propose that every section at \(x\) rotates about the origin \(O\) by the angle \(\alpha x\), as in equation (16.2), and that at the same time its points move along the axis by a length that depends only on the place \((y, z)\) in the section and not on \(x\). Why not on \(x\)? Every piece of the prism with the same section and length carries the same moment (section 16.1) and therefore deforms in the same way. Why proportional to \(\alpha\)? The problem is linear and \(\alpha\) is the only parameter of the load, so every displacement is a multiple of it.

Let us draw what we propose (figure 16.18). On the left is the section \(20 \times 40\) mm viewed along the axis \(x\), with the coordinate axes \(y\) and \(z\) and the origin \(O\). The point \(P\) is the corner with the coordinates \(y = a = 10\) mm and \(z = b = 20\) mm. As before, \(P\) moves in the plane of the section by the vector \(\vek d\), perpendicular to \(OP\). On the right is the view from the side, with the axes \(x\) and \(z\). The grey vertical line is the section before loading, drawn at one place \(x\). After loading the points of the face \(y = a\) move along the axis: at \(z = 0\) not at all, above in the direction \(+x\), below in the direction \(-x\), at the corner \(z = b\) by \(0{,}832\) \(\mu\)m and at most \(0{,}953\) \(\mu\)m at \(z = 16{,}6\) mm, at \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = 50\) N m. The points of the face \(y = -a\) move in the opposite direction, so the section does not rotate as a rigid body but bulges like a saddle. The length of the arrow between the grey line and the curve at \(z = 15\) mm is the displacement \(\alpha\psi\) of that point, \(0{,}934\) \(\mu\)m. The displacements are drawn \(12\,600\) times larger than in reality, and the curves are computed with the series of section 16.5 and serve here only as a view of what we are looking for.

The word warping translates the Slovenian zvitje. Let us write the proposal as a definition.

Definition 16.15 Warping function (funkcija zvitja)

Let a prism with section \(S\) be uniformly twisted (definition 16.1) with the twist per unit length \(\alpha \ne 0\). The displacement of the point \((x, y, z)\), the rotation about the axis and the displacement along it, has the form

\[u_{\mathrm{x}} = \alpha\,\psi(y, z) , \qquad u_{\mathrm{y}} = -\alpha\,x\,z , \qquad u_{\mathrm{z}} = \alpha\,x\,y , \tag{16.19}\]

where \(\psi : S \to \mathbb{R}\) is a twice continuously differentiable function on an open set that contains \(S\). We call the function \(\psi\) the warping function (funkcija zvitja), and measure its values in mm\(^2\).

Explanation

The unit is such that \(\alpha\psi\) is a length: \(\alpha\) is in rad/mm (rad is a number without a unit) and \(\psi\) in mm\(^2\). The value \(\alpha\psi(y, z)\) is the displacement of the point of the section along the axis, which the arrow \(\alpha\psi\) on the right half of figure 16.18 shows. For the circle \(\psi = 0\) (section 16.1). The letter \(\psi\) has several meanings in the book: a trial function in chapter 6 and a shape function in chapter 7. In this chapter it means only the warping function.

Source: SADD section 9.3 (PDF pp. 246 and 247): the in-plane displacement is a rigid rotation about the center of twist \(O\), where \(u = v = 0\), the rotation is a linear function of the axial coordinate, and the displacement along the axis, which SADD calls \(w\), is an unknown function of the coordinates in the section only (SADD (9.3.3)). SADD has the axis \(z\) along the bar, we have the axis \(x\), its \(w(x, y)\) is our \(\alpha\psi(y, z)\) and its \(\alpha\) is our \(\alpha\). The name warping function with the symbol \(\psi\) and the definition are ours.

What does the displacement (16.19) do to the strains? The first part, \((0, -\alpha xz, \alpha xy)\), is the rotation of the section about \(O\) by the angle \(\alpha x\) that we already know, and the second part, \((\alpha\psi, 0, 0)\), is a displacement that has only a component along the axis and depends only on the place in the section. Chapter 11 called this form, with a component along the axis, the antiplane state and announced it as the setting of torsion. Let us draw them on a small square of material (figure 16.19) and compute the strains of both parts together.

On the left is the square in the plane \(xy\) at the point \((y, z)\) with sides \(\Delta x\) and \(\Delta y\). The twist tilts the side \(\Delta x\) toward the axis \(y\) by the angle \(-\alpha z\), because \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = -\alpha x z\). The warping tilts the side \(\Delta y\) toward the axis \(x\) by the angle \(\alpha\psi_{\mathrm{y}}\), because \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = \alpha\psi\) and the displacement along the axis varies with \(y\). The right angle between the sides therefore changes by the shear angle, the sum of the two tilts. On the right is the square in the plane \(yz\): the twist only rotates it, the sides stay equally long and perpendicular, so there is no deformation in this plane.

Lemma 16.16 Strains in torsion (deformacije pri torziji)

Let the displacement have the form (16.19). Then at every point of the prism, as the small square shows,

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0 , \qquad \gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \alpha\,(\psi_{\mathrm{y}} - z) , \qquad \gamma_{\mathrm{xz}} = 2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \alpha\,(\psi_{\mathrm{z}} + y) , \tag{16.20}\]

where \(\psi_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\psi_{\mathrm{z}}\) mean \(\partial\psi/\partial y\) and \(\partial\psi/\partial z\). The strains depend only on \((y, z)\) and not on \(x\). By Hooke's law (theorem 3.19) the only nonzero stresses are

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = G\alpha\,(\psi_{\mathrm{y}} - z) , \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = G\alpha\,(\psi_{\mathrm{z}} + y) . \tag{16.21}\]
Idea

Every term is a derivative of a product in which one factor is \(x\) or does not depend on the variable we differentiate by. In the figure these are the twist, which only rotates the square, and the tilt due to \(\psi\).

Proof
  1. By definition 1.121, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial x = \alpha\,\partial\psi/\partial x = 0\), because \(\psi\) does not depend on \(x\). Similarly \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial y = -\alpha x\,\partial z/\partial y = 0\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{z}}/\partial z = 0\). The sides of the square do not get longer.
  2. \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = \tfrac12(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial z + \partial u_{\mathrm{z}}/\partial y) = \tfrac12(-\alpha x + \alpha x) = 0\). The square in the plane \(yz\) only rotates.
  3. \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \tfrac12(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial y + \partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial x) = \tfrac12\alpha(\psi_{\mathrm{y}} - z)\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \tfrac12(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial z + \partial u_{\mathrm{z}}/\partial x) = \tfrac12\alpha(\psi_{\mathrm{z}} + y)\). The shear strains are by convention twice these. This is the shear angle, the sum of the tilt due to warping and of the twist.
  4. The trace of the strain is zero by step 1, so by Hooke's law \(\ten\sigma = 2G\,\ten\varepsilon\). Only the components with the indices \(xy\) and \(xz\) are nonzero (and the symmetric \(yx\), \(zx\) by theorem 2.41). Only the shear strain is nonzero, so the stress is shear only.

Source: SADD section 9.3.1, equations (9.3.4) and (9.3.5) (PDF p. 247): from the displacement (9.3.3) it obtains the same two shear strains. The split into rotation and antiplane part is ours.

Let us see what this means for our strip, the right half of figure 16.18. At the middle of the long face, at the point \((a, 0)\), the rotation alone gives the stress \(G\alpha\,(-z, y) = (0,\ G\alpha a)\), that is \(6{,}83\) MPa at \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = 50\) N m and the true \(\alpha = 9{,}064 \cdot 10^{-6}\) rad/mm, which section 16.5 computes for the strip. Section 16.1 computed \(\alpha\) with the polar moment \(I_{\mathrm{p}}\), got a smaller \(\alpha\) and therefore only \(3{,}75\) MPa there. The true stress \(12{,}71\) MPa, which section 16.5 computes, we obtain only with the warping: \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\) and \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xz}} = 1{,}686 \cdot 10^{-4}\). The stress there is tangent to the face \(y = a\), as the free face demands, and almost twice as large as the rotation alone would give.

Did we overlook something in the rotation? The field (16.19) is the linear form of the true rotation and neglects \(R(1 - \cos\varphi)\) (section 16.1). For the strip \(20 \times 40\) mm and the length \(1\) m the corner is \(22{,}36\) mm from the axis. The ratio \((1 - \cos\varphi)/\sin\varphi = \tan(\varphi/2)\) tells by what fraction of its displacement the corner at the end lies off compared with the true rotation. At \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = 50\) N m, \(\varphi(L) = 0{,}00906\) rad, this is \(0{,}453\,\%\), and at \(500\) N m, \(\varphi(L) = 0{,}0906\) rad, it is \(4{,}53\,\%\). This error concerns the position of the points, not the stresses. The stresses depend on the strains, which at \(50\) N m are at most \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xz}} = 1{,}686 \cdot 10^{-4}\). At \(500\) N m, however, the model fails earlier because of the material: the largest shear stress of the strip is \(127{,}1\) MPa, above the shear yield stress \(110\) MPa by the Tresca condition (section 16.5), and the strip is no longer linearly elastic there. For this reason we took \(50\) N m for the strip.

Now we look for \(\psi\). Let us draw what the prism demands (figure 16.20). The body is a prism with the section \(S\) and the length \(L\). The field equations must hold in it, which is the first demand. The lateral surface is free, so the stress vector there is zero, which is the second demand. On the end section the stress vector is \((0, \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}})\), which gives a resultant of zero and the moment \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\), which is the third demand. Let us begin with the boundary.

Let us draw the free lateral surface on a section that we can test (figure 16.21). For the boundary take an ellipse with the semi-axes \(10\) mm along \(y\) and \(20\) mm along \(z\), drawn with the axes and the origin \(O\), and on it the point \(B\) with the coordinates \(y = 8{,}660\) mm and \(z = 10{,}000\) mm. There the outward normal \(\vek n\) has the components \(n_{\mathrm{y}} = 0{,}9608\) and \(n_{\mathrm{z}} = 0{,}2774\), and the tangent is perpendicular to it. We divide the stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\) by \(G\alpha\) to get a vector in mm, and write it as \(\vek s = (\psi_{\mathrm{y}} - z,\ \psi_{\mathrm{z}} + y)\). It is the sum of two parts: the rotation vector \(\vek q = (-z, y)\) with the components \(-10{,}000\) mm and \(8{,}660\) mm, which is perpendicular to \(OB\) and so tangent to the circle about \(O\) through \(B\) of radius \(|OB| = 13{,}23\) mm, and the gradient \(\nabla\psi = (\psi_{\mathrm{y}}, \psi_{\mathrm{z}})\). For \(\psi = 0{,}6\,yz\), a function that section 16.3 finds for the ellipse, \(\nabla\psi\) has the components \(6{,}000\) mm and \(5{,}196\) mm. Their sum is the stress vector \(\vek s\) with the components \(-4{,}000\) mm and \(13{,}856\) mm. The free face cannot carry a force, so \(\vek s\) must be tangent to the boundary, that is perpendicular to \(\vek n\): indeed \(\vek q \cdot \vek n = -7{,}206\) and \(\nabla\psi \cdot \vek n = 7{,}206\), and the sum \(\vek s \cdot \vek n\) is zero. With \(\psi = 0\) the sum would be \(\vek q\) alone and \(\vek s \cdot \vek n = -7{,}206 \ne 0\). So the condition becomes an equation for the normal derivative of \(\psi\) on the boundary.

For the theorem we also need the divergence theorem in the plane, which we obtain from the theorem for the prism. Let us draw it (figure 16.22). On the right is the prism with its end faces and mantle, on the left the section \(S\) with its boundary, the outward normal \(\vek n\) and the field \(\vek v\), which does not depend on \(x\).

Lemma 16.17 Divergence theorem in the section (izrek o divergenci v prerezu)

Let \(S\) be a section for which the prism \(S \times [0, \ell]\) is a body from corollary 4.68, cut so that every outer piece of the lateral surface is described by \((t, x) \mapsto (x, \gamma(t))\) for \(t \in [t_0, t_1]\) and \(x \in [0, \ell]\). Here \(\gamma = (\gamma_{\mathrm{y}}, \gamma_{\mathrm{z}})\) is an arc of the boundary \(\partial S\), continuously differentiable with \(\gamma'(t) \ne \vek 0\). This holds for all sections in this chapter: the circle, the annulus (for example four curved boxes in cylindrical coordinates, definition 4.65), the ellipse, the triangle, the rectangle. The boundary \(\partial S\) has the outward normal \(\vek n = (n_{\mathrm{y}}, n_{\mathrm{z}})\). Let \(\vek v = (v_{\mathrm{y}}, v_{\mathrm{z}})\) be a field continuously differentiable on an open set that contains \(S\). Then

\[\int_{\mathrm{S}} \Big( \frac{\partial v_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial v_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial z} \Big)\,\mathrm{d}A = \oint_{\partial \mathrm{S}} \big( v_{\mathrm{y}}\,n_{\mathrm{y}} + v_{\mathrm{z}}\,n_{\mathrm{z}} \big)\,\mathrm{d}s , \tag{16.22}\]

where the integral of a function \(f\) over the boundary is the sum of the integrals over its arcs,

\[\oint_{\partial \mathrm{S}} f\,\mathrm{d}s = \sum_{\gamma} \int_{\mathrm{t}_0}^{t_1} f\big(\gamma(t)\big)\,|\gamma'(t)|\,\mathrm{d}t .\]

For \(f = 1\) this is the length of the boundary by definition 8.5, so we call \(s\) the arc length of the boundary.

Proof
  1. The field \((0, v_{\mathrm{y}}, v_{\mathrm{z}})\) does not depend on \(x\) and is continuously differentiable. Its divergence is the sum in the expression on the left. The field lies in the plane of the section.
  2. By corollary 4.68 the integral of the divergence over the prism equals the flux through its surface. The integral over the prism is \(\ell\) times the integral over the section, because the function does not depend on \(x\). The surface of the prism is the end faces and mantle.
  3. On both end faces the normal is \(\pm\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), so \((0, v_{\mathrm{y}}, v_{\mathrm{z}})\cdot\vek n = 0\). A piece of the lateral surface described by \(\vek r(t, x) = (x, \gamma_{\mathrm{y}}(t), \gamma_{\mathrm{z}}(t))\) has the derivatives \(\partial\vek r/\partial t = (0, \gamma_{\mathrm{y}}', \gamma_{\mathrm{z}}')\) and \(\partial\vek r/\partial x = (1, 0, 0)\), so its surface vector is \(\pm(0, \gamma_{\mathrm{z}}', -\gamma_{\mathrm{y}}')\), of length \(|\gamma'(t)|\). It points outward, so it equals \(|\gamma'(t)|\,(0, n_{\mathrm{y}}, n_{\mathrm{z}})\). The field does not depend on \(x\), so by definition 4.65 the flux through this piece is \(\int_0^\ell\!\int_{\mathrm{t}_0}^{t_1}(v_{\mathrm{y}}n_{\mathrm{y}} + v_{\mathrm{z}}n_{\mathrm{z}})\,|\gamma'(t)|\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x = \ell\int_{\mathrm{t}_0}^{t_1}(v_{\mathrm{y}}n_{\mathrm{y}} + v_{\mathrm{z}}n_{\mathrm{z}})\,|\gamma'(t)|\,\mathrm{d}t\). The sum over all pieces of the lateral surface is \(\ell\oint(v_{\mathrm{y}}n_{\mathrm{y}} + v_{\mathrm{z}}n_{\mathrm{z}})\,\mathrm{d}s\). The end faces contribute nothing, the mantle gives the flux through the boundary.
  4. Cancel \(\ell\). We cancel the length of the prism.

Source: The plane form of the divergence theorem is in SADD (1.8.11) (PDF p. 28), which it uses in (9.3.15) and (9.3.17) (PDF p. 250). The derivation from corollary 4.68 with a prism is not from the source.

Now we have everything for the theorem. It joins the three demands of figure 16.20: the field equation for \(\psi\) in the body, the condition on the boundary, and the forces at the ends.

Theorem 16.18 Saint-Venant torsion (Saint-Venantova torzija)

Let \(S\) be a bounded section for which lemma 16.17 holds and whose interior is connected. Let \(\psi\) be twice continuously differentiable on an open set that contains \(S\) (for a rectangle this fails at the corners, section 16.5), and let \(\alpha \ne 0\). Let the material be as in theorems 11.6 and 11.4. Let us write two conditions for \(\psi\), the equation in the body and the condition on the free surface:

\[\nabla^2\psi = \frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial y^2} + \frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial z^2} = 0 \quad \text{in } S , \qquad \frac{\partial\psi}{\partial n} = \psi_{\mathrm{y}}\,n_{\mathrm{y}} + \psi_{\mathrm{z}}\,n_{\mathrm{z}} = z\,n_{\mathrm{y}} - y\,n_{\mathrm{z}} \quad \text{on } \partial S . \tag{16.23}\]

For the displacement (16.19) the following holds:

  1. It satisfies the Navier-Lamé equations (theorem 11.6) with zero body force exactly when the first equation \(\nabla^2\psi = 0\) holds. This is the demand for the body.
  2. The lateral surface is free exactly when the second condition holds, the boundary condition for \(\partial\psi/\partial n\). This is the demand for the lateral surface.
  3. If both conditions hold, the resultant force on every end section is zero, and the moment about the axis \(x\) is On the end these are the resultant and the moment.
    \[M_{\mathrm{x}} = G\,J\,\alpha , \qquad J = \int_{\mathrm{S}} \big( y^2 + z^2 + y\,\psi_{\mathrm{z}} - z\,\psi_{\mathrm{y}} \big)\,\mathrm{d}A . \tag{16.24}\]
  4. This field is, up to a small rigid displacement, the only one with such surface forces on the whole surface of the prism. The surface forces act on the lateral surface and the end. The number \(J\) is called the torsion constant (torzijska konstanta), \(GJ\) the torsional rigidity (torzijska togost), \(J\) in mm\(^4\).
Idea

We check everything that the boundary value problem demands, as for the circle. The free surface brings a condition on the boundary, and the resultant and the moment we get with the divergence theorem in the plane. Uniqueness is a theorem of chapter 11. The three demands are the body, lateral surface and end.

Proof
  1. From equation (16.19), \(\nabla^2u_{\mathrm{x}} = \alpha(\psi_{\mathrm{yy}} + \psi_{\mathrm{zz}})\), \(\nabla^2u_{\mathrm{y}} = \nabla^2u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\) (the components are linear in every variable), and \(\operatorname{div}\vek u = \partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial x + \partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial y + \partial u_{\mathrm{z}}/\partial z = 0 + 0 + 0\). By theorem 11.6 and \(\mu = G\) (definition 3.18) the left side of equation (11.3) equals \((G\alpha\,\nabla^2\psi,\ 0,\ 0)\), so the equation holds exactly when \(\nabla^2\psi = 0\). This is point 1.
  2. On the lateral surface the normal is \(\vek n = (0, n_{\mathrm{y}}, n_{\mathrm{z}})\). By Cauchy's formula (theorem 2.26), \(t_{\mathrm{x}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}n_{\mathrm{y}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}n_{\mathrm{z}}\), \(t_{\mathrm{y}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}n_{\mathrm{z}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}n_{\mathrm{y}} = 0\) and \(t_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\), because by lemma 16.16 all other stresses are zero. The component \(t_{\mathrm{x}}\) is zero exactly when \(G\alpha(\psi_{\mathrm{y}}n_{\mathrm{y}} + \psi_{\mathrm{z}}n_{\mathrm{z}} - zn_{\mathrm{y}} + yn_{\mathrm{z}}) = 0\), and since \(G\alpha \ne 0\) this is \(\psi_{\mathrm{y}}n_{\mathrm{y}} + \psi_{\mathrm{z}}n_{\mathrm{z}} = zn_{\mathrm{y}} - yn_{\mathrm{z}}\). This is point 2.
  3. Let (16.23) hold. On the end section at \(x = L\) with the normal \(+\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) the stress vector is \(\vek t = (\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}) = (0, \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}})\) by theorem 2.41. By equation (16.21), \(\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}/\partial y + \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}/\partial z = G\alpha\,\nabla^2\psi = 0\).
  4. The field \(\vek v = (y\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}},\ y\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}})\) is continuously differentiable on an open set that contains \(S\) and has the divergence \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} + y\,(\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}/\partial y + \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}/\partial z) = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) by step 3. By lemma 16.17, \(\int_{\mathrm{S}}\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\mathrm{d}A = \oint y\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}n_{\mathrm{y}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}n_{\mathrm{z}})\,\mathrm{d}s = 0\), since the expression in the parentheses is zero on the boundary (step 2). Likewise with the field \((z\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}},\ z\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}})\) for \(\int_{\mathrm{S}}\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\,\mathrm{d}A = 0\). The force in the direction \(x\) is zero because \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0\). So the resultant is zero. This shows that the resultant on the end is zero.
  5. Because of \(t_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\) and the zero resultant, the moments about the axes \(y\) and \(z\), \(\int(z\,t_{\mathrm{x}} - L\,t_{\mathrm{z}})\,\mathrm{d}A = -L\int\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\,\mathrm{d}A = 0\) and \(\int(L\,t_{\mathrm{y}} - y\,t_{\mathrm{x}})\,\mathrm{d}A = L\int\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\mathrm{d}A = 0\), are zero. The moment about the axis \(x\) is \(\int(y\,t_{\mathrm{z}} - z\,t_{\mathrm{y}})\,\mathrm{d}A = \int(y\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} - z\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}})\,\mathrm{d}A = G\alpha\int(y\psi_{\mathrm{z}} + y^2 - z\psi_{\mathrm{y}} + z^2)\,\mathrm{d}A = G\alpha J\). At \(x = 0\) the normal is \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and the resultant and the moment are opposite. This is point 3. The moment on the end is given by the moment.
  6. The forces on the whole surface are thus given and there is no body force. Let \(\tilde{\vek u}\) be another field with the same surface forces. By theorem 11.4, point 2, the difference \(\tilde{\vek u} - \vek u\) is a small rigid displacement. This is point 4. The surface forces on the lateral surface and the end are thus given.
Explanation

The torsion constant \(J\) replaces the polar moment \(I_{\mathrm{p}}\) of the circle. Example (figure 16.25): for the rectangle \(20 \times 40\) mm, \(J = 73\,178\) mm\(^4\) and \(I_{\mathrm{p}} = 133\,333\) mm\(^4\) (section 16.5). The product \(GJ\) is what chapter 12 took as a model in equation (12.16), and chapter 13 took \(GI_{\mathrm{p}}\) for the circle in equation (13.20). The theorem proves the equation for every section for which the function \(\psi\) that solves the conditions (16.23) is twice continuously differentiable on an open set that contains \(S\): for the circle and the annulus (\(\psi = 0\)) and for the ellipse (\(\psi = 0{,}6\,yz\) for the semi-axes \(10\) and \(20\) mm, section 16.3). The rectangle has corners where \(\psi\) does not meet this condition (section 16.5). The proof would need cutting out small squares around the corners and a limit, which we do not carry out here, so for the rectangle the theorem is not proved here. The numbers for the strip in this section are computed with the series of section 16.5. The ends are loaded only with the distribution \((\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}})\) of equation (16.21). A different loading of the end with the same moment differs, by Saint-Venant's principle (section 11.11, stated there and not proved), only near the end. The solution is thus exact for this loading, and for others it holds far from the ends.

Source: SADD section 9.3.2, equations (9.3.19) to (9.3.24) (PDF p. 251): \(\nabla^2 w = 0\) in the section, the condition on the normal derivative on the boundary, \(T = \alpha\mu J\) with \(J\) defined as an integral over the section. The proof with the Navier-Lamé equations, the divergence theorem and uniqueness is ours. SADD's \(T\) is our \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\), its \(J\) is our \(GJ\).

We wrote the boundary so that the derivative of the function \(\psi\) along the normal is known and the value is not. Such problems have an unusual property: they cannot be solved for every right-hand side.

Let us draw this (figure 16.23). On the left is the circle with the radius \(20\) mm, and on its boundary we would like \(\partial\psi/\partial n = 10\) mm everywhere, which the orange arrows with the datum \(g = 10\) show, all outward. Since \(\nabla^2\psi = 0\), the field \(\nabla\psi\) has neither a source nor a sink in the interior, so as much as flows out would also have to flow in. The total outflow through the boundary would be \(2\pi \cdot 20 \cdot 10 = 1256{,}6\) mm\(^2\), and there is no inflow anywhere. Such a \(\psi\) does not exist. On the right is the rectangle \(20 \times 40\) mm and the arrows on its boundary show the right-hand side of the torsion condition, \(g = z\,n_{\mathrm{y}} - y\,n_{\mathrm{z}}\). Where \(g > 0\), an orange arrow points out, where \(g < 0\), a blue one points in. On the face \(y = a\) we have \(g = z\): for \(z > 0\) the arrows point out, for \(z < 0\) in, and the sum over this face is zero. On the face \(z = b\) we have \(g = -y\), and so on for all four faces. Outflow and inflow balance.

The other side of the same lemma is uniqueness (figure 16.24). Let \(w\) be the difference of two solutions. On the section \(S\) the normal derivative is zero on the boundary. If \(|\nabla w|^2\) were \(h > 0\) at the point \(P\), by continuity it would be at least \(h/2\) on a small square \(Q\) around it, as the bar shows.

Lemma 16.19 Solvability condition and uniqueness (pogoj rešljivosti in enoličnost)

Let \(S\) be a section as in theorem 16.18 and let \(g\) be a continuous function on the boundary \(\partial S\), like the arrows of the datum. Then:

  1. If \(\psi\) is a harmonic function, twice continuously differentiable on an open set that contains \(S\), with \(\partial\psi/\partial n = g\) on the boundary, then \(\oint_{\partial \mathrm{S}}g\,\mathrm{d}s = 0\). For the circle with the flux everywhere outward this is impossible.
  2. Two such functions differ by a constant. The difference \(w\) has a zero normal derivative on the boundary and \(|\nabla w|^2\) is zero everywhere.
  3. The torsion datum \(g = z\,n_{\mathrm{y}} - y\,n_{\mathrm{z}}\) satisfies \(\oint_{\partial \mathrm{S}}g\,\mathrm{d}s = 0\) for every section. On the rectangle the arrows outward and inward balance.
Idea

The flux of the field \(\nabla\psi\) through the boundary is the integral of its divergence, which is \(\nabla^2\psi = 0\). For uniqueness we use energy, as for the uniqueness of the boundary value problem. In the figure the flux of the circle shows it: there is no sink, so there is no solution.

Proof
  1. The field \(\vek v = \nabla\psi = (\psi_{\mathrm{y}}, \psi_{\mathrm{z}})\) is continuously differentiable and has the divergence \(\nabla^2\psi = 0\). By lemma 16.17, \(0 = \oint(\psi_{\mathrm{y}}n_{\mathrm{y}} + \psi_{\mathrm{z}}n_{\mathrm{z}})\,\mathrm{d}s = \oint g\,\mathrm{d}s\).
  2. Let \(\psi_1\) and \(\psi_2\) be two such functions and \(w = \psi_1 - \psi_2\). Then \(\nabla^2w = 0\) and \(\partial w/\partial n = 0\) on the boundary. The field \(w\nabla w\) has the divergence \(|\nabla w|^2 + w\nabla^2w = |\nabla w|^2\), and by lemma 16.17, \(\int_{\mathrm{S}}|\nabla w|^2\,\mathrm{d}A = \oint w\,\partial w/\partial n\,\mathrm{d}s = 0\). The function \(|\nabla w|^2\) is continuous and nonnegative, so it is zero everywhere. Indeed, if it were equal to \(h > 0\) at an interior point \(P\), then by continuity it would be at least \(h/2\) on a small square \(Q\) around \(P\) that lies in \(S\), and by monotonicity (lemma 4.32, points 2 and 6) the integral would be at least \(h/2\) times the area of the square \(Q\), so greater than zero. On the boundary it is zero by continuity. So \(\nabla w = 0\). The function \((x, y, z) \mapsto w(y, z)\) then has zero partial derivatives on the prism \((0, \ell) \times\) interior of \(S\). This is a connected open set (definition 1.136), since a broken line in the connected interior of \(S\) is completed with a segment along the axis \(x\). By lemma 1.139, \(w\) is constant on the interior of \(S\), and by continuity on all of \(S\). In the figure this is the square \(Q\) around the point \(P\), where \(|\nabla w|^2\) is at least \(h/2\).
  3. The field \(\vek v = (z, -y)\) has the divergence \(\partial z/\partial y + \partial(-y)/\partial z = 0\), and by lemma 16.17, \(0 = \oint(z\,n_{\mathrm{y}} - y\,n_{\mathrm{z}})\,\mathrm{d}s\).
Explanation

Point 3 says that the condition demanded by point 1 always holds in torsion, and so there is no contradiction. The condition is necessary. That it is also sufficient, that is, that a solution exists for every such section, we do not prove here. SADD places such problems in potential theory (section 9.3.2, PDF p. 252). For the sections of this chapter we find the solution: the circle (\(\psi = 0\)), the ellipse (section 16.3) and the rectangle with a series (section 16.5). The constant that point 2 leaves is the displacement along the axis \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = \alpha c\) of all points of the section together, a rigid displacement. In the rectangle the flux through the boundary is zero, as the balance of the arrows outward and inward shows, in the circle it is not.

Example for the condition: for a circle with the radius \(20\) mm and the datum \(g = 10\) mm, \(\oint g\,\mathrm{d}s = 1256{,}6\) mm\(^2 \ne 0\), so there is no solution. The rectangle \(20 \times 40\) mm gives four faces with \(\int g\,\mathrm{d}s = 0\) each: \(\int_{-\mathrm{b}}^{b}z\,\mathrm{d}z = 0\) on \(y = \pm a\) and \(\int_{-\mathrm{a}}^{a}y\,\mathrm{d}y = 0\) on \(z = \pm b\). At a corner \(g\) has two values, \(z = 20\) mm on the face \(y = a\) and \(-y = -10\) mm on the face \(z = b\), because there are two normals there. The datum is thus not continuous at the corners, and the rectangle demands more care (section 16.5). In the figure this is the circle with its flux and the rectangle with balanced arrows.

Example for guessing: for the strip, \(\psi = 0\) gives \(\partial\psi/\partial n = 0\), and on the face \(y = a\) the amount \(z\) is missing, that is \(20\) mm at the corner, \(10\) mm at \(z = 10\) mm and \(0\) at \(z = 0\). The function \(\psi = 0{,}6\,yz\) of the ellipse gives \(\psi_{\mathrm{y}} = 0{,}6z\) and \(0{,}4z\) is missing, that is \(8{,}0\) mm at the corner. Neither of them is thus the solution for the rectangle. The missing part is the difference between the datum and what \(\psi\) gives.

Source: The proof by the divergence lemma is ours, not from the source. SADD section 9.3.2 (PDF p. 252) states that such boundary problems belong to potential theory (Kellogg 1969). The numbers are ours (num-02.py).

What does the warping do to the torsion constant? The expression (16.24) has the terms \(y\psi_{\mathrm{z}} - z\psi_{\mathrm{y}}\) of unknown sign, so we do not know whether \(J\) is larger or smaller than \(I_{\mathrm{p}}\) and whether it is positive at all. The answer is one equation, shown by figure 16.25: the warping removes a part of the polar moment, what remains is the torsion constant.

Let us draw what the lemma says. At the top is the strip \(20 \times 40\) mm: the long bar is \(I_{\mathrm{p}} = 133\,333\) mm\(^4\). Below it the same length is divided into the \(J = 73\,178\) mm\(^4\) and the \(\int|\nabla\psi|^2 = 60\,155\) mm\(^4\), the part that the warping removes. At the bottom is the square \(20 \times 20\) mm with the same division: the removal is smaller, \(J/I_{\mathrm{p}} = 0{,}8435\).

Lemma 16.20 The torsion constant is smaller than the polar moment (torzijska konstanta je manjša od polarnega momenta)

Let \(\psi\) be the warping function of the section \(S\) as in theorem 16.18, satisfying (16.23). Let \(I_{\mathrm{p}} = \int_{\mathrm{S}} (y^2 + z^2)\,\mathrm{d}A\) be the polar second moment of area of the section about \(O\), as in lemma 16.2. Then, as figure 16.25 shows: \(I_{\mathrm{p}}\) is the long bar, \(\int|\nabla\psi|^2\) is the removal, \(J\) is what remains.

  1. \(J = I_{\mathrm{p}} - \int_{\mathrm{S}} |\nabla\psi|^2\,\mathrm{d}A\), so \(J \le I_{\mathrm{p}}\). In the figure this is the bar \(I_{\mathrm{p}}\) without the removed part.
  2. \(J = \int_{\mathrm{S}} \big( (\psi_{\mathrm{y}} - z)^2 + (\psi_{\mathrm{z}} + y)^2 \big)\,\mathrm{d}A > 0\). The bar \(J\) always has a positive length.
  3. \(J = I_{\mathrm{p}}\) exactly when \(\psi\) is constant. Then the removal is zero and the bars \(I_{\mathrm{p}}\) and \(J\) are equal.
Idea

The term with \(\psi\) in \(J\) we turn with the divergence theorem in the plane into an integral over the boundary, where the boundary condition links it to the energy \(\int|\nabla\psi|^2\). The removed part is \(\int|\nabla\psi|^2\) in the figure.

Proof
  1. The field \(\vek v = (-z\,\psi,\ y\,\psi)\) has the divergence \(-z\psi_{\mathrm{y}} + y\psi_{\mathrm{z}}\). By lemma 16.17, \(\int_{\mathrm{S}}(y\psi_{\mathrm{z}} - z\psi_{\mathrm{y}})\,\mathrm{d}A = \oint\psi\,(-z\,n_{\mathrm{y}} + y\,n_{\mathrm{z}})\,\mathrm{d}s = -\oint\psi\,\partial\psi/\partial n\,\mathrm{d}s\) by the boundary condition.
  2. The field \(\psi\nabla\psi\) has the divergence \(|\nabla\psi|^2 + \psi\nabla^2\psi = |\nabla\psi|^2\), so \(\int_{\mathrm{S}}|\nabla\psi|^2\,\mathrm{d}A = \oint\psi\,\partial\psi/\partial n\,\mathrm{d}s\). From step 1, \(\int_{\mathrm{S}}(y\psi_{\mathrm{z}} - z\psi_{\mathrm{y}})\,\mathrm{d}A = -\int_{\mathrm{S}}|\nabla\psi|^2\,\mathrm{d}A\), and in the expression for \(J\) from equation (16.24) point 1 follows.
  3. Expand the squares: \((\psi_{\mathrm{y}} - z)^2 + (\psi_{\mathrm{z}} + y)^2 = |\nabla\psi|^2 + y^2 + z^2 + 2\,(y\psi_{\mathrm{z}} - z\psi_{\mathrm{y}})\). By step 2 the integral is \(\int|\nabla\psi|^2 + I_{\mathrm{p}} - 2\int|\nabla\psi|^2 = I_{\mathrm{p}} - \int|\nabla\psi|^2 = J\), which is the equality of point 2.
  4. Positivity in point 2: if \(J = 0\), the continuous nonnegative function under the integral would be zero everywhere (as in step 2 of the proof of lemma 16.19), so \(\psi_{\mathrm{y}} = z\) and \(\psi_{\mathrm{z}} = -y\). The mixed derivatives would be \(\psi_{\mathrm{yz}} = 1\) and \(\psi_{\mathrm{zy}} = -1\), which by theorem 1.131 is not possible for a twice continuously differentiable \(\psi\). So \(J > 0\). That would mean that the shear angle is zero everywhere.
  5. Point 3: by point 1, \(J = I_{\mathrm{p}}\) exactly when \(\int|\nabla\psi|^2 = 0\). As in step 2 of the proof of lemma 16.19, then \(\nabla\psi = 0\) and \(\psi\) is constant on the interior of \(S\), and by continuity on all of \(S\). Conversely, for constant \(\psi\) the integral is zero. In the figure this is the case where the removal is zero.
Explanation

Result 2 says that \(J\) is the integral of a sum of squares of shear strains, \((\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}}^2 + \gamma_{\mathrm{xz}}^2)/\alpha^2\) by lemma 16.16. This sum, multiplied by \(G/2\), is the strain energy density (definition 3.59 with Hooke's law \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = G\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = G\gamma_{\mathrm{xz}}\)), so \(J\) cannot be negative. Result 1 says that warping subtracts \(\int|\nabla\psi|^2 \ge 0\) from \(I_{\mathrm{p}}\). Warping thus always reduces the stiffness: plane sections (\(\psi = 0\)) give \(J = I_{\mathrm{p}}\) and therefore always overestimate the stiffness. For the strip \(20 \times 40\) mm, \(\int|\nabla\psi|^2 = 60\,155\) mm\(^4\), which is \(0{,}4512\) of \(I_{\mathrm{p}} = 133\,333\) mm\(^4\), and \(J = 73\,178\) mm\(^4\), the ratio \(J/I_{\mathrm{p}} = 0{,}5488\). For the square \(20 \times 20\) mm, \(\int|\nabla\psi|^2 = 4174\) mm\(^4\), \(I_{\mathrm{p}} = 26\,667\) mm\(^4\) and \(J = 22\,492\) mm\(^4\), the ratio \(0{,}8435\): the square bulges less than the strip. The strip and the square have corners, so the lemma is not proved for them here (explanation of theorem 16.18). The numbers confirm it all the same: we computed \(\int|\nabla\psi|^2\) directly from the series for \(\psi\), and \(J\) with the series for \(J\) of section 16.5, and the difference \(I_{\mathrm{p}} - J\) agrees with \(\int|\nabla\psi|^2\) to the last digit given, \(60\,155\) and \(4174\) mm\(^4\) (num-02.py). For the ellipse with the semi-axes \(10\) and \(20\) mm, \(\psi = k\,yz\) with \(k = 0{,}6\), and \(\int|\nabla\psi|^2 = k^2\int(y^2 + z^2)\,\mathrm{d}A = k^2I_{\mathrm{p}}\), so \(J = (1 - k^2)I_{\mathrm{p}} = 0{,}640\,I_{\mathrm{p}} = 50\,265\) mm\(^4\) (derived in section 16.3).

The origin \(O\) does not matter. Let us move it by \((y_0, z_0)\) and denote the coordinates from the new origin by \(\bar y = y - y_0\), \(\bar z = z - z_0\). The function \(\bar\psi = \psi - z_0\,y + y_0\,z\) is harmonic, because the added function is linear, its normal derivative is \(\partial\psi/\partial n - z_0n_{\mathrm{y}} + y_0n_{\mathrm{z}} = \bar z\,n_{\mathrm{y}} - \bar y\,n_{\mathrm{z}}\), which is the boundary condition for the new coordinates. At the same time \(\bar\psi_{\mathrm{y}} - \bar z = \psi_{\mathrm{y}} - z\) and \(\bar\psi_{\mathrm{z}} + \bar y = \psi_{\mathrm{z}} + y\), so the stresses are the same, and by point 2 so is \(J\). For the strip moved by \(3\) mm and \(-4\) mm, at the point \((5, 7)\) mm the ratio \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}/(G\alpha) = -1{,}3228\) mm before and after the move, \(J\) is \(73\,178\) mm\(^4\) in both cases, and the polar moment about a point \(5\) mm from the centroid is \(153\,333\) mm\(^4\) instead of \(133\,333\) mm\(^4\), which is \(I_{\mathrm{p}} + A\,d^2\). The number \(J\) thus does not change, and \(I_{\mathrm{p}}\) depends on the center. The warping function is thus determined up to an added linear function (a shift of the center of twist) and a constant (lemma 16.19, a rigid displacement along the axis). Figure 16.26 shows the new origin and the point where the stress vector does not change.

Source: We do not find the equation \(J = I_{\mathrm{p}} - \int|\nabla\psi|^2\) and the positivity in SADD (9.3.24), where \(J\) is given only as an integral. The proof by energy is ours. The numbers are from num-02.py.

Let us draw the shift of the center of twist (figure 16.26). On the left is the strip with the original origin \(O\) and the new origin at the point \((y_0, z_0) = (3, -4)\) mm, the shift is the vector between them. At the point \((5, 7)\) mm the vector \((\psi_{\mathrm{y}} - z, \psi_{\mathrm{z}} + y)\) is the same for both origins, so \(J\) is the same too. On the right is the annulus with its center at the new origin: the displacement along the axis \(\alpha\psi\) is there a tilted line, because \(\psi\) is affine, and the section stays plane.

When is the warping zero? The answer is given by point 3 of the lemma and by a conclusion that chapter 13 used without proof. The answer is in the figure: the annulus has a center from which every point of its boundary is equally far.

Let us draw the boundaries for which the warping is zero (figure 16.27). In the figure is the annulus with two circles about the center \(O\). On a circle the normal \(\vek n\) is parallel to the radius and the tangent is perpendicular to it, so the distance \(r\) from \(O\) does not change along the tangent. Two arcs that meet at the junction have the same \(r\) there and lie on the same circle.

Corollary 16.21 Sections stay plane exactly for circles and annuli (ravni prerezi natanko pri krogu in kolobarju)

Let \(S\) be a section as in theorem 16.18. Then for the annulus and the strip:

  1. The warping function is constant exactly when every connected part of the boundary is a circle with the center at \(O\). This is a circle with its center at \(O\).
  2. The warping function is affine, \(\psi = c + p\,y + q\,z\) with constants \(c\), \(p\), \(q\), exactly when every connected part of the boundary is a circle with the same center. Then the displacement along the axis \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = \alpha\psi\) is an affine function of the place in the section, and the section stays plane, it only tilts. In the figure this is the annulus with its tilted line.

So the sections stay plane for the circle and for the annulus bounded by two circles with the same center, and for no other boundary. If the center is at \(O\), then \(\psi = 0\). Such is the annulus in the figure.

Proof
  1. Let \(\psi\) be constant. Then \(\partial\psi/\partial n = 0\) and the boundary condition (16.23) gives \(z\,n_{\mathrm{y}} - y\,n_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\) on the boundary. The tangent of the boundary is \(\vek t = (-n_{\mathrm{z}}, n_{\mathrm{y}})\) and the derivative of \(r^2 = y^2 + z^2\) along it is \(2\,(y\,t_{\mathrm{y}} + z\,t_{\mathrm{z}}) = 2\,(-y\,n_{\mathrm{z}} + z\,n_{\mathrm{y}}) = 0\). The distance \(r\) from \(O\) is thus constant along every connected smooth curve of the boundary. In the figure this is the tangent, along which \(r\) is constant.
  2. Smooth arcs of the boundary that meet have the same \(r\) at the junction, so they lie on the same circle about \(O\). A closed curve that lies on a circle is the whole circle. Every connected part of the boundary is thus a circle about \(O\). In the figure these are the arcs at the junction.
  3. Conversely, let the boundary be made of circles about \(O\). The normal there is parallel to the position vector \((y, z)\), so \(z\,n_{\mathrm{y}} - y\,n_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\), and the function \(\psi = 0\) satisfies \(\nabla^2\psi = 0\) and the boundary condition. By lemma 16.19, point 2, it is the only solution up to a constant. Steps 1 to 3 prove point 1. In the figure the normal is parallel to the radius.
  4. For point 2 we move the center of twist to the point \((y_0, z_0)\). The function \(\bar\psi = \psi - z_0\,y + y_0\,z\) is harmonic and satisfies the boundary condition (16.23) for the coordinates \(\bar y = y - y_0\), \(\bar z = z - z_0\) (computed in the explanation of lemma 16.20). Let \(\psi = c + p\,y + q\,z\). With \(z_0 = p\) and \(y_0 = -q\), \(\bar\psi = c\) is constant, and by point 1 for the new center every connected part of the boundary is a circle with the center \((-q, p)\). The center of twist is shifted in the figure.
  5. Conversely, let every connected part of the boundary be a circle with the center \((y_0, z_0)\). By point 1 for this center, \(\bar\psi\) is constant, \(\bar\psi = c\). Then \(\psi = c + z_0\,y - y_0\,z\) is a warping function for \(O\) that is affine, and by lemma 16.19, point 2, every other one differs from it only by a constant. This is the annulus with its center at the new origin.
Explanation

The circle and the annulus thus do not bulge because for them the right-hand side of the boundary condition for \(\partial\psi/\partial n\) is zero. This is the proof of the claim of chapter 13 that the circle does not warp because of axial symmetry: there the symmetry was the argument, here it is a conclusion from the equation. For every other boundary, for example the strip of section 16.1, \(\psi\) is not affine and the section bulges: for \(20 \times 40\) mm the largest displacement along the axis is only \(0{,}953\) \(\mu\)m at \(50\) N m, but the warping nevertheless reduces the stiffness to \(J = 0{,}549\,I_{\mathrm{p}}\). The strip in the figure has straight sides, not circles.

Source: The proof is ours. SADD section 9.3 (PDF p. 246) says that circular sections stay plane because of symmetry, and the same conclusion for every section with a different boundary makes the warping unavoidable.

The task is thus: find a harmonic function \(\psi\) with a known normal derivative on the boundary and from it compute \(J\) and the stresses. For the circle the solution is \(\psi = 0\). For other shapes the form with a normal derivative is awkward: the boundary condition is not a value of the function but its derivative. The next section therefore looks for another function, one that is constant on the boundary and from which we read the stresses directly.
