Chapter 1 Strain

1.7 Mathematics: partial derivatives

At the end of the previous section we found that in a general body the displacement changes from point to point in all three directions. For the bar we measured how fast the displacement changes along the bar with the derivative (definition 1.9). In this section we extend the derivative to functions of three variables. First we look at a body that needs this extension: the hanging bar, this time with all three components of its displacement. Then we define the derivative in which only one of the three coordinates changes, compute it for this bar and look at what the numbers tell us.

1.7.1 The hanging bar in space

A steel bar of length \(L = 10\) m hangs from the ceiling and stretches under its own weight. Let it have a round cross section with a diameter of 10 mm. How it is held matters for its field, so let us first draw it (figure 1.89, left).

Let us draw the left bar as the field describes it, seen from the side. The blackwhite outline is the top part of the bar before loading. The grey dashed sections below it show that the bar continues downward, only the top is drawn. The orange outline is the same bar after loading. It is narrower, because the bar gets longer and at the same time thinner. The contraction is in fact very small, in the figure it is greatly enlarged so that it can be seen. The orange dashed sections below are the continuation of this outline. The field we will take from the books holds for a bar of which only one point is fixed, the centre \(O\) of its top end. This point does not move (CM (7.3.16), PDF p. 305). The weight of the bar is carried by forces that act upward on the top face and are spread evenly over it, the same on every square millimetre. So the arrows are all equally long, and they point up, because the weight pulls the bar down and the forces hold it. The books set no other conditions for the top face, but their solution implies that every cross section carries the part of the bar below it evenly over its whole face, the top one included (CM Example 7.3.2, PDF p. 305, SADD Example 5.1, PDF p. 115). The fixing of the point \(O\) carries no weight and only prevents the bar from moving as a whole. That the bar does not turn either, CM ensures by adding the condition that the material right next to the point \(O\) does not turn (CM, PDF p. 306). The top face is not glued to anything, so, like every cross section, it can get a little narrower and, as we will see, curve a little. In the figure this is the orange arc at the top: the centre \(O\) stays in place, while the rim is a little higher. Such holding is an idealization that gives a simple field, and so a real ceiling does not hold the bar like this. How other ways of fixing change the field near the ceiling, we look at at the end of this section.

We set up the coordinate system as in section 1.6: the origin \(O\) is at the centre of the top end of the bar, the axis \(x\) points vertically down along the axis of the bar, the axis \(y\) and the axis \(z\) (it points toward the reader) are horizontal, perpendicular to the bar. So the coordinate \(x\) of a point is its depth below the ceiling, and the coordinates \(y\) and \(z\) say how far from the axis of the bar it lies and on which side. Figure 1.90 shows the bar in these axes, cut along its axis. The blackwhite rectangle is the bar before loading, drawn much thicker than it is. The blackwhite dots are chosen points of the bar before loading, arranged in a grid. The orange dots are the same points after loading, and the blue arrow from a blackwhite dot to an orange dot is the displacement of the point, which the field gives below. The orange outline is the bar after loading. Points move down the more, the lower they lie, and toward the axis the more, the farther from the axis and the higher they lie. The displacements are greatly enlarged, the displacements toward the axis much more than the downward ones, so that they can be seen.

In section 1.2 we used, for the points on the axis of the bar, the downward displacement

\[u(x) = a\,(2Lx - x^2) , \qquad a = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1} .\]

In section 1.6 we saw that a bar that stretches also gets thinner: when a steel bar gets longer by a thousandth of its length, its thickness decreases by about \(0{,}3\) thousandths. So the points off the axis also move toward the axis. The whole displacement field of the hanging bar, that is, the rule that assigns to every point of the bar its displacement vector (definition 1.23), is

\[\begin{aligned} u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y, z) &= a\,(2Lx - x^2) - 0{,}3\,a\,(y^2 + z^2) , \\ u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y, z) &= -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\,y , \\ u_{\mathrm{z}}(x, y, z) &= -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\,z . \end{aligned} \tag{1.18}\]

The number \(0{,}3\) is the ratio of thinning to stretching for steel, Poisson's ratio from section 1.6. For another material its own ratio would stand here. Where the other numbers in the field come from, we explain below: the number \(0{,}6\) right after it, and the term \(-0{,}3\,a\,(y^2 + z^2)\), which curves the cross section a little, we first describe and at the end of section 1.7.2 show why it has exactly this number.

We took the field from SADD (5.7.4) (PDF p. 115) and CM (7.3.20) (PDF p. 306), where it is written in differently placed axes and with symbols for the properties of steel. CM determines the constant term in the component of the displacement along the bar from the condition that the point \(O\) does not move, and does not write it in (7.3.20) (CM, PDF p. 306). In our notation this term is zero, since \(u_{\mathrm{x}}(0, 0, 0) = 0\). How we derive it, we will see in chapter 11. The two books derive the field under these assumptions (CM Example 7.3.2, PDF pp. 305-306, SADD Example 5.1, PDF p. 115):

  • the steel is the same everywhere and behaves the same in all directions: three sections of the same length along the axes \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\) are pulled equally and all lengthen by the same fraction \(\varepsilon\),
  • after unloading it returns to its original shape: a bar of length \(L\) is pulled by a force \(F\) to the length \(L + \Delta L\) and after unloading it is again \(L\) long,
  • the strains are small, below about a thousandth, and the displacements are small compared with the bar: the end of the bar moves by \(u\), less than a thousandth of the length \(L\), and in the circle the end is greatly enlarged,
  • the weight acts on every small piece vertically downward: the weight \(G\) acts on every piece of the bar, one arrow per piece,
  • the bar is held in the idealized way, as we described.

Where one of the assumptions does not hold, for example near a real ceiling or under a load so large that the steel deforms permanently (the second assumption does not hold), the field does not hold. For now we take it as given and look at what it says.

On the axis of the bar \(y = 0\) and \(z = 0\). Putting them into (1.18) gives

\[\begin{aligned} u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, 0, 0) &= a\,(2Lx - x^2) - 0{,}3\,a\,(0^2 + 0^2) = a\,(2Lx - x^2) , \\ u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, 0, 0) &= 0 , \\ u_{\mathrm{z}}(x, 0, 0) &= 0 . \end{aligned}\]

So a point on the axis moves only down, by \(u(x)\), and its displacement vector is \(\big(u(x), 0, 0\big)\), as we wrote in section 1.6.

Off the axis the points also move sideways (blue arrows in figure 1.90). Look at the component \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\,y\). The numbers \(a\) and \(L - x\) are not negative, so \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) has the opposite sign to \(y\):

point \(y\) \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) movement
right of the axis \(y > 0\) \(u_{\mathrm{y}} < 0\) to the left, toward the axis
left of the axis \(y < 0\) \(u_{\mathrm{y}} > 0\) to the right, toward the axis
on the axis \(y = 0\) \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = 0\) does not move sideways

Both points off the axis therefore move toward the axis. The displacement is proportional to the coordinate \(y\): twice the \(y\) gives twice \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\), so a point twice as far from the axis moves twice as much. That is why the bar gets thinner. The component \(u_{\mathrm{z}}\) says the same for the direction of the axis \(z\). The factor \(L - x\) says that the bar gets thinner more at the top than lower down, and not at all at the lower end, where \(x = L\). This also agrees with what we know. In section 1.4 the strain along the bar was \(2a\,(L - x)\), largest at the top and zero at the lower end, at both ends as a one-sided derivative (definition 1.9). The bar gets thinner where it stretches.

Figure 1.92 shows a cross section from below, so we look along the bar upward. The axis \(x\) points toward us. We draw the axis \(y\) horizontally to the right, and the axis \(z\) is then up, because the coordinate system is right-handed. The blackwhite circle is the cross section before loading, the blackwhite dots in it are chosen points of the cross section. They lie on three circles, at the rim, in the middle and near the centre. The smaller orange circle is the same cross section after loading, the orange dots are the same points after it. The contraction is greatly enlarged so that it can be seen. The blue arrows join each blackwhite dot to its orange one, so they show the displacement of the point. All of them point toward the centre, because the cross section gets thinner. They are longest at the rim and shorter nearer the centre, because a point moves by the same fraction of its distance from the centre, so a point twice as far moves twice as much. The arrows are drawn only at the outer two circles. At the inner one the displacement is so small that the arrow could no longer be seen, so there is none. The centre lies on the axis of the bar and does not move sideways, so it has only a blackwhite dot. The circle therefore shrinks into a smaller circle.

By how much does a point move toward the axis, and where does the number \(0{,}6\) come from? Look at a small piece of material at the depth \(x\) (figure 1.93), seen from the side, so the axis \(x\) points down and the axis \(y\) to the right. The blackwhite square on the left is the piece before loading, its height is \(\Delta x\) and its width is \(\Delta y\). Along the bar it is stretched by the strain \(\varepsilon = 2a\,(L - x)\), which we computed in section 1.4. The orange rectangle on the right is the same piece after loading. Its height \(\Delta x\) therefore grows to \((1 + \varepsilon)\,\Delta x\). Across the bar it contracts by \(0{,}3\) times that: its width \(\Delta y\) decreases to \((1 - 0{,}3\,\varepsilon)\,\Delta y\). To make it visible, the strain is exaggerated: \(\varepsilon = 0{,}3\) is drawn, in fact it is at most \(2a\,L = 3{,}9 \cdot 10^{-6}\), so tens of thousands of times smaller. The grey dashed square is the piece before loading, moved next to the orange one and aligned with it at the top edge, so that the lengthening and the narrowing can be seen. We met the thinning rule in section 1.6 for a whole bar, but it also holds for such a small piece, because the neighbouring parts of the bar pull it only up and down and do not squeeze it sideways (CM Example 7.3.2, PDF p. 305). The fraction by which it contracts is

\[0{,}3\,\varepsilon = 0{,}3 \cdot 2a\,(L - x) = 0{,}6\,a\,(L - x) .\]

The formula for \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) says exactly this: a point at the distance \(y\) from the axis comes closer to the axis by this fraction of the distance \(y\),

\[u_{\mathrm{y}} = -0{,}3 \cdot 2a\,(L - x) \cdot y = -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\,y .\]

So the number \(0{,}6\) is \(0{,}3\) times the \(2\) from the strain \(2a\,(L - x)\). The same holds for \(u_{\mathrm{z}}\) in the direction of the axis \(z\). The number \(0{,}3\) in the second term of \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\), on the other hand, is half of \(0{,}6\). Why exactly half, we will see at the end of section 1.7.2: with this term the right angle between a fibre along the bar and a fibre across it stays a right angle.

How large are these displacements? Take the point \(P\) at the depth 5 m, 4 mm to the right of the axis, in the plane \(z = 0\). So its coordinates are \(x_{\mathrm{P}} = 5\) m, \(y_{\mathrm{P}} = 0{,}004\) m and \(z_{\mathrm{P}} = 0\). By (1.18) it moves down by

\[\begin{aligned} u_{\mathrm{x}}(P) &= a\,(2 \cdot 10 \cdot 5 - 5^2)\ \text{m}^2 - 0{,}3\,a\,(0{,}004^2 + 0^2)\ \text{m}^2 \\ &= 1{,}464 \cdot 10^{-5}\ \text{m} - 9{,}4 \cdot 10^{-13}\ \text{m} \end{aligned}\]

and sideways by

\[u_{\mathrm{y}}(P) = -0{,}6\,a\,(10 - 5)\ \text{m} \cdot 0{,}004\ \text{m} = -2{,}342 \cdot 10^{-9}\ \text{m} ,\]

while \(u_{\mathrm{z}}(P) = 0\). It moves down by \(14{,}64\) µm and toward the axis by \(2{,}342\) nm. A micrometre, µm, is a millionth of a metre, \(10^{-6}\) m, and a nanometre, nm, a billionth of a metre, \(10^{-9}\) m, that is, a thousandth of a micrometre. The displacement toward the axis is more than six thousand times smaller than the downward one, which is why we enlarged it much more in figure 1.90.

The second term of \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(-0{,}3\,a\,(y^2 + z^2)\), says that a point off the axis moves down a little less than the point on the axis at the same depth. At the point \(P\) the difference is only \(9{,}4 \cdot 10^{-13}\) m, more than ten million times less than the downward displacement. So a cross section that was flat before loading becomes very slightly curved: its centre moves a little lower than its rim (figure 1.94). Let us draw this, seen from the side, with the displacements hugely enlarged. The two blackwhite vertical lines are the edges of a piece of the bar before loading, the grey dashed continuations show that the bar continues up and down, only a piece is drawn. The orange dashed continuations show the same for the edges after loading. The grey dash-dotted vertical line is the axis of the bar. The blackwhite horizontal line is the flat cross section before loading, the blackwhite dots are chosen points on it. The orange curve is the same cross section after loading, the orange dots are the same points after it. Each blue arrow leads from a point before loading to the same point after it. All of them point down, because the bar stretches under its weight. The arrows to the left and right of the axis also point toward the axis, because the bar gets thinner, while the arrow on the axis points only down, because a point on the axis does not move sideways. The two orange vertical lines are the edges after loading, closer to the axis, because the bar has narrowed. The curve is lower in the middle than at the rim, the reason we give in the next paragraph. The first grey dimension line on the right shows by how much the rim moves down, the second grey dimension line tells by how much the centre moves lower than the rim. This is \(0{,}3\,a\,y^2\), where \(y\) is the distance of the rim from the axis, and it is a very small difference.

Where does this shape come from? We might expect the opposite, that the rim of the cross section sags more than the centre, like the rim of an umbrella held at its middle. But in this field the bar does not hang from the point \(O\): its weight is carried by the force spread evenly over the top face, and every cross section carries the part of the bar below it evenly over its whole face (CM Example 7.3.2, PDF p. 305). The curving has another source, the thinning, which changes with depth. Let us look at the right half of a short piece of the bar around one depth, drawn relative to the point of the axis at that depth (figure 1.95). So the common downward displacement is not drawn, only the difference between points is seen. The axis of the bar is the dash-dotted line on the left, on it \(y = 0\), and the axis \(y\) points to the right, perpendicular to the bar. The deformation is hugely enlarged so that it can be seen. In blackwhite is the state before loading. The blackwhite vertical lines are fibres that are parallel to the axis before loading. The last one, the thicker, is the edge of the bar. The blackwhite horizontal line is the cross section, which is perpendicular to them before loading. The grey corner marks show that the angle between a fibre and the cross section is a right angle. In orange is the state after loading.

The argument has five steps.

  1. Higher up the bar gets thinner more than lower down, so a point at the distance \(y\) from the axis moves toward the axis more when it lies higher.
  2. A vertical fibre at the distance \(y\) from the axis therefore leans: its upper end moves toward the axis more than its lower end. In the figure these are the straight orange lines that lean toward the axis. Farther from the axis a fibre leans more, because there the points move toward the axis more.
  3. The material is not sheared, the right angle between a leaning fibre and the cross section after loading (the orange curve, the grey corner marks next to the orange lines) stays a right angle, up to a negligibly small correction. This is a claim from the book: neighbouring parts of the bar pass each other only vertical forces, no sideways ones (CM Example 7.3.2, PDF p. 305). We will check it by computation at the end of section 1.7.2, until then it is a prediction and not a proof.
  4. For the angle to stay a right angle, the cross section must turn by the same amount as the leaning fibre next to it. The fibre leans at the top toward the axis, so to the left, and so it turns counterclockwise. The cross section turns in the same direction, so its outer, right end rises.
  5. The cross section therefore rises more and more toward the rim and takes the shape of the letter U, the orange curve. The centre on the axis stays, the rim rises.

The same holds for the top face: it curves too, its centre \(O\) stays in place and its rim rises a little (figure 1.89, left).

Let us return to how the bar is held. The field (1.18) describes a bar that is fixed only at the point \(O\): this point does not move, since \(\vek u(0, 0, 0) = (0, 0, 0)\). The other points of the top face move: a point on the rim of the top face, 5 mm from the axis, moves toward the axis by \(0{,}6\,a \cdot 10\ \text{m} \cdot 0{,}005\ \text{m} = 5{,}856 \cdot 10^{-9}\) m. A real ceiling holds the bar differently. Let us also draw the right bar. The hatched horizontal line is the ceiling, the blackwhite outline below it is the bar before loading, welded to the ceiling over its whole top face. The orange outline is the bar after loading. At the top it cannot narrow, so there it is as wide as the blackwhite one, and it narrows only lower down. The dashed sections below are again the continuation of the bar, grey before loading, orange after it. If the bar is welded to the ceiling over its whole top face, the face can neither narrow nor curve. If it were attached so that it could slide along the ceiling, which is rare, it would stay flat and at the same height, but it could narrow. In each case the field near the ceiling would differ a little from (1.18). Far from the place where the body is held, though, the field is almost the same however exactly the forces holding it are spread, as long as together they pull equally (SADD §5.6, PDF pp. 114-115). How far, the book says only in words: the differences die out with the distance from the place where the body is held, but it gives no exact distance. In figure 1.89 the outlines of the two bars are the same lower down.

Now we can ask what we asked for the bar with the derivative: how fast does the displacement change as we move away from a point? In a body we can move in any direction. Let us start with the directions of the axes.

1.7.2 Partial derivative

For the bar the displacement was a function of one variable, \(u(x)\), and the rate at which it changes was the derivative (definition 1.9), the limit of the difference quotient \((u(x + \Delta x) - u(x))/\Delta x\) as \(\Delta x\) goes to zero. Now each component of the displacement is a function of three variables, whose input is the three coordinates of a point and whose output is one number.

Figure 1.96 has three parts. On the left is the cut of the bar by the plane \(z = 0\), seen from the side. The axis \(x\) points down, the axis \(y\) to the right, the origin \(O\) is at the top end of the axis. The ceiling is not drawn, because only the point \(O\) is fixed. We choose a point \(P\) \(= (x_{\mathrm{P}}, y_{\mathrm{P}}, z_{\mathrm{P}})\) halfway down the bar, a little to the right of the axis. Suppose we move down from it, along the vertical line through \(P\), drawn in grey dashes (figure 1.96, left). On this line only the coordinate \(x\) changes, while the coordinates \(y\) and \(z\) stay \(y_{\mathrm{P}}\) and \(z_{\mathrm{P}}\). So on it the component \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) is a function of a single variable, which assigns to the number \(x\) the number

\[u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y_{\mathrm{P}}, z_{\mathrm{P}}) = a\,(2Lx - x^2) - 0{,}3\,a\,(y_{\mathrm{P}}^2 + z_{\mathrm{P}}^2) .\]

The second term is the same fixed number along the whole line. The derivative of this function tells how fast the downward displacement changes as we move down from \(P\). On its graph it is the slope of the tangent above \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\) (figure 1.96, middle), just as the derivative in figure 1.38 was the slope of the tangent. In the middle there are the axes: \(x\) is drawn horizontally here, \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) vertically. The curve is \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) as a function of \(x\). It rises, because the bar stretches, but ever more slowly, because the derivative \(2a\,(L - x)\) gets smaller and smaller, and it ends at the length \(L\). The grey dashed line marks \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\), the dot is the point of the curve above it, and the blue tangent touches the curve there. In the same way, on the horizontal line through \(P\) in the direction of the axis \(y\) only the coordinate \(y\) changes. On it the component \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) is a function of one variable, which assigns to the number \(y\) the number \(u_{\mathrm{y}}(x_{\mathrm{P}}, y, z_{\mathrm{P}}) = -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x_{\mathrm{P}})\,y\). Its graph is a straight line (figure 1.96, right). On the right there are the axes \(y\) and \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\). The line passes through the origin, because \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) is proportional to \(y\), and it falls, because it has the opposite sign. The grey dashed line marks \(y_{\mathrm{P}}\), the dot is the point of the line above it. The line is its own tangent, so the blue section is a part of it. The scales of all three graphs are enlarged so that the shape can be seen.

From history

Derivatives of functions of several variables in which only one variable changes were computed already by Newton, Leibniz and the Bernoulli family, but as a rule without a special notation (CAJNOT2, PDF p. 244). In 1728 Euler used the letters \(P\), \(Q\) and \(R\) for them, for the derivatives with respect to \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\) (CAJNOT2, PDF p. 245). Jean le Rond d'Alembert was led to equations with derivatives in several variables in 1744 by the equilibrium and motion of fluids, in 1746 by the winds and in 1747 by the vibrating string that we met in section 1.3.1 (CAJORI, PDF p. 257-258). In 1755 Euler wrote the derivative of the function \(P\) with respect to \(y\) in parentheses. The notation meant that the function \(P\) is differentiated with only \(y\) changing, and the resulting change is divided by \(\mathrm{d}y\) (CAJNOT2, PDF p. 245). In 1786 Adrien-Marie Legendre wrote that, to avoid all ambiguity, he would denote by \(\partial v/\partial x\) how much the function \(v\) changes per unit change of \(x\) when only \(x\) changes, and by \(\mathrm{d}v/\mathrm{d}x\) the complete change of the function \(v\) divided by \(\mathrm{d}x\) (CAJNOT2, PDF p. 249). The following year he no longer used the notation. Perhaps also because the letter d was claimed in other fields of mathematics, the notation did not catch on at once (CAJNOT2, PDF p. 249-250). Lagrange wrote such derivatives with a prime, just like the derivative of a function of one variable, for example \(F'(x)\), \(F'(y)\) and \(F'(z)\) for the derivatives of the function \(F(x, y, z)\) with respect to \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\) (CAJNOT2, PDF p. 253). The danger of this notation is that \(F'(x)\) could be read as the derivative of a function of the single variable \(x\) (CAJNOT2, PDF p. 253). In 1841 Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi wrote that Lagrange's notation fails if, for a function of three or more variables, we want to differentiate twice or more (CAJNOT2, PDF p. 253). We see the reason as follows. In \(F'(x)\) the name of the variable in the brackets says with respect to which one we differentiate. For a second derivative we would have to give two variables and their order, while the primes say only that we differentiate, not with respect to what. In 1896 Stäckel remarked that this defect can be removed with several indices (CAJNOT2, PDF p. 253).

The two notations for the same derivative with respect to \(y\) that this story mentions, side by side:

\[\text{Euler: } \Big(\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}y}\Big) , \qquad \text{Legendre: } \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} , \qquad \text{Lagrange: } F'(y) .\]

Back to the displacement in the bar. A derivative in which we change one variable, for example \(x\) in \(u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y_{\mathrm{P}}, z_{\mathrm{P}})\), and hold the other two fixed has a name of its own. Before naming it, let us draw what we measure with it (figure 1.97). The graph is the same as in the middle of the previous figure, \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) as a function of \(x\) along the vertical line on which \(y\) and \(z\) are fixed, so the axis \(x\) is horizontal and the axis \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) vertical. For the drawing we take \(a = 1\), \(L = 1\) and the constant term \(0{,}1\), not the real numbers, so that the shape can be seen. The blackwhite curve is \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) at these numbers. We choose the point at \(x = 0{,}3\), where the value is \(u_{\mathrm{x}}(x) = 0{,}41\), and move by the step \(\Delta x = 0{,}4\) to the point at \(x + \Delta x\), where the value is \(0{,}81\). The vertical distance between the values is \(\Delta u_{\mathrm{x}}\), the change of the displacement over this step. The orange secant passes through both points. Its slope is \(\Delta u_{\mathrm{x}} / \Delta x = 1\). As we shrink the step toward zero, the secant turns into the blue tangent at the first point, which has the slope \(2 - 2 \cdot 0{,}3 = 1{,}4\).

Definition 1.25 Partial derivative (parcialni odvod)

Let \(f\) be a function of three variables, that is, a function whose input is three numbers \(x\), \(y\), \(z\), the coordinates of a point, and whose output is one number \(f(x, y, z)\). Let \(f\) be defined at all points whose distance from the point \((x, y, z)\) is less than some number \(r > 0\). The partial derivatives (parcialni odvodi) of the function \(f\) with respect to \(x\), to \(y\) and to \(z\) at the point \((x, y, z)\) are the limits

\[\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(x, y, z) &= \lim_{\Delta \mathrm{x} \to 0} \frac{f(x + \Delta x, y, z) - f(x, y, z)}{\Delta x} , \\ \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(x, y, z) &= \lim_{\Delta \mathrm{y} \to 0} \frac{f(x, y + \Delta y, z) - f(x, y, z)}{\Delta y} , \\ \frac{\partial f}{\partial z}(x, y, z) &= \lim_{\Delta \mathrm{z} \to 0} \frac{f(x, y, z + \Delta z) - f(x, y, z)}{\Delta z} , \end{aligned} \tag{1.19}\]

if they exist. So the partial derivative with respect to \(x\) is the derivative of the function of one variable that we get by holding \(y\) and \(z\) fixed, and the same for the other two variables.

Source: LEBL2 Def. 8.3.8 (PDF p. 39), for functions of \(n\) variables. KREYSZIG Appendix A3.2, (1) and (2) (PDF p. 1210), for functions of two variables, for three variables on PDF p. 1211.

So a partial derivative is an ordinary derivative from definition 1.9. The only new thing is that a function of three variables has three variables we can change, and so three partial derivatives at every point. Each tells how fast \(f\) changes as we move away from the point along one of the axes. Partial derivatives of functions of any number of variables are defined in the same way: each time we change one variable and hold the others fixed (LEBL2 Def. 8.3.8).

We read \(\partial f / \partial x\) as "the partial derivative of \(f\) with respect to \(x\)". The sign \(\partial\) is a rounded d. It recalls the notation \(\mathrm{d}f / \mathrm{d}x\) for the ordinary derivative from section 1.3.4, and its different shape warns that \(f\) also depends on other variables, which we hold fixed while differentiating. This also gives a meaning to the notation \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial x\) that we mentioned with the definition of the strain at a point 1.10.

The notation with the rounded d is called the Legendre-Jacobi notation in textbooks (CAJNOT2, PDF p. 251).

Some books write the partial derivative more briefly with a subscript, \(f_{\mathrm{x}}\) (KREYSZIG Appendix A3.2, PDF p. 1209), or with a comma in the subscript, \(u_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j}}\) (SADD (2.1.4), PDF p. 40). In our book the subscript of \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) already means the component of the displacement vector in the direction of the axis \(x\) (definition 1.22). So we will always write partial derivatives with the sign \(\partial\). The notation \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial y\) thus means the rate at which the component \(x\) of the displacement changes as we move in the direction of the axis \(y\).

We draw this in the left part of figure 1.98. The axes: \(x\) points down, \(y\) to the right. The point \(P\) and the point \(C\) lie at the same depth, the blackwhite dots are before the load, the orange ones after it. The step \(\Delta y\) leads from \(P\) to \(C\). The blue arrows are the displacements downward, that is the component \(x\). At \(C\) the arrow is shorter, because the downward displacement is smaller farther from the axis, as the term \(-0{,}3\,a\,y^2\) shows. The difference \(\Delta u_{\mathrm{x}}\) is the difference of the lengths of the two arrows, and its quotient with the step \(\Delta y\) is \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial y\). The figure is greatly magnified.

Why does the definition require \(f\) to be defined at all points whose distance from \((x, y, z)\) is less than \(r\)? The difference quotient with respect to \(x\) uses the values at the points \((x + \Delta x, y, z)\) for all \(\Delta x\) with \(0 < |\Delta x| < r\), and these points must be in the domain of the function, as the definition of the limit 1.5 requires. In a body this holds at points around which there is still some material in all directions. We call such points interior points (notranje točke) of the body. The point \(P\) is interior: it is 1 mm from the surface of the bar and 5 m from both ends, so we can take \(r = 1\) mm. We draw this (figure 1.99), namely a cross section of the bar right next to its surface. The blackwhite vertical line is the surface of the bar, the material is to the left of it. The point \(P\) is 1 mm from the surface. The blue dashed circle around it has radius \(r\) and just touches the surface, so it lies entirely in the material. Let us also take the point \(S\) on the surface. The orange arrow is a step from it horizontally to the right, in the direction of the axis \(y\) outward (the axis \(y\) is horizontal here and points out of the material). It ends in the orange empty circle \(Q\), which is outside the material, where there is no displacement at all. The arrow is orange because this is a step that does not fit. On the surface of a body, then, a step outward leaves the body, and the definition as written does not apply there. The dimensions are exaggerated so that they can be seen. That is why LEBL2 defines partial derivatives only on sets in which every point is interior, the so-called open sets (LEBL2 Def. 8.3.8, PDF p. 39). In this section we compute only at interior points of the bar.

Let us compute the partial derivatives of the hanging bar. The difference quotients we will get all have the same form as the quotient of the displacement \(u(x)\) in section 1.3.4: a fixed number with a multiple of the change added. There we found the limit of such an expression with steps 6 to 12. So that we do not repeat the steps for every quotient, we do them once for a general expression.

We first draw the lemma (figure 1.100) for a number \(c > 0\). The axes are the horizontal \(h\) and the vertical \(g\). The blackwhite line is the graph \(g(h) = d + c\,h\), which is increasing for \(c > 0\). It crosses the vertical axis at the value \(d\), which is a grey dashed level, because the expression must approach it. The blue band around it is the allowed error, from \(d - \eta\) to \(d + \eta\). The blue segment on the axis \(h\) from \(-\delta\) to \(\delta\) is the band \(\delta\) that we are looking for. The grey dashed verticals carry it up to the places where the line leaves the blue band, so for every \(h\) inside the segment the line is inside the band. The numbers in the figure are chosen only for the drawing.

Lemma 1.26 Limit of a number plus a multiple (limita vsote števila in večkratnika)

For any numbers \(d\) and \(c\),

\[\lim_{\mathrm{h} \to 0} \,(d + c\,h) = d . \tag{1.20}\]
Proof

The function \(g(h) = d + c\,h\) is defined for every \(h\), so all the points the definition of the limit 1.5 talks about are in its domain. We write the definition out for this function. We must show: for every \(\eta > 0\) there is a \(\delta > 0\) such that \(|(d + c\,h) - d| < \eta\) for every \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta\).

  1. Pick any error \(\eta > 0\), in the figure the blue band. For it we must find a band \(\delta\).
  2. Simplify the difference that the condition bounds. The numbers \(d\) cancel, \((d + c\,h) - d = c\,h\). In the figure this is the vertical measure between the level \(d\) and the line at the chosen \(h\), which the horizontal measure shows. It has length \(c\,h\), because with every step \(h\) to the right the line rises by \(c\,h\).
  3. The absolute value of a product is the product of the absolute values, \(|c\,h| = |c|\,|h|\) (lemma 1.6, item 2). So the condition we must reach is \(|c|\,|h| < \eta\).
  4. If \(c = 0\), then \(|c|\,|h| = 0 < \eta\) for every \(h\). In the right part of the figure this is the horizontal line at the level \(d\), which lies inside the band at every \(h\). The condition holds for any band, so we take, for example, \(\delta = 1\).
  5. If \(c \neq 0\), then \(|c| > 0\). We divide the inequality \(|c|\,|h| < \eta\) by the positive number \(|c|\), so it does not turn around, and it holds exactly when \(|h| < \eta / |c|\). We take the band \(\delta = \eta / |c|\), which is positive, in the figure the segment from \(-\delta\) to \(\delta\).
  6. Check the case \(c \neq 0\). For every \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta\) we have \(|h| < \eta / |c|\), so \(|c|\,|h| < \eta\), and by steps 2 and 3 this is \(|(d + c\,h) - d|\).
  7. We picked the error \(\eta\) freely and found a band for it in both cases. The condition of the definition holds, so the limit of the expression \(d + c\,h\) at the point \(0\) is \(d\).

Source: The proof is not from a source. It follows the example \(g(h) = 5 - h\) of section 1.3.3, which is the case \(d = 5\), \(c = -1\) of this lemma.

We will use the lemma as follows. A difference quotient is defined only for \(\Delta x \neq 0\). If for every such \(\Delta x\) it equals the expression \(d + c\,\Delta x\), with numbers \(d\) and \(c\) that do not depend on \(\Delta x\), it has the same limit as this expression, because the definition of the limit looks only at the values for \(\Delta x \neq 0\). The change \(\Delta x\) plays the role of \(h\). So the limit is \(d\).

We start with the partial derivative of the component \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) with respect to \(x\) at any interior point \((x, y, z)\) of the bar. By the definition of the partial derivative 1.25 we keep \(y\) and \(z\) fixed and change only \(x\), so we move along the vertical line through the point.

  1. We get the value at the point \((x + \Delta x, y, z)\) from (1.18) by putting \(x + \Delta x\) in place of \(x\) and leaving \(y\) and \(z\): \(u_{\mathrm{x}}(x + \Delta x, y, z) = a\,(2L(x + \Delta x) - (x + \Delta x)^2) - 0{,}3\,a\,(y^2 + z^2)\).
  2. We subtract \(u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y, z) = a\,(2Lx - x^2) - 0{,}3\,a\,(y^2 + z^2)\). The term \(-0{,}3\,a\,(y^2 + z^2)\) is the same in both values, because \(y\) and \(z\) did not change, so it cancels. What remains is \(a\,(2L(x + \Delta x) - (x + \Delta x)^2) - a\,(2Lx - x^2)\).
  3. This is the same difference \(\Delta u_{\mathrm{x}}\) as in steps 1 to 4 of section 1.3.4 for the displacement \(u(x)\). We repeat the computation in small steps. We expand the square:
    \[(x + \Delta x)^2 = x^2 + 2x\,\Delta x + \Delta x^2 .\]
    We multiply out the bracket with \(2L\):
    \[2L(x + \Delta x) = 2Lx + 2L\,\Delta x .\]
    The terms \(2Lx\) and \(-x^2\) cancel, and what remains is
    \[a\,(2L\,\Delta x - 2x\,\Delta x - \Delta x^2) .\]
    We factor out \(\Delta x\):
    \[a\,\Delta x\,(2L - 2x - \Delta x) .\]
  4. We divide by \(\Delta x \neq 0\), and the factor \(\Delta x\) cancels:
    \[\frac{u_{\mathrm{x}}(x + \Delta x, y, z) - u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y, z)}{\Delta x} = a\,(2L - 2x - \Delta x) = 2a\,(L - x) + (-a)\,\Delta x .\]
  5. The quotient has the form \(d + c\,\Delta x\) with the numbers \(d = 2a\,(L - x)\) and \(c = -a\), which do not depend on \(\Delta x\). By lemma 1.26 on the limit of a number plus a multiple, its limit is \(d\):
    \[\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial x}(x, y, z) = 2a\,(L - x) .\]

The partial derivative does not depend on \(y\) and \(z\). At every depth the downward displacement changes with depth equally fast, on the axis and off it. In the figure this is the grey curve, \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) on the axis, higher by the constant term \(-0{,}3\,a\,(y^2 + z^2)\), and the grey tangent above the same \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\), parallel to the blue one. On the axis of the bar \(u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, 0, 0) = u(x)\), and the partial derivative is the derivative \(u'(x) = 2a\,(L - x)\), the strain at a point (1.7) that we computed in section 1.4. At the point \(P\), at the depth \(x_{\mathrm{P}} = 5\) m, it is \(2a\,(10 - 5)\ \text{m} = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6}\).

Next, the partial derivative of the component \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) with respect to \(y\). We hold \(x\) and \(z\) fixed and change only \(y\), so we move along the horizontal line. On the graph of \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) the step \(\Delta y\) is the horizontal measure from the point \(P\) onward, and the difference of the displacements, which we denote by \(\Delta u_{\mathrm{y}}\), is the vertical measure up to the point on the line at \(y + \Delta y\).

  1. From (1.18), \(u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y + \Delta y, z)\) \(= -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\,(y + \Delta y)\).
  2. We subtract \(u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y, z) = -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\,y\). We multiply out the bracket \((y + \Delta y)\), the terms with \(y\) cancel, and what remains is
    \[u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y + \Delta y, z) - u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y, z) = -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\,\Delta y . \tag{1.21}\]
  3. We divide by \(\Delta y \neq 0\). The quotient is \(-0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\) for every \(\Delta y \neq 0\). This is the form \(d + c\,\Delta y\) with \(d = -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\) and \(c = 0\).
  4. By lemma 1.26 on the limit of a number plus a multiple, the slope of the line is
    \[\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial y}(x, y, z) = -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x) .\]

Here the quotient is the same for every change \(\Delta y\), long or short, because the graph of \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) along the horizontal line is a straight line (figure 1.96, right). At the point \(P\) it is \(-0{,}6\,a\,(10 - 5)\ \text{m} = -5{,}856 \cdot 10^{-7}\).

Let us compare the two partial derivatives. At every depth \(x < L\) their ratio is

\[\frac{-0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)}{2a\,(L - x)} = -0{,}3 .\]

What does this mean? At the depth \(x\) take the point on the axis, \((x, 0, 0)\), and the point \((x, \Delta y, 0)\), which lies \(\Delta y\) to the right of it. By (1.21) with \(y = 0\), the components \(y\) of their displacements differ by \(-0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\,\Delta y\). Their separation in the direction of the axis \(y\) is before loading and after it

\[\begin{aligned} \text{pred: } & \Delta y , \\ \text{po: } & \Delta y - 0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\,\Delta y = (1 - 0{,}3\,\varepsilon)\,\Delta y , \end{aligned} \qquad \varepsilon = 2a\,(L - x) .\]

It decreases therefore by the fraction \(0{,}6\,a\,(L - x) = 0{,}3 \cdot 2a\,(L - x)\), that is, by \(0{,}3\) times the strain along the bar at that depth (a piece in figure 1.93). This is the thinning from section 1.6: a bar that gets longer by a thousandth gets thinner by \(0{,}3\) thousandths, and now we see that this holds at every depth separately.

We compute two more partial derivatives in steps, because in them the component of the displacement and the variable do not have the same direction. First \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) with respect to \(y\). We hold \(x\) and \(z\) fixed.

  1. In the difference \(u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y + \Delta y, z) - u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y, z)\) the term \(a\,(2Lx - x^2)\) cancels, because \(x\) did not change, and so does the term with \(z^2\). What remains is \(-0{,}3\,a\,\big((y + \Delta y)^2 - y^2\big)\).
  2. We expand the square:
    \[(y + \Delta y)^2 = y^2 + 2y\,\Delta y + \Delta y^2 .\]
    The terms \(y^2\) cancel, and what remains is
    \[-0{,}3\,a\,(2y\,\Delta y + \Delta y^2) .\]
    We factor out \(\Delta y\):
    \[-0{,}3\,a\,\Delta y\,(2y + \Delta y) .\]
  3. We divide by \(\Delta y \neq 0\). The quotient is \(-0{,}3\,a\,(2y + \Delta y) = -0{,}6\,a\,y + (-0{,}3\,a)\,\Delta y\), of the form \(d + c\,\Delta y\) with \(d = -0{,}6\,a\,y\) and \(c = -0{,}3\,a\).
  4. By lemma 1.26 on the limit of a number plus a multiple, the limit of the difference \(\Delta u_{\mathrm{x}}\) divided by the step \(\Delta y\) is
    \[\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial y}(x, y, z) = -0{,}6\,a\,y .\]

So as we move right, away from the axis, the downward displacement decreases a little. This is the curving of the cross section that we noticed above (figure 1.94): the rim of the cross section moves down less than its centre. At the point \(P\) it is \(-0{,}6\,a \cdot 0{,}004\ \text{m} = -4{,}685 \cdot 10^{-10}\).

Then \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) with respect to \(x\). We hold \(y\) and \(z\) fixed. We draw this in the middle part of figure 1.98. The axes are as before. The point \(P\) and the point \(A\) lie at the same \(y\), and \(A\) is lower by the step \(\Delta x\). The blue arrows are the displacements toward the axis, that is the component \(y\). At \(A\) the arrow is shorter, because lower down the bar gets thinner less. The difference \(\Delta u_{\mathrm{y}}\) is the difference of their lengths.

  1. From (1.18), \(u_{\mathrm{y}}(x + \Delta x, y, z)\) \(= -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x - \Delta x)\,y\).
  2. We split the bracket, \(-0{,}6\,a\,(L - x - \Delta x)\,y = -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\,y + 0{,}6\,a\,y\,\Delta x\). When we subtract \(u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y, z) = -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\,y\), what remains is \(0{,}6\,a\,y\,\Delta x\).
  3. We divide by \(\Delta x \neq 0\). The quotient is \(0{,}6\,a\,y\) for every \(\Delta x \neq 0\), of the form \(d + c\,\Delta x\) with \(d = 0{,}6\,a\,y\) and \(c = 0\).
  4. By lemma 1.26 on the limit of a number plus a multiple, the limit of the difference \(\Delta u_{\mathrm{y}}\) divided by the step \(\Delta x\) is
    \[\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial x}(x, y, z) = 0{,}6\,a\,y .\]

So as we move down, the displacement toward the axis decreases, because lower down the bar gets thinner less. At the point \(P\) it is \(0{,}6\,a \cdot 0{,}004\ \text{m} = 4{,}685 \cdot 10^{-10}\).

We get the remaining five partial derivatives with the same steps. Where the variable we differentiate with respect to does not appear in the function, the value does not change when this variable changes. The difference is zero, the quotient is zero for every change, and by lemma 1.26 on the limit of a number plus a multiple, with \(d = 0\) and \(c = 0\) the partial derivative is zero. The right part of figure 1.98 shows this for \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) along the axis \(z\). The axes are \(z\) and \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\). The horizontal line is \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\), because it does not depend on \(z\), and both dots on it lie at the same height. The step \(\Delta z\) therefore does not change the value. For example, \(u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y, z + \Delta z) - u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y, z) = 0\), because \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) does not depend on \(z\), and so \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial z = 0\). The component \(u_{\mathrm{z}}\) has the same form as \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\), only with \(z\) in place of \(y\), and the component \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) contains \(z\) in the same way as \(y\). So we get \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial z = -0{,}6\,a\,z\), \(\partial u_{\mathrm{z}} / \partial x = 0{,}6\,a\,z\), \(\partial u_{\mathrm{z}} / \partial y = 0\) and \(\partial u_{\mathrm{z}} / \partial z = -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\).

In all the computations the variables we held fixed behaved like fixed numbers. So we compute a partial derivative as an ordinary derivative in which we treat the other variables as numbers.

The table has two patterns. The first is on the diagonal, where the direction of the component matches the direction of the variable. Along the bar we have \(2a\,(L - x)\), which is the lengthening of the height of the piece in figure 1.93, and in both directions across it \(-0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\), which is \(-0{,}3\) times as much and the narrowing of the width of the piece. This is the thinning we explained above. The bar gets thinner in the direction of the axis \(z\) as much as in the direction of the axis \(y\). The reason is not the round cross section, since the field holds for a bar with any cross section that is the same along the whole length (SADD Example 5.1, PDF p. 115). The reason is that steel behaves the same in all directions across the bar and that nothing squeezes the bar from the side.

The second pattern is the pairs off the diagonal: \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial y = -0{,}6\,a\,y\) and \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial x = 0{,}6\,a\,y\) are equal in size and opposite in sign, and so are \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial z\) and \(\partial u_{\mathrm{z}} / \partial x\). Let us see what such a pair means on two fibres from the point \(P\) (figure 1.102). One goes down to the point \(A = (x + \Delta x, y, z)\), the other sideways to the point \(C = (x, y + \Delta y, z)\), and before loading they are at a right angle. Let us draw them from the side, with the axis \(x\) down and the axis \(y\) to the right. The point \(P\) is the common starting point of both fibres. The blackwhite vertical line is the fibre to the point \(A\), and the blackwhite horizontal line is the fibre to the point \(C\). The fibre \(PA\) is the vertical fibre and the fibre \(PC\) is the horizontal fibre, each is drawn in the figure in blackwhite before loading and in orange after it. The grey corner mark at \(P\) says that the angle between them is a right angle. In orange is the state after loading, the displacements are hugely enlarged so that they can be seen. The orange line is the vertical fibre after loading. It has leaned, and it is a little longer, because the bar stretches along its length. The orange line is the horizontal fibre after loading. It has risen and is a little shorter, because the bar gets thinner. The orange dots at the ends of the orange lines are the positions of the points \(A\) and \(C\) after loading.

The lower end of the vertical fibre moves sideways relative to \(P\) by \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial x \cdot \Delta x\) (the blue measure below \(A\)), and the side end of the horizontal fibre up by \(|\partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial y| \cdot \Delta y\) (the blue measure to the right of \(C\)). We call the slope of a fibre the ratio of the sideways shift of the end to the length of the fibre after loading. Because the two partial derivatives are equal in size, the two slopes are almost equal, and because they have opposite signs, both fibres turn the same way. The two blue arcs in the figure show these turns, both counterclockwise. They turn together, as if they were parts of the same rigid bar, and the right angle between them stays a right angle.

The slopes are not exactly equal, because the lengths of the fibres also change a little. Let \(s = \partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial x\), so \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial y = -s\), and the strains \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial x\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial y\). The vertical fibre gets longer to \((1 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}})\,\Delta x\), the horizontal one shrinks to \((1 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}})\,\Delta y\), where \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\) is negative. The slopes are therefore

\[\frac{s}{1 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}} \quad \text{in} \quad \frac{s}{1 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}} ,\]

and their difference is

\[s\,\Big(\frac{1}{1 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}} - \frac{1}{1 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}}\Big) = s\,\frac{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} - \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}}{(1 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}})\,(1 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}})} .\]

The strains \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\) are much smaller than \(1\), so the denominator is almost \(1\), and the difference is in absolute value about \(\dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial x}\Big(\dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial x} - \dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial y}\Big)\). At the point \(P\) this is \(4{,}685 \cdot 10^{-10} \cdot 2{,}538 \cdot 10^{-6} \approx 1{,}2 \cdot 10^{-15}\), more than a hundred thousand times smaller than the partial derivatives themselves. For small displacements we neglect such a product, as we neglected the square of the shift for the sheared cube in section 1.6.2. So the material near the point \(P\) only turns a little, and it is not sheared.

Now we also see why the second term of \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) has the number \(0{,}3\), half of \(0{,}6\). The partial derivative of the term \(-0{,}3\,a\,y^2\) with respect to \(y\) is \(-0{,}6\,a\,y\), exactly the opposite of \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial x = 0{,}6\,a\,y\). This half is not a property of steel. For every material the number in the second term of \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) is its ratio of thinning, and the number in \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(u_{\mathrm{z}}\) is twice this ratio (CM (7.3.20), PDF p. 306). So the number in \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) is always half the number in \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\), and the right angle stays a right angle for every material. The books also find that the shear strains of the hanging bar are zero (CM Example 7.3.2, Eq. (3), PDF p. 305, SADD (5.7.3), PDF p. 115). We will check this exactly when we compute the strains of short fibres in any direction, in section 1.17.4.

The partial derivatives tell how fast the displacement changes as we move away from a point along one of the axes. But a fibre can lie in any direction. How does the displacement change when we take a short step from the point \(P\) in any direction? We can split the step into three steps along the axes, just as we split a vector in lemma 1.21 on a vector as a sum of steps along the axes, and each of them changes only one coordinate. We continue with this in the next part of this section.

1.7.3 A step in any direction

We find by how much the displacement changes over a step from the point \(P\) to the point \(Q\) in any direction. We first split the step into three steps along the axes.

In this section we find by how much the displacement changes over a short step in any direction, and we show on the hanging bar that for a short step this change is almost exactly the sum of the products of the partial derivatives with the components of the step. For a general field we prove it in section 1.15.3. Let us return to the fibre of section 1.6.1. A fibre between the points \(P\) and \(Q\) of a body changes under loading only by the difference of the displacements of its ends,

\[\overrightarrow{P_1 Q_1} = \overrightarrow{PQ} + \big(\vek u(Q) - \vek u(P)\big) ,\]

where \(P_1\) and \(Q_1\) are the positions of the ends after loading ((1.15)). So for a short fibre in any direction we need the difference of displacements \(\vek u(Q) - \vek u(P)\). Let \(P = (x, y, z)\) be an interior point of the body, that is, a point with some material around it in all directions, and let the fibre reach to the point \(Q = (x + \Delta x,\ y + \Delta y,\ z + \Delta z)\). The vector of the fibre is then \(\overrightarrow{PQ}\)\( = (\Delta x, \Delta y, \Delta z)\) ((1.8)). We compute each component of the difference of displacements separately. To begin, take the component \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\).

We can also get from \(P\) to \(Q\) in three steps along the axes, just as in lemma 1.21 on a vector as a sum of steps along the axes we wrote a vector as a sum of steps (figure 1.103). The axes are placed as for the hanging bar: the axis \(x\) points down, the axis \(y\) to the right, the axis \(z\) toward the reader, and by the convention of oblique projection we draw it obliquely down and to the left. The point \(P\) is the start of the step, the blue arrow to the point \(Q\) is the whole step, the vector \(\overrightarrow{PQ}\). The four grey dashed edges are parts of the edges of the box whose opposite corners are \(P\) and \(Q\). They do not mean a step, but help to see the space. The blackwhite arrows are the three steps along the axes. First we step by \(\Delta x\) in the direction of the axis \(x\) to the point \(A\) \(= (x + \Delta x,\ y,\ z)\), then by \(\Delta y\) in the direction of the axis \(y\) to the point \(B\) \(= (x + \Delta x,\ y + \Delta y,\ z)\), and finally by \(\Delta z\) in the direction of the axis \(z\) to the point \(Q\). Each step changes only one coordinate. The blackwhite arrow for \(\Delta y\) has a gap in the figure where the blue arrow passes in front of it.

We split the difference \(u_{\mathrm{x}}(Q) - u_{\mathrm{x}}(P)\) into three changes, one for each step.

  1. We add and subtract the values at the intermediate points, \(u_{\mathrm{x}}(B) - u_{\mathrm{x}}(B) = 0\) and \(u_{\mathrm{x}}(A) - u_{\mathrm{x}}(A) = 0\). Adding zero does not change the value:
    \[u_{\mathrm{x}}(Q) - u_{\mathrm{x}}(P) = u_{\mathrm{x}}(Q) - u_{\mathrm{x}}(B) + u_{\mathrm{x}}(B) - u_{\mathrm{x}}(A) + u_{\mathrm{x}}(A) - u_{\mathrm{x}}(P) .\]
  2. We arrange the terms in pairs, read from \(P\) toward \(Q\):
    \[\begin{aligned} u_{\mathrm{x}}(Q) - u_{\mathrm{x}}(P) = {} & \big(u_{\mathrm{x}}(A) - u_{\mathrm{x}}(P)\big) + \big(u_{\mathrm{x}}(B) - u_{\mathrm{x}}(A)\big) \\ & + \big(u_{\mathrm{x}}(Q) - u_{\mathrm{x}}(B)\big) . \end{aligned} \tag{1.22}\]

This equality is exact and holds for every function of three variables that is defined at the points \(P\), \(A\), \(B\) and \(Q\), not only for a displacement. For a displacement all these points are in the body if the fibre is shorter than the number \(r\) up to which there is still material in all directions around the interior point \(P\). This is because the distances \(|PA| = |\Delta x|\) and \(|PB| = \sqrt{\Delta x^2 + \Delta y^2}\) are not larger than the length of the fibre \(|PQ|\). Each bracket is the change in one step along one axis. The first bracket starts at \(P\) and changes only \(x\). It is the numerator of the difference quotient from which, by the definition of the partial derivative 1.25, \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial x\) at the point \(P\) arises. The second bracket changes only \(y\), but it starts not at \(P\) but at \(A\). So it is the numerator of the quotient for \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial y\) at the point \(A\). The third bracket changes only \(z\) and starts at \(B\), so it is the numerator of the quotient for \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial z\) at the point \(B\).

Let us try this on the hanging bar, where

\[\begin{aligned} u_{\mathrm{x}} &= a\,(2Lx - x^2) - 0{,}3\,a\,(y^2 + z^2) , \\ u_{\mathrm{y}} &= -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\,y , \\ u_{\mathrm{z}} &= -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\,z . \end{aligned}\]

The component \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) teaches the most, so we start with it. We write equality (1.22) for it in the same way as for \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\).

  1. The first step, from \(P\) to \(A\), changes only \(x\). As in the computation of \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial x\) in section 1.7.2,
    \[u_{\mathrm{y}}(A) - u_{\mathrm{y}}(P) = -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x - \Delta x)\,y + 0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\,y = 0{,}6\,a\,y\,\Delta x .\]
    This is exactly \(\dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial x}(P)\,\Delta x\), since \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial x = 0{,}6\,a\,y\).
  2. The second step, from \(A\) to \(B\), changes only \(y\), but at the depth \(x + \Delta x\), where \(A\) lies:
    \[\begin{aligned} u_{\mathrm{y}}(B) - u_{\mathrm{y}}(A) &= -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x - \Delta x)\,(y + \Delta y) + 0{,}6\,a\,(L - x - \Delta x)\,y \\ &= -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x - \Delta x)\,\Delta y . \end{aligned}\]
  3. We split the bracket \((L - x - \Delta x)\) into \((L - x)\) and \(-\Delta x\):
    \[-0{,}6\,a\,(L - x - \Delta x)\,\Delta y = -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\,\Delta y + 0{,}6\,a\,\Delta x\,\Delta y .\]
    The first part is \(\dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial y}(P)\,\Delta y\), since \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial y = -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\). The second part, \(0{,}6\,a\,\Delta x\,\Delta y\), is there because the step starts at \(A\), at the depth \(x + \Delta x\). There the partial derivative with respect to \(y\) is \(-0{,}6\,a\,(L - x - \Delta x)\), not \(-0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\) as at \(P\).

Figure 1.104 shows this on two lines. The axis \(y\) is horizontal, the axis \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) is vertical and points up. The values are negative, so the lines lie below the axis \(y\). The blackwhite line is \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) as a function of \(y\) at the depth \(x\), where \(P\) lies. The blue line is the same at the depth \(x + \Delta x\), where \(A\) lies. Both pass through the origin, because on the axis of the bar \(y = 0\) and \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = 0\). The blue one is less steep, because the bar thins less lower down. The step \(\Delta y\) is the distance between \(y\) and \(y + \Delta y\) on the axis \(y\), the vertical grey dashed lines carry them to the lines. On each line there are two dots, the values at \(y\) and at \(y + \Delta y\). The horizontal grey dashed lines carry the heights of the dots to the measures: one the height of the blackwhite dot at \(y + \Delta y\), the other the height of the blue dot at \(y\). The grey measure beside the blackwhite one is how much \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) changes on the blackwhite line, the grey measure beside the blue one on the blue line. The blue one is smaller. The blackwhite measure is to the left of the step, because on the right the blue line would cross the measure. The difference between the measures is the term \(0{,}6\,a\,\Delta x\,\Delta y\). The scales are enlarged so that the difference can be seen.

  1. The third step, from \(B\) to \(Q\), changes only \(z\). The component \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) does not depend on \(z\), so \(u_{\mathrm{y}}(Q) - u_{\mathrm{y}}(B) = 0\), which is \(\dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial z}(P)\,\Delta z\), since \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial z = 0\).
  2. We add all three changes by (1.22):
    \[u_{\mathrm{y}}(Q) - u_{\mathrm{y}}(P) = 0{,}6\,a\,y\,\Delta x - 0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\,\Delta y + 0 \cdot \Delta z + 0{,}6\,a\,\Delta x\,\Delta y .\]

For \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(u_{\mathrm{z}}\) we take the same steps. In \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) the term \(-0{,}3\,a\,(y^2 + z^2)\) does not change with \(x\), so the second and third steps are the same as if they started at \(P\). We computed the changes in all three steps already in section 1.7.2, before taking the limit: \(a\,(2L - 2x - \Delta x)\,\Delta x\), \(-0{,}3\,a\,(2y + \Delta y)\,\Delta y\) and \(-0{,}3\,a\,(2z + \Delta z)\,\Delta z\). In \(u_{\mathrm{z}}\) the second step is zero, because \(u_{\mathrm{z}}\) does not depend on \(y\), and the third starts at \(B\) at the depth \(x + \Delta x\), just like the second step for \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\). Multiplying out the brackets and adding the changes, we get for all three components

\[\begin{aligned} u_{\mathrm{x}}(Q) - u_{\mathrm{x}}(P) &= 2a\,(L - x)\,\Delta x - 0{,}6\,a\,y\,\Delta y - 0{,}6\,a\,z\,\Delta z \\ &\quad - a\,\Delta x^2 - 0{,}3\,a\,\Delta y^2 - 0{,}3\,a\,\Delta z^2 , \\ u_{\mathrm{y}}(Q) - u_{\mathrm{y}}(P) &= 0{,}6\,a\,y\,\Delta x - 0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\,\Delta y + 0 \cdot \Delta z \\ &\quad + 0{,}6\,a\,\Delta x\,\Delta y , \\ u_{\mathrm{z}}(Q) - u_{\mathrm{z}}(P) &= 0{,}6\,a\,z\,\Delta x + 0 \cdot \Delta y - 0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\,\Delta z \\ &\quad + 0{,}6\,a\,\Delta x\,\Delta z . \end{aligned} \tag{1.23}\]

Each line starts with three products. The numbers in front of \(\Delta x\), \(\Delta y\) and \(\Delta z\) are the partial derivatives from the matching row of the table in section 1.7.2, taken at the point \(P\). After them come terms that are products of two components of the step, \(\Delta x^2\), \(\Delta x\,\Delta y\) and the like. We call these terms the remainder (ostanek). In general we define it as everything that is left when we subtract the three products with the partial derivatives at \(P\) from the difference:

\[u_{\mathrm{x}}(Q) - u_{\mathrm{x}}(P) = \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial x}(P)\,\Delta x + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial y}(P)\,\Delta y + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial z}(P)\,\Delta z + R_{\mathrm{x}} , \tag{1.24}\]

and in the same way for \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) with the remainder \(R_{\mathrm{y}}\) and for \(u_{\mathrm{z}}\) with the remainder \(R_{\mathrm{z}}\). This is only a way of writing that holds for every field that has all three partial derivatives at the point \(P\): \(R_{\mathrm{x}}\) is what is left over. For the hanging bar, by (1.23),

\[\begin{aligned} R_{\mathrm{x}} &= -a\,\Delta x^2 - 0{,}3\,a\,\Delta y^2 - 0{,}3\,a\,\Delta z^2 , \\ R_{\mathrm{y}} &= 0{,}6\,a\,\Delta x\,\Delta y , \\ R_{\mathrm{z}} &= 0{,}6\,a\,\Delta x\,\Delta z . \end{aligned}\]

Books often write the difference of displacements without the remainder (SADD (2.1.1), PDF p. 39) and say that they dropped the higher-order terms because the components of the step are small (SADD, PDF p. 40). But how small is the remainder? Take a fibre from the point \(P\) with \(x_{\mathrm{P}} = 5\) m, \(y_{\mathrm{P}} = 4\) mm and \(z_{\mathrm{P}} = 0\) in the direction of the unit vector \(\vek n = \big(\tfrac13, \tfrac23, \tfrac23\big)\), which has the length \(\sqrt{1/9 + 4/9 + 4/9} = 1\). A fibre of length \(h\) has the vector \(\overrightarrow{PQ} = h\,\vek n\). For two lengths, let us compute for \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) the part with the partial derivatives and the remainder \(R_{\mathrm{x}}\):

length of the fibre \(h\) part with the partial derivatives remainder \(R_{\mathrm{x}}\) remainders \(R_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(R_{\mathrm{z}}\)
1 mm \(6{,}504 \cdot 10^{-10}\) m \(-7{,}374 \cdot 10^{-14}\) m \(2{,}603 \cdot 10^{-14}\) m
0,1 mm \(6{,}504 \cdot 10^{-11}\) m \(-7{,}374 \cdot 10^{-16}\) m \(2{,}603 \cdot 10^{-16}\) m

Let us compare the sizes of the two parts at two fibre lengths (figure 1.105). The horizontal axis is the fibre length \(h\) in millimetres, from \(0{,}1\) to \(1\). Both axes are logarithmic, so every factor of \(10\) means the same distance: the vertical segment shows the height of a factor of \(10\). The vertical values are in metres and are written next to the points. The blue line is the part with partial derivatives, which we label \(D\) in the figure, with the values from the table at both lengths. For a fibre ten times shorter it drops by a factor of \(10\). The orange line is the absolute value of the remainder \(|R_{\mathrm{x}}|\), which drops by a factor of \(100\). The grey line is the absolute value of the remainders \(|R_{\mathrm{y}}|\) and \(|R_{\mathrm{z}}|\), which are equal for this fibre because \(n_{\mathrm{y}} = n_{\mathrm{z}}\), and the values are in the last column of the table. It too drops by a factor of \(100\). The orange line is steeper than the blue one because the remainder is proportional to \(h^2\), and the part \(D\) to \(h\): a power of \(h\) is a straight line on a logarithmic axis, and its slope, measured in decades vertically per decade horizontally, is the exponent. The decade is drawn four times longer horizontally than vertically, so on paper the slopes are not \(1\) and \(2\), but their ratio \(2 : 1\) is kept. Why? In the part with partial derivatives each term is the product of a fixed number and one component of the step, for example \(2a\,(L - x)\,\Delta x\), and each component is proportional to \(h\). In the remainder, though, each term is a product of two components, for example \(\Delta x\,\Delta y\), and so it is proportional to \(h \cdot h = h^2\). This is the same rule we met at the sheared cube in section 1.6.2: next to a small number, its square is smaller by roughly a factor of that small number.

We can write this exactly. A fibre in the direction of the unit vector \(\vek n = (n_{\mathrm{x}}, n_{\mathrm{y}}, n_{\mathrm{z}})\) is in figure 1.103 the blue arrow \(\overrightarrow{PQ}\), and its components are the blackwhite steps \(\Delta x\), \(\Delta y\) and \(\Delta z\): \(\Delta x = h\,n_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\Delta y = h\,n_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\Delta z = h\,n_{\mathrm{z}}\). For \(h > 0\) it points in the direction of the vector \(\vek n\), for \(h < 0\) in the opposite direction. We allow negative \(h\) because the definition of the limit 1.5 looks at numbers \(h\) on both sides of zero. The length of the fibre is \(|h|\), since \(\sqrt{h^2 n_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + h^2 n_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + h^2 n_{\mathrm{z}}^2} = |h|\,\sqrt{n_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + n_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + n_{\mathrm{z}}^2} = |h|\). We take \(0 < |h| < r\), so that the fibre stays in the body. The goal is to show that the remainder divided by the length of the fibre goes to zero as we shorten the fibre. Then for ever shorter fibres the remainder is an ever smaller part of the difference of displacements, and the difference of displacements is almost exactly the sum of the products with the partial derivatives, which we need for the strain of a short fibre. The computation has four steps.

  1. We put the components of the step into \(R_{\mathrm{x}}\). Each term gets the factor \(h^2\), which we take out:
    \[R_{\mathrm{x}} = -a\,h^2 n_{\mathrm{x}}^2 - 0{,}3\,a\,h^2 n_{\mathrm{y}}^2 - 0{,}3\,a\,h^2 n_{\mathrm{z}}^2 = -a\,\big(n_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + 0{,}3\,n_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + 0{,}3\,n_{\mathrm{z}}^2\big)\,h^2 .\]
  2. We divide by \(h\):
    \[\frac{R_{\mathrm{x}}}{h} = -a\,\big(n_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + 0{,}3\,n_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + 0{,}3\,n_{\mathrm{z}}^2\big)\,h .\]
    This is the form \(d + c\,h\), where \(d = 0\) and \(c = -a\,(n_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + 0{,}3\,n_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + 0{,}3\,n_{\mathrm{z}}^2)\), a number that does not depend on \(h\).
  3. By lemma 1.26 on the limit of a number plus a multiple, the limit is \(d = 0\). The length of the fibre, though, is \(|h|\), not \(h\). The quotients \(R_{\mathrm{x}}/h\) and \(R_{\mathrm{x}}/|h|\) differ at most in sign, so they are equally far from zero, and the band \(\delta\) that the lemma gives for an error \(\eta\) works for \(R_{\mathrm{x}}/|h|\) too. So the remainder divided by the length \(|h|\) of the fibre goes to zero.
  4. We get the same for the other two remainders: \(R_{\mathrm{y}} / h = 0{,}6\,a\,n_{\mathrm{x}} n_{\mathrm{y}}\,h\) and \(R_{\mathrm{z}} / h = 0{,}6\,a\,n_{\mathrm{x}} n_{\mathrm{z}}\,h\) have the form \(0 + c\,h\), so they go to zero.

What does this tell us about the difference of displacements? We divide (1.24) by \(h\) and put in \(\Delta x = h\,n_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\Delta y = h\,n_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(\Delta z = h\,n_{\mathrm{z}}\):

\[\frac{u_{\mathrm{x}}(Q) - u_{\mathrm{x}}(P)}{h} = \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial x}(P)\,n_{\mathrm{x}} + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial y}(P)\,n_{\mathrm{y}} + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial z}(P)\,n_{\mathrm{z}} + \frac{R_{\mathrm{x}}}{h} .\]

The first three terms do not depend on \(h\), and the last is \(c\,h\). By lemma 1.26 on the limit of a sum of a number and a multiple, the quotient approaches the sum of the first three terms as \(h\) goes to zero. At the point \(P\) and in the direction \(\vek n\) this sum is \(1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6} \cdot \tfrac13 - 4{,}685 \cdot 10^{-10} \cdot \tfrac23 + 0 \cdot \tfrac23 = 6{,}5035 \cdot 10^{-7}\), and the quotient is \(6{,}5028 \cdot 10^{-7}\) at \(h = 1\) mm and \(6{,}5035 \cdot 10^{-7}\) at \(h = 0{,}1\) mm (figure 1.106). On the axis \(h\) is the length of the fibre in millimetres, from \(0\) to \(1\) mm, on the axis \(q\) the quotient. The blackwhite line is the quotient at every length of the fibre. The right dot is the fibre of length \(1\) mm, a grey dashed vertical line joins it to the axis, and the left dot is the fibre shortened to \(0{,}1\) mm. The empty circle is at length zero, where there is no fibre, so the quotient is not defined there and the circle is empty. The line approaches it. The blue value \(d\) on the axis \(q\) is the limit, \(6{,}5035 \cdot 10^{-7}\), the sum of the products of the partial derivatives at \(P\) with the components of the direction. The grey dashed level carries it across the figure. The difference between the quotient and the value \(d\) is \(R_{\mathrm{x}} / h\). The vertical scale is greatly enlarged, so that it can be seen that the line falls slightly. In fact the quotients at \(1\) mm and \(0{,}1\) mm differ only in the fourth digit. This is the same picture as for the hanging bar in section 1.4, where the strain of ever shorter pieces approached the derivative. So on the hanging bar the difference of displacements of a short fibre, divided by its length, is almost exactly the sum of the products of the partial derivatives at \(P\) with the components of the direction. We will need this for the strain of a short fibre in an arbitrary direction.

For the hanging bar we computed the remainder exactly, because each component of its field is a sum of terms with at most two coordinate factors, like \(x^2\) and \(x\,y\). That is why the remainder was exactly a number times \(h^2\). A field with a term like \(x^3\) would also give terms with \(h^3\) in the remainder. But what about a general field? Equality (1.22) is true for every field. The first bracket starts at \(P\), so by the definition of the partial derivative 1.25 its quotient approaches the partial derivative at \(P\). The second and third brackets, though, start at the points \(A\) and \(B\), which move as we shorten the fibre. For the bar this gave terms like \(0{,}6\,a\,\Delta x\,\Delta y\). For a general field two facts would finish the job.

We draw the first fact for \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) along the step from \(A\) to \(B\), where only \(y\) changes (figure 1.107). The axes: \(y\) horizontal, \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) vertical. The curve is \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) along the step. The point \(A\) is at \(y\), the point \(B\) at \(y + \Delta y\). The orange secant joins the two points, and its slope is the change of \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) divided by \(\Delta y\). The blue tangent at the point at \(y^*\) between them is parallel to the secant. The grey tangent at the point \(A\) has a slightly different slope.

  1. For a function of one variable that has a derivative at every point between two numbers and no jump at the end points: the change of the function between the numbers equals the derivative at some point between them times the difference of the two numbers. We will prove this statement in section 1.14. For the second bracket this function is \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) along the step from \(A\) to \(B\), where only \(y\) changes, and the statement reads
    \[u_{\mathrm{x}}(B) - u_{\mathrm{x}}(A) = \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial y}\ \text{(at the point at } y^*\text{)} \cdot \Delta y .\]
    In the figure the orange secant has the slope \(\big(u_{\mathrm{x}}(B) - u_{\mathrm{x}}(A)\big)/\Delta y\), and the blue tangent at \(y^*\) has the slope \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial y\) at that point. The two lines are parallel, so the slopes are equal, and that is the equation divided by \(\Delta y\). We may use the statement if \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) has a partial derivative with respect to \(y\) at all points from \(A\) to \(B\), which the assumption of item 2 ensures. The same holds for the third bracket, where only \(z\) changes from \(B\) to \(Q\).
  2. We assume that the partial derivatives exist at all points near \(P\) and change there without jumps: at points close enough to \(P\) they differ only a little from those at \(P\). This is an assumption about the field, which has to be checked for every field. Then the partial derivative at the point between \(A\) and \(B\) differs a little from the one at \(P\), and the remainder is small compared with the length of the fibre.

With these two facts LEBL2 proves that the remainder divided by the length of the step goes to zero for every field whose partial derivatives have no jumps (LEBL2 Prop. 8.4.6, PDF pp. 48-49). For a graph the first fact looks self-evident: between two points of a graph without jumps or sharp corners there is a point where the tangent is parallel to the line through both points. A proof, though, must show that such a point really exists, and it turns out to depend on the fact that the number line has no holes.

So for a few sections we now turn to mathematics. First we look at why the number line has no holes. Then we look at endless strings of numbers that approach some number, then at functions whose graph has no jumps, and finally at the statement about the derivative at a point in between. Then we return to the fibre and show that the remainder divided by the length of the fibre goes to zero for every such field.
