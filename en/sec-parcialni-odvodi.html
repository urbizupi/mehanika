Chapter 1 Strain

1.7 Mathematics: partial derivatives

At the end of the previous section we found that in a general body the displacement changes from point to point in all three directions. For the bar we measured how fast the displacement changes along the bar with the derivative (definition 1.9). In this section we extend the derivative to functions of three variables. First we look at a body that needs this extension: the hanging bar, this time with all three components of its displacement. Then we define the derivative in which only one of the three coordinates changes, compute it for this bar and look at what the numbers tell us.

1.7.1 The hanging bar in space

A steel bar of length \(L = 10\) m hangs from the ceiling and stretches under its own weight. Let it have a round cross section with a diameter of 10 mm. How it is held matters for its field, so let us say it exactly (figure 1.41, left). The field we will take from the books holds for a bar of which only one point is fixed, the centre \(O\) of its top end, which does not move (CM (7.3.16), PDF p. 305). The books set no other condition for the top face. Their solution, though, implies that every cross section carries the part of the bar below it evenly over its whole face, the top one included (CM Example 7.3.2, PDF p. 305, SADD Example 5.1, PDF p. 115). So the weight of the bar is carried by a force that acts upward on the top face and is spread evenly over it, the same on every square millimetre. The fixed point \(O\) carries no weight and only keeps the bar from moving as a whole. That the bar does not turn as a whole either, CM ensures with one more condition: the material right next to the point \(O\) does not turn (CM, PDF p. 306). The top face is not glued to anything, so, like every cross section, it can get a little narrower and, as we will see, curve a little. No real ceiling holds a bar like this. This way of holding is an idealization that gives a simple field. How other ways of holding change the field near the ceiling, we look at at the end of this section.

We set up the coordinate system as in section 1.6: the origin \(O\) is at the centre of the top end of the bar, the axis \(x\) points vertically down along the axis of the bar, and the axes \(y\) and \(z\) are horizontal, perpendicular to the bar. So the coordinate \(x\) of a point is its depth below the ceiling, and the coordinates \(y\) and \(z\) say how far from the axis of the bar it lies and on which side.

In section 1.2 we used, for the points on the axis of the bar, the downward displacement

\[u(x) = a\,(2Lx - x^2) , \qquad a = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1} .\]

In section 1.6 we saw that a bar that stretches also gets thinner: when a steel bar gets longer by a thousandth of its length, its thickness decreases by about \(0{,}3\) thousandths. So the points off the axis also move toward the axis. The whole displacement field of the hanging bar, that is, the rule that assigns to every point of the bar its displacement vector (definition 1.23), is

\[\begin{aligned} u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y, z) &= a\,(2Lx - x^2) - 0{,}3\,a\,(y^2 + z^2) , \\ u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y, z) &= -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\,y , \\ u_{\mathrm{z}}(x, y, z) &= -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\,z . \end{aligned} \tag{1.18}\]

The number \(0{,}3\) is the ratio of thinning to stretching for steel, Poisson's ratio from section 1.6. For another material its own ratio would stand here. Where the other numbers in the field come from, we explain below.

We took the field from SADD (5.7.4) (PDF p. 115) and CM (7.3.20) (PDF p. 306), where it is written in differently placed axes and with symbols for properties of steel. CM (7.3.20) lacks a constant term in the component of the displacement along the bar, which CM determines just before from the condition that the point \(O\) does not move (CM, PDF p. 306). How the field is derived we will see in chapter 11. The two books derive it under these assumptions. The steel is the same everywhere and behaves the same in all directions. After unloading it returns to its original shape. The strains are small, below about a thousandth, and the displacements are small compared with the bar. The weight acts on every small piece vertically downward, and the bar is held in the idealized way we described (CM Example 7.3.2, PDF pp. 305-306, SADD Example 5.1, PDF p. 115). Where one of the assumptions does not hold, for example near a real ceiling or under a load so large that the steel deforms permanently, the field does not hold. For now we take it as given and look at what it says.

On the axis of the bar \(y = 0\) and \(z = 0\). Putting them into (1.18) gives

\[\begin{aligned} u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, 0, 0) &= a\,(2Lx - x^2) - 0{,}3\,a\,(0^2 + 0^2) = a\,(2Lx - x^2) , \\ u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, 0, 0) &= 0 , \\ u_{\mathrm{z}}(x, 0, 0) &= 0 . \end{aligned}\]

So a point on the axis moves only down, by \(u(x)\), and its displacement vector is \(\big(u(x), 0, 0\big)\), as we wrote in section 1.6.

Off the axis the points also move sideways (figure 1.42). Look at the component \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\,y\). The numbers \(a\) and \(L - x\) are not negative, so \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) has the opposite sign to \(y\). A point to the right of the axis, with \(y > 0\), moves to the left, and a point to the left of the axis, with \(y < 0\), moves to the right. So both move toward the axis. The displacement is proportional to \(y\): a point twice as far from the axis moves twice as much. That is why the bar gets thinner. The component \(u_{\mathrm{z}}\) says the same for the direction of the axis \(z\). The factor \(L - x\) says that the bar gets thinner more at the top than lower down, and not at all at the lower end, where \(x = L\). This also agrees with what we know. In section 1.4 the strain along the bar was \(2a\,(L - x)\), largest at the top and zero at the lower end, at both ends as a one-sided derivative (definition 1.9). The bar gets thinner where it stretches.

Figure 1.43 shows a cross section from below. Every point of the cross section moves toward its centre, the more the farther from the centre it lies, and the circle shrinks into a smaller circle.

By how much does a point move toward the axis, and where does the number \(0{,}6\) come from? Look at a small piece of material at the depth \(x\) (figure 1.44). Along the bar it is stretched by the strain \(\varepsilon = 2a\,(L - x)\), which we computed in section 1.4. So its height \(\Delta x\) grows to \((1 + \varepsilon)\,\Delta x\). Across the bar it contracts by \(0{,}3\) times that: its width \(\Delta y\) decreases to \((1 - 0{,}3\,\varepsilon)\,\Delta y\). We met the thinning rule in section 1.6 for a whole bar, but it also holds for such a small piece, because the neighbouring parts of the bar pull it only up and down and do not squeeze it sideways (CM Example 7.3.2, PDF p. 305). The fraction by which it contracts is

\[0{,}3\,\varepsilon = 0{,}3 \cdot 2a\,(L - x) = 0{,}6\,a\,(L - x) .\]

The formula for \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) says exactly this: a point at the distance \(y\) from the axis comes closer to the axis by this fraction of the distance \(y\),

\[u_{\mathrm{y}} = -0{,}3 \cdot 2a\,(L - x) \cdot y = -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\,y .\]

So the number \(0{,}6\) is \(0{,}3\) times the \(2\) from the strain \(2a\,(L - x)\). The same holds for \(u_{\mathrm{z}}\) in the direction of the axis \(z\). The number \(0{,}3\) in the second term of \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\), on the other hand, is half of \(0{,}6\). Why exactly half, we will see at the end of section 1.7.2: with this term the right angle between a fibre along the bar and a fibre across it stays a right angle.

How large are these displacements? Take the point \(P\) at the depth 5 m, 4 mm to the right of the axis, in the plane \(z = 0\). So its coordinates are \(x_{\mathrm{P}} = 5\) m, \(y_{\mathrm{P}} = 0{,}004\) m and \(z_{\mathrm{P}} = 0\). By (1.18) it moves down by

\[\begin{aligned} u_{\mathrm{x}}(P) &= a\,(2 \cdot 10 \cdot 5 - 5^2)\ \text{m}^2 - 0{,}3\,a\,(0{,}004^2 + 0^2)\ \text{m}^2 \\ &= 1{,}464 \cdot 10^{-5}\ \text{m} - 9{,}4 \cdot 10^{-13}\ \text{m} \end{aligned}\]

and sideways by

\[u_{\mathrm{y}}(P) = -0{,}6\,a\,(10 - 5)\ \text{m} \cdot 0{,}004\ \text{m} = -2{,}342 \cdot 10^{-9}\ \text{m} ,\]

while \(u_{\mathrm{z}}(P) = 0\). It moves down by \(14{,}64\) µm and toward the axis by \(2{,}342\) nm. A nanometre, nm, is a billionth of a metre, \(10^{-9}\) m, that is, a thousandth of a micrometre. The displacement toward the axis is more than six thousand times smaller than the downward one, which is why figure 1.42 exaggerates it much more.

The second term of \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(-0{,}3\,a\,(y^2 + z^2)\), says that a point off the axis moves down a little less than the point on the axis at the same depth. At the point \(P\) the difference is only \(9{,}4 \cdot 10^{-13}\) m, more than ten million times less than the downward displacement. So a cross section that was flat before loading becomes very slightly curved: its centre moves a little lower than its rim (figure 1.45).

Where does this shape come from? We might expect the opposite, that the rim of the cross section sags more than the centre, like the rim of an umbrella held at its middle. But in this field the bar does not hang from the point \(O\): its weight is carried by the force spread evenly over the top face, and every cross section carries the part of the bar below it evenly over its whole face (CM Example 7.3.2, PDF p. 305). The curving has another source, the thinning, which changes with depth (figure 1.46). Higher up the bar gets thinner more than lower down, so a point at the distance \(y\) from the axis moves toward the axis more when it lies higher. A vertical fibre at the distance \(y\) from the axis therefore leans: its upper end moves toward the axis more than its lower end. The material, though, is not sheared, and the right angle between a vertical fibre and a horizontal fibre of the cross section stays a right angle, up to a negligibly small correction. The reason is that neighbouring parts of the bar pass each other only vertical forces, no sideways ones (CM Example 7.3.2, PDF p. 305). We will show it by computation at the end of section 1.7.2. For the angle to stay a right angle, the horizontal fibre of the cross section must turn by the same amount as the vertical fibre next to it, so its outer end rises. Farther from the axis the vertical fibres lean more, because there the points move toward the axis more. So the cross section rises more and more toward the rim and takes the shape of the letter U. The same holds for the top face: it curves too, its centre \(O\) stays in place and its rim rises a little (figure 1.41, left).

Let us return to how the bar is held. The field (1.18) describes a bar that is fixed only at the point \(O\): this point does not move, since \(\vek u(0, 0, 0) = (0, 0, 0)\). The other points of the top face move: a point on the rim of the top face, 5 mm from the axis, moves toward the axis by \(0{,}6\,a \cdot 10\ \text{m} \cdot 0{,}005\ \text{m} = 5{,}856 \cdot 10^{-9}\) m. A real ceiling holds the bar differently. If the bar is welded to the ceiling over its whole top face (figure 1.41, right), the face can neither narrow nor curve. If it were attached so that it could slide along the ceiling, which is rare, it would stay flat and at the same height, but it could narrow. In each case the field near the ceiling would differ a little from (1.18). Far from the place where the body is held, though, the field is almost the same however exactly the forces holding it are spread, as long as together they pull equally (SADD §5.6, PDF pp. 114-115). How far, the book says only in words: the differences die out with the distance from the place where the body is held, but it gives no exact distance. In figure 1.41 the outlines of the two bars are the same lower down.

Now we can ask what we asked for the bar with the derivative: how fast does the displacement change as we move away from a point? In a body we can move in any direction. Let us start with the directions of the axes.

1.7.2 Partial derivative

For the bar the displacement was a function of one variable, \(u(x)\), and the rate at which it changes was the derivative (definition 1.9), the limit of the difference quotient \((u(x + \Delta x) - u(x))/\Delta x\) as \(\Delta x\) goes to zero. Now each component of the displacement is a function of three variables, whose input is the three coordinates of a point and whose output is one number.

Suppose we move down from the point \(P = (x_{\mathrm{P}}, y_{\mathrm{P}}, z_{\mathrm{P}})\), along the vertical line through \(P\) (figure 1.47, left). On this line only the coordinate \(x\) changes, while the coordinates \(y\) and \(z\) stay \(y_{\mathrm{P}}\) and \(z_{\mathrm{P}}\). So on it the component \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) is a function of a single variable, which assigns to the number \(x\) the number

\[u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y_{\mathrm{P}}, z_{\mathrm{P}}) = a\,(2Lx - x^2) - 0{,}3\,a\,(y_{\mathrm{P}}^2 + z_{\mathrm{P}}^2) .\]

The second term is the same fixed number along the whole line. The derivative of this function tells how fast the downward displacement changes as we move down from \(P\). On its graph it is the slope of the tangent above \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\) (figure 1.47, middle), just as the derivative in figure 1.19 was the slope of the tangent. In the same way, on the horizontal line through \(P\) in the direction of the axis \(y\) only the coordinate \(y\) changes. On it the component \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) is a function of one variable, which assigns to the number \(y\) the number \(u_{\mathrm{y}}(x_{\mathrm{P}}, y, z_{\mathrm{P}}) = -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x_{\mathrm{P}})\,y\). Its graph is a straight line (figure 1.47, right).

From history

Derivatives of functions of several variables in which only one variable changes were computed already by Newton, Leibniz and the Bernoulli family, but as a rule without a special notation (CAJNOT2, PDF p. 244). In 1728 Euler used the letters \(P\), \(Q\) and \(R\) for them, for the derivatives with respect to \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\) (CAJNOT2, PDF p. 245). Jean le Rond d'Alembert was led to equations with derivatives in several variables in 1744 by the equilibrium and motion of fluids, in 1746 by the winds and in 1747 by the vibrating string that we met in section 1.3.1 (CAJORI, PDF p. 257-258). In 1755 Euler wrote the derivative of the function \(P\) with respect to \(y\) in parentheses. The notation meant that the function \(P\) is differentiated with only \(y\) changing, and the resulting change is divided by \(\mathrm{d}y\) (CAJNOT2, PDF p. 245). In 1786 Adrien-Marie Legendre wrote that, to avoid all ambiguity, he would denote by \(\partial v/\partial x\) how much the function \(v\) changes per unit change of \(x\) when only \(x\) changes, and by \(\mathrm{d}v/\mathrm{d}x\) the complete change of the function \(v\) divided by \(\mathrm{d}x\) (CAJNOT2, PDF p. 249). The following year he no longer used the notation. Perhaps also because the letter d was claimed in other fields of mathematics, the notation did not catch on at once (CAJNOT2, PDF p. 249-250). Lagrange wrote such derivatives with a prime, just like the derivative of a function of one variable, for example \(F'(x)\), \(F'(y)\) and \(F'(z)\) for the derivatives of the function \(F(x, y, z)\) with respect to \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\) (CAJNOT2, PDF p. 253). The danger of this notation is that \(F'(x)\) could be read as the derivative of a function of the single variable \(x\) (CAJNOT2, PDF p. 253). In 1841 Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi wrote that Lagrange's notation fails if, for a function of three or more variables, we want to differentiate twice or more (CAJNOT2, PDF p. 253). We see the reason as follows. In \(F'(x)\) the name of the variable in the brackets says with respect to which one we differentiate. For a second derivative we would have to give two variables and their order, while the primes say only that we differentiate, not with respect to what. Stäckel remarked in 1896 that this defect can be removed with several indices (CAJNOT2, PDF p. 253).

Back to the displacement in the bar. A derivative in which we change one variable, for example \(x\) in \(u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y_{\mathrm{P}}, z_{\mathrm{P}})\), and hold the other two fixed has a name of its own.

Definition 1.25 Partial derivative (parcialni odvod)

Let \(f\) be a function of three variables, that is, a function whose input is three numbers \(x\), \(y\), \(z\), the coordinates of a point, and whose output is one number \(f(x, y, z)\). Let \(f\) be defined at all points whose distance from the point \((x, y, z)\) is less than some number \(r > 0\). The partial derivatives (parcialni odvodi) of the function \(f\) with respect to \(x\), to \(y\) and to \(z\) at the point \((x, y, z)\) are the limits

\[\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(x, y, z) &= \lim_{\Delta \mathrm{x} \to 0} \frac{f(x + \Delta x, y, z) - f(x, y, z)}{\Delta x} , \\ \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(x, y, z) &= \lim_{\Delta \mathrm{y} \to 0} \frac{f(x, y + \Delta y, z) - f(x, y, z)}{\Delta y} , \\ \frac{\partial f}{\partial z}(x, y, z) &= \lim_{\Delta \mathrm{z} \to 0} \frac{f(x, y, z + \Delta z) - f(x, y, z)}{\Delta z} , \end{aligned} \tag{1.19}\]

if they exist. So the partial derivative with respect to \(x\) is the derivative of the function of one variable that we get by holding \(y\) and \(z\) fixed, and the same for the other two variables.

Source: LEBL2 Def. 8.3.8 (PDF p. 39), for functions of \(n\) variables. KREYSZIG Appendix A3.2, (1) and (2) (PDF p. 1210), for functions of two variables, for three variables on PDF p. 1211.

So a partial derivative is an ordinary derivative from definition 1.9. The only new thing is that a function of three variables has three variables we can change, and so three partial derivatives at every point. Each tells how fast \(f\) changes as we move away from the point along one of the axes. Partial derivatives of functions of any number of variables are defined in the same way: each time we change one variable and hold the others fixed (LEBL2 Def. 8.3.8).

We read \(\partial f / \partial x\) as "the partial derivative of \(f\) with respect to \(x\)". The sign \(\partial\) is a rounded d. It recalls the notation \(\mathrm{d}f / \mathrm{d}x\) for the ordinary derivative from section 1.3.4, and its different shape warns that \(f\) also depends on other variables, which we hold fixed while differentiating. This also gives a meaning to the notation \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial x\) that we mentioned with the definition of the strain at a point 1.10.

The notation with the rounded d is called the Legendre-Jacobi notation in textbooks (CAJNOT2, PDF p. 251).

Some books write the partial derivative more briefly with a subscript, \(f_{\mathrm{x}}\) (KREYSZIG Appendix A3.2, PDF p. 1209), or with a comma in the subscript, \(u_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j}}\) (SADD (2.1.4), PDF p. 40). In our book the subscript of \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) already means the component of the displacement vector in the direction of the axis \(x\) (definition 1.22). So we will always write partial derivatives with the sign \(\partial\). The notation \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial y\) thus means the rate at which the component \(x\) of the displacement changes as we move in the direction of the axis \(y\).

Why does the definition require \(f\) to be defined at all points whose distance from \((x, y, z)\) is less than \(r\)? The difference quotient with respect to \(x\) uses the values at the points \((x + \Delta x, y, z)\) for all \(\Delta x\) with \(0 < |\Delta x| < r\), and these points must be in the domain of the function, as the definition of the limit 1.5 requires. In a body this holds at points around which there is still some material in all directions. We call such points interior points (notranje točke) of the body. The point \(P\) is interior: it is 1 mm from the surface of the bar and 5 m from both ends, so we can take \(r = 1\) mm. On the surface of a body, though, a step outward leaves the body, and the definition as written does not apply there. That is why LEBL2 defines partial derivatives only on sets in which every point is interior, the so-called open sets (LEBL2 Def. 8.3.8, PDF p. 39). In this section we compute only at interior points of the bar.

Let us compute the partial derivatives of the hanging bar. The difference quotients we will get all have the same form as the quotient of the displacement \(u(x)\) in section 1.3.4: a fixed number with a multiple of the change added. There we found the limit of such an expression with steps 6 to 12. So that we do not repeat the steps for every quotient, we do them once for a general expression.

Lemma 1.26 Limit of a number plus a multiple (limita vsote števila in večkratnika)

For any numbers \(d\) and \(c\),

\[\lim_{\mathrm{h} \to 0} \,(d + c\,h) = d . \tag{1.20}\]
Proof

The function \(g(h) = d + c\,h\) is defined for every \(h\), so all the points the definition of the limit 1.5 talks about are in its domain. We write the definition out for this function. We must show: for every \(\eta > 0\) there is a \(\delta > 0\) such that \(|(d + c\,h) - d| < \eta\) for every \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta\).

  1. Pick any error \(\eta > 0\). For it we must find a band \(\delta\).
  2. Simplify the difference that the condition bounds. The numbers \(d\) cancel, \((d + c\,h) - d = c\,h\).
  3. The absolute value of a product is the product of the absolute values, \(|c\,h| = |c|\,|h|\) (lemma 1.6, item 2). So the condition we must reach is \(|c|\,|h| < \eta\).
  4. If \(c = 0\), then \(|c|\,|h| = 0 < \eta\) for every \(h\). The condition holds for any band, so we take, for example, \(\delta = 1\).
  5. If \(c \neq 0\), then \(|c| > 0\). We divide the inequality \(|c|\,|h| < \eta\) by the positive number \(|c|\), so it does not turn around, and it holds exactly when \(|h| < \eta / |c|\). We take the band \(\delta = \eta / |c|\), which is positive.
  6. Check the case \(c \neq 0\). For every \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta\) we have \(|h| < \eta / |c|\), so \(|c|\,|h| < \eta\), and by steps 2 and 3 this is \(|(d + c\,h) - d|\).
  7. We picked the error \(\eta\) freely and found a band for it in both cases. The condition of the definition holds, so the limit of the expression \(d + c\,h\) at the point \(0\) is \(d\).

Source: The proof is not from a source. It follows the example \(g(h) = 5 - h\) of section 1.3.3, which is the case \(d = 5\), \(c = -1\) of this lemma.

We will use the lemma as follows. A difference quotient is defined only for \(\Delta x \neq 0\). If for every such \(\Delta x\) it equals the expression \(d + c\,\Delta x\), with numbers \(d\) and \(c\) that do not depend on \(\Delta x\), it has the same limit as this expression, because the definition of the limit looks only at the values for \(\Delta x \neq 0\). The change \(\Delta x\) plays the role of \(h\). So the limit is \(d\).

We start with the partial derivative of the component \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) with respect to \(x\) at any interior point \((x, y, z)\) of the bar. We keep \(y\) and \(z\) fixed and change only \(x\), as the definition of the partial derivative 1.25 prescribes.

  1. We get the value at the point \((x + \Delta x, y, z)\) from (1.18) by putting \(x + \Delta x\) in place of \(x\) and leaving \(y\) and \(z\): \(u_{\mathrm{x}}(x + \Delta x, y, z) = a\,(2L(x + \Delta x) - (x + \Delta x)^2) - 0{,}3\,a\,(y^2 + z^2)\).
  2. We subtract \(u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y, z) = a\,(2Lx - x^2) - 0{,}3\,a\,(y^2 + z^2)\). The term \(-0{,}3\,a\,(y^2 + z^2)\) is the same in both values, because \(y\) and \(z\) did not change, so it cancels. What remains is \(a\,(2L(x + \Delta x) - (x + \Delta x)^2) - a\,(2Lx - x^2)\).
  3. This is the same difference as in steps 1 to 4 of section 1.3.4 for the displacement \(u(x)\). We repeat the computation. We expand the square, \((x + \Delta x)^2 = x^2 + 2x\,\Delta x + \Delta x^2\), and multiply out \(2L(x + \Delta x) = 2Lx + 2L\,\Delta x\). The terms \(2Lx\) and \(-x^2\) cancel, and what remains is \(a\,(2L\,\Delta x - 2x\,\Delta x - \Delta x^2) = a\,\Delta x\,(2L - 2x - \Delta x)\).
  4. We divide by \(\Delta x \neq 0\), and the factor \(\Delta x\) cancels:
    \[\frac{u_{\mathrm{x}}(x + \Delta x, y, z) - u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y, z)}{\Delta x} = a\,(2L - 2x - \Delta x) = 2a\,(L - x) + (-a)\,\Delta x .\]
  5. The quotient has the form \(d + c\,\Delta x\) with the numbers \(d = 2a\,(L - x)\) and \(c = -a\), which do not depend on \(\Delta x\). By lemma 1.26 on the limit of a number plus a multiple, its limit is \(d\):
    \[\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial x}(x, y, z) = 2a\,(L - x) .\]

The partial derivative does not depend on \(y\) and \(z\). At every depth the downward displacement changes with depth equally fast, on the axis and off it. On the axis of the bar \(u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, 0, 0) = u(x)\), and the partial derivative is the derivative \(u'(x) = 2a\,(L - x)\), the strain at a point (1.7) that we computed in section 1.4. At the point \(P\), at the depth \(x_{\mathrm{P}} = 5\) m, it is \(2a\,(10 - 5)\ \text{m} = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6}\).

Next, the partial derivative of the component \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) with respect to \(y\). We hold \(x\) and \(z\) fixed and change only \(y\).

  1. From (1.18), \(u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y + \Delta y, z) = -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\,(y + \Delta y)\).
  2. We subtract \(u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y, z) = -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\,y\). We multiply out the bracket \((y + \Delta y)\), the terms with \(y\) cancel, and what remains is
    \[u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y + \Delta y, z) - u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y, z) = -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\,\Delta y . \tag{1.21}\]
  3. We divide by \(\Delta y \neq 0\). The quotient is \(-0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\) for every \(\Delta y \neq 0\). This is the form \(d + c\,\Delta y\) with \(d = -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\) and \(c = 0\).
  4. By lemma 1.26 on the limit of a number plus a multiple,
    \[\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial y}(x, y, z) = -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x) .\]

Here the quotient is the same for every change \(\Delta y\), long or short, because the graph of \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) along the horizontal line is a straight line (figure 1.47, right). At the point \(P\) it is \(-0{,}6\,a\,(10 - 5)\ \text{m} = -5{,}856 \cdot 10^{-7}\).

Let us compare the two partial derivatives. At every depth \(x < L\) their ratio is

\[\frac{-0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)}{2a\,(L - x)} = -0{,}3 .\]

What does this mean? At the depth \(x\) take the point on the axis, \((x, 0, 0)\), and the point \((x, \Delta y, 0)\), which lies \(\Delta y\) to the right of it. By (1.21) with \(y = 0\), the components \(y\) of their displacements differ by \(-0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\,\Delta y\). So their separation in the direction of the axis \(y\), \(\Delta y\) before loading, is \(\Delta y - 0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\,\Delta y\) after loading. It decreases by the fraction \(0{,}6\,a\,(L - x) = 0{,}3 \cdot 2a\,(L - x)\), that is, by \(0{,}3\) times the strain along the bar at that depth. This is the thinning from section 1.6: a bar that gets longer by a thousandth gets thinner by \(0{,}3\) thousandths, and now we see that this holds at every depth separately.

We compute two more partial derivatives in steps, because in them the component of the displacement and the variable do not have the same direction. First \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) with respect to \(y\). We hold \(x\) and \(z\) fixed.

  1. In the difference \(u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y + \Delta y, z) - u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y, z)\) the term \(a\,(2Lx - x^2)\) cancels, because \(x\) did not change, and so does the term with \(z^2\). What remains is \(-0{,}3\,a\,\big((y + \Delta y)^2 - y^2\big)\).
  2. We expand the square, \((y + \Delta y)^2 = y^2 + 2y\,\Delta y + \Delta y^2\), the terms \(y^2\) cancel, and what remains is \(-0{,}3\,a\,(2y\,\Delta y + \Delta y^2) = -0{,}3\,a\,\Delta y\,(2y + \Delta y)\).
  3. We divide by \(\Delta y \neq 0\). The quotient is \(-0{,}3\,a\,(2y + \Delta y) = -0{,}6\,a\,y + (-0{,}3\,a)\,\Delta y\), of the form \(d + c\,\Delta y\) with \(d = -0{,}6\,a\,y\) and \(c = -0{,}3\,a\).
  4. By lemma 1.26 on the limit of a number plus a multiple,
    \[\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial y}(x, y, z) = -0{,}6\,a\,y .\]

So as we move right, away from the axis, the downward displacement decreases a little. This is the curving of the cross section that we noticed above: the rim of the cross section moves down less than its centre. At the point \(P\) it is \(-0{,}6\,a \cdot 0{,}004\ \text{m} = -4{,}685 \cdot 10^{-10}\).

Then \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) with respect to \(x\). We hold \(y\) and \(z\) fixed.

  1. From (1.18), \(u_{\mathrm{y}}(x + \Delta x, y, z) = -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x - \Delta x)\,y\).
  2. We split the bracket, \(-0{,}6\,a\,(L - x - \Delta x)\,y = -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\,y + 0{,}6\,a\,y\,\Delta x\). When we subtract \(u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y, z) = -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\,y\), what remains is \(0{,}6\,a\,y\,\Delta x\).
  3. We divide by \(\Delta x \neq 0\). The quotient is \(0{,}6\,a\,y\) for every \(\Delta x \neq 0\), of the form \(d + c\,\Delta x\) with \(d = 0{,}6\,a\,y\) and \(c = 0\).
  4. By lemma 1.26 on the limit of a number plus a multiple,
    \[\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial x}(x, y, z) = 0{,}6\,a\,y .\]

So as we move down, the displacement toward the axis decreases, because lower down the bar gets thinner less. At the point \(P\) it is \(0{,}6\,a \cdot 0{,}004\ \text{m} = 4{,}685 \cdot 10^{-10}\).

We get the remaining five partial derivatives with the same steps. Where the variable we differentiate with respect to does not appear in the function, the value does not change when this variable changes. The difference is zero, the quotient is zero for every change, and by lemma 1.26 on the limit of a number plus a multiple, with \(d = 0\) and \(c = 0\) the partial derivative is zero. For example, \(u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y, z + \Delta z) - u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y, z) = 0\), because \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) does not depend on \(z\), and so \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial z = 0\). The component \(u_{\mathrm{z}}\) has the same form as \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\), only with \(z\) in place of \(y\), and the component \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) contains \(z\) in the same way as \(y\). So we get \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial z = -0{,}6\,a\,z\), \(\partial u_{\mathrm{z}} / \partial x = 0{,}6\,a\,z\), \(\partial u_{\mathrm{z}} / \partial y = 0\) and \(\partial u_{\mathrm{z}} / \partial z = -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\).

In all the computations the variables we held fixed behaved like fixed numbers. So we compute a partial derivative as an ordinary derivative in which we treat the other variables as numbers.

Let us collect all nine partial derivatives in a table. The row gives the component of the displacement, the column the variable we differentiate with respect to.

w.r.t. \(x\) w.r.t. \(y\) w.r.t. \(z\)
\(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) \(2a\,(L - x)\) \(-0{,}6\,a\,y\) \(-0{,}6\,a\,z\)
\(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) \(0{,}6\,a\,y\) \(-0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\) \(0\)
\(u_{\mathrm{z}}\) \(0{,}6\,a\,z\) \(0\) \(-0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\)

At the point \(P\) with \(x_{\mathrm{P}} = 5\) m, \(y_{\mathrm{P}} = 4\) mm and \(z_{\mathrm{P}} = 0\) these partial derivatives have the following values. All of them are numbers without a unit, since a displacement in metres is divided by a distance in metres.

at the point \(P\) w.r.t. \(x\) w.r.t. \(y\) w.r.t. \(z\)
\(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) \(1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6}\) \(-4{,}685 \cdot 10^{-10}\) \(0\)
\(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) \(4{,}685 \cdot 10^{-10}\) \(-5{,}856 \cdot 10^{-7}\) \(0\)
\(u_{\mathrm{z}}\) \(0\) \(0\) \(-5{,}856 \cdot 10^{-7}\)

The table has two patterns. The first is on the diagonal, where the direction of the component matches the direction of the variable. Along the bar we have \(2a\,(L - x)\), and in both directions across it \(-0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\), which is \(-0{,}3\) times as much. This is the thinning we explained above. The bar gets thinner in the direction of the axis \(z\) as much as in the direction of the axis \(y\). The reason is not the round cross section, since the field holds for a bar with any cross section that is the same along the whole length (SADD Example 5.1, PDF p. 115). The reason is that steel behaves the same in all directions across the bar and that nothing squeezes the bar from the side.

The second pattern is the pairs off the diagonal: \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial y = -0{,}6\,a\,y\) and \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial x = 0{,}6\,a\,y\) are equal in size and opposite in sign, and so are \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial z\) and \(\partial u_{\mathrm{z}} / \partial x\). Let us see in words what such a pair means. From the point \(P\) draw two short fibres, one down in the direction of the axis \(x\), the other sideways in the direction of the axis \(y\). The lower end of the first fibre moves a little sideways relative to \(P\), and the side end of the second moves a little up relative to \(P\). Because the two partial derivatives are equal in size, the two fibres lean by the same ratio of the sideways shift of the end to the length of the fibre, and because they have opposite signs, the two fibres turn the same way. So the right angle between the fibres stays a right angle. More precisely, the fibres do not lean by quite the same ratio, because their lengths also change a little: the vertical fibre gets longer by the fraction \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial x\), the horizontal one shorter by the fraction \(-\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial y\). So the difference between the ratios is about \(\dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial x}\Big(\dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial x} - \dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial y}\Big)\), at the point \(P\) equal to \(4{,}685 \cdot 10^{-10} \cdot 2{,}538 \cdot 10^{-6} \approx 1{,}2 \cdot 10^{-15}\), more than a hundred thousand times smaller than the partial derivatives themselves. For small displacements we neglect such a product, as we neglected the square of the shift for the sheared cube in section 1.6.2. So the material near the point \(P\) only turns a little, and it is not sheared. Now we also see why the second term of \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) has the number \(0{,}3\), half of \(0{,}6\). The partial derivative of the term \(-0{,}3\,a\,y^2\) with respect to \(y\) is \(-0{,}6\,a\,y\), exactly the opposite of \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial x = 0{,}6\,a\,y\). This half is not a property of steel. For every material the number in the second term of \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) is its ratio of thinning, and the number in \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(u_{\mathrm{z}}\) is twice this ratio (CM (7.3.20), PDF p. 306). So the number in \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) is always half the number in \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\), and the right angle stays a right angle for every material. The books also find that the shear strains of the hanging bar are zero (CM Example 7.3.2, Eq. (3), PDF p. 305, SADD (5.7.3), PDF p. 115). We will check this exactly when we compute the strains of short fibres in any direction, in section 1.17.4.

The partial derivatives tell how fast the displacement changes as we move away from a point along one of the axes. But a fibre can lie in any direction. How does the displacement change when we take a short step from the point \(P\) in any direction? We can split the step into three steps along the axes, just as we split a vector in lemma 1.21 on a vector as a sum of steps along the axes, and each of them changes only one coordinate. We continue with this in the next part of this section.

1.7.3 A step in any direction

In this section we find by how much the displacement changes over a short step in any direction, and we show on the hanging bar that for a short step this change is almost exactly the sum of the products of the partial derivatives with the components of the step. For a general field we prove it in section 1.15.3. Let us return to the fibre of section 1.6.1. A fibre between the points \(P\) and \(Q\) of a body changes under loading only by the difference of the displacements of its ends,

\[\overrightarrow{P_1 Q_1} = \overrightarrow{PQ} + \big(\vek u(Q) - \vek u(P)\big) ,\]

where \(P_1\) and \(Q_1\) are the positions of the ends after loading ((1.15)). So for a short fibre in any direction we need the difference of displacements \(\vek u(Q) - \vek u(P)\). Let \(P = (x, y, z)\) be an interior point of the body, that is, a point with some material around it in all directions, and let the fibre reach to the point \(Q = (x + \Delta x,\ y + \Delta y,\ z + \Delta z)\). The vector of the fibre is then \(\overrightarrow{PQ} = (\Delta x, \Delta y, \Delta z)\) ((1.8)). We compute each component of the difference of displacements separately. To begin, take the component \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\).

We can also get from \(P\) to \(Q\) in three steps along the axes, just as in lemma 1.21 on a vector as a sum of steps along the axes we wrote a vector as a sum of steps (figure 1.48). First we step by \(\Delta x\) in the direction of the axis \(x\) to the point \(A = (x + \Delta x,\ y,\ z)\), then by \(\Delta y\) in the direction of the axis \(y\) to the point \(B = (x + \Delta x,\ y + \Delta y,\ z)\), and finally by \(\Delta z\) in the direction of the axis \(z\) to the point \(Q\). Each step changes only one coordinate.

We split the difference \(u_{\mathrm{x}}(Q) - u_{\mathrm{x}}(P)\) into three changes, one for each step.

  1. We add and subtract the values at the intermediate points, \(u_{\mathrm{x}}(B) - u_{\mathrm{x}}(B) = 0\) and \(u_{\mathrm{x}}(A) - u_{\mathrm{x}}(A) = 0\). Adding zero does not change the value:
    \[u_{\mathrm{x}}(Q) - u_{\mathrm{x}}(P) = u_{\mathrm{x}}(Q) - u_{\mathrm{x}}(B) + u_{\mathrm{x}}(B) - u_{\mathrm{x}}(A) + u_{\mathrm{x}}(A) - u_{\mathrm{x}}(P) .\]
  2. We arrange the terms in pairs, read from \(P\) toward \(Q\):
    \[\begin{aligned} u_{\mathrm{x}}(Q) - u_{\mathrm{x}}(P) = {} & \big(u_{\mathrm{x}}(A) - u_{\mathrm{x}}(P)\big) + \big(u_{\mathrm{x}}(B) - u_{\mathrm{x}}(A)\big) \\ & + \big(u_{\mathrm{x}}(Q) - u_{\mathrm{x}}(B)\big) . \end{aligned} \tag{1.22}\]

This equality is exact and holds for every function of three variables that is defined at the points \(P\), \(A\), \(B\) and \(Q\), not only for a displacement. For a displacement all these points are in the body if the fibre is shorter than the number \(r\) up to which there is still material in all directions around the interior point \(P\). This is because the distances \(|PA| = |\Delta x|\) and \(|PB| = \sqrt{\Delta x^2 + \Delta y^2}\) are not larger than the length of the fibre \(|PQ|\). Each bracket is the change in one step along one axis. The first bracket starts at \(P\) and changes only \(x\). It is the numerator of the difference quotient from which, by the definition of the partial derivative 1.25, \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial x\) at the point \(P\) arises. The second bracket changes only \(y\), but it starts not at \(P\) but at \(A\). So it is the numerator of the quotient for \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial y\) at the point \(A\). The third bracket changes only \(z\) and starts at \(B\), so it is the numerator of the quotient for \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial z\) at the point \(B\).

Let us try this on the hanging bar, where

\[\begin{aligned} u_{\mathrm{x}} &= a\,(2Lx - x^2) - 0{,}3\,a\,(y^2 + z^2) , \\ u_{\mathrm{y}} &= -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\,y , \\ u_{\mathrm{z}} &= -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\,z . \end{aligned}\]

The component \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) teaches the most, so we start with it. We write equality (1.22) for it in the same way as for \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\).

  1. The first step, from \(P\) to \(A\), changes only \(x\). As in the computation of \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial x\) in section 1.7.2,
    \[u_{\mathrm{y}}(A) - u_{\mathrm{y}}(P) = -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x - \Delta x)\,y + 0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\,y = 0{,}6\,a\,y\,\Delta x .\]
    This is exactly \(\dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial x}(P)\,\Delta x\), since \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial x = 0{,}6\,a\,y\).
  2. The second step, from \(A\) to \(B\), changes only \(y\), but at the depth \(x + \Delta x\), where \(A\) lies:
    \[\begin{aligned} u_{\mathrm{y}}(B) - u_{\mathrm{y}}(A) &= -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x - \Delta x)\,(y + \Delta y) + 0{,}6\,a\,(L - x - \Delta x)\,y \\ &= -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x - \Delta x)\,\Delta y . \end{aligned}\]
  3. We split the bracket \((L - x - \Delta x)\) into \((L - x)\) and \(-\Delta x\):
    \[-0{,}6\,a\,(L - x - \Delta x)\,\Delta y = -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\,\Delta y + 0{,}6\,a\,\Delta x\,\Delta y .\]
    The first part is \(\dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial y}(P)\,\Delta y\), since \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial y = -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\). The second part, \(0{,}6\,a\,\Delta x\,\Delta y\), is there because the step starts at \(A\), at the depth \(x + \Delta x\). There the partial derivative with respect to \(y\) is \(-0{,}6\,a\,(L - x - \Delta x)\), not \(-0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\) as at \(P\) (figure 1.49).
  4. The third step, from \(B\) to \(Q\), changes only \(z\). The component \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) does not depend on \(z\), so \(u_{\mathrm{y}}(Q) - u_{\mathrm{y}}(B) = 0\), which is \(\dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial z}(P)\,\Delta z\), since \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial z = 0\).
  5. We add the three changes by (1.22):
    \[u_{\mathrm{y}}(Q) - u_{\mathrm{y}}(P) = 0{,}6\,a\,y\,\Delta x - 0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\,\Delta y + 0 \cdot \Delta z + 0{,}6\,a\,\Delta x\,\Delta y .\]

For \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(u_{\mathrm{z}}\) we take the same steps. In \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) the term \(-0{,}3\,a\,(y^2 + z^2)\) does not change with \(x\), so the second and third steps are the same as if they started at \(P\). We computed the changes in all three steps already in section 1.7.2, before taking the limit: \(a\,(2L - 2x - \Delta x)\,\Delta x\), \(-0{,}3\,a\,(2y + \Delta y)\,\Delta y\) and \(-0{,}3\,a\,(2z + \Delta z)\,\Delta z\). In \(u_{\mathrm{z}}\) the second step is zero, because \(u_{\mathrm{z}}\) does not depend on \(y\), and the third starts at \(B\) at the depth \(x + \Delta x\), just like the second step for \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\). Multiplying out the brackets and adding the changes, we get for all three components

\[\begin{aligned} u_{\mathrm{x}}(Q) - u_{\mathrm{x}}(P) &= 2a\,(L - x)\,\Delta x - 0{,}6\,a\,y\,\Delta y - 0{,}6\,a\,z\,\Delta z \\ &\quad - a\,\Delta x^2 - 0{,}3\,a\,\Delta y^2 - 0{,}3\,a\,\Delta z^2 , \\ u_{\mathrm{y}}(Q) - u_{\mathrm{y}}(P) &= 0{,}6\,a\,y\,\Delta x - 0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\,\Delta y + 0 \cdot \Delta z \\ &\quad + 0{,}6\,a\,\Delta x\,\Delta y , \\ u_{\mathrm{z}}(Q) - u_{\mathrm{z}}(P) &= 0{,}6\,a\,z\,\Delta x + 0 \cdot \Delta y - 0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\,\Delta z \\ &\quad + 0{,}6\,a\,\Delta x\,\Delta z . \end{aligned} \tag{1.23}\]

Each line starts with three products. The numbers in front of \(\Delta x\), \(\Delta y\) and \(\Delta z\) are the partial derivatives from the matching row of the table in section 1.7.2, taken at the point \(P\). After them come terms that are products of two components of the step, \(\Delta x^2\), \(\Delta x\,\Delta y\) and the like. We call these terms the remainder (ostanek). In general we define it as everything that is left when we subtract the three products with the partial derivatives at \(P\) from the difference:

\[u_{\mathrm{x}}(Q) - u_{\mathrm{x}}(P) = \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial x}(P)\,\Delta x + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial y}(P)\,\Delta y + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial z}(P)\,\Delta z + R_{\mathrm{x}} , \tag{1.24}\]

and in the same way for \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) with the remainder \(R_{\mathrm{y}}\) and for \(u_{\mathrm{z}}\) with the remainder \(R_{\mathrm{z}}\). This is only a way of writing that holds for every field that has all three partial derivatives at the point \(P\): \(R_{\mathrm{x}}\) is what is left over. For the hanging bar, by (1.23),

\[\begin{aligned} R_{\mathrm{x}} &= -a\,\Delta x^2 - 0{,}3\,a\,\Delta y^2 - 0{,}3\,a\,\Delta z^2 , \\ R_{\mathrm{y}} &= 0{,}6\,a\,\Delta x\,\Delta y , \\ R_{\mathrm{z}} &= 0{,}6\,a\,\Delta x\,\Delta z . \end{aligned}\]

Books often write the difference of displacements without the remainder (SADD (2.1.1), PDF p. 39) and say that they dropped the higher-order terms because the components of the step are small (SADD, PDF p. 40). But how small is the remainder? Take a fibre from the point \(P\) with \(x_{\mathrm{P}} = 5\) m, \(y_{\mathrm{P}} = 4\) mm and \(z_{\mathrm{P}} = 0\) in the direction of the unit vector \(\vek n = \big(\tfrac13, \tfrac23, \tfrac23\big)\), which has the length \(\sqrt{1/9 + 4/9 + 4/9} = 1\). A fibre of length \(h\) has the vector \(\overrightarrow{PQ} = h\,\vek n\). For two lengths, let us compute for \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) the part with the partial derivatives and the remainder \(R_{\mathrm{x}}\):

length of the fibre \(h\) part with the partial derivatives remainder \(R_{\mathrm{x}}\)
1 mm \(6{,}504 \cdot 10^{-10}\) m \(-7{,}374 \cdot 10^{-14}\) m
0,1 mm \(6{,}504 \cdot 10^{-11}\) m \(-7{,}374 \cdot 10^{-16}\) m

For a fibre ten times shorter the part with the partial derivatives is ten times smaller, but the remainder a hundred times smaller. Why? In the part with the partial derivatives each term is the product of a fixed number and one component of the step, for example \(2a\,(L - x)\,\Delta x\), and each component is proportional to \(h\). In the remainder, though, each term is a product of two components, for example \(\Delta x\,\Delta y\), and so it is proportional to \(h \cdot h = h^2\). This is the same rule we met at the sheared cube in section 1.6.2: next to a small number, its square is smaller by roughly a factor of that small number.

We can write this exactly. A fibre in the direction of the unit vector \(\vek n = (n_{\mathrm{x}}, n_{\mathrm{y}}, n_{\mathrm{z}})\) has the components \(\Delta x = h\,n_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\Delta y = h\,n_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\Delta z = h\,n_{\mathrm{z}}\). For \(h > 0\) it points in the direction of the vector \(\vek n\), for \(h < 0\) in the opposite direction. We allow negative \(h\) because the definition of the limit 1.5 looks at numbers \(h\) on both sides of zero. The length of the fibre is \(|h|\), since \(\sqrt{h^2 n_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + h^2 n_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + h^2 n_{\mathrm{z}}^2} = |h|\,\sqrt{n_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + n_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + n_{\mathrm{z}}^2} = |h|\). We take \(0 < |h| < r\), so that the fibre stays in the body. Let us show that the remainder divided by \(h\) goes to zero as we shorten the fibre.

  1. We put the components of the step into \(R_{\mathrm{x}}\). Each term gets the factor \(h^2\), which we take out:
    \[R_{\mathrm{x}} = -a\,h^2 n_{\mathrm{x}}^2 - 0{,}3\,a\,h^2 n_{\mathrm{y}}^2 - 0{,}3\,a\,h^2 n_{\mathrm{z}}^2 = -a\,\big(n_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + 0{,}3\,n_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + 0{,}3\,n_{\mathrm{z}}^2\big)\,h^2 .\]
  2. We divide by \(h\):
    \[\frac{R_{\mathrm{x}}}{h} = -a\,\big(n_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + 0{,}3\,n_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + 0{,}3\,n_{\mathrm{z}}^2\big)\,h .\]
    This is the form \(d + c\,h\), where \(d = 0\) and \(c = -a\,(n_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + 0{,}3\,n_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + 0{,}3\,n_{\mathrm{z}}^2)\), a number that does not depend on \(h\).
  3. By lemma 1.26 on the limit of a number plus a multiple, the limit is \(d = 0\). The length of the fibre, though, is \(|h|\), not \(h\). The quotients \(R_{\mathrm{x}}/h\) and \(R_{\mathrm{x}}/|h|\) differ at most in sign, so they are equally far from zero, and the band \(\delta\) that the lemma gives for an error \(\eta\) works for \(R_{\mathrm{x}}/|h|\) too. So the remainder divided by the length \(|h|\) of the fibre goes to zero.
  4. We get the same for the other two remainders: \(R_{\mathrm{y}} / h = 0{,}6\,a\,n_{\mathrm{x}} n_{\mathrm{y}}\,h\) and \(R_{\mathrm{z}} / h = 0{,}6\,a\,n_{\mathrm{x}} n_{\mathrm{z}}\,h\) have the form \(0 + c\,h\), so they go to zero.

What does this tell us about the difference of displacements? We divide (1.24) by \(h\) and put in \(\Delta x = h\,n_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\Delta y = h\,n_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(\Delta z = h\,n_{\mathrm{z}}\):

\[\frac{u_{\mathrm{x}}(Q) - u_{\mathrm{x}}(P)}{h} = \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial x}(P)\,n_{\mathrm{x}} + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial y}(P)\,n_{\mathrm{y}} + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial z}(P)\,n_{\mathrm{z}} + \frac{R_{\mathrm{x}}}{h} .\]

The first three terms do not depend on \(h\), and the last one is \(c\,h\). By lemma 1.26 on the limit of a number plus a multiple, the quotient approaches the sum of the first three terms as \(h\) goes to zero. At the point \(P\) and in the direction \(\vek n\) this sum is \(1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6} \cdot \tfrac13 - 4{,}685 \cdot 10^{-10} \cdot \tfrac23 + 0 \cdot \tfrac23 = 6{,}5035 \cdot 10^{-7}\), while the quotient is \(6{,}5028 \cdot 10^{-7}\) for \(h = 1\) mm and \(6{,}5035 \cdot 10^{-7}\) for \(h = 0{,}1\) mm (figure 1.50). It is the same picture as for the hanging bar in section 1.4, where the strain of ever shorter pieces approached the derivative. So for the hanging bar the difference of displacements of a short fibre, divided by its length, is almost exactly the sum of the products of the partial derivatives at \(P\) with the components of the direction. We will need this for the strain of a short fibre in any direction.

For the hanging bar we computed the remainder exactly, because each component of its field is a sum of terms with at most two coordinate factors, like \(x^2\) and \(x\,y\). That is why the remainder was exactly a number times \(h^2\). A field with a term like \(x^3\) would also give terms with \(h^3\) in the remainder. But what about a general field? Equality (1.22) is true for every field. The first bracket starts at \(P\), so by the definition of the partial derivative 1.25 its quotient approaches the partial derivative at \(P\). The second and third brackets, though, start at the points \(A\) and \(B\), which move as we shorten the fibre. For the bar this gave terms like \(0{,}6\,a\,\Delta x\,\Delta y\). For a general field two facts would finish the job.

  1. For a function of one variable that has a derivative at every point between two numbers and no jump at the end points, the change between the numbers equals the derivative at some point between them times the difference of the two numbers. We will prove this statement in section 1.14. For the second bracket this function is \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) along the step from \(A\) to \(B\), where only \(y\) changes. If \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) has a partial derivative with respect to \(y\) at all points from \(A\) to \(B\), which the assumption of item 2 ensures, by this statement the change equals the partial derivative \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial y\) at some point between \(A\) and \(B\), times \(\Delta y\). The same holds for the third bracket, where only \(z\) changes from \(B\) to \(Q\).
  2. We assume that the partial derivatives exist at all points near \(P\) and change there without jumps: at points close enough to \(P\) they differ only a little from those at \(P\). This is an assumption about the field, which has to be checked for every field. Then the partial derivative at the point between \(A\) and \(B\) differs a little from the one at \(P\), and the remainder is small compared with the length of the fibre.

With these two facts LEBL2 proves that the remainder divided by the length of the step goes to zero for every field whose partial derivatives have no jumps (LEBL2 Prop. 8.4.6, PDF pp. 48-49). For a graph the first fact looks self-evident: between two points of a graph without jumps or sharp corners there is a point where the tangent is parallel to the line through both points. A proof, though, must show that such a point really exists, and it turns out to depend on the fact that the number line has no holes.

So for a few sections we now turn to mathematics. First we look at why the number line has no holes. Then we look at endless strings of numbers that approach some number, then at functions whose graph has no jumps, and finally at the statement about the derivative at a point in between. Then we return to the fibre and show that the remainder divided by the length of the fibre goes to zero for every such field.
