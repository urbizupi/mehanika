Chapter 8 Analytical dynamics (analitična dinamika)

8.2 Mathematics: angle, radian, sine and cosine (matematika: kot, radian, sinus in kosinus)

Take again mechanism D from section 8.1 (figure 8.21). Bar \(AB\) has length \(a\) and turns about pin \(A\), so hinge \(B\) always lies on the circle of radius \(a\) about \(A\). Its height above the horizontal through \(A\) is \(a\sin\theta\), where \(\theta\) is the angle of bar \(AB\) to the horizontal. Both bars have length \(a\), so the perpendicular from \(B\) halves the distance from \(A\) to the slider \(C\) into two pieces of length \(a\cos\theta\): the right triangles to the left and to the right of it have the same hypotenuse \(a\) and a common leg, so by the Pythagorean theorem their other legs are equal too. The slider \(C\) runs along the horizontal guide through \(A\). The spring therefore has length \(2a\cos\theta\). When the load \(P\) pushes the hinge down, the angle decreases from \(\theta_0 = 30^\circ\) to \(\theta\) and the spring stretches by \(e\). The strain energy of the spring depends on \(e\), and the potential energy of the load on the height \(a\sin\theta\). Their sum is therefore a function of the angle \(\theta\). In equilibrium its derivative with respect to \(\theta\) is zero, which we will show for mechanism D in the third part of this section. So we must be able to differentiate \(\sin\theta\) and \(\cos\theta\). Until now we read them as school values at angles in degrees, for example for the angle between two vectors in section 2.3, but we never differentiated them. Two questions are open. What is an angle as a number that we can put into a formula? And why is \((\sin\theta)' = \cos\theta\) only when the angle is measured by arc length on the unit circle and not in degrees?

The school answer that this is "known" is not enough, because we will build all oscillations in the book on these derivatives. The route this section takes uses only the tools the book already has: derivatives and the mean value theorem from chapter 1, and the integral and the fundamental theorem of calculus from chapter 3. The starting point is arc length. An angle is a number that tells how long the arc of the unit circle between two points is. The functions \(\cos\theta\) and \(\sin\theta\) are the coordinates of the point on this arc. The textbook LEBL2 (§11.4, PDF p. 164-167) takes another route, through an infinite sum for the function \(\mathrm e^{x}\) with an imaginary argument, which the book does not have. By its remark on p. 167, "t measures the arclength on a circle of radius 1 by the angle in radians", so in the end we arrive at the same functions. Our route is not from a source.

The section has five parts. First we define how long a path along a curve is, and with it the number \(\pi\) and the measuring of angles by arcs. Then we define \(\cos\theta\) and \(\sin\theta\) through the inverse function of the arc length. Next we differentiate them. From the derivatives we get two things that all following sections need: the formulas for the sum of angles and all solutions of the equation \(y'' + \omega^2 y = 0\). At the end we bound the error of the approximation \(\sin\theta \approx \theta\), because we will support every linearization in this chapter with a number.

8.2.1 Length of a curve and the radian (dolžina krivulje in radian)

How long is a curve? For a segment we know the answer, for an arc we do not. We replace the arc by a broken line with vertices on the arc and add up the lengths of its segments. Take the upper half of the circle with unit radius and centre at the origin \(O\), that is the points \((x, \sqrt{1 - x^2})\) for \(-1 \le x \le 1\), which are at the distance \(\sqrt{x^2 + (1 - x^2)} = 1\) from \(O\) (definition 1.17). First four segments with vertices at \(x = 1\), \(0{,}5\), \(0\), \(-0{,}5\) and \(-1\) (figure 8.22, left). Their lengths by definition 1.17 are \(1\), \(0{,}5176\), \(0{,}5176\) and \(1\), in total \(3{,}0353\). With eight segments (spacing \(0{,}25\) in \(x\), figure on the right) we get \(3{,}1045\). As we keep dividing the segments, the lengths are these:

number of segments \(4\) \(8\) \(16\) \(64\) \(256\)
length of the broken line \(3{,}0353\) \(3{,}1045\) \(3{,}1285\) \(3{,}1400\) \(3{,}1414\)

Why do the lengths increase? A new point on the arc between two vertices replaces one segment by two, which together are not shorter, because the straight path between two points is the shortest. The lengths increase toward a number that we will call \(\pi\). Because each segment replaces a small piece of the arc, the broken line and the arc become more and more alike.

For a curve given by functions of time or of another parameter \(t\) we can write this idea as an integral (figure 8.23, left). Let \(\vek r(t) = (x(t), y(t))\) be the point of the curve at the parameter \(t\) in \([a, b]\). We divide the parameter into short pieces \([t_{\mathrm{i}-1}, t_{\mathrm{i}}]\) of length \(\Delta t_{\mathrm{i}}\) and join the points \(\vek r(t_{\mathrm{i}})\) into a broken line. Take one of its segments, from \(\vek r(t_{\mathrm{i}-1})\) to \(\vek r(t_{\mathrm{i}})\). By the definition of the derivative (definition 1.9) the displacement along it has the horizontal component approximately \(x'(t_{\mathrm{i}})\,\Delta t_{\mathrm{i}}\) and the vertical one approximately \(y'(t_{\mathrm{i}})\,\Delta t_{\mathrm{i}}\), so the segment, as the hypotenuse, has by the Pythagorean theorem length approximately \(\sqrt{x'(t_{\mathrm{i}})^2 + y'(t_{\mathrm{i}})^2}\,\Delta t_{\mathrm{i}}\). This is a term of a Riemann sum of the function \(|\vek r'| = \sqrt{x'^2 + y'^2}\) (definition 3.47), and for a continuous function \(|\vek r'|\) the sum of all terms approaches the integral as the pieces get shorter (theorem 3.53, item 3). This is a motivation, not a proof, because the "approximately" in the first step was left without a bound. A proof that the broken lines really converge to the integral is not needed in this book: we take the integral as the definition. KREYSZIG (§9.5, equation (10), PDF p. 411) defines the length as the limit of the lengths of broken lines and states that for a curve with a continuous derivative it equals this integral. The description of the circle that we will use has no derivative at its ends. So the definition also accepts an interval without an endpoint: there we take the least upper bound of the lengths of closed pieces.

For the length we shall also look at pieces of the parameter, so let us draw them (figure 8.24). The axes are the parameter \(t\) horizontally and the speed \(|\vek r'(t)|\) vertically, and the points \(a\), \(b\), \(c\) and \(d\) are the ends of the intervals on the axis \(t\). On the left is the speed \(|\vek r'(t)|\) on the closed interval \([a, b]\), the area under it is the length of the whole curve, and a piece \([c, d]\) catches only part of that area. On the right the interval is not closed: the empty circle at \(b\) says the end is missing, pieces \([c, d]\) ever closer to the end catch ever more area, and their supremum is all the area up to the end.

Definition 8.5 Length of a curve (dolžina krivulje)

A curve in the plane is given here by the points \(\vek r(t) = (x(t), y(t))\), where \(x\) and \(y\) are functions of the parameter \(t\) on an interval with endpoints \(a < b\).

  1. Let the interval be closed, \([a, b]\), let \(x\) and \(y\) be differentiable on \([a, b]\) and let the function \(|\vek r'(t)| = \sqrt{x'(t)^2 + y'(t)^2}\) be continuous on \([a, b]\). The length (dolžina) of the curve is the number
    \[s = \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \sqrt{x'(t)^2 + y'(t)^2}\,\mathrm{d}t . \tag{8.3}\]
  2. Let the interval lack at least one of its endpoints and let item 1 hold on every closed interval \([c, d]\) that lies in it. If the lengths \(\int_{\mathrm{c}}^d |\vek r'(t)|\,\mathrm{d}t\) of these pieces are bounded above, the length of the curve is their supremum.

Source: KREYSZIG §9.5, (10) (PDF p. 411), where the length is the limit of the lengths of broken lines. LEBL2 §11.4.3 (PDF p. 167) states the same formula as the arc length. The motivation by a Riemann sum and item 2 are not from a source.

Would the supremum from item 2 give anything other than item 1 on a closed interval? No. Let item 1 hold on \([a, b]\) and let \([c, d]\) be a piece. The integrand is not negative, so by additivity and the bound of the integral (lemma 3.54, items 2 and 3) the area of the piece is not larger than the area of the whole, \(\int_{\mathrm{c}}^d |\vek r'| \le \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b |\vek r'|\), and the interval \([a, b]\) itself is one of the pieces. The supremum of the lengths of the pieces is therefore the integral from item 1, and the two items of the definition agree.

Length behaves under magnification as we expect (figure 8.23, right). Take an arc of radius \(1\) and length \(s\) between two rays from \(O\). If we multiply all its points by \(\rho > 0\), every point moves away from \(O\) along its ray to \(\rho\) times the distance, and we get an arc of radius \(\rho\). Every derivative is multiplied by \(\rho\) (lemma 1.82, item 2), the integrand by \(\rho\) and the length by \(\rho\) (lemma 3.54, item 1), in item 2 also the supremum. An arc on a circle of radius \(\rho\) therefore has length \(\rho s\), \(\rho\) times that of the corresponding arc on the unit circle. In the figure \(\rho = 2\) and the orange arc is twice as long as the grey one.

Now we view the upper half circle as the curve \(\vek r(t) = (t, q(t))\) with \(q(t) = \sqrt{1 - t^2}\). The function \(q\) is differentiable on \((-1, 1)\) with the derivative \(q'(t) = -t/q(t)\) (lemma 4.16, \(r = 1\)), so

\[|\vek r'(t)|^2 = 1 + \frac{t^2}{q(t)^2} = \frac{q(t)^2 + t^2}{q(t)^2} = \frac{1}{q(t)^2} , \qquad |\vek r'(t)| = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - t^2}} . \tag{8.4}\]

This is the speed at which the point runs along the half circle when \(t\) increases uniformly (figure 8.25). At \(t = 0\) it equals \(1\), because there the circle is horizontal and the point moves only horizontally. Toward the ends \(t = \pm 1\) it grows beyond every bound, because there the circle is almost vertical and already a small step of \(t\) means a long piece of arc. On a circle of radius \(\rho\) the same computation with lemma 4.16 gives the integrand \(\rho/\sqrt{\rho^2 - t^2}\). For \(\rho = 5\) mm and \(t\) from \(-3\) to \(3\) mm this is the integral that SymPy computed in chapter 4 and that we called there the length of the arc above the chord, \(6{,}435\) mm. The definition therefore agrees with what we assumed there.

The arc from the point with abscissa \(x\), \(-1 < x < 1\), to the point \((1, 0)\) is the curve \(\vek r(t)\) for \(x \le t < 1\). The difficulty is the upper limit: at \(t = 1\) the function \(q\) has no derivative and the integrand \(1/\sqrt{1 - t^2}\) grows beyond every bound, so we cannot use item 1 of definition 8.5 there. Item 2 does apply: on every piece \([c, d]\) with \(x \le c < d < 1\) the integrand is continuous. We must prove that the lengths of the pieces are bounded above, that is, that the shape under the curve from \(x\) toward \(1\), which reaches infinitely high, still has a finite area. The blue curve \(1/\sqrt{1 - t}\), which lies above the blackwhite one and whose area we will compute, will help. For this we need the derivative of the function \(\sqrt{x}\), which the book does not yet have in general form.

Figure 8.26 shows the idea. On the graph of the function \(\sqrt{x}\) take the point above \(A\) and the point above \(B\). The secant through them has a slope that is the quotient of the difference in height \(\sqrt B - \sqrt A\) and the horizontal difference \(B - A\). Because \(B - A = (\sqrt B - \sqrt A)(\sqrt B + \sqrt A)\), this slope is \(1/(\sqrt A + \sqrt B)\), in the figure \(1/(1 + 2) = 1/3\). As \(B\) approaches \(A\), \(\sqrt B\) approaches \(\sqrt A\) and the denominator approaches \(2\sqrt A\), and the secant turns into the tangent with the slope \(1/(2\sqrt A)\), in the figure \(1/2\). The two in the denominator is the sum of two square roots that merge into one.

Lemma 8.6 Derivative of a square root (odvod kvadratnega korena)

Let the function \(p\) be defined for all numbers whose distance from \(t\) is less than some number \(r > 0\), differentiable at \(t\), and \(p(t) > 0\). Then there is \(r_1 > 0\) such that \(p(s) > 0\) for \(|s - t| < r_1\), the function \(\sqrt{p}\) is continuous and differentiable at \(t\), and

\[\big(\sqrt{p}\,\big)'(t) = \frac{p'(t)}{2\sqrt{p(t)}} . \tag{8.5}\]
Proof

We must show that the difference quotient of \(\sqrt p\) converges to the right-hand side. We remove the root with a difference of squares, as for other roots in the book (lemma 4.69). In figure 8.26 the difference quotient is the slope of the secant, and the limit is the slope of the tangent.

  1. For positive numbers \(A\) and \(B\) the roots \(\sqrt A\) and \(\sqrt B\) are positive (theorem 1.37). From \((\sqrt B - \sqrt A)(\sqrt B + \sqrt A) = B - A\) it follows for the difference in height and the horizontal difference that
    \[\sqrt B - \sqrt A = \frac{B - A}{\sqrt A + \sqrt B} , \qquad |\sqrt B - \sqrt A| \le \frac{|B - A|}{\sqrt A} .\]
  2. The function \(p\) is differentiable at \(t\), hence continuous there (lemma 1.80). The definition of continuity 1.59 with the error \(p(t)/2\) gives a band \(r_1 \le r\) such that \(|p(s) - p(t)| < p(t)/2\), hence \(p(s) > p(t)/2 > 0\), for every \(s\) with \(|s - t| < r_1\). There \(\sqrt p\) is defined.
  3. By step 1 with \(A = p(t)\) and \(B = p(t + h)\) we have \(|\sqrt{p(t + h)} - \sqrt{p(t)}| \le |p(t + h) - p(t)|/\sqrt{p(t)}\): the difference in height is not greater than the horizontal one divided by \(\sqrt A\). The right-hand side tends to \(0\) as \(h\) tends to \(0\), because \(p\) is continuous at \(t\), so \(\sqrt p\) is continuous at \(t\). The same holds at every point where \(p\) is continuous and positive.
  4. Write \(A = p(t)\), \(B = p(t + h)\), \(D = \sqrt A + \sqrt B\) and \(\sigma = \sqrt A\). By step 1 the difference quotient is \((\sqrt B - \sqrt A)/h = (B - A)/(h D)\). We subtract \(p'(t)/(2\sigma)\) and add and subtract \(p'(t)/D\):
    \[\frac{\sqrt B - \sqrt A}{h} - \frac{p'(t)}{2\sigma} = \frac{1}{D}\Big(\frac{B - A}{h} - p'(t)\Big) + p'(t)\,\frac{2\sigma - D}{2\sigma D} , \qquad 2\sigma - D = \sqrt A - \sqrt B .\]
  5. Since \(D \ge \sigma\), we have \(1/D \le 1/\sigma\) and \(1/(2\sigma D) \le 1/(2A)\), so by the triangle inequality (lemma 1.6) the absolute value of the left-hand side is at most
    \[\frac{1}{\sqrt A}\,\Big|\frac{B - A}{h} - p'(t)\Big| + \frac{|p'(t)|}{2A}\,\big|\sqrt A - \sqrt B\big| .\]
    The first term tends to \(0\) by the definition of the derivative, the second by step 3, as the secant turns into the tangent. For an error \(\eta\) we take a band in which each term is less than \(\eta/2\), so the difference quotient converges to \(p'(t)/(2\sqrt{p(t)})\).

Source: LEBL1 Example 4.1.4 (PDF p. 156) differentiates \(\sqrt x\) with the same difference of squares. The general form and the proof are not from a source.

We will use the lemma twice: for the function \(\sqrt{1 - t}\) in the next lemma and for differentiating \(\sin\theta\). Now we prove that the arc is finite and that its length is a differentiable function of the end of the arc.

Lemma 8.7 Arc length of the unit circle (dolžina loka enotske krožnice)

Let \(f(t) = 1/\sqrt{1 - t^2}\) for \(-1 < t < 1\).

  1. For \(0 \le x < 1\) the integral \(G(x) = \int_0^x f(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\) exists, the function \(G\) is strictly increasing and \(G(x) < 2\). Hence there is a number \(A = \sup G(x)\), \(0 < A \le 2\), and \(G(x)\) converges to \(A\) as \(x\) tends to \(1\) from the left.
  2. With \(G(x) = -G(-x)\) for \(-1 < x < 0\) we extend \(G\) to \((-1, 1)\). The function \(G\) is differentiable at every point of \((-1, 1)\) and \(G'(x) = f(x)\).
  3. For \(-1 < x < b < 1\) we have \(\int_{\mathrm{x}}^b f(t)\,\mathrm{d}t = G(b) - G(x)\). The function \(\varphi(x) = A - G(x)\) on \((-1, 1)\), with the values \(\varphi(1) = 0\) and \(\varphi(-1) = 2A\), is continuous on \([-1, 1]\), strictly decreasing and differentiable on \((-1, 1)\) with the derivative
    \[\varphi'(x) = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - x^2}} . \tag{8.6}\]
  4. For \(-1 < x < 1\) the number \(\varphi(x)\) is the supremum of the lengths \(\int_{\mathrm{c}}^d f(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\) of the pieces \([c, d]\) with \(x \le c < d < 1\), and the number \(\varphi(-1)\) is the supremum of the lengths of the pieces with \(-1 < c < d < 1\). By definition 8.5, item 2, \(\varphi(x)\) is therefore the length of the arc of the upper half circle from the point with abscissa \(x\) to the point \((1, 0)\), and \(\varphi(-1)\) is the length of the whole half circle.
Proof

We must show four things: that \(f\) is continuous and bounded below by \(1\), that the integral from \(0\) to \(x\) has a finite upper bound as \(x\) tends to \(1\), that \(G\) is an antiderivative of \(f\) also on the negative side, and that \(\varphi\) is the supremum of the lengths of the pieces. All four are statements about the areas in figure 8.25.

1. Existence and boundedness.

  1. The function \(q(t) = \sqrt{1 - t^2}\) is differentiable on \((-1, 1)\) (lemma 4.16) and hence continuous (lemma 1.80), and \(q(t) > 0\). For \(s\) near \(t\) we therefore have \(q(s) > q(t)/2\) (as in step 2 of lemma 8.6), and \(|1/q(s) - 1/q(t)| = |q(t) - q(s)|/(q(s)\,q(t)) \le 2\,|q(t) - q(s)|/q(t)^2\), which tends to \(0\) as \(s \to t\). The function \(f = 1/q\) is continuous on \((-1, 1)\), so it is integrable on every \([0, x]\) with \(x < 1\) (theorem 3.53) and \(G(x)\) exists.
  2. For \(0 \le t < 1\) we have \(0 < 1 - t^2 \le 1\), so \(\sqrt{1 - t^2} \le 1\) (lemma 1.29) and \(f(t) \ge 1\): the blackwhite curve does not fall below the line \(1\). For \(0 \le x < y < 1\), by additivity and the bound of the integral (lemma 3.54, items 2 and 3), \(G(y) - G(x) = \int_{\mathrm{x}}^y f(t)\,\mathrm{d}t \ge y - x > 0\). The area above the piece \([x, y]\) is therefore at least the area of a rectangle of height \(1\), and the function \(G\) is strictly increasing.
  3. For \(0 \le t < 1\) we have \(1 - t^2 = (1 - t)(1 + t) \ge 1 - t > 0\), so \(\sqrt{1 - t^2} \ge \sqrt{1 - t}\) (lemma 1.29) and \(f(t) \le 1/\sqrt{1 - t}\): the blue curve lies above the blackwhite one. By lemma 8.6 with \(p(t) = 1 - t\), \(p' = -1\) (lemma 1.82, items 1, 2 and 4) and by item 2 of the same lemma, the function \(w(t) = -2\sqrt{1 - t}\) is differentiable on \([0, 1)\) with the derivative \(w'(t) = -2 \cdot (-1)/(2\sqrt{1 - t}) = 1/\sqrt{1 - t}\), hence continuous. The function \(1/\sqrt{1 - t}\) is also continuous on \([0, x]\), as the reciprocal of a continuous positive function (computation from step 1). By the fundamental theorem of calculus (theorem 3.55, item 1), \(\int_0^x \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - t}}\,\mathrm{d}t = w(x) - w(0) = 2 - 2\sqrt{1 - x} < 2\). By monotonicity of the integral (lemma 3.54, item 4), \(G(x) \le 2 - 2\sqrt{1 - x} < 2\). At \(x = 0{,}5\) the blue area is \(G(0{,}5) = 0{,}5236\), and the bound is \(2 - 2\sqrt{0{,}5} = 0{,}5858\).
  4. The set of values \(G(x)\) is nonempty and bounded above by \(2\): the area from \(0\) to \(x\) grows with \(x\) but never reaches \(2\). So by the completeness axiom (definition 1.33) it has a supremum \(A\). We have \(0 < G(1/2) \le A \le 2\). For \(\eta > 0\) there is \(x_\eta\) with \(G(x_\eta) > A - \eta\), and for \(x_\eta < x < 1\), because \(G\) increases, \(A - \eta < G(x) \le A\). This is the definition of the limit from the left (definition 1.8), and \(G(x) \to A\) as \(x \to 1^-\). Since \(G\) is strictly increasing, \(G(x) < A\) for every \(x < 1\).

2. Differentiability.

  1. For \(0 < x < 1\) we choose \(c\) with \(x < c < 1\). The function \(f\) is continuous on \([0, c]\), so by the fundamental theorem of calculus (item 2) \(G\) is differentiable at \(x\) with \(G'(x) = f(x)\), and at \(0\) with the right derivative \(f(0) = 1\). The area therefore grows as fast as the curve is high at its right edge.
  2. For \(-1 < x < 0\) and small \(h\) the number \(-x - h\) lies in \((0, 1)\) and
    \[\frac{G(x + h) - G(x)}{h} = \frac{-G(-x - h) + G(-x)}{h} = \frac{G(-x - h) - G(-x)}{-h} ,\]
    which by step 1 converges to \(G'(-x) = f(-x) = f(x)\), because \((-t)^2 = t^2\): the graph is symmetric about the vertical axis, and the grey area from \(-x\) to \(0\) equals the blue one from \(0\) to \(x\). At \(x = 0\), for \(h < 0\) the quotient is \((G(h) - G(0))/h = G(-h)/(-h)\) and converges to the right derivative \(f(0) = 1\), and for \(h > 0\) to the same number. For an error \(\eta\) we take the smaller of the two bands from definition 1.8 and get a band from the definition of the limit 1.5. Hence \(G'(0) = 1 = f(0)\).

3. The function \(\varphi\).

  1. For \(-1 < x < b < 1\) the function \(G\) is continuous on \([x, b]\) (lemma 1.80) and \(G' = f\), so by the fundamental theorem of calculus (item 1) \(\int_{\mathrm{x}}^b f(t)\,\mathrm{d}t = G(b) - G(x)\). As \(b \to 1^-\), this tends by step 4 of the first part to \(A - G(x) = \varphi(x)\), which is the whole area from \(x\) toward \(1\).
  2. For \(-1 < x < y < 1\) we have \(\varphi(x) - \varphi(y) = G(y) - G(x) = \int_{\mathrm{x}}^y f(t)\,\mathrm{d}t \ge y - x > 0\), because \(f(t) = f(-t) \ge 1\) by step 2 of the first part (lemma 3.54, item 3). When \(x\) moves to the right, the orange shape shrinks by at least a rectangle of height \(1\), so the function \(\varphi\) is strictly decreasing on \((-1, 1)\), differentiable with \(\varphi' = -G' = -f\) (item 2) and hence continuous.
  3. As \(x \to 1^-\), \(G(x) \to A\) and \(\varphi(x) \to 0 = \varphi(1)\), because the orange shape is squeezed against the asymptote. As \(x \to -1^+\), \(-x \to 1^-\) and \(G(x) = -G(-x) \to -A\), so \(\varphi(x) \to 2A = \varphi(-1)\). For \(-1 < x < 1\) we have \(\varphi(x) = A - G(x) > 0\), because \(G(x) < A\), and \(\varphi(x) = A + G(-x) < 2A\), because \(G(-x) < A\). The function \(\varphi\) is therefore continuous on \([-1, 1]\) and strictly decreasing also at the endpoints.

4. Supremum of the lengths of the pieces.

  1. By step 2 of the third part, \(G = A - \varphi\) is strictly increasing on \((-1, 1)\). For a piece \([c, d]\) with \(x \le c < d < 1\), by step 1 of the third part, \(\int_{\mathrm{c}}^d f = G(d) - G(c) < A - G(x) = \varphi(x)\), because \(G(d) < A\) and \(G(c) \ge G(x)\). The number \(\varphi(x)\) is therefore an upper bound of the lengths of the pieces: the orange shape contains the shape above every piece.
  2. The pieces \([x, d]\) have the lengths \(G(d) - G(x)\), which tend to \(A - G(x) = \varphi(x)\) as \(d \to 1^-\). For every \(\eta > 0\) some piece is therefore longer than \(\varphi(x) - \eta\), and no smaller upper bound exists. This is the supremum: the shapes above the pieces \([x, d]\) fill the orange shape up to an arbitrarily small remainder.
  3. For \(-1 < c < d < 1\) we have \(\int_{\mathrm{c}}^d f = G(d) + G(-c) < 2A = \varphi(-1)\), and the pieces \([-d, d]\) have the lengths \(2G(d)\), which tend to \(2A\), the whole area under the curve between the asymptotes. As in step 2, \(\varphi(-1)\) is the supremum.

It does not study the integral \(\int \mathrm{d}t/\sqrt{1 - t^2}\).

Definition 8.8 Arc length, the number pi, and radian (dolžina loka, število pi in radian)

Let \(\varphi\) be the function from lemma 8.7. The number \(\pi\) is the length of the unit half circle, \(\pi = \varphi(-1) = 2A\). For a point \((x, \sqrt{1 - x^2})\) of the upper half circle, \(\varphi(x)\) is the length of the arc along the circle from \((1, 0)\) to this point (lemma 8.7, item 4). The angle (kot) between the segments from \(O\) to \((1, 0)\) and from \(O\) to \((x, \sqrt{1 - x^2})\), measured in radians (radian, symbol rad), is this number \(\varphi(x)\): the angle is the length of the arc that its two sides cut off on the unit circle.

Source: LEBL2 §11.4.3 (PDF p. 167): "\(t\) measures the arclength on a circle of radius 1 by the angle in radians". KREYSZIG A3.1 (PDF p. 1204): \(1^\circ = 0{,}0174532925\) radian. The definition with the function \(\varphi\) is not from a source.

We test the definition at once. At \(x = 0\) the point is \((0, 1)\), a right angle, and \(\varphi(0) = A = \pi/2\) by item 3 of the lemma, the area from \(0\) toward \(1\), that is a quarter of the circle. With the program mpmath for numerical quadrature, which we use only for checking, \(\pi = 3{,}141593\), \(\varphi(0) = 1{,}570796\) and \(\varphi(0{,}5) = 1{,}047198\), which is \(\pi/3\) to six digits. The lower half circle, the curve \((t, -q(t))\), has the same integrand \(\sqrt{1 + q'(t)^2}\) and therefore the same length. The circumference of the unit circle is therefore \(2\pi\), as we announced in chapter 4. The definition gives the length for the chosen description \(\vek r(t)\). One arc can have several descriptions, for example \((t, \sqrt{\rho^2 - t^2})\) and \(\rho\,(u, \sqrt{1 - u^2})\) with \(t = \rho u\). KREYSZIG (§9.5, PDF p. 411) states that every continuously differentiable description that does not turn back gives the same number, which the book does not prove. In this section every arc is described by its abscissa, \((t, \pm q(t))\), or by a magnification of that description, and the circumference of the circle is the sum of the lengths of the two half circles. On a circle of radius \(\rho\) the two sides cut off, after magnification by \(\rho\), an arc of length \(\rho\,\varphi(x)\). The angle in radians is therefore also the ratio of the arc length to the radius, as we wrote it in chapter 5 and in section 8.1. Why does \(180^\circ = \pi\ \text{rad}\) hold? The degree divides the half circle into 180 equal arcs, each arc has length \(\pi/180 = 0{,}017453\), so \(1^\circ = 0{,}017453\) rad and \(1\ \text{rad} = 57{,}296^\circ\). This factor will be the key to differentiation.

8.2.2 Cosine and sine (kosinus in sinus)

Now we want to turn the question around: for an angle \(\theta\), find the point on the circle (figure 8.27). The function \(\varphi\) takes the abscissa of a point as input and gives the angle as output. The function we seek takes the angle as input and gives the abscissa as output: it is the same curve, read from the \(\theta\) axis instead of the \(x\) axis, so it is drawn mirrored across the diagonal. For this we need the inverse function. The book has the inverse function theorem (theorem 4.51), but only for points of the plane and of space. For a function of one variable we prove it separately, in the form that LEBL1 gives (Lemma 4.4.1, PDF p. 176-177, and Proposition 3.6.6, PDF p. 152).

Lemma 8.9 Inverse of a strictly monotone function (obratna funkcija strogo monotone funkcije)

Let \(g\) be a continuous function on the closed interval \(I = [a, b]\) (definition 1.44) that is strictly monotone, which means that for \(x < y\) always \(g(x) < g(y)\) or always \(g(x) > g(y)\). Let \(J\) be the closed interval with the endpoints \(g(a)\) and \(g(b)\).

  1. The function \(g\) is a bijection from \(I\) onto \(J\), and its inverse function \(h\colon J \to I\) is strictly monotone and continuous.
  2. If \(a < x_0 < b\), \(g\) is differentiable at \(x_0\) and \(g'(x_0) \neq 0\), then \(h\) is differentiable at \(y_0 = g(x_0)\) and
    \[h'(y_0) = \frac{1}{g'(x_0)} . \tag{8.7}\]
Proof

We must show four things: that \(g\) takes exactly the values in \(J\), that \(h\) is monotone, that it is continuous, and that its quotient is the reciprocal of the quotient of \(g\). We write the proof for a decreasing \(g\), then \(J = [g(b), g(a)]\). For an increasing one the same holds with reversed inequalities.

1. The inverse function.

  1. For \(a < x < b\), because \(g\) decreases, \(g(b) < g(x) < g(a)\), so all values of \(g\) lie in \(J\). For every \(y\) with \(g(b) < y < g(a)\) there is, by the intermediate value theorem (theorem 2.79), a point \(x \in (a, b)\) with \(g(x) = y\): a continuous curve from the height \(g(a)\) to the height \(g(b)\) crosses every horizontal line in between. The function \(g\) takes the endpoints of \(J\) at \(b\) and \(a\).
  2. If \(x \neq x'\), then \(g(x) \neq g(x')\) by strict monotonicity, so \(g\) is injective: a decreasing curve crosses every horizontal line at most once. By step 1 it is surjective onto \(J\), and the inverse function \(h\) exists.
  3. Let \(y < y'\) and \(h(y) \le h(y')\). Because \(g\) is decreasing, \(g(h(y)) \ge g(h(y'))\), that is \(y \ge y'\), which is false. Hence \(h(y) > h(y')\) and \(h\) is strictly decreasing, like the mirrored curve.
  4. Let \(y_0 \in J\), \(x_0 = h(y_0)\) and \(\eta > 0\). For each of the points \(x_0 - \eta\) and \(x_0 + \eta\) that lies in \(I\), the difference \(g(x_0 - \eta) - y_0 > 0\) or \(y_0 - g(x_0 + \eta) > 0\), respectively. Let \(\delta\) be the smallest of these differences (or \(1\) if neither point lies in \(I\)). Let \(y \in J\) with \(|y - y_0| < \delta\) and \(x = h(y)\). If \(x \ge x_0 + \eta\), then \(x_0 + \eta \in I\) (an interval) and \(y = g(x) \le g(x_0 + \eta) \le y_0 - \delta\), which is a contradiction. In the same way \(x \le x_0 - \eta\) is impossible. Hence \(|h(y) - h(y_0)| < \eta\) and \(h\) is continuous at \(y_0\).

2. The derivative.

  1. Since \(a < x_0 < b\), we have \(g(b) < y_0 < g(a)\), so \(h\) is defined on both sides of the point \(y_0\), as the definition of the derivative 1.9 requires. Let \(y \in J\), \(y \neq y_0\), and \(x = h(y)\). Then \(x \neq x_0\) and \(y - y_0 = g(x) - g(x_0)\). In figure 8.27 these are the differences \(\Delta x\) and \(\Delta\theta\) between the two points: the slope of the secant of the function \(g\) is \(\Delta\theta/\Delta x\), and the slope of the same secant on the mirrored curve is \(\Delta x/\Delta\theta\), because the differences swap roles. So
    \[\frac{h(y) - h(y_0)}{y - y_0} = \frac{x - x_0}{g(x) - g(x_0)} = \frac{1}{Q(x)} , \qquad Q(x) = \frac{g(x) - g(x_0)}{x - x_0} \neq 0 .\]
  2. Let \(\eta > 0\) and \(m = g'(x_0) \neq 0\). By the definition of the derivative there is \(\rho > 0\), not larger than \(x_0 - a\) and \(b - x_0\), such that \(|Q(x) - m| < \min\{|m|/2,\ \eta\,m^2/2\}\) for \(0 < |x - x_0| < \rho\). For such \(x\) we have \(|Q(x)| > |m|/2\) and
    \[\Big|\frac{1}{Q(x)} - \frac{1}{m}\Big| = \frac{|m - Q(x)|}{|Q(x)|\,|m|} \le \frac{2\,|m - Q(x)|}{m^2} < \eta .\]
  3. By the first part there is \(\delta > 0\) such that \(|h(y) - x_0| < \rho\) for \(|y - y_0| < \delta\), and \(h(y) \neq x_0\) for \(y \neq y_0\). For \(0 < |y - y_0| < \delta\), by step 2, \(|(h(y) - h(y_0))/(y - y_0) - 1/m| < \eta\). This is the definition of the limit, so \(h'(y_0) = 1/m\).

Source: LEBL1 Lemma 4.4.1 (PDF p. 176-177) and Proposition 3.6.6 (PDF p. 152). The proof is ours, with the same idea: the inverse of a strictly monotone function is continuous, and its difference quotient is the reciprocal of the quotient of \(g\).

We apply the lemma to the function \(\varphi\) from lemma 8.7. It is continuous and strictly decreasing on \([-1, 1]\), with the values \(\varphi(1) = 0\) and \(\varphi(-1) = \pi\). By the first item of lemma 8.9 the function \(\varphi\colon [-1, 1] \to [0, \pi]\) is a bijection with a continuous, strictly decreasing inverse function \(h\colon [0, \pi] \to [-1, 1]\). The function \(\cos\) will be this inverse function: for an angle \(\theta\) between \(0\) and \(\pi\) we find the point whose arc from \((1, 0)\) has length \(\theta\), and take its abscissa.

In the left figure the tangents at \(x = \pm 1\) are vertical, because the derivative of \(\varphi\) is infinite there. The lemma does not hold there, so we use it only on the open interval. In the middle the tangents at \(\theta = 0\) and \(\theta = \pi\) are horizontal, the mirror images of the vertical ones. On the right the cosine near \(0\) is magnified: the secant from \(0\) to \(h = 1\) has slope \(-0{,}4597\), equal to the slope of the tangent at \(\xi = 0{,}4777\). On the \(\theta\) axis \(\xi\) and \(h\) are marked.

The definition extends to all angles in two steps (figure 8.28). On the left, on the unit circle in the coordinate system, the point \(P\) on the arc of length \(\theta\) lies in the upper half plane. The mirror image of \(P\) across the horizontal axis has the same abscissa and the opposite ordinate, its arc has length \(\theta\) in the opposite direction, so it belongs to the angle \(-\theta\). On the right the axis of the angle \(\theta\) is divided into strips of length \(2\pi\), and a shift by \(2\pi k\) puts every angle into the strip \((-\pi, \pi]\), where it is already defined.

Definition 8.10 Sine and cosine functions (kosinus in sinus)

Let \(h\) be the inverse function of the function \(\varphi\) from lemma 8.7.

  1. For \(0 \le \theta \le \pi\), \(\cos\theta = h(\theta)\), that is the number \(x \in [-1, 1]\) for which \(\varphi(x) = \theta\), and \(\sin\theta\)\( = \sqrt{1 - \cos^2\theta}\), the root of a nonnegative number, because \(\cos^2\theta \le 1\).
  2. For \(-\pi \le \theta < 0\), \(\cos\theta = \cos(-\theta)\) and \(\sin\theta = -\sin(-\theta)\).
  3. For any real number \(\theta\) let \(k\) be the integer for which \(-\pi < \theta - 2\pi k \le \pi\). Then \(\cos\theta = \cos(\theta - 2\pi k)\) and \(\sin\theta = \sin(\theta - 2\pi k)\).

Both functions take an angle in radians as input and give a number as output. The point \((\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\) lies on the unit circle, for \(0 \le \theta \le \pi\) on the upper half circle at the distance \(\theta\) along the arc from \((1, 0)\), for \(-\pi \le \theta < 0\) at the mirror image of that point across the horizontal axis, on an arc of length \(|\theta|\) in the opposite direction.

Source: The definition with the function \(\varphi\) is not from a source. LEBL2 §11.4.2 (PDF p. 164-166) defines them by a series, and the number \(\pi\) as twice the smallest positive zero of the cosine.

Agreement with the school definition, where \((\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\) is the point on the circle at the angle \(\theta\) from \((1, 0)\), is here the definition itself: the angle is the arc, and the arc is measured along the circle (figure 8.29). In the figure \(\cos\theta\) is the horizontal distance of the point \(P\) from the \(y\) axis, and \(\sin\theta\) is its height above the \(x\) axis. The difference is that the definition is now exact, and we also get everything that follows from it. Exact now is also the angle \(\gamma\) between two nonzero vectors from section 2.3: the number \(\vek a\cdot\vek b/(|\vek a|\,|\vek b|)\) lies in \([-1, 1]\), and the cosine is a bijection from \([0, \pi]\) onto \([-1, 1]\), so \(\gamma\) is the only number in \([0, \pi]\) with this cosine. For \(\vek a = (1, 0)\) and \(\vek b = (x, \sqrt{1 - x^2})\) this is \(\varphi(x)\), as in definition 8.8.

Does the definition make sense? In item 3 the integer \(k\) must exist and be unique. The condition \(-\pi < \theta - 2\pi k \le \pi\) means \(u \le k < u + 1\) with \(u = (\theta - \pi)/(2\pi)\). Two integers in a half-open interval of length \(1\) differ by less than \(1\), so they are equal. Existence: by the Archimedean property (theorem 1.36) there is a natural number \(N > |u|\). The set of natural numbers \(m\) with \(m - N \ge u\) is not empty, because it contains \(2N\), so it has a smallest element \(m_0\) (section 1.8.1). The number \(k = m_0 - N\) satisfies \(k \ge u\). Also \(k - 1 < u\): for \(m_0 > 1\) because \(m_0 - 1\) is not in the set, and for \(m_0 = 1\) because \(k - 1 = -N < u\). The points \(\theta = \pm\pi\) do not contradict each other, because \(\cos\pi = -1\), hence \(\sin\pi = 0\) and \(\sin(-\pi) = -\sin\pi = 0\).

We read the properties off the circle (figure 8.30). The points at the angles \(0\), \(\pi/2\) and \(\pi\) are \((1, 0)\), \((0, 1)\) and \((-1, 0)\). The quarter of the arc up to \(\pi/2\) lies to the right of the \(y\) axis, the upper half circle above the \(x\) axis.

Lemma 8.11 Basic properties of sine and cosine (osnovne lastnosti sinusa in kosinusa)

For all real numbers \(\theta\):

  1. \(\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1\), hence \(|\cos\theta| \le 1\) and \(|\sin\theta| \le 1\).
  2. \(\cos 0 = 1\), \(\sin 0 = 0\), \(\cos(\pi/2) = 0\), \(\sin(\pi/2) = 1\), \(\cos\pi = -1\), \(\sin\pi = 0\).
  3. On \([0, \pi]\) the cosine is strictly decreasing, \(\cos\theta > 0\) for \(0 \le \theta < \pi/2\) and \(\sin\theta > 0\) for \(0 < \theta < \pi\).
  4. \(\cos(-\theta) = \cos\theta\), \(\sin(-\theta) = -\sin\theta\), \(\cos(\theta + 2\pi) = \cos\theta\) and \(\sin(\theta + 2\pi) = \sin\theta\).
Proof

We must show four items. The first and the third are in the definition, the second follows from the values of the function \(\varphi\) at three points, the fourth from the structure of the definition.

  1. On \([0, \pi]\), \(\sin^2\theta = 1 - \cos^2\theta\) by the definition, which is the first item. In figure 8.29 this is the Pythagorean theorem in the right triangle with the legs \(\cos\theta\) and \(\sin\theta\) and the hypotenuse \(OP\) of length \(1\). For \(-\pi \le \theta < 0\) and for any \(\theta\) we reduce to this with the last two items of the definition, since both contain only a sign or a shift. From \(\sin^2 \ge 0\) and \(\cos^2 \ge 0\) it follows that neither square is larger than \(1\), so neither absolute value is larger than \(1\) (lemma 1.29).
  2. From \(\varphi(1) = 0\) we get \(\cos 0 = 1\), from \(\varphi(0) = A = \pi/2\) we get \(\cos(\pi/2) = 0\) and from \(\varphi(-1) = \pi\) we get \(\cos\pi = -1\). The sine is the root of \(1 - \cos^2\): \(0\), \(1\) and \(0\). The graph of the cosine therefore passes through the points \((0, 1)\), \((\pi/2, 0)\) and \((\pi, -1)\).
  3. The cosine is the inverse function of the strictly decreasing function \(\varphi\), so by lemma 8.9 it is strictly decreasing, as its graph shows. On \([0, \pi/2)\) therefore \(\cos\theta > \cos(\pi/2) = 0\). On \((0, \pi)\) we have \(-1 < \cos\theta < 1\), so \(\sin^2\theta = 1 - \cos^2\theta > 0\), and the root is positive by definition.
  4. Periodicity: the number \(k\) for \(\theta + 2\pi\) is one larger than the number \(k\) for \(\theta\), so \(\theta\) and \(\theta + 2\pi\) have the same \(\theta - 2\pi k\) and the same values by item 3 of the definition. Evenness and oddness: let \(r = \theta - 2\pi k \in (-\pi, \pi]\). By items 1 and 2 of the definition, \(\cos(-r) = \cos r\) and \(\sin(-r) = -\sin r\) for every \(r \in [-\pi, \pi]\). If \(r < \pi\), then \(-\theta - 2\pi(-k) = -r\) with \(-r \in (-\pi, \pi)\), so \(-\theta\) has in item 3 of the definition the number \(-k\) and the value \(-r\). Therefore \(\cos(-\theta) = \cos(-r) = \cos r = \cos\theta\) and \(\sin(-\theta) = \sin(-r) = -\sin r = -\sin\theta\). If \(r = \pi\), then \(-\theta - 2\pi(-k - 1) = \pi\), so \(\cos(-\theta) = \cos\pi = \cos\theta\) and \(\sin(-\theta) = \sin\pi = 0 = -\sin\theta\).

Source: The proof is not from a source. LEBL2 Proposition 11.4.2 (PDF p. 164-165) states evenness, oddness, \(\cos^2 + \sin^2 = 1\) and the period \(2\pi\) for the functions defined by a series.

8.2.3 Derivative of sine and cosine (odvod sinusa in kosinusa)

Now we have the functions and can ask for their derivatives. The idea is short (figure 8.27). The cosine is the inverse function of \(\varphi\), which has the derivative \(-1/\sqrt{1 - x^2}\). Because the differences \(\Delta x\) and \(\Delta\theta\) swap roles under the mirroring, the slope of the cosine is the reciprocal of the slope of \(\varphi\), that is \(-\sqrt{1 - \cos^2} = -\sin\). The difficulties are at the endpoints \(0\) and \(\pi\), where \(\varphi'\) does not exist at \(\pm 1\) and \(|\varphi'(x)|\) grows beyond every bound: there the tangent to the graph of \(\varphi\) is vertical, and the tangent to the graph of the cosine is horizontal. The reciprocal does not help there. There is also a difficulty in the extension by oddness and the period. For the endpoints we need a lemma, which we prove with the mean value theorem.

Lemma 8.12 Derivative at an endpoint from the limit of the derivatives (odvod v krajišču iz limite odvodov)

Let \(f\) be continuous on \([a, b]\) and differentiable on \((a, b)\).

  1. If \(f'(t)\) tends to a number \(L\) as \(t\) tends to \(a\) from the right, then \(f\) has the right derivative \(L\) at \(a\).
  2. Mirrored: if \(f'(t)\) tends to a number \(L\) as \(t\) tends to \(b\) from the left, then \(f\) has the left derivative \(L\) at \(b\).
Proof

We must show that the difference quotient at the endpoint converges to \(L\). By the mean value theorem the quotient is the derivative at some point inside. The right panel of figure 8.27 shows this on the cosine at the endpoint \(a = 0\).

  1. For \(0 < h < b - a\) the function \(f\) is continuous on \([a, a + h]\) and differentiable on \((a, a + h)\), so by the mean value theorem 1.85 there is \(\xi_{\mathrm{h}} \in (a, a + h)\) with \((f(a + h) - f(a))/h = f'(\xi_{\mathrm{h}})\): the secant from \(a\) to \(a + h\) is parallel to the tangent at the point \(\xi_{\mathrm{h}}\). In the figure \(h = 1\), \(\xi_{\mathrm{h}} = 0{,}4777\) and the common slope is \(-0{,}4597\).
  2. Let \(\eta > 0\). By the hypothesis there is \(\delta > 0\) such that \(|f'(t) - L| < \eta\) for \(a < t < a + \delta\). For \(0 < h < \delta\) we have \(a < \xi_{\mathrm{h}} < a + h < a + \delta\), so \(|(f(a + h) - f(a))/h - L| < \eta\). As \(h\) shrinks, the point \(\xi_{\mathrm{h}}\), which lies between \(a\) and \(a + h\), moves toward \(a\), and the slope of the secant approaches \(L\). This is the definition of the limit from the right (definition 1.8), so the right derivative at \(a\) equals \(L\).
  3. Item 2 is the mirror image: the quotient \((f(b) - f(b - h))/h\) is \(f'(\xi_{\mathrm{h}})\) with \(\xi_{\mathrm{h}} \in (b - h, b)\).

Source: The proof is not from a source, it uses theorem 1.85 from chapter 1.

In \(\cos(\omega t + c)\) the argument grows \(\omega\) times faster than \(t\) on the horizontal axis (figure 8.31). The curve \(\cos s\) has at the point \(s\) a tangent with the slope \(-\sin s\). The curve \(\cos \omega t\) is compressed in the horizontal direction by the factor \(\omega\), so its tangent at the corresponding point is \(\omega\) times steeper.

Theorem 8.13 Derivatives of sine and cosine (odvoda sinusa in kosinusa)

The functions \(\cos\) and \(\sin\) are differentiable at every real number \(\theta\), and

\[\cos'\theta = -\sin\theta , \qquad \sin'\theta = \cos\theta . \tag{8.8}\]

For numbers \(\omega \neq 0\) and \(c\) also

\[\big(\cos(\omega t + c)\big)' = -\omega\sin(\omega t + c) , \qquad \big(\sin(\omega t + c)\big)' = \omega\cos(\omega t + c) . \tag{8.9}\]
Proof

We must show the derivatives on the open interval \((0, \pi)\) with the inverse function, at the endpoints with the lemma on the derivative at an endpoint, on \((-\pi, 0)\) and at \(0\) and \(\pi\) by symmetry, and elsewhere by the period. The last item is a change of variable.

1. The derivatives on \((0, \pi)\).

  1. Let \(0 < \theta_0 < \pi\) and \(x_0 = \cos\theta_0\). Since \(\varphi(1) = 0 < \theta_0 < \pi = \varphi(-1)\) and \(\varphi\) is strictly decreasing, \(-1 < x_0 < 1\). By lemma 8.7, \(\varphi'(x_0) = -1/\sqrt{1 - x_0^2} \neq 0\). By lemma 8.9 the cosine is differentiable at \(\theta_0\) and \(\cos'\theta_0 = 1/\varphi'(x_0) = -\sqrt{1 - \cos^2\theta_0} = -\sin\theta_0\), because the differences swap roles.
  2. Let \(p(\theta) = 1 - \cos^2\theta\). By step 1 and the product rule (lemma 1.82, item 3), \(p'(\theta) = -2\cos\theta\,\cos'\theta = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta\), and \(p > 0\) on \((0, \pi)\) (lemma 8.11, item 3). By lemma 8.6, \(\sin'\theta = p'(\theta)/(2\sqrt{p(\theta)}) = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta/(2\sin\theta) = \cos\theta\).

2. The endpoints.

  1. The cosine is continuous on \([0, \pi]\) (lemma 8.9). The sine is continuous on \((0, \pi)\), because it is differentiable there, and at \(0\) we have \(\sin\theta = \sqrt{1 - \cos^2\theta}\) and \(1 - \cos^2\theta \to 0\) as \(\theta \to 0^+\), so for every \(\eta > 0\) and small enough \(\theta\) the value \(1 - \cos^2\theta < \eta^2\) and \(\sin\theta < \eta\) (lemma 1.29). Hence \(\sin\theta \to 0 = \sin 0\). The same at \(\pi\), where \(\cos\theta \to -1\).
  2. As \(\theta \to 0^+\), \(\cos'\theta = -\sin\theta \to 0\) and \(\sin'\theta = \cos\theta \to 1\). By lemma 8.12 the cosine has the right derivative \(0\) at \(0\) and the sine the right derivative \(1\). This is the horizontal tangent to the graph of the cosine at \((0, 1)\). As \(\theta \to \pi^-\), \(-\sin\theta \to 0\) and \(\cos\theta \to -1\), so the left derivative of the cosine at \(\pi\) is \(0\) and that of the sine is \(-1\).

3. Symmetry and period.

  1. For \(-\pi < \theta < 0\) we have \(-\theta \in (0, \pi)\) and \(\cos\theta = \cos(-\theta)\), and for small \(|h|\) also \(\cos(\theta + h) = \cos(-\theta - h)\). With \(u = -h\), \(\big(\cos(\theta + h) - \cos\theta\big)/h = -\big(\cos(-\theta + u) - \cos(-\theta)\big)/u\), which by the first part converges to \(-\cos'(-\theta) = \sin(-\theta) = -\sin\theta\). Similarly \(\sin\theta = -\sin(-\theta)\), the quotient of the sine at \(\theta\) is \(\big(\sin(-\theta + u) - \sin(-\theta)\big)/u\) and converges to \(\sin'(-\theta) = \cos(-\theta) = \cos\theta\).
  2. At \(\theta = 0\): the left quotient of the cosine is, for \(h > 0\), \((\cos(-h) - 1)/(-h) = -(\cos h - 1)/h \to 0\), the left quotient of the sine is \(\sin(-h)/(-h) = \sin h/h \to 1\). The limits of the quotient from the left and from the right are equal. For an error \(\eta\) we take the smaller of the two bands from definition 1.8 and get the band that the definition of the limit 1.5 requires. Hence \(\cos'0 = 0 = -\sin 0\) and \(\sin'0 = 1 = \cos 0\).
  3. At \(\theta = \pi\): for \(h > 0\), \(\cos(\pi + h) = \cos(-\pi + h) = \cos(\pi - h)\) by item 3 of the definition and evenness, so the right quotient is \((\cos(\pi - h) - \cos\pi)/h = -(\cos(\pi - h) - \cos\pi)/(-h) \to 0\). Similarly \(\sin(\pi + h) = \sin(-\pi + h) = -\sin(\pi - h)\) and the right quotient is \(-\sin(\pi - h)/h = (\sin(\pi - h) - \sin\pi)/(-h) \to -1\). The limits from the left and from the right are equal, so as in step 2 \(\cos'\pi = 0 = -\sin\pi\) and \(\sin'\pi = -1 = \cos\pi\).
  4. For any \(\theta\), by the period (lemma 8.11, item 4, applied \(|k|\) times), \(\cos(\theta + h) = \cos(\theta - 2\pi k + h)\) and \(\cos\theta = \cos(\theta - 2\pi k)\), so the difference quotient at \(\theta\) equals the quotient at \(\theta - 2\pi k \in (-\pi, \pi]\). The derivative at \(\theta\) is therefore the derivative at \(\theta - 2\pi k\), that is \(-\sin(\theta - 2\pi k) = -\sin\theta\). The same for the sine.

4. A linear inner function.

  1. Let \(s = \omega t + c\) and \(h \neq 0\), \(u = \omega h \neq 0\). Then \(\big(\cos(\omega(t + h) + c) - \cos(\omega t + c)\big)/h = \omega\,(\cos(s + u) - \cos s)/u\). By the first three parts the quotient in \(u\) converges to \(-\sin s\). For an error \(\eta\) let \(\delta'\) be a band in which the quotient in \(u\) differs from \(-\sin s\) by less than \(\eta/|\omega|\). For \(0 < |h| < \delta'/|\omega|\) we have \(0 < |u| < \delta'\), and the quotient in \(h\) differs from \(-\omega\sin s\) by less than \(\eta\). Hence the derivative is \(-\omega\sin(\omega t + c)\). The same holds for the sine.

The first formula means that the path \(\theta \mapsto (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\) along the circle has the vector of derivatives with respect to \(\theta\) equal to \((-\sin\theta, \cos\theta)\). If we think of \(\theta\) as time, this is the velocity of the point (figure 8.32). Why does this make sense? The point travels along the unit circle and \(\theta\) is the length of the arc it has travelled, so the magnitude of its velocity must equal \(1\). This agrees with the theorem: \(|(-\sin\theta, \cos\theta)|^2 = \sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1\) (lemma 8.11, item 1). The path is on the circle, so \(\cos^2 + \sin^2 = 1\) all the time, and the derivative of this sum is \(2\cos\theta\cos'\theta + 2\sin\theta\sin'\theta = 0\), which means that the velocity is perpendicular to the radius. The only unit vectors perpendicular to \((\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\) are \(\pm(-\sin\theta, \cos\theta)\), and the sign \(+\) says that the point travels counterclockwise, from the \(x\) axis toward the \(y\) axis. This is another view of the same result and not a proof: we proved the theorem by computation.

Now we understand why the angle must be in radians (figure 8.33). The function \(\theta \mapsto \cos\theta\) takes an angle in radians as input. For an angle of \(\alpha\) degrees this is \(\cos(\pi\alpha/180)\), because \(1^\circ = \pi/180\) rad. The graph is the same curve, only the axis of the angle is marked in degrees. By the second formula of the theorem with \(\omega = \pi/180\) and \(c = 0\),

\[\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\alpha}\cos\Big(\frac{\pi\alpha}{180}\Big) = -\frac{\pi}{180}\sin\Big(\frac{\pi\alpha}{180}\Big) = -0{,}017453\,\sin\Big(\frac{\pi\alpha}{180}\Big) . \tag{8.10}\]

The derivative with respect to the angle in degrees gets the extra factor \(0{,}017453\) rad per degree, and every formula with a derivative with respect to an angle would have to carry it along. In the figure this is visible at \(90^\circ\): the tangent falls by \(1\) over the horizontal distance of \(1\) rad, that is over \(57{,}3^\circ\). The slope is \(-1\) per radian, but only \(-1/57{,}3 = -0{,}017453\) per degree. In radians the factor is \(1\).

8.2.4 Addition formulas and oscillation (adicijska izreka in nihanje)

Until now we used the formulas for the cosine and the sine of a sum of angles as school knowledge, for double angles and the cosine of a difference of angles in chapters 2 and 5. Now we prove them, together with the solutions of the equation \(y'' + \omega^2 y = 0\), and both from the same lemma.

Let us draw the idea (figure 8.34). Take the values of the functions \(g\) and \(f\) at the time \(t\) as the point \((g, f)\) in the plane with the axes \(g\) and \(f\). The sum \(f^2 + g^2\) is by the Pythagorean theorem the square of the distance of this point from \(O\). The conditions \(f' = -\omega g\) and \(g' = \omega f\) say that the velocity of the point is \((g', f') = \omega(f, -g)\). It is perpendicular to the radius, because the scalar product is \((g, f)\cdot\omega(f, -g) = \omega(gf - fg) = 0\). A point that moves perpendicular to the radius neither moves away from \(O\) nor approaches it, like the point in figure 8.32. So it runs along a circle and \(f^2 + g^2\) is constant. If it starts at \(O\), this circle is a single point, and the point stays at \(O\). For oscillations we will recognise energy in this sum.

Lemma 8.15 Lemma of the conserved sum of squares (lema o ohranjeni vsoti kvadratov)

Let \(I\) be an interval or the set of all real numbers \(\mathbb{R}\), \(\omega \ge 0\) a number, \(f\) and \(g\) differentiable functions on \(I\) with \(f' = -\omega g\) and \(g' = \omega f\), and \(t_0 \in I\) a point with \(f(t_0) = g(t_0) = 0\). Then \(f(t) = g(t) = 0\) for every \(t \in I\).

Proof

We must show that \(E = f^2 + g^2\) is a constant function. Since \(E(t_0) = 0\), \(E\) will then be zero everywhere.

  1. By the product rule (lemma 1.82, item 3), \(E' = 2ff' + 2gg' = -2\omega fg + 2\omega gf = 0\) on \(I\). This is twice the scalar product of the radius and the velocity, which are perpendicular.
  2. The function \(E\) is differentiable, hence continuous (lemma 1.80). For \(t > t_0\) in \(I\) the interval \([t_0, t]\) lies in \(I\), and by corollary 1.86 \(E(t) = E(t_0)\). For \(t < t_0\) the same holds on \([t, t_0]\). The point stays on the circle on which it was at \(t_0\).
  3. Hence \(f(t)^2 + g(t)^2 = 0\), a sum of two numbers that are not negative, so each of them equals \(0\): the point is at \(O\) all the time.

Source: The proof is not from a source. The lemma says that the solution of the linear system \(f' = -\omega g\), \(g' = \omega f\) with a zero start is zero.

Corollary 8.16 Addition formulas (adicijska izreka)

For all real numbers \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\)

\[\begin{aligned} \cos(\alpha + \beta) &= \cos\alpha\cos\beta - \sin\alpha\sin\beta , \\ \sin(\alpha + \beta) &= \sin\alpha\cos\beta + \cos\alpha\sin\beta . \end{aligned} \tag{8.11}\]
Proof

We must show that the functions formed by the differences of the left and right sides satisfy the hypotheses of the lemma of the conserved sum of squares with \(\omega = 1\). The number \(\beta\) is fixed, the variable is \(\alpha\).

  1. Let \(f(\alpha) = \cos(\alpha + \beta) - \cos\alpha\cos\beta + \sin\alpha\sin\beta\) and \(g(\alpha) = \sin(\alpha + \beta) - \sin\alpha\cos\beta - \cos\alpha\sin\beta\).
  2. By theorem 8.13 (second formula with \(\omega = 1\), \(c = \beta\)) and the rules of differentiation (lemma 1.82), \(f'(\alpha) = -\sin(\alpha + \beta) + \sin\alpha\cos\beta + \cos\alpha\sin\beta = -g(\alpha)\) and \(g'(\alpha) = \cos(\alpha + \beta) - \cos\alpha\cos\beta + \sin\alpha\sin\beta = f(\alpha)\).
  3. At \(\alpha = 0\), \(f(0) = \cos\beta - \cos\beta + 0 = 0\) and \(g(0) = \sin\beta - 0 - \sin\beta = 0\) (lemma 8.11, item 2): the point \((g, f)\) starts at \(O\).
  4. By lemma 8.15 on \(I = \mathbb{R}\) with \(\omega = 1\) and \(t_0 = 0\), \(f = g = 0\), which are both formulas.

Source: KREYSZIG A3.1, (6) (PDF p. 1204) states the formulas without proof. The proof is not from a source.

With \(\beta = \alpha\) and \(\cos^2 + \sin^2 = 1\) we get the double angles that we used in chapters 2 and 5: \(\sin 2\alpha = 2\sin\alpha\cos\alpha\) and \(\cos 2\alpha = \cos^2\alpha - \sin^2\alpha = 2\cos^2\alpha - 1\). With \(\beta \to -\beta\) (evenness and oddness, lemma 8.11, item 4) we also get \(\cos(\alpha - \beta) = \cos\alpha\cos\beta + \sin\alpha\sin\beta\).

The proof with the lemma is a computation. Where the formulas come from is shown by one more view (figure 8.35). Take the point \(P_\beta\) on the unit circle on the arc \(\beta\) from the \(x\) axis. Its coordinates are \(\cos\beta\) along the \(x\) axis and \(\sin\beta\) vertically. Now we rotate the whole picture about \(O\) by the angle \(\alpha\). The axes go over into the rotated axes \(x'\) and \(y'\) with the unit direction vectors \((\cos\alpha, \sin\alpha)\) and \((-\sin\alpha, \cos\alpha)\). The second is perpendicular to the first, just as the velocity is to the radius in figure 8.32. The point \(P_\beta\) goes over into the point \(P_{\alpha+\beta}\) on the arc \(\alpha + \beta\) from the \(x\) axis, which has the same coordinates in the rotated axes: \(\cos\beta\) along \(x'\) and \(\sin\beta\) along \(y'\). So

\[(\cos(\alpha + \beta), \sin(\alpha + \beta)) = \cos\beta\,(\cos\alpha, \sin\alpha) + \sin\beta\,(-\sin\alpha, \cos\alpha) ,\]

and the first component is the first formula, the second the second. This view is not a proof, because it assumes that a rotation preserves the lengths of arcs and right angles, which we have not proved in the book. The proof with the lemma does not need this, but the view tells why exactly these two products appear in the formulas.

Example 8.17 The angle of 60 degrees (kot 60 stopinj)

At \(x = 0{,}5\) we found numerically \(\varphi(0{,}5) = 1{,}047198\), which equals \(\pi/3\) to six digits. Let us prove that this holds exactly. Let \(\theta = \varphi(0{,}5)\), so \(\cos\theta = 1/2\).

  1. By the double angle, \(\cos 2\theta = 2 \cdot \tfrac14 - 1 = -\tfrac12\) and \(\sin 2\theta = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta = \sin\theta\).
  2. By the addition formula (corollary 8.16), \(\cos 3\theta = \cos 2\theta\cos\theta - \sin 2\theta\sin\theta = -\tfrac14 - \sin^2\theta = -\tfrac14 - (1 - \tfrac14) = -1\).
  3. The point \(\theta\) lies in \((0, \pi)\) and \(\cos\theta = \tfrac12 > 0 = \cos(\pi/2)\), so because the cosine decreases, \(\theta < \pi/2\): the point at the height \(\tfrac12\) lies to the left of \(\pi/2\). Hence \(0 < 3\theta < 3\pi/2\).
  4. On \([0, \pi)\) we have \(\cos > -1\), because the cosine is strictly decreasing and \(\cos\pi = -1\): the curve touches the line at the height \(-1\) in the left panel only at \(\pi\). So \(\cos 3\theta = -1\) implies \(3\theta \notin (0, \pi)\). For \(\pi < 3\theta < 3\pi/2\), \(\cos 3\theta = \cos(2\pi - 3\theta)\) by the period and evenness, where \(\pi/2 < 2\pi - 3\theta < \pi\), so again \(\cos 3\theta > -1\). The only possibility is \(3\theta = \pi\).

Hence \(\theta = \pi/3\), \(\cos(\pi/3) = \tfrac12\) and \(\sin(\pi/3) = \sqrt{1 - \tfrac14} = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\). The angle \(60^\circ\) is a third of the half circle, so its measure in radians is also a third of the number \(\pi\), and the definition agrees with the school value.

Before we write the theorem, let us see what it will say (figure 8.37). We displace the pendulum from the vertical by the angle \(\theta\) and release it. Its angle in time draws a curve that starts at the height of the initial angle with a horizontal tangent, because the pendulum is at rest when released. The theorem says that these two initial data, the value and the slope, determine the whole curve, and that this curve is a sum of a cosine and a sine with the weights that these two data give.

How the two initial data give the weights is shown by figure 8.38, drawn for \(t_0 = 0\) and \(\omega = 1\) in the axes \(t\) and \(y\). The cosine with the weight \(A = 0{,}8\) starts at the height \(A\) with a horizontal tangent. The sine with the weight \(B = 0{,}6\) starts at zero with the slope \(B\omega\). At every time we add them, as the bars at \(t = 1\): \(0{,}432 + 0{,}505 = 0{,}937\). So the sum starts at the height \(A\), because the sine adds nothing there, and with the slope \(B\omega\), because the cosine adds no slope there. For a given initial value \(y(t_0)\) and slope \(y'(t_0)\) we therefore choose \(A = y(t_0)\) and \(B = y'(t_0)/\omega\).

Theorem 8.18 Solutions of the harmonic oscillator equation (rešitve enačbe harmonskega nihala)

Let \(\omega > 0\), \(I\) an interval or \(\mathbb{R}\), \(t_0 \in I\) and \(y\) a twice differentiable function on \(I\) with \(y''(t) + \omega^2 y(t) = 0\) for every \(t \in I\). Then

\[y(t) = y(t_0)\cos\omega(t - t_0) + \frac{y'(t_0)}{\omega}\sin\omega(t - t_0) \qquad \text{for every } t \in I . \tag{8.12}\]

For \(t_0 = 0\) this is \(y = A\cos\omega t + B\sin\omega t\) with \(A = y(0)\) and \(B = y'(0)/\omega\). Conversely, for any numbers \(A\) and \(B\) the function \(A\cos\omega(t - t_0) + B\sin\omega(t - t_0)\) is a solution. When \(t\) is time, such a function is called a harmonic oscillation (harmonično nihanje).

Proof

We must show that the right-hand side is a solution with the same initial data as \(y\) and that two solutions with equal data coincide. Indeed their difference is a solution with zero data, and that is zero by the lemma of the conserved sum of squares.

  1. Let \(z(t) = A\cos\omega(t - t_0) + B\sin\omega(t - t_0)\). By theorem 8.13 (second formula, \(c = -\omega t_0\)) and the rules of differentiation (lemma 1.82, items 1 and 2), \(z'(t) = -\omega A\sin\omega(t - t_0) + \omega B\cos\omega(t - t_0)\) and \(z''(t) = -\omega^2 A\cos\omega(t - t_0) - \omega^2 B\sin\omega(t - t_0) = -\omega^2 z(t)\). Hence \(z'' + \omega^2 z = 0\) on \(I\) and every function of this form is a solution. From now on let \(A = y(t_0)\) and \(B = y'(t_0)/\omega\), so that \(z\) is the right-hand side of (8.12).
  2. At \(t = t_0\), \(z(t_0) = y(t_0)\) and \(z'(t_0) = y'(t_0)\), because \(\cos 0 = 1\) and \(\sin 0 = 0\): both curves start at the same height with the same slope.
  3. Let \(d = y - z\). By the rules of differentiation (lemma 1.82, items 1 and 2), \(d'' + \omega^2 d = 0\), and by step 2, \(d(t_0) = d'(t_0) = 0\).
  4. Let \(f = d'/\omega\) and \(g = d\). Then \(f' = d''/\omega = -\omega d = -\omega g\), \(g' = d' = \omega f\) and \(f(t_0) = g(t_0) = 0\). By lemma 8.15, \(g = d = 0\) on \(I\), so \(y = z\): the point \((d, d'/\omega)\) starts at \(O\) and stays there.

Source: KREYSZIG §2.4, equation (4) (PDF p. 89), states the general solution \(A\cos\omega_0 t + B\sin\omega_0 t\) of the oscillation of a mass on a spring and calls the motion a harmonic oscillation. The proof that these are all solutions is ours.

8.2.5 Small angles (majhni koti)

We will use the approximations \(\sin\theta \approx \theta\) and \(\cos\theta \approx 1\) when the angle is small. The goal of this approximation is an equation that is linear in \(\theta\) and is solved by theorem 8.18. How large an angle is still small is told by the next lemma.

Lemma 8.20 Small angles (majhni koti)

For every real number \(\theta\)

\[0 \le 1 - \cos\theta \le \frac{\theta^2}{2} , \qquad |\sin\theta - \theta| \le \frac{|\theta|^3}{6} . \tag{8.13}\]

Hence \(|\sin\theta/\theta - 1| \le \theta^2/6\) for \(\theta \neq 0\), and \(\sin\theta/\theta \to 1\) as \(\theta \to 0\).

Proof

We must show the two bounds for \(\theta > 0\), by three successive integrations that start with the inequality \(\cos t \le 1\) (figure 8.39). Each integration turns an inequality between two curves into an inequality between the areas under them, and the areas are the curves of the next step. For \(\theta < 0\) and \(\theta = 0\) they follow from the evenness of the cosine and the oddness of the sine.

  1. The cosine and the sine are differentiable, hence continuous (lemma 1.80), and \(|\cos t| \le 1\), \(|\sin t| \le 1\) (lemma 8.11, item 1).
  2. For \(\theta > 0\) the sine is an antiderivative of the cosine, so by the fundamental theorem of calculus (theorem 3.55, item 1) \(\sin\theta = \sin\theta - \sin 0 = \int_0^\theta \cos t\,\mathrm{d}t \le \int_0^\theta 1\,\mathrm{d}t = \theta\) by monotonicity of the integral (lemma 3.54, item 4). In the left panel this is the grey area \(\sin\theta\) under the cosine, which is not larger than the area \(\theta\) under the line \(1\). Hence \(\sin t \le t\) for every \(t \ge 0\).
  3. An antiderivative of the sine is \(-\cos\), so \(1 - \cos\theta = \int_0^\theta \sin t\,\mathrm{d}t \le \int_0^\theta t\,\mathrm{d}t = \theta^2/2\) by step 2 (an antiderivative of the function \(t\) is \(t^2/2\) by lemma 1.82, items 2 and 5). In the middle panel this is the grey area under the sine against the triangle under the line \(t\). The left inequality \(0 \le 1 - \cos\theta\) is \(\cos\theta \le 1\).
  4. An antiderivative of the function \(1 - \cos t\) is \(t - \sin t\), so \(\theta - \sin\theta = \int_0^\theta (1 - \cos t)\,\mathrm{d}t\). By step 3, applied at every \(t\), the integrand lies between \(0\) and \(t^2/2\), so (monotonicity) \(0 \le \theta - \sin\theta \le \int_0^\theta (t^2/2)\,\mathrm{d}t = \theta^3/6\). In the right panel this is the grey area under the curve \(1 - \cos t\) against the area under the curve \(t^2/2\). At \(\theta = 1{,}2\) this is \(0{,}2680 \le 0{,}288\).
  5. For \(\theta < 0\) we insert \(-\theta > 0\) and use evenness and oddness (lemma 8.11, item 4): \(1 - \cos\theta = 1 - \cos(-\theta)\) and \(|\sin\theta - \theta| = |\sin(-\theta) - (-\theta)|\). At \(\theta = 0\) both sides are zero.
  6. Division by \(|\theta|\) gives \(|\sin\theta/\theta - 1| \le \theta^2/6\): in figure 8.40 the blackwhite curve \(|\sin\theta/\theta - 1|\) lies below the blue parabola \(\theta^2/6\), and at \(\theta = 0\) it is not defined. This is also \(\sin'0 = \cos 0 = 1\) from theorem 8.13.
