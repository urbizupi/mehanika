Chapter 1 Strain

1.1 A bar that stretches

The simplest case is a straight bar (palica), a body that is much longer than it is thick. If we cut the bar perpendicular to its length, we get a face called a cross section (prečni prerez). The axis of the bar is the straight line along the bar through the centres of its cross sections. We pull the bar at both ends along its axis. The forces that act on a body are called its loading (obremenitev), so we speak of the bar before loading and after it. Before loading it has length \(L\). When we pull it, it has length \(L + \Delta L\). The number \(\Delta L\) is the elongation (raztezek) of the bar, that is, the change of its length. In this book the Greek letter \(\Delta\) (delta) always means a change: we read \(\Delta L\) as "the change of the length \(L\)". If we compress the bar, it gets shorter, and the elongation is negative.

Is the elongation a good measure of how strongly the material of the bar is stretched? Take two bars of the same steel with the same cross section, one 1 m long and the other 2 m, and pull them with the same force. The longer one stretches twice as much. This is no surprise. We can think of the longer bar as two shorter ones placed end to end (figure 1.4). The force that pulls the longer bar pulls each of the two halves, so each half stretches as much as the short bar, and together they stretch twice as much. But the material in the two bars is loaded the same: every centimetre of the longer bar stretches exactly as much as every centimetre of the shorter one. So the elongation does not tell us how hard the material is loaded. Mostly it tells us how long the bar is.

What matters for the material is the stretch of every centimetre, of every metre, that is, the elongation per unit length. We get it by dividing the elongation by the length.

Definition 1.2 Strain of a bar (deformacija palice)

A bar with initial length \(L\) that stretches by \(\Delta L\) has the strain (deformacija)

\[\varepsilon = \frac{\Delta L}{L} . \tag{1.1}\]

Source: BARBER §1.2.1 (PDF p. 32), EP Example 2.3.1(d) (p. 60), SADD §2.2 (PDF p. 42).

In everyday language "deformation" means a change of shape. In mechanics the number that measures this change is called the strain. Strain is a ratio of two lengths, so it has no unit: the metres cancel. For the 1 m bar and the 2 m bar of our example we get the same strain, which is what we wanted. Other books also call this number the relative elongation or the engineering strain, and often write it \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), where \(x\) is the direction of the axis of the bar. Why two indices when there is only one direction? For a bar one would indeed be enough. In a body that is not a thin bar, however, we will describe the strain of the material around a point by several numbers, and each of them will be fixed by two directions. When the two directions are the same, as in \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), the number measures how strongly the material is stretched in that direction. When they differ, as in \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\), the number describes how the right angle between the directions \(x\) and \(y\) changes. A deck of cards pushed sideways at the top leans over: the cards keep their length, but the right angles in the side face of the deck become slanted (figure 1.5). So the index \(xx\) says that the number measures a change of length in the direction \(x\) and not a change of angle. We will meet these numbers properly when we describe strain in space (SADD §2.2, PDF pp. 41-46).

Dividing the elongation by the original length seems obvious today, but it was not always so. In 1826, in the first printed edition of his book on strength of materials, Navier described a material by the ratio of the force per unit area of the cross section to the elongation per unit length (TIMO, PDF p. 81-82). The elongation per unit length is our strain (1.1). How he arrived at this ratio we will tell in section 2.2.

During his visits to the Venetian arsenal, Galileo Galilei noticed that larger things are weaker than smaller ones of the same shape. In his words, a small obelisk or column can be set up or laid down without danger, but very large ones collapse at the slightest push, and only because of their own weight (TIMO, PDF p. 19). To prove this, in his book published in 1638 he first took a bar that is pulled along its length, and found that the strength of the bar is proportional to the area of its cross section and does not depend on its length (TIMO, PDF p. 19). Why? A bar with twice the cross section is like two equal bars side by side, which share the force. A longer bar, however, is not weaker, because each of its cross sections is pulled by the same force as in a shorter bar, if we neglect the weight of the bar (section 1.2). Then he built the same bar with one end into a wall, so that it stuck out horizontally, and loaded it at the free end. It breaks, he wrote, at the edge of the wall, around which the bar turns like a lever around its support (figure 1.6, left) (TIMO, PDF p. 19-20). He assumed that at the break the whole cross section at the wall resists equally (TIMO, PDF p. 20). A force tends to break the bar off the more strongly, the larger it is and the farther from the edge it acts, as with a lever. Galileo enlarged the bar in his mind by the same factor in all dimensions, say twice (figure 1.6, right). Its weight becomes eight times larger, because the bar is twice as long, twice as wide and twice as high, and it acts twice as far from the edge, so it tends to break the bar off sixteen times more strongly. The resistance of the cross section grows with its area, which becomes four times larger, and with the distance from the edge to the middle of the cross section, which becomes twice as large, so only eight times in all (TIMO, PDF p. 21). A larger bar is therefore weaker, and a big enough one breaks already under its own weight (TIMO, PDF p. 21). So in his view ships, palaces and temples of any size cannot be built (TIMO, PDF p. 21).

In 1678 Robert Hooke published a treatise on springs, the first published treatise on how materials change their shape under a force and return to their original shape when unloaded (TIMO, PDF p. 26). He fixed the upper end of a wire 20 to 40 feet long, about 6 to 12 m, to a nail, and hung a pan for weights from its lower end. He put weights into the pan and measured by how much the wire stretched (figure 1.7). The elongations were always in the same ratio as the weights that caused them (TIMO, PDF p. 26, 28). From such experiments with wires, springs and wood he concluded that the change of shape is proportional to the force (SADD, PDF p. 7, TIMO, PDF p. 28). When bending a strip of wood he also noticed that the fibres on the convex side are stretched and those on the concave side are compressed (figure 1.8, first view) (TIMO, PDF p. 28). Mariotte corrected Galileo's calculation, because he took into account that the fibres stretch (TIMO, PDF pp. 29-30). In the cross section of a bent beam one part of the fibres is in tension and the other part in compression (figure 1.8, second to fourth view) (TIMO, PDF p. 29-31).

So lengthening and shortening came from pulled wires and bent beams. The second kind of change came from sliding. In 1773 Charles-Augustin Coulomb presented to the French Academy a memoir that dealt, among other things, with why masonry pillars and the soil behind retaining walls fail (TIMO, PDF p. 55). He assumed that a pillar pressed from above breaks along an inclined plane, along which one piece slides over the other (figure 1.9, left). In his view the failure happens when the part of the force that lies in this plane becomes larger than the resistance of the material to sliding along it (TIMO, PDF p. 59). Why does a force that presses the pillar straight down have a part in an inclined plane at all? Let us cut the pillar in our mind along an inclined flat surface and look only at the upper piece (figure 1.10). We neglect the weight of the pillar. Then only two things act on the upper piece: the load, which pushes it down along the axis of the pillar, and the lower piece across the cut. The upper piece is at rest, so the lower piece must push it up with a force of the same size, also along the axis, just as the pulls on the two cuts of a piece cut out of the bar pulled by a force at its lower end are equal (figure 1.12, middle). But a force along the axis is not perpendicular to the inclined cut. So we can split it into a part perpendicular to the cut and a part lying in the cut, although nobody pushes the pillar sideways. With the part in the cut the lower piece holds the upper one back from sliding down along the cut. If the material cannot carry that much, the upper piece slides, as on the left of figure 1.9. The force at which this happens, calculated in this way, agreed well with his experiments on brick (TIMO, PDF p. 59). The slide was resisted by the material sticking together across the plane, which we call cohesion (kohezija), and by friction (trenje) along it (TIMO, PDF p. 59). For the soil behind a retaining wall he likewise assumed that it slides along some flat plane (TIMO, PDF p. 69). In 1784 he determined how a wire resists twisting from the oscillations of a metal cylinder hung on it (TIMO, PDF p. 59), and in a twisted wire, too, parts of the material shift relative to each other, as we will see in chapter 16. So lengthening and shortening on one side and sliding on the other were at first two separate kinds of failure.

Why there are exactly two kinds was shown by Cauchy, who by the autumn of 1822 already had the idea of the force per unit area at a point of a body (SADD, PDF p. 7, LOVE, PDF p. 25). In doing so he generalised the notion of hydrostatic pressure (LOVE, PDF p. 25). Let us see what such pressure is. Water at rest presses on every surface perpendicular to it, however the surface is tilted. Such a force per unit area is called pressure (tlak) (CM §8.1.1, PDF p. 384). Why is pressure perpendicular to the surface? A solid body can resist a force that tries to shift one part of it relative to another, but a fluid cannot. Under such a force a fluid deforms without end, that is, it flows (CM §8.1.1, PDF p. 384). If the force between the water and the surface were not perpendicular to the surface, it would also have a part lying in the surface, and this part would shift the water next to the surface along it. The water would flow. A fluid at rest therefore acts on a surface only with pressure, with no part lying in the surface (CM §8.1.1, PDF p. 384). Let us ask what happens if in a solid body the force can point in any direction. We cut the body in our minds with a flat surface and ask with what force per unit area one part of the body acts on the other across this surface (figure 1.9, right). Such a force has a size and a direction. Every force can be split into a part perpendicular to the surface and a part lying in the surface. The first part pulls or pushes, as in a bar that we pull. The second tries to shift one part of the body relative to the other, as in the deck of cards. So there are two kinds of failure because a force has only these two parts with respect to a surface. The change of shape is similar. A short piece of material can change its length, and two pieces that were perpendicular can change the angle between them.

So the material close to a point can change its shape in two ways. Its lengths can change: in some direction the material gets longer when we pull it, or shorter when we push it together, which is called compression (stiskanje). Or the right angles between directions can change, as in the leaning deck, where the cards slide past each other. This is called shear (strig). Each of the numbers with two indices measures one of these two changes.

Definition 1.3 Normal and shear strain (normalna in strižna deformacija)

A normal strain (normalna deformacija) in a direction is the change of length of a short piece of material lying in that direction, divided by its original length. It is positive when the material gets longer and negative when it gets shorter. A shear strain (strižna deformacija) of two perpendicular directions is a number that describes how the right angle between them changes. It is positive when the right angle between the positive directions gets smaller. Which number exactly measures this change we settle in section 1.17.

Source: SADD §2.2 (PDF pp. 42-43).

Cauchy's memoir was presented to the Academy in September 1822 but was not published. Its contents appeared in the 1827 volume of his Exercices de mathématique (LOVE, PDF p. 25). He showed that the force per unit area at a point is determined by six numbers. He described the change of shape by six numbers too (LOVE, PDF p. 25). These are three normal strains, one for each axis, and three shear strains, one for each pair of axes. Together they describe completely how the material close to a point is deformed, as long as the deformation is small (SADD §2.2, PDF p. 43). We will derive them when we describe strain in space, in section 1.17.

Which of the two changes we see depends on the directions we look along. In the leaning deck the cards keep their length, and only the right angle between the directions \(x\) and \(y\) changes. But a line drawn on the side face of the deck from one bottom corner to the opposite top corner gets longer, and the line between the other two corners gets shorter. Along these two directions the same deformation is stretching and compression. We will check this with numbers on a sheared cube in section 1.6.2. Cauchy also showed that at a point there are three perpendicular surfaces on which the force is perpendicular to them, with no part lying in the surface (LOVE, PDF p. 25). The same holds for the change of shape: for every small deformation there are three mutually perpendicular directions in which there is no shear at all, only stretching or compression (LOVE, PDF p. 25, SADD §2.4, PDF p. 49). We will meet them in definition 2.62. We will prove that such directions exist in chapter 2, in section 2.11.4.

We will meet Cauchy's cut in chapter 2, in section 2.5, the split of the force into two parts in definition 2.18 and the surfaces without shear in section 2.11. Karl Culmann (CAJORI, PDF p. 312) in 1866 (TIMO, PDF p. 224) and Otto Mohr later represented the force per unit area at a point by a circle, which engineers still draw today (SADD, PDF p. 71), and we tell their story in section 2.12.3. For the history in more detail, SADD recommends the books by Isaac Todhunter and Karl Pearson, by Love and by Timoshenko (SADD, PDF p. 7).

How large are strains? A steel bar 2 m long that stretches by 1 mm has the strain

\[\varepsilon = \frac{0{,}001\ \text{m}}{2\ \text{m}} = 0{,}0005 ,\]

five ten-thousandths. A rubber band stretched from 10 cm to 15 cm has the strain \(5\ \text{cm} / 10\ \text{cm} = 0{,}5\), a thousand times more. For structures one more number matters. If ordinary structural steel is stretched by more than about one thousandth of its length, it does not return to its original length when we let go, but stays permanently longer. This bound follows from the data for steel in BOWER (Tables 3.1 and 3.9, PDF pp. 100 and 156). We will see how to compute it once we know how the strain of a bar depends on the force that pulls it. So the strains in steel structures are almost always smaller than \(0{,}001\). This is one of the most important facts in this book, because many simplifications will rest on it.

For a compressed bar \(\Delta L\) is negative, so the strain is negative too. A positive strain means lengthening, a negative one shortening.

The number \(\Delta L / L\) is not the only measure of stretching, only the simplest. Books use others too, and we will meet them when we need them. The stretch (razmerje dolžin) \(\lambda = (L + \Delta L)/L\) compares the new length with the old one and equals \(1 + \varepsilon\). The Green-Lagrange strain \(\big((L + \Delta L)^2 - L^2\big)/(2L^2)\) compares the squares of the lengths and equals \(\varepsilon + \varepsilon^2/2\) (CM (3.4.15), PDF p. 131). The logarithmic strain is the logarithm of the ratio \((L + \Delta L)/L\) (BOWER, PDF p. 589). For \(\varepsilon = 0{,}001\) the Green-Lagrange strain is \(0{,}0010005\) and the logarithmic strain \(0{,}0009995\), both differ from \(\varepsilon\) by half a per mille of its value. For the rubber band with \(\varepsilon = 0{,}5\) they are \(0{,}625\) and \(0{,}405\). So for small strains all the measures give almost the same number, for large strains they differ a lot, and then we must say which measure we use. Until we say otherwise, the strain of a bar means \(\Delta L / L\).

The formula \(\varepsilon = \Delta L / L\) has a hidden assumption. When we divided the longer bar into two halves, we assumed that both stretch the same. More than that, we assumed that every centimetre of the bar stretches the same as every other. What if this is not so?
