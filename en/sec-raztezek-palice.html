Chapter 1 Strain

1.1 A bar that stretches

The simplest case is a straight bar (palica), a body that is much longer than it is thick. We pull the bar at its free end along its length, the other end is held by a wall (figure 1.5). The forces that act on a body are called its loading (obremenitev), so we speak of the bar before loading and after it. Before loading it has length \(L\). When we pull it, it has length \(L + \Delta L\). The number \(\Delta L\) is the elongation (raztezek) of the bar, that is, the change of its length. In this book the Greek letter \(\Delta\) (delta) always means a change: we read \(\Delta L\) as "the change of the length \(L\)". If we compress the bar, it gets shorter, and the elongation is negative.

Let us draw the bar. The hatched line on the left is the wall that holds the end at rest. The blackwhite bar is the bar before loading, the distance \(L\) above it is its length. The orange bar is the same bar after loading, the orange arrow points away from the wall, because we pull the free end. The grey dashed line and the distance \(\Delta L\) show by how much the free end has moved, and the distance \(L + \Delta L\) below it is the new length of the bar. Under compression the orange bar would be shorter than the blackwhite one and \(\Delta L\) would be negative.

Now we cut the bar in the mind. If we cut it perpendicular to its length, we get a face called a cross section (prečni prerez). In figure 1.6 the bar is cut in the mind into a left piece and a right piece. The pieces are drawn apart so that the cross section can be seen, but the bar is in reality whole. The blue face on the left piece is the cross section, and the blue dot on it is its centre. We will need the cross section later, when we divide the force by the area of the cross section. The cut face of the right piece looks to the left, so we do not see it in the figure. The axis of the bar is the straight line along the bar through the centres of its cross sections, in the figure grey dash-dot. We pull the bar at both ends along its axis. The blackwhite arrows point away from the bar, because we pull it, and they lie on the axis, because we pull along it.

Is the elongation a good measure of how strongly the material of the bar is stretched? Take two bars of the same steel with the same cross section, one 1 m long and the other 2 m, and pull them with the same force. The longer one stretches twice as much: if the 1 m bar becomes longer by 1 mm, the 2 m bar becomes longer by 2 mm. This is no surprise. We can think of the longer bar as two shorter ones placed end to end (figure 1.7). The force that pulls the longer bar pulls each of the two halves: each half is at rest, so the other half (or the wall) pulls it with the same force \(F\) with which we pull the free end. Each half is of the same steel, has the same cross section and the length of the shorter bar, so it stretches as much as the shorter bar, and together they stretch twice as much. But the material in the two bars is loaded the same: every centimetre of the longer bar stretches exactly as much as every centimetre of the shorter one. So the elongation does not tell us how hard the material is loaded. Mostly it tells us how long the bar is.

Let us first draw the shorter bar. The hatched line on the left is a wall. It only marks the end at rest. The wall holds it with the same force \(F\) with which we would pull it, so the elongation is the same as when pulling both ends. At the top is the blackwhite bar, the shorter bar before loading. Below it is the orange bar, the same bar after loading. The orange arrow points away from the wall, because we pull the free end. The distance \(L\) is the length of the bar before loading. The grey vertical line and the distance \(\Delta L\) show by how much the free end has moved. The elongation is enlarged in the figure so that it can be seen.

Below it is the bar of twice the length. The blackwhite bar is before loading. The grey dotted line in its middle divides it into two halves, each of length \(L\) and \(L\), like the shorter bar. At the bottom is the orange bar after loading, with the same orange force. Its orange dotted line is the previous middle, now moved. Each half is now \(L + \Delta L\) long and \(L + \Delta L\). The whole bar is longer by \(2\,\Delta L\).

For the material, what matters is the stretch of every centimetre, of every metre, that is, the elongation per unit length. We get it by dividing the elongation by the length.

Definition 1.2 Strain of a bar (deformacija palice)

A bar with initial length \(L\) that stretches by \(\Delta L\) has the strain (deformacija)

\[\varepsilon = \frac{\Delta L}{L} . \tag{1.1}\]

Source: BARBER §1.2.1 (PDF p. 32), EP Example 2.3.1(d) (p. 60), SADD §2.2 (PDF p. 42).

In everyday language the word deformation means a change of shape. In mechanics the number that measures this change is also called by it (strain). Strain is a ratio of two lengths, so it has no unit: the metres cancel. For the 1 m bar and the 2 m bar of our example we get the same strain, which is what we wanted. Say the 1 m bar becomes longer by 1 mm and the 2 m bar by 2 mm. Division gives the same number in both cases:

\[\frac{1\ \text{mm}}{1\ \text{m}} = \frac{0{,}001\ \text{m}}{1\ \text{m}} = 0{,}001 , \qquad \frac{2\ \text{mm}}{2\ \text{m}} = \frac{0{,}002\ \text{m}}{2\ \text{m}} = 0{,}001 .\]

Other books also call this number the relative elongation or the specific strain, and often write it \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), where \(x\) is the direction of the axis of the bar.

Why two indices when there is only one direction? For a bar one would indeed be enough. We give the explanation here, because the story below it ends with two kinds of change of shape, which are told apart by exactly two indices: a change of length, as in the elongation of a bar, and a change of angle. In a body that is not a thin bar, however, we will describe the strain of the material around a point by several numbers, and each of them will be fixed by two directions, for example \(x\) and \(y\), shown in figure 1.8. When the two directions are the same, as in \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), the number measures how strongly the material is stretched in that direction. When they differ, as in \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\), the number describes how the right angle between the directions \(x\) and \(y\) changes. A deck of playing cards pushed sideways at the top leans over: the cards keep their length, but the right angles in the side face of the deck become slanted (figure 1.8).

Let us draw the deck from the side. The axes \(x\) and \(y\) on the left show the directions: \(x\) horizontal, \(y\) vertical. The hatched line is the table on which the deck lies. The cards are stacked as thin rectangles, each exactly as long as every other. The side face of the deck is therefore a vertical line. The blue angle in the bottom left corner is the right angle between the directions \(x\) and \(y\). On the right the cards after the push are orange. The orange arrow points sideways, because we push the deck at the top. The top card moves most, the bottom one does not move, because it lies on the table, and each one in between moves a little more than the one below it. So the side face is slanted. Its approximation is the orange straight line through the bottom left corners of the cards. The axis labels are the same as before, so that we see that the angle between \(x\) and the side face has changed. The blue arc is this angle. It is smaller than a right angle, because the face has leaned toward \(x\).

So the index \(xx\) says that the number measures a change of length in the direction \(x\) and not a change of angle. We will meet these numbers properly when we describe strain in space (SADD §2.2, PDF pp. 41-46).

Dividing the elongation by the length seems obvious today. It was reached through the experiments and calculations of six men: Navier, whom we have already met, Galileo, Hooke, Mariotte, Coulomb and Cauchy. Let us take two things from their stories: the elongation per unit of length is the natural measure, and there are two kinds of change of shape, a change of length (the elongation of a bar) and a change of angle (the leaning deck of cards).

From history

Dividing the elongation by the original length seems obvious today, but it was not always so. In 1826, in the first printed edition of his book on strength of materials, Navier described a material by the ratio of the force per unit area of the cross section to the elongation per unit length (TIMO, PDF pp. 81-82). The elongation per unit length is our strain (1.1). How he arrived at this ratio we will tell in section 2.2.

During his visits to the Venetian arsenal, Galileo Galilei noticed that larger things are weaker than smaller ones of the same shape. In his words, a small obelisk or column can be set up or laid down without danger, but very large ones collapse at the slightest push, and only because of their own weight (TIMO, PDF p. 19). To prove this, in his book published in 1638 he first took a bar that is pulled along its length, and found that the strength of the bar is proportional to the area of its cross section and does not depend on its length (TIMO, PDF p. 19). Why? A bar with twice the cross section is like two equal bars side by side, which share the force. A longer bar, however, is not weaker, because each of its cross sections is pulled by the same force as in a shorter bar, if we neglect the weight of the bar (section 1.2).

Let us draw three bars. The hatched line on the left is a wall that holds the resting end of all three. Above is the bar, pulled at the free end by a force \(F\) that points away from the wall. The blue line across the bar is its cross section with area \(A\). In the middle are two equal bars on top of each other, together with the twice larger cross section \(2A\). Each of the two is pulled by its own force \(F\), so every part of the cross section carries as much as before. Below is the bar twice as long, cut in the middle so that we can see what the cross section carries. The blue arrows show that one piece pulls the other with the same force \(F\) as at the end. So the cross section carries no more than in the short bar.

Then he built the same bar with one end into a wall, so that it stuck out horizontally, and loaded it at the free end (figure 1.10, left). The hatched vertical line on the left is a wall. From it sticks out the bar of length \(L\) and depth \(d\). It is drawn grey dashed, because this is its position before it breaks. It breaks, he wrote, at the edge of the wall \(B\), around which the bar turns like a lever around its support (TIMO, PDF p. 19-20). In orange is the bar after the break, turned about \(B\). The turn is greatly exaggerated, so that we see about which point the bar turns. At the free end of the broken bar is the force \(F\), which pulls this end down. So \(B\) is the lower edge of the section at the wall. When the free end goes down, the upper part of the bar moves away from the wall, and the lower edge stays against it.

The bar breaks off at the section at the wall, which is the blue vertical line. Galileo assumed that at the break the whole section resists equally (TIMO, PDF p. 20). So the blue arrows are all equally long. They point toward the wall, because the section holds the bar at the wall while the force breaks it off from the wall. A force tends to break the bar off the more strongly, the larger it is and the farther from the edge it acts, as with a lever: for the strength of the breaking off the rule of the lever holds, the force multiplied by the distance from the edge to the place where the force acts. The resistance is similar. Every part of the section resists equally, and their distances from the edge \(B\) are spread evenly from \(0\) to the depth \(d\). Let us divide the section into \(n\) equal strips. Each resists with a force \(f\), the centre of the \(i\)-th strip is at the distance \(y_{\mathrm{i}} = (i - \tfrac12)\,d/n\) from the edge \(B\), so that the distances are spread evenly from \(0\) to \(d\). The rule of the lever adds up over the strips:

\[\sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{n} f\,y_{\mathrm{i}} = f\,(y_1 + \dots + y_{\mathrm{n}}) = (n f)\,\bar y , \qquad \bar y = \frac{y_1 + \dots + y_{\mathrm{n}}}{n} = \frac d2 ,\]

where \(n f\) is the total resistance and \(\bar y\) the average distance of the strips from the edge. The sum is therefore the same as if the total resistance acted at the distance \(d/2\). So the whole section resists as if all the resistance acted at a distance \(d/2\) from the edge. It is drawn to the left of the wall, from \(B\) to the middle of the section.

Galileo enlarged the bar in his mind by the same factor in all dimensions, say twice (figure 1.10, right). This is the right bar, built into the wall at the edge \(B\) like the left one. Now only its own weight breaks it, which we count as one force at the middle of the bar. We compare it with the left bar, which would also be broken only by its own weight, at its middle, at a distance \(L/2\) from the edge. Beside each quantity of the right bar is written how many times larger it is than on the left. All four quantities are in the table (\(W\) is the weight of the left bar, \(A\) the area of its cross section):

quantity left bar right bar factor
weight \(W\) \(8\,W\) \(\times 8\)
distance of the weight from the edge \(B\) \(L/2\) \(L\) \(\times 2\)
area of the cross section \(A\) \(4\,A\) \(\times 4\)
distance of the resistance from the edge \(d/2\) \(d\) \(\times 2\)

The weight becomes eight times larger, because the bar is twice as long, twice as wide and twice as high (\(2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 = 8\)). It acts at a distance \(L\) from the edge, that is, twice as far. The area of the section, the blue line at the wall, becomes four times larger, because the width and the depth of the section are twice as large (\(2 \cdot 2 = 4\)). The blue arrows are again the resistance of the section, the distance of the resistance becomes twice as large (TIMO, PDF p. 21). The larger bar compared with the smaller:

\[\begin{aligned} \text{breaking} &= \text{weight} \cdot \text{distance} = 8 \cdot 2 = 16,\\ \text{resistance} &= \text{area} \cdot \text{distance from the edge} = 4 \cdot 2 = 8. \end{aligned}\]

So the breaking off grows twice as much as the resistance. A larger bar is therefore weaker, and a big enough one breaks already under its own weight (TIMO, PDF p. 21). So in his view ships, palaces and temples of any size cannot be built (TIMO, PDF p. 21).

In 1678 Robert Hooke published a treatise on a spring, the first published treatise on how materials change their shape under a force and return to their original shape when unloaded (TIMO, PDF p. 26). He fixed the upper end of a wire 20 to 40 feet long, that is, about 6 to 12 m, to a nail, and hung a pan for weights from its lower end. He put weights into the pan and measured by how much the wire stretched (figure 1.11). The elongations were always in the same ratio as the weights that caused them (TIMO, PDF p. 26, 28). From such experiments with wires, springs and wood he concluded that the change of shape is proportional to the force (a doubled force gives a doubled elongation, \(\Delta L = k\,F\) with a constant \(k\)) (SADD, PDF p. 7, TIMO, PDF p. 28).

Let us draw the experiment. The hatched lines at the top are the ceiling, the blackwhite dot on them is the nail to which the wire is fixed. The hatched line at the bottom is the floor. The figure shows the same wire three times. On the left hangs the wire with an empty pan, blackwhite, because this is the state without weights. The distance \(h\) is the distance from the bottom of the pan to the floor, which Hooke measured with compasses. It is drawn for all three wires. Two slanted strokes in the wire mean that the wire is in reality much longer, several metres, and in the figure we shorten it. In the middle there is one weight in the pan. The force \(F\) is its weight and points down, because weight pulls down. The orange wire and the orange pan show the state after loading. The grey dashed line is the position of the empty pan. The upper end of the wire is fixed, so the pan goes down by exactly as much as the wire stretches, and this is the elongation \(\Delta L_1\). So \(h\) is smaller. On the right there are two equal weights in the orange pan on the orange wire, so the force \(2F\) is twice as large. The elongation \(\Delta L_2\) is twice as large as \(\Delta L_1\), as Hooke measured: each weight stretches the wire by the same elongation. The elongations are greatly enlarged in the figure.

When bending a strip of wood he also noticed that the fibres (vlakna), thin threads along which the wood is built, are stretched on the convex side and compressed on the concave side (figure 1.12, view (1)) (TIMO, PDF p. 28). A stretched fibre is in tension (nateg), a compressed one in compression.

Let us draw view (1). The hatched line is a wall, into which a thin strip of wood is built. The grey dashed line is the upper edge and the free end of the strip as it is before loading. In orange is the strip after loading, bent downward. The bend is greatly exaggerated so that it can be seen. The force \(F\) acts on the free end and points down, because it pulls the end down. The upper blue arrows each point to their own side, because the fibres on the convex, upper side are stretched. The lower blue arrows point toward each other, because the fibres on the concave, lower side are compressed.

Mariotte corrected Galileo's calculation, because he took into account that the fibres stretch. From his experiments in tension he found that for all the materials tested the elongations are proportional to the forces (TIMO, PDF p. 29). He assumed that at the break the bar turns about the lower edge of the section (the point \(D\) in views (2) and (3) of the same figure), as with Galileo (TIMO, PDF p. 30). In a turn about an edge a fibre moves the more, the farther from the edge it is, so it gets longer by more. Since the elongations are proportional to the forces, the forces in the fibres are in the same ratio as their distances from the edge (view (3)) (TIMO, PDF p. 30). For a material that stretches in proportion to the force up to the break, the lower part of the section is compressed and the upper part stretched (view (4)) (TIMO, PDF p. 31). The turn is then about the point where pull changes to push, and \(D\) in view (4) is no longer the lower edge. Why is this point in the middle of the section and not at the edge? Only the force \(F\) acts on the strip, pointing down, and there is no horizontal force, so the sum of all horizontal forces in the section is zero: the pull and the push balance. If all the fibres pulled, the sum would not be zero. Let the point without force be at a distance \(y_0\) from the lower edge of the section of height \(d\), and let the force in a fibre grow in proportion to the distance from it, \(c\,|y - y_0|\), where \(c\) is a constant. The fibres are spread evenly over the height, so the sum of the forces is the area of the triangle formed by the ends of the arrows: the pulling triangle has height \(d - y_0\), the pushing one height \(y_0\). Pull and push are equal if the triangles are equal:

\[\tfrac12\,c\,(d - y_0)^2 = \tfrac12\,c\,y_0^2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad d - y_0 = y_0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad y_0 = \frac d2 .\]

So the point without force is in the middle of the section (for a rectangular section, as drawn). In a beam (tram, a horizontal bar loaded across its length) there are therefore fibres in tension and in compression in the same cross section.

Views (2) to (4) show the section of the bar at the wall and the forces with which the fibres resist, in blue. The bar lies to the right of the wall and we do not see it in the views, only its section at the wall is drawn. An arrow toward the wall means pull, an arrow into the bar means push. In view (2), Galileo, the wall is hatched, the blackwhite vertical line is the section of depth \(d\) and the blackwhite dot \(D\) is the lower edge of the section, about which the bar turns. The blue arrows are equally long, because Galileo assumed equal resistance over the whole depth. They point toward the wall, so they pull. The grey vertical line joins their ends and shows that they are equally long.

In view (3), Mariotte, the wall and the section are as before and the dot \(D\) is again the lower edge. The arrows grow with the distance from \(D\): the lowest is the shortest, the highest the longest, because the elongations of the fibres, and therefore the forces in them, are proportional to the distance from the edge. The grey slanted line joins their ends and shows that they grow evenly. In view (4), the correction, the wall and the section are again the same. The blackwhite dot is the point where pull turns into push, drawn at the middle of the section only as an illustration. The upper arrows pull, because they point toward the wall, and the lower ones push, because they point into the bar. The grey slanted lines show that the arrows grow in proportion to the distance from this point.

So lengthening and shortening came from pulled wires and bent beams. The second kind of change came from sliding. In 1773 Charles-Augustin Coulomb presented to the French Academy a memoir that dealt, among other things, with why masonry pillars and the soil behind retaining walls fail (TIMO, PDF p. 55). He assumed that a pillar pressed from above breaks along an inclined plane, along which one piece slides over the other (figure 1.13, left). In his view the failure happens when the part of the force that lies in this plane becomes larger than the resistance of the material to sliding along it (TIMO, PDF p. 59).

Let us draw the left side first. The hatched line is the ground. The blackwhite piece on it is the lower part of the masonry pillar, its inclined upper side is the plane along which the pillar breaks. The grey dashed line is the upper piece of the pillar as it was before the failure. The three arrows at the top are the load that presses the pillar, and they point down. The orange piece is the upper piece after the failure: it has slid down along the inclined plane, because the load pushes it down, and along the plane it can move only along it. The slide is enlarged in the figure. We do not need the right half of the figure yet: we explain it with Cauchy, when we come to the force per unit area at a point.

Why does a force that presses the pillar straight down have a part in an inclined plane at all? Let us cut the pillar in our mind along an inclined flat surface and look only at the upper piece (figure 1.14). We neglect the weight of the pillar. Then only the load, which pushes the upper piece down along the axis of the pillar, and the lower piece across the cut act on it. The upper piece is at rest, so the lower piece must push it up with a force of the same size, also along the axis. But a force along the axis is not perpendicular to the inclined cut. So we can split it into a part perpendicular to the cut and a part lying in the cut, although nobody pushes the pillar sideways. With the part in the cut the lower piece holds the upper one back from sliding down along the cut. If the material cannot carry that much, the upper piece slides, as on the left of figure 1.13.

The blue slanted line is the cut along which we cut the pillar in the mind. Only the upper piece is drawn, the lower piece is grey and dashed, because we do not consider it. The three arrows at the top are the load. They point down, because the load pushes the pillar down. The blackwhite vertical arrow at the point in the middle of the cut is the force with which the lower piece pushes the upper one. It points up, along the axis and opposite to the load, because the upper piece is at rest. The force is in reality spread over the whole cut, and it is drawn as one arrow at the middle. We split it into two parts. The blue arrow is the part perpendicular to the cut, and the blue dashed arrow is the part lying in the cut. We start the second arrow where the first ends, so the two parts add up to the blackwhite arrow. The grey mark at the point says that the first part is indeed perpendicular to the cut. The angle \(\alpha\) is the angle between the axis of the pillar, that is, the force, and the perpendicular to the cut. It is equal to the angle between the cut and the horizontal. If \(F\) is the magnitude of the force, the two parts are

\[F_{\perp} = F\cos\alpha , \qquad F_{\parallel} = F\sin\alpha ,\]

where \(F_{\perp}\) is the part perpendicular to the cut and \(F_{\parallel}\) the part in the cut. In the figure \(\alpha \approx 37^\circ\), \(\cos\alpha = 0{,}8\) and \(\sin\alpha = 0{,}6\), so \(F_{\perp} = 0{,}8\,F\) and \(F_{\parallel} = 0{,}6\,F\). At \(\alpha = 45^\circ\) both parts would be equal, \(F/\sqrt{2} \approx 0{,}71\,F\). For a horizontal cut, \(\alpha = 0\), \(F_{\parallel} = 0\) and the piece does not slide, the more inclined the cut, the larger \(F_{\parallel}\). The arrow at the point is the sum of the forces over the whole cut, drawn as a single one.

Coulomb compared the calculated force at which the upper piece slides with experiments on brick, and it agreed well with them (TIMO, PDF p. 59). The slide was resisted by the material sticking together across the plane, which we call cohesion (kohezija), and by friction (trenje) along it (TIMO, PDF p. 59). Cohesion is the force with which the material holds together across a plane before the piece starts to slide, and friction acts only when the pieces slide over each other. For the soil behind a retaining wall he likewise assumed that it slides along some flat plane that passes through the lower edge of the wall (figure 1.15, left), and the slide is resisted by cohesion and friction (TIMO, PDF p. 69). In 1784 he determined how a wire resists twisting from the oscillations of a metal cylinder hung on it (figure 1.15, right) (TIMO, PDF p. 59), and in a twisted wire, too, parts of the material shift relative to each other, as we will see in chapter 16. So lengthening and shortening on one side and sliding on the other were at first two separate kinds of change that can lead to failure.

Let us draw the left side first. The hatched line is the ground, the wall stands on it. To the right of it is the soil, its surface is at the height of the top of the wall. Coulomb assumed that the soil next to the wall would slide along a flat plane through the lower edge of the wall. This is the blue slanted line, drawn at an angle of \(30^\circ\) to the wall as an example. The blue wedge between the wall, the plane and the surface is the part of the soil that would slide. The orange arrow shows where it slides: down the plane towards the wall, because its weight pulls it downwards, but along the plane it can move only along it. The short lines across the plane are the soil sticking together across the plane, the cohesion, and the blue arrow along the plane is friction. Both resist the slide, so the friction arrow points up along the plane.

Now the right side. The wire hangs from the ceiling, the line is the wire, and the rectangle at its end is a metal cylinder. The circle is the same cylinder from above, the dot in the middle is the wire, which we see from its end. The grey dashed line is the position of the cylinder at rest. When we rotate it, the wire twists, and the orange line shows the position of the cylinder after the rotation by the angle \(\varphi\). The arrow \(M\) is the turning effect of the wire, that is, how strongly the wire turns the cylinder back, which returns the cylinder towards rest, so it points back towards the grey line. The cylinder therefore oscillates, and the orange dashed line is the position on the other side, where it turns after returning. Both rotations are enlarged.

There are two changes: a change of length, as in the elongation of a bar, and a change of a right angle, as in the leaning deck of cards. By the autumn of 1822 Cauchy already had the idea of the force per unit area at a point of a body (SADD, PDF p. 7, LOVE, PDF p. 25), and in doing so he generalised the notion of hydrostatic pressure (LOVE, PDF p. 25).

Let us return to the numbers with two indices by which we will describe the deformation of a material around a point: some measure changes of lengths, others changes of right angles, as in the deck of cards. Why there are exactly two kinds of change is shown by Cauchy's generalisation of hydrostatic pressure, with which the story ended. Let us see what such pressure is. We begin with a fluid, because there the force on a surface is simplest: it has only one part (figure 1.16).

Let us draw the vessel. The vessel is open at the top, the water in it is at rest, its surface is the blue line. At the bottom the arrows point down, at the wall horizontally: the water presses perpendicular to the surface. A surface need not be horizontal or vertical. A small plate, which we hold in the water at an angle, has the area \(A\) and the arrows on it again point perpendicular to it, not vertically down.

Water at rest presses on every surface perpendicular to it, however the surface is tilted. Such a force per unit area is called pressure (tlak) (CM §8.1.1, PDF p. 384). If \(F\) is the force with which the water presses on a surface of area \(A\), the pressure is

\[p = \frac{F}{A} .\]

Why is pressure perpendicular to the surface? A solid body can resist a force that tries to shift one part of it relative to another, but a fluid cannot. Under such a force a fluid deforms without end, that is, it flows (CM §8.1.1, PDF p. 384). If the force between the water and the surface were not perpendicular to the surface, it would also have a part lying in the surface, and this part would shift the water next to the surface along it. The water would flow. A fluid at rest therefore acts on a surface only with pressure, with no part lying in the surface (CM §8.1.1, PDF p. 384). Let us ask what happens if in a solid body the force can point in any direction. We cut the body in our minds with a flat surface and ask with what force per unit area one part of the body acts on the other across this surface (figure 1.13, right).

Let us also draw the right side of figure 1.13. The left part of the body is drawn with a blackwhite line, the right part we have left out of consideration. The cut is the blue line. The point on the cut is where we observe the force. The force per unit area at this point has a size and a direction (blackwhite arrow). It is the force with which the right part acts on the left one. Every force can be split into a part perpendicular to the surface (blue arrow) and a part lying in the surface (blue dashed arrow). The grey mark at the end of the first part says that the two parts are perpendicular to each other: the first is perpendicular to the cut, so the second lies along the cut. The first part pulls or pushes, as in a bar that we pull. The second tries to shift one part of the body relative to the other, as in the deck of cards. With respect to a surface there are only two such parts, so there are also two kinds of failure. The change of shape is similar, as we will show in section 1.17. A short piece of material can change its length, and two pieces that were perpendicular can change the angle between them.

So the material close to a point, that is, in a piece around the point as small as we wish, can change its shape in two ways. Its lengths can change: in some direction the material gets longer when we pull it, or shorter when we push it together, which is called compression (stiskanje). Or the right angles between directions can change, as in the leaning deck of cards, where the cards slide past each other. This is called shear (strig). Each of the numbers with two indices measures one of these two changes. Which of the two shows up depends on the directions in which we look: the same shear gives stretching and compression in slanted directions, as we will see with the diagonals of the square on the right of the figure.

Let us draw all three cases. In each, the square is a small piece of material, blackwhite before the change, orange after it. On the left we pull the piece on both sides, so the arrows point away from it. The orange rectangle is wider than the square: the material has become longer in the direction of the pull. In the middle we compress the piece, so the arrows point toward it. The orange rectangle is narrower than the square: the material has become shorter in this direction. On the right the hatched line under the square means that the bottom side stays at rest. The arrow points sideways, because we push the piece sideways at the top. The orange parallelogram is the piece after the push. The horizontal sides keep their length, the top one has moved to the right. The grey mark in the bottom left corner of the blackwhite square is the right angle before the push, the orange arc is this angle after the push. It is smaller than a right angle, because the left side has leaned to the right. The grey dashed diagonals of the square are equally long. After the push one diagonal is longer and the other shorter. The diagonals are drawn because they show that the same shear in slanted directions means stretching and compression.

Each part of the force on a surface causes its own kind of change of shape. The part perpendicular to the surface pulls or pushes, so it changes a length, as in stretching and compression. The part in the surface shifts one part of the body relative to the other, so it changes an angle, as in shear:

part of the force on the surface what it does change of shape
perpendicular to the surface pulls or pushes change of length
in the surface shifts change of angle (shear)
Definition 1.3 Normal and shear strain (normalna in strižna deformacija)

A normal strain (normalna deformacija) in a direction is the change of length of a short piece of material lying in that direction, divided by its original length. It is positive when the material gets longer and negative when it gets shorter. A shear strain (strižna deformacija) of two perpendicular directions is a number that describes how the right angle between them changes. It is positive when the right angle between the positive directions gets smaller (the positive directions are those in which the axes introduced in section 1.5.1 point). So this is only a description of what the shear strain measures: the change of the angle between the directions, expressed in radians, that is, as the length of an arc on a circle of radius \(1\). Which number exactly measures this change we settle in section 1.17. The normal strain is so called because the part of the force perpendicular (normal) to the surface corresponds to it, and the shear strain because the part of the force in the surface does, as in the table above.

Source: SADD §2.2 (PDF pp. 42-43).

From history

Cauchy's memoir was presented to the Academy in September 1822 but was not published. Its contents appeared in the 1827 volume of his Exercices de mathématique (LOVE, PDF p. 25). He showed that the force per unit area at a point is determined by six numbers. He described the change of shape by six numbers too (LOVE, PDF p. 25).

The six numbers that describe the change of shape at a point are three normal strains, one for each axis (one of them is in the figure), and three shear strains, one for each pair of axes (one of them is in the figure). Together they describe completely how the material close to a point is deformed, as long as the deformation is small (SADD §2.2, PDF p. 43). We will derive them when we describe strain in space, in section 1.17.

Which of the two changes we see depends on the directions we look along. In the leaning deck the cards keep their length, and only the right angle between the directions \(x\) and \(y\) changes. Let us look at the side face of the deck as a square with both diagonals (figure 1.17, right). The diagonal from one bottom corner to the opposite top corner gets longer after the push, and the other diagonal gets shorter. Along these two directions the same deformation is stretching and compression. We will check this with numbers on a sheared cube in section 1.6.2. Cauchy also showed that at a point there are three perpendicular surfaces on which the force is perpendicular to them, with no part lying in the surface (LOVE, PDF p. 25). The same holds for the change of shape: for every small deformation there are three mutually perpendicular directions in which there is no shear at all, only stretching or compression (LOVE, PDF p. 25, SADD §2.4, PDF p. 49). We will meet them in definition 2.62. We will prove that such directions exist in chapter 2, in section 2.11.4.

We will meet Cauchy's cut in chapter 2, in section 2.5, the split of the force into two parts in definition 2.18 and the surfaces without shear in section 2.11. Karl Culmann (CAJORI, PDF p. 312) in 1866 (TIMO, PDF p. 224) and Otto Mohr later represented the force per unit area at a point by a circle, which engineers still draw today (SADD, PDF p. 71), and we tell their story in section 2.12.3. For the history in more detail, SADD recommends the books by Isaac Todhunter and Karl Pearson, by Love and by Timoshenko (SADD, PDF p. 7).

How large are strains? A steel bar 2 m long that stretches by 1 mm has the strain

\[\varepsilon = \frac{0{,}001\ \text{m}}{2\ \text{m}} = 0{,}0005 ,\]

that is, five ten-thousandths. A rubber band stretched from 10 cm to 15 cm has the strain \(5\ \text{cm} / 10\ \text{cm} = 0{,}5\), a thousand times more. For structures one more number matters. If ordinary structural steel is stretched by more than about one thousandth of its length, it does not return to its original length when we let go, but stays permanently longer (we call such a change plastic, and a change that disappears on unloading elastic). This bound follows from two numbers for mild steel in BOWER. The force per unit area at which the steel begins to flow permanently is \(220\ \text{MPa}\) (Table 3.9, PDF p. 156). The force per unit area that would stretch the steel by its own length, if proportionality held up to there, is \(196\ \text{GPa}\) (Table 3.1, PDF p. 100). Their ratio is

\[\frac{220\ \text{MPa}}{196\ \text{GPa}} = \frac{220 \cdot 10^{6}}{196 \cdot 10^{9}} = 0{,}0011 ,\]

that is, about one thousandth. Why exactly this ratio is the bound we will see once we know how the strain of a bar depends on the force that pulls it. So the strains in steel structures are almost always smaller than \(0{,}001\): a structure that stretched permanently would no longer serve its purpose. This is one of the most important facts in this book, because many simplifications will rest on it.

Let us draw the strains on one axis. The horizontal axis is the strain \(\varepsilon\) and it is logarithmic: every step between the marks means a ten times larger value, from \(10^{-4}\) to \(10^{0} = 1\). So numbers that differ by a factor of a thousand are visible in the same figure. The blue dot at \(0{,}0005\) is the steel bar. The grey dashed line at \(0{,}001\) is the bound above which steel does not return to its original length after unloading. The orange dot at \(0{,}5\) is the rubber band. The orange bracket below the axis says that the band is stretched a thousand times more than the bar.

The number \(\Delta L / L\) is not the only measure of stretching, only the simplest. Books use others too, and we will meet them when we need them. More measures exist because for large deformations it is not self-evident how to compare the new length with the old one. The stretch (razmerje dolžin) \(\lambda = (L + \Delta L)/L\) compares the new length with the old one and equals \(1 + \varepsilon\). The Green-Lagrange strain \(\big((L + \Delta L)^2 - L^2\big)/(2L^2)\) compares the squares of the lengths and equals \(\varepsilon + \varepsilon^2/2\) (CM (3.4.15), PDF p. 131). The logarithmic strain is the logarithm of the ratio \((L + \Delta L)/L\) (BOWER, PDF p. 589). We compute the Green-Lagrange strain step by step, with \(\lambda = 1 + \varepsilon\):

\[\frac{(L + \Delta L)^2 - L^2}{2L^2} = \frac{\lambda^2 - 1}{2} = \frac{(1 + \varepsilon)^2 - 1}{2} = \frac{2\varepsilon + \varepsilon^2}{2} = \varepsilon + \frac{\varepsilon^2}{2} .\]

The horizontal axis is the strain \(\varepsilon\), from \(0\) to \(0{,}5\), where a grey dashed line is drawn. The vertical axis is the value of the measure. It has no numbers, because we are interested in how the curves separate, and the values at \(0{,}5\) are in the table. The blackwhite line is the strain \(\varepsilon\) itself. The orange curve is the Green-Lagrange strain \(\varepsilon + \varepsilon^2/2\). It lies above the line, because \(\varepsilon^2/2\) is positive: at \(\varepsilon = 0{,}5\) the value is \(0{,}625\) instead of \(0{,}5\). The blue curve is the logarithmic strain \(\ln(1 + \varepsilon)\). It lies below the line, because \(\ln(1 + \varepsilon)\) is smaller than \(\varepsilon\) (table above): at \(\varepsilon = 0{,}5\) the value is \(0{,}405\) instead of \(0{,}5\). The line is not at an angle of 45 degrees, because the scales of the axes differ: the length that means one unit on the vertical axis is smaller than the length that means one unit on the horizontal one. So from the figure we read only the shape of the curves, the values are in the table. For small \(\varepsilon\) the curves almost coincide, for large ones they separate. At \(\varepsilon = 0{,}001\) the measures differ from \(\varepsilon\) by half a per mille of its value.

So for small strains all the measures give almost the same number, for large strains they differ a lot, and then we must say which measure we use. Until we say otherwise, the strain of a bar means \(\Delta L / L\).

The formula \(\varepsilon = \Delta L / L\) has a hidden assumption. When we divided the longer bar into two halves, we assumed that both stretch the same. More than that, we assumed that every centimetre of the bar stretches the same as every other. What if this is not so?
