Chapter 1 Strain

1.1 A bar that stretches

The simplest case is a straight bar (palica), a body that is much longer than it is thick. If we cut the bar perpendicular to its length, we get a face called a cross section (prečni prerez). In figure 1.4 the bar is cut in the mind into a left piece and a right piece. The pieces are drawn apart so that the cross section can be seen, but the bar is in reality whole. The blue face on the left piece is the cross section, and the blue dot on it is its centre. The cut face of the right piece looks to the left, so we do not see it in the figure. The axis of the bar is the straight line along the bar through the centres of its cross sections, in the figure grey dash-dot. We pull the bar at both ends along its axis. The black arrows point away from the bar, because we pull it, and they lie on the axis, because we pull along it. The forces that act on a body are called its loading (obremenitev), so we speak of the bar before loading and after it. Before loading it has length \(L\). When we pull it, it has length \(L + \Delta L\). The number \(\Delta L\) is the elongation (raztezek) of the bar, that is, the change of its length. In this book the Greek letter \(\Delta\) (delta) always means a change: we read \(\Delta L\) as "the change of the length \(L\)". If we compress the bar, it gets shorter, and the elongation is negative.

Is the elongation a good measure of how strongly the material of the bar is stretched? Take two bars of the same steel with the same cross section, one 1 m long and the other 2 m, and pull them with the same force. The longer one stretches twice as much. This is no surprise. We can think of the longer bar as two shorter ones placed end to end (figure 1.5). The force that pulls the longer bar pulls each of the two halves, so each half stretches as much as the short bar, and together they stretch twice as much. But the material in the two bars is loaded the same: every centimetre of the longer bar stretches exactly as much as every centimetre of the shorter one. So the elongation does not tell us how hard the material is loaded. Mostly it tells us how long the bar is.

Let us first draw the shorter bar. The hatched line on the left is a wall. It only marks the end at rest. The wall holds it with the same force \(F\) with which we would pull it, so the elongation is the same as when pulling both ends. At the top is the black bar, the shorter bar before loading. Below it is the orange bar, the same bar after loading. The orange arrow points away from the wall, because we pull the free end. The distance \(L\) is the length of the bar before loading. The grey vertical line and the distance \(\Delta L\) show by how much the free end has moved. The elongation is enlarged in the figure so that it can be seen.

Below it is the bar of twice the length. The black bar is before loading. The grey dotted line in its middle divides it into two halves, each of length \(L\) and \(L\), like the shorter bar. At the bottom is the orange bar after loading, with the same orange force. Its orange dotted line is the previous middle, now moved. Each half is now \(L + \Delta L\) long and \(L + \Delta L\). The whole bar is longer by \(2\,\Delta L\).

What matters for the material is the stretch of every centimetre, of every metre, that is, the elongation per unit length. We get it by dividing the elongation by the length.

Definition 1.2 Strain of a bar (deformacija palice)

A bar with initial length \(L\) that stretches by \(\Delta L\) has the strain (deformacija)

\[\varepsilon = \frac{\Delta L}{L} . \tag{1.1}\]

Source: BARBER §1.2.1 (PDF p. 32), EP Example 2.3.1(d) (p. 60), SADD §2.2 (PDF p. 42).

In everyday language "deformation" means a change of shape. In mechanics the number that measures this change is called the strain. Strain is a ratio of two lengths, so it has no unit: the metres cancel. For the 1 m bar and the 2 m bar of our example we get the same strain, which is what we wanted. Other books also call this number the relative elongation or the specific strain, and often write it \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), where \(x\) is the direction of the axis of the bar.

Why two indices when there is only one direction? For a bar one would indeed be enough. In a body that is not a thin bar, however, we will describe the strain of the material around a point by several numbers, and each of them will be fixed by two directions, for example \(x\) and \(y\), shown in figure 1.6. When the two directions are the same, as in \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), the number measures how strongly the material is stretched in that direction. When they differ, as in \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\), the number describes how the right angle between the directions \(x\) and \(y\) changes. A deck of cards pushed sideways at the top leans over: the cards keep their length, but the right angles in the side face of the deck become slanted (figure 1.6).

Let us draw the deck from the side. The axes \(x\) and \(y\) on the left show the directions: \(x\) horizontal, \(y\) vertical. The hatched line is the table on which the deck lies. The cards are stacked as thin rectangles, each exactly as long as every other. The side face of the deck is therefore a vertical line. The blue angle in the bottom left corner is the right angle between the directions \(x\) and \(y\). On the right the cards after the push are orange. The orange arrow points sideways, because we push the deck at the top. The top card moves most, the bottom one does not move, because it lies on the table, and each one in between moves a little more than the one below it. So the side face is slanted. Its approximation is the orange straight line through the bottom left corners of the cards. The axis labels are the same as before, so that we see that the angle between \(x\) and the side face has changed. The blue arc is this angle. It is smaller than a right angle, because the face has leaned toward \(x\).

So the index \(xx\) says that the number measures a change of length in the direction \(x\) and not a change of angle. We will meet these numbers properly when we describe strain in space (SADD §2.2, PDF pp. 41-46).

From history

Dividing the elongation by the original length seems obvious today, but it was not always so. In 1826, in the first printed edition of his book on strength of materials, Navier described a material by the ratio of the force per unit area of the cross section to the elongation per unit length (TIMO, PDF p. 81-82). The elongation per unit length is our strain (1.1). How he arrived at this ratio we will tell in section 2.2.

During his visits to the Venetian arsenal, Galileo Galilei noticed that larger things are weaker than smaller ones of the same shape. In his words, a small obelisk or column can be set up or laid down without danger, but very large ones collapse at the slightest push, and only because of their own weight (TIMO, PDF p. 19). To prove this, in his book published in 1638 he first took a bar that is pulled along its length, and found that the strength of the bar is proportional to the area of its cross section and does not depend on its length (TIMO, PDF p. 19). Why? A bar with twice the cross section is like two equal bars side by side, which share the force. A longer bar, however, is not weaker, because each of its cross sections is pulled by the same force as in a shorter bar, if we neglect the weight of the bar (section 1.2).

Then he built the same bar with one end into a wall, so that it stuck out horizontally, and loaded it at the free end (figure 1.7, left). The hatched vertical line on the left is a wall. From it sticks out the bar of length \(L\) and depth \(d\). It is drawn grey dashed, because this is its position before it breaks. It breaks, he wrote, at the edge of the wall \(B\), around which the bar turns like a lever around its support (TIMO, PDF p. 19-20). In orange is the bar after the break, turned about \(B\). The turn is greatly exaggerated, so that we see about which point the bar turns. At the free end of the broken bar is the force \(F\), which pulls this end down. So \(B\) is the lower edge of the section at the wall. When the free end goes down, the upper part of the bar moves away from the wall, and the lower edge stays against it.

The bar breaks off at the section at the wall, which is the blue vertical line. Galileo assumed that at the break the whole section resists equally (TIMO, PDF p. 20). So the blue arrows are all equally long. They point toward the wall, because the section holds the bar at the wall while the force breaks it off from the wall. A force tends to break the bar off the more strongly, the larger it is and the farther from the edge it acts, as with a lever. The resistance is similar. Every part of the section resists equally, and their distances from the edge \(B\) are spread evenly from \(0\) to the depth \(d\), on average \(d/2\). So the whole section resists as if all the resistance acted on an arm \(d/2\) from the edge. The arm is drawn to the left of the wall, from \(B\) to the middle of the section.

Galileo enlarged the bar in his mind by the same factor in all dimensions, say twice (figure 1.7, right). This is the right bar, built into the wall at the edge \(B\) like the left one. Now only its own weight breaks it, which we count as one force at the middle of the bar. We compare it with the left bar, which would also be broken only by its own weight, at its middle, at a distance \(L/2\) from the edge. Beside each quantity of the right bar is written how many times larger it is than on the left. The weight becomes eight times larger (\(\times 8\)), because the bar is twice as long, twice as wide and twice as high. It acts at a distance \(L\) from the edge, that is, twice as far (\(\times 2\)). The area of the section, the blue line at the wall, becomes four times larger (\(\times 4\)), because the width and the depth of the section are twice as large. The blue arrows are again the resistance of the section. The arm of the resistance becomes twice as large (\(\times 2\)) (TIMO, PDF p. 21). The larger bar compared with the smaller:

\[\begin{aligned} \text{odlamljanje} &= \text{teža} \cdot \text{razdalja} = 8 \cdot 2 = 16,\\ \text{upor} &= \text{ploščina} \cdot \text{ročica} = 4 \cdot 2 = 8. \end{aligned}\]

So the breaking off grows twice as much as the resistance. A larger bar is therefore weaker, and a big enough one breaks already under its own weight (TIMO, PDF p. 21). So in his view ships, palaces and temples of any size cannot be built (TIMO, PDF p. 21).

In 1678 Robert Hooke published a treatise on a spring, the first published treatise on how materials change their shape under a force and return to their original shape when unloaded (TIMO, PDF p. 26). He fixed the upper end of a wire 20 to 40 feet long, that is, about 6 to 12 m, to a nail, and hung a pan for weights from its lower end. He put weights into the pan and measured by how much the wire stretched (figure 1.8). The elongations were always in the same ratio as the weights that caused them (TIMO, PDF p. 26, 28). From such experiments with wires, springs and wood he concluded that the change of shape is proportional to the force (SADD, PDF p. 7, TIMO, PDF p. 28). When bending a strip of wood he also noticed that the fibres (vlakna), thin threads along which the wood is built, are stretched on the convex side and compressed on the concave side (figure 1.9, view (1)) (TIMO, PDF p. 28). A stretched fibre is in tension (nateg), a compressed one in compression. Mariotte corrected Galileo's calculation, because he took into account that the fibres stretch. From his experiments in tension he found that for all the materials tested the elongations are proportional to the forces (TIMO, PDF p. 29). He assumed that at the break the bar turns about the lower edge of the section (the point \(D\) in views (2) to (4) of the same figure), as with Galileo (TIMO, PDF p. 30). In a turn about an edge a fibre moves the more, the farther from the edge it is, so it gets longer by more. Since the elongations are proportional to the forces, the forces in the fibres are in the same ratio as their distances from the edge (view (3)) (TIMO, PDF p. 30). For a material that stretches in proportion to the force up to the break, the lower part of the section is compressed and the upper part stretched (view (4)) (TIMO, PDF p. 31). In a beam (tram), a horizontal bar loaded across its length, there are therefore fibres in tension and in compression in the same cross section.

Let us draw the experiment. The hatched lines at the top are the ceiling, the black dot on them is the nail to which the wire is fixed. The hatched line at the bottom is the floor. The figure shows the same wire three times. On the left hangs the wire with an empty pan, black, because this is the state without weights. The distance \(h\) is the distance from the bottom of the pan to the floor, which Hooke measured with compasses. It is drawn for all three wires. Two slanted strokes in the wire mean that the wire is in reality much longer, several metres, and in the figure we shorten it. In the middle there is one weight in the pan. The force \(F\) is its weight and points down, because weight pulls down. The orange wire and the orange pan show the state after loading. The grey dashed line is the position of the empty pan. The upper end of the wire is fixed, so the pan goes down by exactly as much as the wire stretches, and this is the elongation \(\Delta L_1\). So \(h\) is smaller. On the right there are two equal weights in the orange pan on the orange wire, so the force \(2F\) is twice as large. The elongation \(\Delta L_2\) is twice as large as \(\Delta L_1\), as Hooke measured: each weight stretches the wire by the same elongation. The elongations are greatly enlarged in the figure.

Let us draw view (1). The hatched line is a wall, into which a thin strip of wood is built. The grey dashed line is the upper edge and the free end of the strip as it is before loading. In orange is the strip after loading, bent downward. The bend is greatly exaggerated so that it can be seen. The force \(F\) acts on the free end and points down, because it pulls the end down. The upper blue arrows each point to their own side, because the fibres on the convex, upper side are stretched. The lower blue arrows point toward each other, because the fibres on the concave, lower side are compressed.

Views (2) to (4) show the section of the bar at the wall and the forces with which the fibres resist, in blue. The bar lies to the right of the wall and we do not see it in the views, only its section at the wall is drawn. An arrow toward the wall means pull, an arrow into the bar means push. In view (2), Galileo, the wall is hatched, the black vertical line is the section of depth \(d\) and the black dot \(D\) is the lower edge of the section, about which the bar turns. The blue arrows are equally long, because Galileo assumed equal resistance over the whole depth. They point toward the wall, so they pull. The grey vertical line joins their ends and shows that they are equally long.

In view (3), Mariotte, the wall and the section are as before and the dot \(D\) is again the lower edge. The arrows grow with the distance from \(D\): the lowest is the shortest, the highest the longest, because the elongations of the fibres, and therefore the forces in them, are proportional to the distance from the edge. The grey slanted line joins their ends and shows that they grow evenly. In view (4), the correction, the wall and the section are again the same. The black dot is the point where pull turns into push, drawn at the middle of the section only as an illustration. The upper arrows pull, because they point toward the wall, and the lower ones push, because they point into the bar. The grey slanted lines show that the arrows grow in proportion to the distance from this point.

So lengthening and shortening came from pulled wires and bent beams. The second kind of change came from sliding. In 1773 Charles-Augustin Coulomb presented to the French Academy a memoir that dealt, among other things, with why masonry pillars and the soil behind retaining walls fail (TIMO, PDF p. 55). He assumed that a pillar pressed from above breaks along an inclined plane, along which one piece slides over the other (figure 1.11, left). In his view the failure happens when the part of the force that lies in this plane becomes larger than the resistance of the material to sliding along it (TIMO, PDF p. 59). Why does a force that presses the pillar straight down have a part in an inclined plane at all? Let us cut the pillar in our mind along an inclined flat surface and look only at the upper piece (figure 1.10). We neglect the weight of the pillar. Then only the load, which pushes the upper piece down along the axis of the pillar, and the lower piece across the cut act on it. The upper piece is at rest, so the lower piece must push it up with a force of the same size, also along the axis. But a force along the axis is not perpendicular to the inclined cut. So we can split it into a part perpendicular to the cut and a part lying in the cut, although nobody pushes the pillar sideways. With the part in the cut the lower piece holds the upper one back from sliding down along the cut. If the material cannot carry that much, the upper piece slides, as on the left of figure 1.11.

The blue slanted line is the cut along which we cut the pillar in the mind. Only the upper piece is drawn, the lower piece is grey and dashed, because we do not consider it. The three arrows at the top are the load. They point down, because the load pushes the pillar down. The black vertical arrow at the point in the middle of the cut is the force with which the lower piece pushes the upper one. It points up, along the axis and opposite to the load, because the upper piece is at rest. The force is in reality spread over the whole cut, and it is drawn as one arrow at the middle. We split it into two parts. The blue arrow is the part perpendicular to the cut, and the blue dashed arrow is the part lying in the cut. We start the second arrow where the first ends, so the two parts add up to the black arrow. The grey mark at the point says that the first part is indeed perpendicular to the cut.

Coulomb compared the calculated force at which the upper piece slides with experiments on brick, and it agreed well with them (TIMO, PDF p. 59). The slide was resisted by the material sticking together across the plane, which we call cohesion (kohezija), and by friction (trenje) along it (TIMO, PDF p. 59). For the soil behind a retaining wall he likewise assumed that it slides along some flat plane (TIMO, PDF p. 69). In 1784 he determined how a wire resists twisting from the oscillations of a metal cylinder hung on it (TIMO, PDF p. 59), and in a twisted wire, too, parts of the material shift relative to each other, as we will see in chapter 16. So lengthening and shortening on one side and sliding on the other were at first two separate kinds of change that can lead to failure.

Why there are exactly two kinds was shown by Cauchy, who by the autumn of 1822 already had the idea of the force per unit area at a point of a body (SADD, PDF p. 7, LOVE, PDF p. 25). In doing so he generalised the notion of hydrostatic pressure (LOVE, PDF p. 25).

Let us return to the numbers with two indices by which we will describe the deformation of a material around a point: some measure changes of lengths, others changes of right angles, as in the deck of cards. Why there are exactly two kinds of change is shown by Cauchy's generalisation of hydrostatic pressure, with which the story ended. Let us see what such pressure is. Water at rest presses on every surface perpendicular to it, however the surface is tilted. Such a force per unit area is called pressure (tlak) (CM §8.1.1, PDF p. 384). Why is pressure perpendicular to the surface? A solid body can resist a force that tries to shift one part of it relative to another, but a fluid cannot. Under such a force a fluid deforms without end, that is, it flows (CM §8.1.1, PDF p. 384). If the force between the water and the surface were not perpendicular to the surface, it would also have a part lying in the surface, and this part would shift the water next to the surface along it. The water would flow. A fluid at rest therefore acts on a surface only with pressure, with no part lying in the surface (CM §8.1.1, PDF p. 384). Let us ask what happens if in a solid body the force can point in any direction. We cut the body in our minds with a flat surface and ask with what force per unit area one part of the body acts on the other across this surface (figure 1.11, right).

Let us first draw the left side. The hatched line is the ground. The black piece on it is the lower part of the masonry pillar, its slanted upper side is the surface along which the pillar breaks. The grey dashed line is the upper piece of the pillar as it was before the failure. The three arrows at the top are the load that presses the pillar, and they point down. The orange piece is the upper piece after the failure: it has slid down along the slanted surface, because the load pushes it down, and along the surface it can move only along it. The slide is enlarged in the figure.

Now the right side. The left part of the body is drawn with a black line, the right part we have left out of consideration. The cut is the blue line. The point on the cut is where we observe the force. The force per unit area at this point has a size and a direction (black arrow). It is the force with which the right part acts on the left one. Every force can be split into a part perpendicular to the surface (blue arrow) and a part lying in the surface (blue dashed arrow). The grey mark at the end of the first part says that the two parts are perpendicular to each other: the first is perpendicular to the cut, so the second lies along the cut. The first part pulls or pushes, as in a bar that we pull. The second tries to shift one part of the body relative to the other, as in the deck of cards. With respect to a surface there are only two such parts, so there are also two kinds of failure. The change of shape is similar, as we will show in section 1.17. A short piece of material can change its length, and two pieces that were perpendicular can change the angle between them.

So the material close to a point can change its shape in two ways. Its lengths can change: in some direction the material gets longer when we pull it, or shorter when we push it together, which is called compression (stiskanje). Or the right angles between directions can change, as in the leaning deck, where the cards slide past each other. This is called shear (strig). Each of the numbers with two indices measures one of these two changes.

Let us draw all three cases. In each, the square is a small piece of material, black before the change, orange after it. On the left we pull the piece on both sides, so the arrows point away from it. The orange rectangle is wider than the square: the material has become longer in the direction of the pull. In the middle we compress the piece, so the arrows point toward it. The orange rectangle is narrower than the square: the material has become shorter in this direction. On the right the hatched line under the square means that the bottom side stays at rest. The arrow points sideways, because we push the piece sideways at the top. The orange parallelogram is the piece after the push. The horizontal sides keep their length, the top one has moved to the right. The grey mark in the bottom left corner of the black square is the right angle before the push, the orange arc is this angle after the push. It is smaller than a right angle, because the left side has leaned to the right. The grey dashed diagonals of the square are equally long. After the push one diagonal is longer and the other shorter. The diagonals are drawn because they show that the same shear in slanted directions means stretching and compression.

Definition 1.3 Normal and shear strain (normalna in strižna deformacija)

A normal strain (normalna deformacija) in a direction is the change of length of a short piece of material lying in that direction, divided by its original length. It is positive when the material gets longer and negative when it gets shorter. A shear strain (strižna deformacija) of two perpendicular directions is a number that describes how the right angle between them changes. It is positive when the right angle between the positive directions gets smaller (the positive directions are those in which the axes introduced in section 1.5.1 point). So this is only a description of what the shear strain measures. Which number exactly measures this change we settle in section 1.17.

Source: SADD §2.2 (PDF pp. 42-43).

From history

Cauchy's memoir was presented to the Academy in September 1822 but was not published. Its contents appeared in the 1827 volume of his Exercices de mathématique (LOVE, PDF p. 25). He showed that the force per unit area at a point is determined by six numbers. He described the change of shape by six numbers too (LOVE, PDF p. 25).

The six numbers that describe the change of shape at a point are three normal strains, one for each axis (one of them is in the figure), and three shear strains, one for each pair of axes (one of them is in the figure). Together they describe completely how the material close to a point is deformed, as long as the deformation is small (SADD §2.2, PDF p. 43). We will derive them when we describe strain in space, in section 1.17.

Which of the two changes we see depends on the directions we look along. In the leaning deck the cards keep their length, and only the right angle between the directions \(x\) and \(y\) changes. Let us look at the side face of the deck as a square with both diagonals (figure 1.12, right). The diagonal from one bottom corner to the opposite top corner gets longer after the push, and the other diagonal gets shorter. Along these two directions the same deformation is stretching and compression. We will check this with numbers on a sheared cube in section 1.6.2. Cauchy also showed that at a point there are three perpendicular surfaces on which the force is perpendicular to them, with no part lying in the surface (LOVE, PDF p. 25). The same holds for the change of shape: for every small deformation there are three mutually perpendicular directions in which there is no shear at all, only stretching or compression (LOVE, PDF p. 25, SADD §2.4, PDF p. 49). We will meet them in definition 2.62. We will prove that such directions exist in chapter 2, in section 2.11.4.

We will meet Cauchy's cut in chapter 2, in section 2.5, the split of the force into two parts in definition 2.18 and the surfaces without shear in section 2.11. Karl Culmann (CAJORI, PDF p. 312) in 1866 (TIMO, PDF p. 224) and Otto Mohr later represented the force per unit area at a point by a circle, which engineers still draw today (SADD, PDF p. 71), and we tell their story in section 2.12.3. For the history in more detail, SADD recommends the books by Isaac Todhunter and Karl Pearson, by Love and by Timoshenko (SADD, PDF p. 7).

How large are strains? A steel bar 2 m long that stretches by 1 mm has the strain

\[\varepsilon = \frac{0{,}001\ \text{m}}{2\ \text{m}} = 0{,}0005 ,\]

five ten-thousandths. A rubber band stretched from 10 cm to 15 cm has the strain \(5\ \text{cm} / 10\ \text{cm} = 0{,}5\), a thousand times more. For structures one more number matters. If ordinary structural steel is stretched by more than about one thousandth of its length, it does not return to its original length when we let go, but stays permanently longer. This bound follows from the data for steel in BOWER (Tables 3.1 and 3.9, PDF pp. 100 and 156). We will see how to compute it once we know how the strain of a bar depends on the force that pulls it. So the strains in steel structures are almost always smaller than \(0{,}001\): a structure that stretched permanently would no longer serve its purpose. This is one of the most important facts in this book, because many simplifications will rest on it.

The strain has the sign of the elongation \(\Delta L\): positive means lengthening, negative shortening.

The number \(\Delta L / L\) is not the only measure of stretching, only the simplest. Books use others too, and we will meet them when we need them. More measures exist because for large deformations it is not self-evident how to compare the new length with the old one. The stretch (razmerje dolžin) \(\lambda = (L + \Delta L)/L\) compares the new length with the old one and equals \(1 + \varepsilon\). The Green-Lagrange strain \(\big((L + \Delta L)^2 - L^2\big)/(2L^2)\) compares the squares of the lengths and equals \(\varepsilon + \varepsilon^2/2\) (CM (3.4.15), PDF p. 131). The logarithmic strain is the logarithm of the ratio \((L + \Delta L)/L\) (BOWER, PDF p. 589). The values are shown by the table and by figure 1.13:

measure \(\varepsilon = 0{,}001\) (steel) \(\varepsilon = 0{,}5\) (rubber band)
\(\varepsilon\) \(0{,}001\) \(0{,}5\)
stretch \(\lambda = 1 + \varepsilon\) \(1{,}001\) \(1{,}5\)
Green-Lagrange \(0{,}0010005\) \(0{,}625\)

The horizontal axis is the strain \(\varepsilon\), from \(0\) to \(0{,}5\), where a grey dashed line is drawn. The vertical axis is the value of the measure. It has no numbers, because we are interested in how the curves separate, and the values at \(0{,}5\) are in the table. The black line is the strain \(\varepsilon\) itself. The orange curve is the Green-Lagrange strain \(\varepsilon + \varepsilon^2/2\). It lies above the line, because \(\varepsilon^2/2\) is positive: at \(\varepsilon = 0{,}5\) the value is \(0{,}625\) instead of \(0{,}5\). The blue curve is the logarithmic strain \(\ln(1 + \varepsilon)\). It lies below the line, because \(\ln(1 + \varepsilon)\) is smaller than \(\varepsilon\) (table above): at \(\varepsilon = 0{,}5\) the value is \(0{,}405\) instead of \(0{,}5\). The line is not at an angle of 45 degrees, because the unit on the vertical axis is smaller than on the horizontal one. For small \(\varepsilon\) the curves almost coincide, for large \(\varepsilon\) they separate. At \(\varepsilon = 0{,}001\) the measures differ from \(\varepsilon\) by half a per mille of its value.

So for small strains all the measures give almost the same number, for large strains they differ a lot, and then we must say which measure we use. Until we say otherwise, the strain of a bar means \(\Delta L / L\).

The formula \(\varepsilon = \Delta L / L\) has a hidden assumption. When we divided the longer bar into two halves, we assumed that both stretch the same. More than that, we assumed that every centimetre of the bar stretches the same as every other. What if this is not so?
