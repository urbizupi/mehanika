Chapter 9 Vibration of a system with one degree of freedom (nihanje sistema z eno prostostno stopnjo)

9.13 Nonlinear vibration with one degree of freedom

Everything in this chapter so far rests on one assumption: the force that returns the mass to equilibrium is proportional to the displacement. Two properties follow from it, and we used them all the time. The frequency of the free vibration does not depend on the amplitude (theorem 8.138), and the responses to two forces add (lemma 9.6). The pendulum has already shown that the first property fails at large angles: at the amplitude \(90^\circ\) the period is \(18\ \%\) longer than the linear one (theorem 8.139). In this section we ask three things. What happens to the frequency when the restoring force grows faster or slower than the displacement? How do we compute this frequency and the shape of the vibration with a formula and not only with an integral? And what is left of the resonance curve when such a system is driven by a harmonic force?

First an example in which such a force arises by itself. Figure 9.162, left, shows a mass \(m\) in the middle of a taut string between two walls, each half of the string has the length \(L\). Before the displacement the string is straight and carries the force \(T_0\). We displace the mass sideways by \(u\). Each half of the string is now \(\sqrt{L^2 + u^2}\) long, so it has lengthened by \(\delta = \sqrt{L^2 + u^2} - L\). The string is linearly elastic with the modulus \(E\) and the cross section \(A_\mathrm{s}\), so the force in it rises to \(T = T_0 + E A_\mathrm{s}\,\delta/L\). The forces \(T\) of the two halves are inclined by the angle \(\theta\) with \(\sin\theta = u/\sqrt{L^2 + u^2}\), and their transverse components pull the mass back. Newton's second law for the mass gives

\[m\ddot u + 2\Big(T_0 + \frac{E A_\mathrm{s}}{L}\big(\sqrt{L^2 + u^2} - L\big)\Big)\frac{u}{\sqrt{L^2 + u^2}} = 0 .\]

This is equation CK (6.62) (PDF p. 190) without the external force. The restoring force is not proportional to \(u\) for two reasons at once: with the displacement the force in the string grows and the slope of the string grows. For \(u\) small against \(L\) we expand the restoring force in powers of \(u\) (SymPy):

\[2T\sin\theta = \frac{2T_0}{L}\,u + \frac{E A_\mathrm{s} - T_0}{L^3}\,u^3 - \frac{3\,(E A_\mathrm{s} - T_0)}{4L^5}\,u^5 + \dots\]

We keep the first two terms. These are two assumptions. The first: \(u/L\) is small. The fifth order stands to the third in the ratio \(\tfrac34(u/L)^2\), at \(u/L = 0{,}04\) this is \(0{,}12\ \%\). At \(u/L = 0{,}5\) it would be \(19\ \%\) and two terms would not be enough. The second: in the coefficient of the cubic term CK (6.64) (PDF p. 191) writes \(E A_\mathrm{s}\) instead of \(E A_\mathrm{s} - T_0\). The difference is the ratio \(T_0/(E A_\mathrm{s})\), the strain with which the string is pretensioned. For a steel string it is of the order \(0{,}001\), so the coefficient changes by \(0{,}1\ \%\). When we divide the equation by \(m\), we get a form that has its own name.

Definition 9.139 Duffing equation (Duffingova enačba)

Let \(\omega_0 > 0\) and \(\varepsilon\) be real numbers. The Duffing equation of free vibration for the function \(x(t)\) is

\[\ddot x + \omega_0^2\,x + \varepsilon\,x^3 = 0 . \tag{9.131}\]

The function \(g(x) = \omega_0^2 x + \varepsilon x^3\) is the restoring force per unit mass. For \(\varepsilon > 0\) we say that the system has a hardening spring (trda vzmet), for \(\varepsilon < 0\) a softening spring (mehka vzmet). For \(\varepsilon = 0\) the equation is linear with the natural circular frequency \(\omega_0\). For a displacement \(A\) the measure of nonlinearity (mera nelinearnosti) is the number without a unit

\[\mu = \frac{\varepsilon A^2}{\omega_0^2} ,\]

the ratio of the cubic part \(\varepsilon A^3\) to the linear part \(\omega_0^2 A\) of the restoring force at the displacement \(A\).

Source: CK, equation (6.64) and the text after it (PDF p. 191): the cubic term with a plus sign, hardening spring, and softening spring. FPGL, equation (2.136) (PDF p. 84): the same equation with damping and a harmonic force, under the name Duffing's equation. The name of the measure \(\mu\) is ours.

On the right side of figure 9.162 there are three such forces above the axis of the displacement \(x\). The linear force \(\omega_0^2 x\) is a straight line. The force of the hardening spring leaves it upwards, the spring is stiffer at a larger displacement. The force of the softening spring stays behind, below the line. At the displacement \(A_0\) the addition \(\varepsilon A_0^3\) is marked above the linear part \(\omega_0^2 A_0\), their ratio is \(\mu\). The names hardening and softening thus say what happens to the slope of the curve as the displacement grows. For the string \(\omega_0^2 = 2T_0/(mL)\) and \(\varepsilon = (E A_\mathrm{s} - T_0)/(mL^3)\), so \(\mu \approx (A/L)^2\big/\big(2T_0/(E A_\mathrm{s})\big)\). With the pretension \(T_0/(E A_\mathrm{s}) = 0{,}001\) and the displacement \(A/L = 0{,}04\) we get \(\mu = 0{,}79\): a displacement of four percent of the length already gives a cubic part that is four fifths of the linear one. The cause is that the linear part comes only from the small pretension, and the cubic one from the stretching of the string, which the large axial stiffness resists.

In the previous sections we wrote the natural circular frequency as \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\). Here we write \(\omega_0\), because it is the frequency of the vibration only in the limit of small amplitudes. Through the whole section we compute one example: \(\omega_0 = 2\pi\cdot 10\) 1/s, so \(f_0 = 10\) Hz and \(T_0 = 0{,}1\) s, the displacement \(x\) in cm, \(\varepsilon = \omega_0^2\sqrt{10} = 12\,484\) 1/(cm\(^2\) s\(^2\)) and the amplitude \(A_0 = 0{,}5\) cm. The measure of nonlinearity is \(\mu = \sqrt{10}\cdot 0{,}25 = 0{,}7906\), which is the blue curve and the addition in figure 9.162.

9.13.1 The period from the energy

We reach the period without solving the equation, on the path we walked for the pendulum. Figure 9.163, left, has the displacement \(x\) on the horizontal axis and the potential energy per unit mass \(V\) on the vertical one. The curve \(V(x) = \tfrac12\omega_0^2 x^2 + \tfrac14\varepsilon x^4\) is a well whose slope is the restoring force \(g = V'\). The dashed parabola \(\tfrac12\omega_0^2 x^2\) is the well of the linear system. The horizontal line is the energy \(E\) per unit mass, which does not change during the motion. It cuts the well at the turning points \(-A\) and \(A\), where the mass stops for an instant. At every displacement \(x\) in between, the distance from the well up to the line \(E\) is the kinetic energy \(\tfrac12\dot x^2\). So we know the speed at every \(x\), \(\dot x = \sqrt{2(E - V(x))}\). We divide the path from \(0\) to \(A\) into pieces of length \(h\). The mass passes the piece at the displacement \(x_{\mathrm{i}}\) in approximately the time \(h/\dot x\), that is \(h/\sqrt{2(E - V(x_{\mathrm{i}}))}\). The sum of these times is a Riemann sum, its limit is an integral, and this is the time of a quarter of the cycle.

The right side of the figure shows the same motion in time, for the example of this section. On the axes are the time and the displacement. The motion starts in the equilibrium position with the largest speed and moves between \(-A\) and \(A\). The first quarter, from the equilibrium position to the turning point, lasts \(t_{\mathrm{A}}\). The whole cycle lasts \(T = 4t_{\mathrm{A}}\). Two instants that are \(2t_{\mathrm{A}}\) apart have opposite displacements. The sinusoid of the linear system with the same amplitude lags behind, its cycle lasts \(T_0\) (the mark on the time axis). The next theorem proves these three claims for every odd restoring force, not only for the cubic one.

Theorem 9.140 Period in a symmetric potential well (nihajni čas v simetrični potencialni jami)

Let \(b > 0\) be a number or \(b = \infty\), \(g\) a continuously differentiable odd function on \((-b, b)\) with \(g(x) > 0\) for \(0 < x < b\), and \(V(x) = \int_0^x g(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\). Let \(x(t)\) be a motion of the equation \(\ddot x + g(x) = 0\), defined for every time \(t\), with \(x(0) = 0\) and \(\dot x(0) = v_0 > 0\), for which \(\dot x\) first becomes zero at the time \(t_{\mathrm{A}} > 0\) at the displacement \(A = x(t_{\mathrm{A}}) \in (0, b)\), which we call the amplitude. Let \(E = \tfrac12 v_0^2\). Then:

  1. For every \(t\), \(\tfrac12\dot x^2 + V(x) = E\), and \(V(A) = E\).
  2. The time of a quarter of the cycle is the limit
    \[t_{\mathrm{A}} = \lim_{\mathrm{a} \to \mathrm{A},\ \mathrm{a} < \mathrm{A}}\ \int_0^{a}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\sqrt{2\,(E - V(x))}} ,\]
    which we write for short as \(\int_0^A \mathrm{d}x\big/\sqrt{2(E - V(x))}\).
  3. For every \(t\), \(x(t + 2t_{\mathrm{A}}) = -x(t)\) and \(x(t + 4t_{\mathrm{A}}) = x(t)\). The motion repeats after the period
    \[T = 4\int_0^{A}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\sqrt{2\,(E - V(x))}} . \tag{9.132}\]
Proof

We must show that the energy is conserved, that the time to the turning point is the integral of the reciprocal of the speed, and that the quarters of the cycle mirror one another. The path is the same as in the proof of theorem 8.139, with \(g(x)\) in place of \(\sin\theta\).

  1. The function \(\tfrac12\dot x^2 + V(x)\) has the derivative \(\dot x\,\ddot x + g(x)\,\dot x = \dot x\,(\ddot x + g(x)) = 0\), where we used \(V' = g\) (theorem 3.55) and the chain rule. So it is constant (corollary 1.86). At \(t = 0\) it equals \(\tfrac12 v_0^2 + V(0) = E\). At \(t_{\mathrm{A}}\) we have \(\dot x = 0\), so \(V(A) = E\). In figure 9.163 this is the horizontal line \(E\), which cuts the well at the turning point.
  2. Since \(V' = g > 0\) on \((0, b)\), \(V\) is strictly increasing for \(0 \le x < b\) (theorem 1.85). So \(E - V(x) = V(A) - V(x) > 0\) for \(0 \le x < A\): the distance to the line \(E\) is positive everywhere before the turning point.
  3. The function \(\dot x\) is continuous, positive at \(0\) and has no zero on \([0, t_{\mathrm{A}})\), so it is positive there (theorem 2.79). The displacement \(x\) strictly increases on \([0, t_{\mathrm{A}}]\) from \(0\) to \(A\) (the first quarter), and by step 1, \(\dot x = \sqrt{2(E - V(x))}\). The inverse function \(t(x)\) is, by lemma 8.9, continuous on \([0, A]\) and differentiable on \((0, A)\) with the derivative \(t'(x) = 1/\dot x = 1\big/\sqrt{2(E - V(x))}\), with the values \(t(0) = 0\) and \(t(A) = t_{\mathrm{A}}\).
  4. Let \(0 < a < A\). By step 2 the function \(1/\sqrt{2(E - V(x))}\) is continuous on \([0, a]\), so \(I(a) = \int_0^a \mathrm{d}x\big/\sqrt{2(E - V(x))}\) has the derivative \(I'(a) = 1/\sqrt{2(E - V(a))} = t'(a)\) (theorem 3.55). The function \(t - I\) has the derivative zero on \((0, A)\) and equals zero at \(0\), so \(t(a) = I(a)\) for every \(a < A\) (corollary 1.86). As \(a \to A\), \(t(a) \to t(A) = t_{\mathrm{A}}\), because \(t\) is continuous. So the limit of point 2 exists and is \(t_{\mathrm{A}}\). The function under the integral grows beyond every bound as \(x \to A\), because the speed goes to zero, but the area under it stays finite.
  5. The equation \(\ddot x + g(x) = 0\) does not change if we replace \(x(t)\) by \(-x(-t)\), because \(g\) is odd, or by \(x(2t_{\mathrm{A}} - t)\), because it contains only the second derivative. The motion \(-x(-t)\) has at \(t = 0\) the state \((0, v_0)\), the same as \(x\). The motion \(x(2t_{\mathrm{A}} - t)\) has at \(t_{\mathrm{A}}\) the state \((A, 0)\), the same as \(x\). By corollary 8.108 on the uniqueness of the motion for a given state (\(g\) is continuously differentiable) both equal \(x(t)\): \(x(-t) = -x(t)\) and \(x(2t_{\mathrm{A}} - t) = x(t)\). Hence \(x(2t_{\mathrm{A}} + s) = x(-s) = -x(s)\), which is the pair of opposite displacements, and \(x(t + 4t_{\mathrm{A}}) = -x(t + 2t_{\mathrm{A}}) = x(t)\), which is the period \(4t_{\mathrm{A}}\).

Source: GR, example 1.7, (E1.7.f) to (E1.7.h) (PDF pp. 49-50), for the pendulum on the same path. The general form for an odd restoring force and the proof in steps are not from a source.

Where does the theorem use that the force is odd? Only in step 5. Without it, it would still hold that the time from the equilibrium position to the turning point is an integral, but the turning points on the left and on the right would not be equally far and the two halves of the cycle would not be mirror images. The assumption that the motion exists for every time and that the speed really turns at some time holds for the hardening spring for every initial speed, because the well grows beyond every bound. For the softening spring it holds only while \(E\) is below the rim of the well, as we shall see with the numbers.

For the Duffing equation the integral can be rearranged into a form without an infinity at the end, as for the pendulum.

Corollary 9.141 Period of the Duffing equation (nihajni čas Duffingove enačbe)

Under the conditions of theorem 9.140 let \(g(x) = \omega_0^2 x + \varepsilon x^3\) with \(\omega_0 > 0\), where \(b = \infty\) for \(\varepsilon \ge 0\) and \(b = \omega_0/\sqrt{-\varepsilon}\) for \(\varepsilon < 0\). Let \(\mu = \varepsilon A^2/\omega_0^2\) (then \(\mu > -1\)) and \(T_0 = 2\pi/\omega_0\). Then

\[T = \frac{4}{\omega_0}\int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\sqrt{1 + \tfrac12\,\mu\,(1 + \sin^2 u)}} , \tag{9.133}\]

and for \(\mu \ge 0\)

\[\frac{T_0}{\sqrt{1 + \mu}} \le T \le \frac{T_0}{\sqrt{1 + \mu/2}} .\]

So for \(\mu > 0\) we have \(T < T_0\), for \(\mu < 0\) we have \(T > T_0\), and for the hardening spring the period is the shorter, the larger the amplitude.

Proof

We must show that with \(x = A\sin u\) the integral of theorem 9.140 turns into the integral of a continuous function.

  1. Since \(V(x) = \tfrac12\omega_0^2 x^2 + \tfrac14\varepsilon x^4\) and \(E = V(A)\),
    \[2\,(E - V(x)) = \omega_0^2\,(A^2 - x^2) + \tfrac12\varepsilon\,(A^4 - x^4) = (A^2 - x^2)\Big(\omega_0^2 + \tfrac12\varepsilon\,(A^2 + x^2)\Big) .\]
  2. Let \(h\) be the inverse function of the sine on \([0, \pi/2]\) (step 4 of the proof of theorem 8.139) and \(u(x) = h(x/A)\) for \(x \in [0, A)\), so \(x = A\sin u\), \(u \in [0, \pi/2)\) and \(u'(x) = 1/(A\cos u)\). By step 1, \(2(E - V(x)) = A^2\cos^2 u\ \omega_0^2\big(1 + \tfrac12\mu(1 + \sin^2 u)\big)\).
  3. Let \(F(u) = \int_0^u \mathrm{d}v\big/\sqrt{1 + \tfrac12\mu(1 + \sin^2 v)}\). The expression under the root is at least \(1 + \mu/2\) for \(\mu \ge 0\) and at least \(1 + \mu\) for \(\mu < 0\), so positive, and \(F\) is differentiable (theorem 3.55). The function \(W(x) = F(u(x))/\omega_0\) has, by the chain rule, the derivative
    \[W'(x) = \frac{1}{\omega_0\sqrt{1 + \tfrac12\mu(1 + \sin^2 u)}}\cdot\frac{1}{A\cos u} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\,(E - V(x))}} ,\]
    equal to the derivative of the function \(t(x)\) of step 3 of the proof of theorem 9.140. Both are zero at \(0\), so \(t(x) = W(x)\) on \([0, A)\) (corollary 1.86). As \(x \to A\), \(u \to \pi/2\) and \(t_{\mathrm{A}} = F(\pi/2)/\omega_0\). With \(T = 4t_{\mathrm{A}}\) this is equation (9.133).
  4. For \(\mu \ge 0\) we have \(1 + \mu/2 \le 1 + \tfrac12\mu(1 + \sin^2 u) \le 1 + \mu\), so the function under the integral is between \(1/\sqrt{1 + \mu}\) and \(1/\sqrt{1 + \mu/2}\), and the integral over an interval of length \(\pi/2\) between \(\pi/(2\sqrt{1 + \mu})\) and \(\pi/(2\sqrt{1 + \mu/2})\). Multiplied by \(4/\omega_0\) these are the bounds of the corollary. For \(\mu < 0\) the function under the integral is larger than \(1\) and \(T > T_0\). At every \(u\) the function under the integral decreases with \(\mu\), and \(\mu = \varepsilon A^2/\omega_0^2\) grows with \(A\) for \(\varepsilon > 0\).

Source: The form of the integral and the proof are not from a source. The substitution \(x = A\sin u\) is the same as in GR (E1.7.g) (PDF p. 50) for the pendulum.

Why the cycle of the hardening spring is shorter can be seen in figure 9.163. At the same amplitude the well of the hardening spring is everywhere above the parabola, so the line \(E\) lies higher and the kinetic energy at every displacement is larger than in the linear system. Step 1 of the proof says by how much: by the factor \(1 + \tfrac12\mu(1 + x^2/A^2)\), which is \(1 + \mu/2\) at the equilibrium position and \(1 + \mu\) at the turning point. The mass is faster everywhere and the path is the same, so it arrives sooner. Hence also the bounds of the corollary. For the example with \(\mu = 0{,}7906\) the bounds are \(0{,}0747\) s and \(0{,}0847\) s, and the numerical integration of equation (9.133) gives

\[t_{\mathrm{A}} = 0{,}019866\ \text{s} , \qquad T = 0{,}079463\ \text{s} , \qquad f = 1/T = 12{,}584\ \text{Hz} .\]

This is the quarter of the cycle and the period on the right side of the figure. The frequency is \(25{,}8\ \%\) higher than \(f_0 = 10\) Hz, which the system would have at a small amplitude.

For small \(\mu\) we also get a formula. With \(1/\sqrt{1 + s} \approx 1 - s/2\) and \(\int_0^{\pi/2}(1 + \sin^2 u)\,\mathrm{d}u = 3\pi/4\),

\[T \approx \frac{4}{\omega_0}\Big(\frac{\pi}{2} - \frac{\mu}{4}\cdot\frac{3\pi}{4}\Big) = T_0\Big(1 - \frac{3\mu}{8}\Big) .\]

At \(A = 0{,}1\) cm we have \(\mu = 0{,}0316\) and the formula gives \(T = 0{,}098814\) s, the integral \(0{,}098836\) s. At \(\mu = 0{,}7906\) it gives \(0{,}0704\) s instead of \(0{,}0795\) s, so for our example it is no longer usable. We reach a better formula and the shape of the vibration on another path.

9.13.2 Expansion in a small parameter

The integral gives a number, but it does not say what shape the vibration has and which frequencies it contains. So we try to write the solution as a linear vibration with a correction, \(x = x_0 + \varepsilon x_1\), with the initial values \(x(0) = A_0\) and \(\dot x(0) = 0\). This is a perturbation expansion (razvoj po majhnem parametru): we insert into equation (9.131) and collect the terms without \(\varepsilon\) and the terms with \(\varepsilon\) to the first power.

  1. Without \(\varepsilon\): \(\ddot x_0 + \omega_0^2 x_0 = 0\) with \(x_0(0) = A_0\), \(\dot x_0(0) = 0\), so \(x_0 = A_0\cos\omega_0 t\).
  2. With \(\varepsilon\): \(\ddot x_1 + \omega_0^2 x_1 = -x_0^3 = -A_0^3\cos^3\omega_0 t\). The correction \(x_1\) is the response of the linear system to the force \(-x_0^3\).
  3. We write the third power of the cosine with cosines of multiple angles. From the addition theorem (corollary 8.16), \(\cos 2\tau = 2\cos^2\tau - 1\) and \(\sin 2\tau = 2\sin\tau\cos\tau\), so \(\cos 3\tau = \cos 2\tau\cos\tau - \sin 2\tau\sin\tau = (2\cos^2\tau - 1)\cos\tau - 2(1 - \cos^2\tau)\cos\tau = 4\cos^3\tau - 3\cos\tau\). Hence
    \[\cos^3\tau = \tfrac34\cos\tau + \tfrac14\cos 3\tau .\]
  4. The force on the right is therefore \(-\tfrac34 A_0^3\cos\omega_0 t - \tfrac14 A_0^3\cos 3\omega_0 t\). The second part has three times the frequency and gives the bounded response \(\dfrac{A_0^3}{32\,\omega_0^2}\cos 3\omega_0 t\), because \((-9\omega_0^2 + \omega_0^2)\cdot\dfrac{A_0^3}{32\omega_0^2} = -\dfrac{A_0^3}{4}\). The first part, however, has exactly the natural frequency of the linear system. This is the undamped resonance of section 9.6, and by theorem 9.67 the response is \(-\dfrac{3A_0^3}{8\,\omega_0}\,t\sin\omega_0 t\), which grows in proportion to time. With the initial values \(x_1(0) = 0\), \(\dot x_1(0) = 0\),
    \[x_1(t) = -\frac{3A_0^3}{8\,\omega_0}\,t\sin\omega_0 t + \frac{A_0^3}{32\,\omega_0^2}\big(\cos 3\omega_0 t - \cos\omega_0 t\big) .\]

The term with the factor \(t\) is called a secular term (sekularni člen), after the Latin saeculum, century: in celestial mechanics, where such an expansion arose, it is a term that shows only over centuries. Figure 9.164, top, shows what it does here, on the axes of the time in seconds and the displacement. The exact motion (time stepping) stays between the lines \(\pm A_0\), as point 1 of theorem 9.140 demands: the energy is conserved, so the displacement cannot exceed the amplitude. The expansion \(x_0 + \varepsilon x_1\), however, swings ever more strongly, inside the envelope \(A_0 + \dfrac{3\varepsilon A_0^3}{8\omega_0}\,t\). The envelope reaches \(2A_0\) after \(0{,}054\) s, that is after \(0{,}68\) of a cycle. After \(0{,}3\) s the expansion shows \(2{,}63\) cm instead of at most \(0{,}5\) cm. So the expansion is wrong already after a part of one cycle.

Why does a growing term arise? The exact motion has a frequency that is not \(\omega_0\), but we built the expansion from cosines with the frequency \(\omega_0\). Let us see how a vibration with the slightly higher frequency \(\omega_0 + \delta\) is written with the frequency \(\omega_0\):

\[\cos(\omega_0 + \delta)t = \cos\omega_0 t\,\cos\delta t - \sin\omega_0 t\,\sin\delta t \approx \cos\omega_0 t - \delta\,t\,\sin\omega_0 t ,\]

where the approximation holds while \(\delta t\) is small against \(1\). The secular term is thus the way in which an expansion with the wrong frequency writes a small shift of the frequency. A comparison with step 4 gives \(A_0\,\delta = 3\varepsilon A_0^3/(8\omega_0)\), so \(\delta = 3\mu\omega_0/8 = 18{,}6\) 1/s. The expansion holds only while \(\delta t \ll 1\), and \(\delta t = 1\) is exactly at \(t = 0{,}054\) s, where the envelope doubles the amplitude. So the growth is not a property of the system, but a consequence of measuring time with the wrong clock.

The remedy was proposed by Lindstedt and Poincaré: we leave the frequency \(\omega\) unknown and measure time with it. We introduce the stretched time \(\tau = \omega t\) and the function \(y\) with \(x(t) = y(\omega t)\), so \(\ddot x = \omega^2 y''\), where the prime means the derivative with respect to \(\tau\). We look for \(y\) with period \(2\pi\) in \(\tau\). Equation (9.131) becomes \(\omega^2 y'' + \omega_0^2 y + \varepsilon y^3 = 0\). Now we expand both, \(y = y_0 + \varepsilon y_1\) and \(\omega^2 = \omega_0^2 + \varepsilon k_1\), with an unknown number \(k_1\), and the initial values are \(y(0) = A\), \(y'(0) = 0\).

  1. Without \(\varepsilon\): \(\omega_0^2\,(y_0'' + y_0) = 0\), so \(y_0 = A\cos\tau\).
  2. With \(\varepsilon\): \(\omega_0^2\,(y_1'' + y_1) = -k_1\,y_0'' - y_0^3 = \big(k_1 A - \tfrac34 A^3\big)\cos\tau - \tfrac14 A^3\cos 3\tau\), where we used \(\cos^3\tau = \tfrac34\cos\tau + \tfrac14\cos 3\tau\).
  3. The term with \(\cos\tau\) on the right is again a force at the natural frequency of the equation \(y_1'' + y_1 = \dots\), and it would give the secular term \(\tau\sin\tau\). But now we have the free number \(k_1\). We choose it so that this force is absent:
    \[k_1 A - \tfrac34 A^3 = 0 , \qquad k_1 = \tfrac34 A^2 .\]
  4. What is left is \(\omega_0^2\,(y_1'' + y_1) = -\tfrac14 A^3\cos 3\tau\) with \(y_1(0) = 0\), \(y_1'(0) = 0\), which gives \(y_1 = \dfrac{A^3}{32\,\omega_0^2}\,(\cos 3\tau - \cos\tau)\). The part with \(\cos 3\tau\) is the response to the force, and the part with \(-\cos\tau\) is a solution of the equation without the force, which makes \(y_1(0) = 0\), so that the amplitude of the whole motion is exactly \(A\).

The result is a frequency that depends on the amplitude, and a motion with a third harmonic. What exactly we have proved by this is said by the theorem.

Theorem 9.142 The Lindstedt-Poincaré approximation for the Duffing equation (približek z raztegnjenim časom za Duffingovo enačbo)

Let \(\omega_0 > 0\), \(\varepsilon\) and \(A \ne 0\) be real numbers, \(\mu = \varepsilon A^2/\omega_0^2\), and let \(\omega_0^2 + \tfrac34\varepsilon A^2 > 0\). Let

\[\omega = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 + \tfrac34\,\varepsilon A^2} = \omega_0\sqrt{1 + \tfrac34\,\mu} , \tag{9.134}\]
\[x_{\mathrm{LP}}(t) = A\cos\omega t + \frac{\varepsilon A^3}{32\,\omega_0^2}\big(\cos 3\omega t - \cos\omega t\big) . \tag{9.135}\]

Then:

  1. \(x_{\mathrm{LP}}(0) = A\), \(\dot x_{\mathrm{LP}}(0) = 0\), the function \(x_{\mathrm{LP}}\) has the period \(2\pi/\omega\) and \(x_{\mathrm{LP}}(t + \pi/\omega) = -x_{\mathrm{LP}}(t)\).
  2. The function \(x_{\mathrm{LP}}\) satisfies the Duffing equation up to the residual \(R(t) = \ddot x_{\mathrm{LP}} + \omega_0^2 x_{\mathrm{LP}} + \varepsilon x_{\mathrm{LP}}^3\), for which
    \[|R(t)| \le \omega_0^2\,|A|\,\Big(\frac{27}{64}\,\mu^2 + \frac{3}{256}\,|\mu|^3 + \frac{1}{4096}\,\mu^4\Big)\]
    for every \(t\). The residual has no terms without \(\varepsilon\) and no terms with \(\varepsilon\) to the first power.
Proof

We must show that the terms up to the first power of \(\varepsilon\) cancel, and bound what is left. We write \(\tau = \omega t\), \(y_0 = A\cos\tau\), \(y_1 = \dfrac{A^3}{32\omega_0^2}(\cos 3\tau - \cos\tau)\) and \(k_1 = \tfrac34 A^2\), so \(x_{\mathrm{LP}} = y_0 + \varepsilon y_1\) and \(\omega^2 = \omega_0^2 + \varepsilon k_1\).

  1. Point 1: \(y_0(0) = A\), \(y_1(0) = 0\), the derivatives \(y_0' = -A\sin\tau\) and \(y_1' = \dfrac{A^3}{32\omega_0^2}(-3\sin 3\tau + \sin\tau)\) are zero at \(\tau = 0\). The period \(2\pi\) in \(\tau\) is the period \(2\pi/\omega\) in \(t\). Since \(\cos(\tau + \pi) = -\cos\tau\) and \(\cos(3\tau + 3\pi) = -\cos 3\tau\), both terms change sign under \(\tau \to \tau + \pi\).
  2. We insert and order by powers of \(\varepsilon\):
    \[R = \omega_0^2\,(y_0'' + y_0) + \varepsilon\big[\omega_0^2\,(y_1'' + y_1) + k_1 y_0'' + y_0^3\big] + \varepsilon^2\big[k_1 y_1'' + 3y_0^2 y_1\big] + 3\varepsilon^3 y_0 y_1^2 + \varepsilon^4 y_1^3 .\]
  3. The first bracket is zero, because \(y_0'' = -y_0\). In the second, \(\omega_0^2(y_1'' + y_1) = \dfrac{A^3}{32}\big(-9\cos 3\tau + \cos\tau + \cos 3\tau - \cos\tau\big) = -\tfrac14 A^3\cos 3\tau\) and \(k_1 y_0'' + y_0^3 = -\tfrac34 A^3\cos\tau + \tfrac34 A^3\cos\tau + \tfrac14 A^3\cos 3\tau = \tfrac14 A^3\cos 3\tau\). The sum is zero.
  4. Three terms are left. Since \(|\cos| \le 1\), we have \(|y_0| \le |A|\), \(|y_1| \le \dfrac{|A|^3}{16\,\omega_0^2}\) and \(|y_1''| = \dfrac{|A|^3}{32\omega_0^2}\,|-9\cos 3\tau + \cos\tau| \le \dfrac{10\,|A|^3}{32\,\omega_0^2}\). So
    \[\varepsilon^2\,\big|k_1 y_1'' + 3y_0^2 y_1\big| \le \varepsilon^2\Big(\frac34\cdot\frac{10}{32} + \frac{3}{16}\Big)\frac{|A|^5}{\omega_0^2} = \frac{27}{64}\,\mu^2\,\omega_0^2\,|A| ,\]
    and likewise \(3|\varepsilon|^3|y_0|\,y_1^2 \le \dfrac{3}{256}|\mu|^3\omega_0^2|A|\) and \(\varepsilon^4|y_1|^3 \le \dfrac{1}{4096}\mu^4\omega_0^2|A|\).

Source: The procedure of Lindstedt and Poincaré is in none of the books of the project. The derivation, the theorem and the bound of the residual were made here and checked with SymPy. The relation (9.134) between the frequency of the free vibration and the amplitude is also given by FPGL, equation (2.142) at zero force and without damping (PDF p. 85, problem 2.17(b), PDF pp. 93-94).

The theorem says that \(x_{\mathrm{LP}}\) almost solves the equation, and how much is missing. It does not say how close \(x_{\mathrm{LP}}\) is to the exact motion. For that we would need an estimate of how a small residual in the equation accumulates in the solution with time, and we do not prove this here. So we compare the approximation with the exact motion on numbers. The residual is measured exactly by the measure of nonlinearity \(\mu\): at \(\mu = 0{,}0316\) the bound is \(0{,}00042\ \omega_0^2 A\), at \(\mu = 0{,}7906\) it is \(0{,}27\ \omega_0^2 A\) (the actual largest residual is \(0{,}12\ \omega_0^2 A\)). For our example the approximation is therefore no longer very accurate, and it is interesting how much it still gets right.

Figure 9.164, bottom, shows the approximation with the stretched time by equation (9.135) over the exact motion. Both stay between \(\pm A_0\). In the first cycle they differ by at most \(0{,}012\) cm, after \(0{,}3\) s (that is \(3{,}8\) cycles) by \(0{,}036\) cm, because the small error in the frequency slowly drives them apart. The linear vibration \(A_0\cos\omega_0 t\) missed by \(1{,}0\) cm in the same time, that is by two amplitudes.

Equation (9.134) agrees with what the integral gave for small \(\mu\): from \(\omega = \omega_0\sqrt{1 + \tfrac34\mu} \approx \omega_0(1 + \tfrac38\mu)\) it follows that \(T \approx T_0(1 - \tfrac38\mu)\). Two paths, the energy and the expansion, give the same first correction. The expansion gives more: the shape of the vibration. It is not a pure cosine, it also contains a third harmonic.

Corollary 9.143 An odd restoring force gives odd harmonics only (liha povratna sila da le lihe harmonike)

Under the conditions of theorem 9.140 let \(T = 4t_{\mathrm{A}}\), \(\Omega = 2\pi/T\) and \(c_{\mathrm{n}} = \dfrac{1}{T}\int_0^T x(t)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}n\Omega t}\,\mathrm{d}t\) the complex Fourier coefficients of the motion \(x\) (lemma 9.122). Then \(c_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\) for every even integer \(n\), also for \(n = 0\).

Proof

We must show that the contributions of the two halves of the cycle cancel for even \(n\).

  1. We split the integral into \([0, T/2]\) and \([T/2, T]\). In the second we write \(t = s + T/2\). By point 3 of theorem 9.140, \(x(s + T/2) = -x(s)\) (the pair of opposite displacements), and \(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}n\Omega(s + T/2)} = \mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}n\pi}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}n\Omega s} = (-1)^n\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}n\Omega s}\).
  2. So \(c_{\mathrm{n}} = \dfrac{1 - (-1)^n}{T}\int_0^{T/2} x(s)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}n\Omega s}\,\mathrm{d}s\), which is zero for even \(n\).

Source: Not from a source. We used the same reasoning for the square-wave force in section 9.12.

The odd harmonics are thus a consequence of the symmetry of the well and not of the cubic form of the force. Every odd restoring force gives a motion that in the second half of the cycle is the mirror image of the first, and such a motion has no even harmonics. If the force is not odd, this fails. For \(\ddot x + \omega_0^2 x + \beta x^2 = 0\) with the same \(\omega_0\), \(\beta = \omega_0^2/2\) 1/(cm s\(^2\)) and \(x(0) = 0{,}5\) cm, time stepping gives a motion between \(-0{,}604\) cm and \(0{,}5\) cm, with the mean value \(-0{,}079\) cm and with the second harmonic \(0{,}027\) cm beside the first \(0{,}551\) cm.

9.13.3 A harmonic force: the bent resonance curve and the jump

Now we damp the system with the hardening spring and drive it with a harmonic force, as we did the linear one in section 9.6:

\[\ddot x + 2\zeta\omega_0\,\dot x + \omega_0^2\,x + \varepsilon\,x^3 = p_0\cos\Omega t , \qquad p_0 = \frac{Q_0}{m} . \tag{9.136}\]

Here \(p_0\) is the amplitude of the force per unit mass and \(\Omega\) the circular frequency of the force. In the lectures the damping is written as \(d = 2\zeta\omega_0\) and the force as \(F = Q_0/m\), both per unit mass, and the excitation frequency as \(\omega\). For the linear system the resonance curve had its peak at the natural frequency. Here the natural frequency is not one: by equation (9.134) it grows with the amplitude. A system that the force drives harder has a higher natural frequency, so the peak moves as it grows. We therefore expect a curve whose peak leans to the right. By how much is said by a calculation with one harmonic.

Theorem 9.145 Amplitude equation of the forced Duffing oscillator (amplitudna enačba vsiljenega Duffingovega nihanja)

Let \(\omega_0 > 0\), \(\zeta > 0\), \(\Omega > 0\), \(p_0 > 0\) and \(\varepsilon\) be real numbers, \(a > 0\) and \(\varphi\) real numbers and \(x_1(t) = a\cos(\Omega t - \varphi)\). The equality

\[\ddot x_1 + 2\zeta\omega_0\,\dot x_1 + \omega_0^2\,x_1 + \varepsilon\,x_1^3 - p_0\cos\Omega t = \tfrac14\,\varepsilon a^3\cos 3(\Omega t - \varphi)\]

holds for every \(t\) exactly when

\[\big(\omega_0^2 - \Omega^2 + \tfrac34\varepsilon a^2\big)\,a = p_0\cos\varphi , \qquad 2\zeta\omega_0\Omega\,a = p_0\sin\varphi . \tag{9.137}\]

Then the amplitude \(a\) satisfies the amplitude equation

\[a^2\Big[\big(\omega_0^2 - \Omega^2 + \tfrac34\varepsilon a^2\big)^2 + \big(2\zeta\omega_0\Omega\big)^2\Big] = p_0^2 . \tag{9.138}\]

Conversely, to every positive solution \(a\) of equation (9.138) there belongs exactly one \(\varphi \in (0, \pi)\) with which (9.137) holds.

Proof

We must show that the terms with \(\cos\theta\) and \(\sin\theta\), where \(\theta = \Omega t - \varphi\), cancel exactly under the conditions (9.137).

  1. The derivatives are \(\dot x_1 = -\Omega a\sin\theta\) and \(\ddot x_1 = -\Omega^2 a\cos\theta\). The third power is \(x_1^3 = a^3\big(\tfrac34\cos\theta + \tfrac14\cos 3\theta\big)\). We write the force with the same angle: \(\cos\Omega t = \cos(\theta + \varphi) = \cos\varphi\cos\theta - \sin\varphi\sin\theta\) (corollary 8.16).
  2. The left side of the equality is therefore
    \[\Big[\big(\omega_0^2 - \Omega^2 + \tfrac34\varepsilon a^2\big)a - p_0\cos\varphi\Big]\cos\theta + \Big[-2\zeta\omega_0\Omega\,a + p_0\sin\varphi\Big]\sin\theta + \tfrac14\varepsilon a^3\cos 3\theta .\]
  3. So the equality holds for every \(t\) exactly when \(B_1\cos\theta + B_2\sin\theta = 0\) for every \(\theta\), where \(B_1\) and \(B_2\) are the square brackets. At \(\theta = 0\) this gives \(B_1 = 0\), and at \(\theta = \pi/2\) it gives \(B_2 = 0\). These are the conditions (9.137).
  4. We square the conditions and add them. Since \(\cos^2\varphi + \sin^2\varphi = 1\), we get (9.138).
  5. Conversely: if \(a > 0\) satisfies (9.138), the point with the coordinates \(\big((\omega_0^2 - \Omega^2 + \tfrac34\varepsilon a^2)a/p_0,\ 2\zeta\omega_0\Omega a/p_0\big)\) is on the unit circle and has a positive second coordinate. To such a point there belongs exactly one angle \(\varphi \in (0, \pi)\).

Source: FPGL, equations (2.136) to (2.142) (PDF pp. 84-85), where the response is written as \(a\cos(\omega t + \phi)\), so with a phase angle of the opposite sign, and with the units of force (\(m\), \(c\), \(k\), \(h\), \(f_0\)). Our equation is (2.142) divided by \(m^2\). The statement as an equality with a residual and the proof of the converse are not from a source.

What does the theorem say and what not? The function \(a\cos(\Omega t - \varphi)\) does not solve equation (9.136). The term \(\tfrac14\varepsilon a^3\cos 3\theta\) is left, a force with three times the frequency, which a single cosine cannot balance. The calculation with one harmonic (harmonic balance) is thus an assumption: the third harmonic of the response is small against the first. It holds when \(\mu\) is moderate. In the example below the third harmonic is \(1{,}7\ \%\) of the first, and the amplitude equation misses the largest displacement by \(0{,}9\ \%\). It does not hold for responses that are not periodic with the period of the force. FPGL (PDF pp. 84-87) states that equation (9.136) can have such responses too. We do not treat them here.

We read equation (9.138) beside the linear one. At \(\varepsilon = 0\) we have \(a = p_0\big/\sqrt{(\omega_0^2 - \Omega^2)^2 + (2\zeta\omega_0\Omega)^2}\), which is \((Q_0/k)\,D(r)\) of theorem 9.62. At \(\varepsilon \ne 0\) the place of \(\omega_0^2\) is taken by the expression \(\omega_0^2 + \tfrac34\varepsilon a^2\), the square of the frequency of the free vibration with the amplitude \(a\) of equation (9.134). So the system responds as a linear one whose natural frequency depends on the amplitude with which it currently vibrates. The curve \(\Omega^2 = \omega_0^2 + \tfrac34\varepsilon a^2\) in the plane of the frequency and the amplitude is called the backbone curve (hrbtna krivulja). On it the first bracket in (9.138) is zero, \(\cos\varphi = 0\), the lag is \(90^\circ\) as at the linear resonance (theorem 9.66), and the amplitude is \(a = p_0/(2\zeta\omega_0\Omega)\).

For \(s = a^2\) equation (9.138) is an equation of the third degree. It has one or three positive solutions. This is the essential novelty: at the same force and the same frequency three different vibrations are possible.

Example 9.146 A frequency sweep up and down (prelet frekvence navzgor in navzdol)

The system is that of example 9.144, \(f_0 = 10\) Hz and \(\varepsilon = \omega_0^2\sqrt{10}\), with the damping \(\zeta = 0{,}03\) and the force \(p_0 = \omega_0^2\cdot 0{,}05\) cm \(= 197{,}4\) cm/s\(^2\). The system always ends on one of the two values and never on the middle one. Which one it chooses depends on the initial displacement and also on the initial velocity relative to the force, so the boundary is not simply the amplitude \(0{,}45\) cm: from rest at \(0{,}4\) cm the system already ends above. Three things follow from the example that are not possible in a linear system.

First, the steady state depends on the initial state. In a linear system the free motion dies out and one single steady state is left, whatever the start (theorem 9.62). Here there are two in the band between \(11{,}54\) and \(13{,}71\) Hz, and the history decides. That is why the sweep up and the sweep down give different curves, which close into a loop with the jumps. This loop is often called hysteresis (histereza). Elsewhere in this book the word hysteresis means something else, damping by the loss of energy in the material (section 20.3). Here the losses are not the cause: the loop arises because the system has two steady states.

Second, the response is not proportional to the force. At \(10\) Hz we multiplied the force by \(0{,}5\), \(1\), \(2\) and \(4\). The linear system would give \(0{,}417\), \(0{,}833\), \(1{,}667\) and \(3{,}333\) cm. Time stepping gives \(0{,}210\), \(0{,}273\), \(0{,}348\) and \(0{,}443\) cm: doubling the force increases the amplitude by only \(28\ \%\). The cause is again the backbone curve. A larger amplitude moves the natural frequency up, away from \(10\) Hz, and the system is no longer in resonance. So superposition (lemma 9.6) does not hold, because the term \(\varepsilon x^3\) does not map a sum to a sum.

Third, a nonlinear system has no single frequency response. The frequency response \(H(\Omega)\) of a linear system is the ratio of the response to the force, which does not depend on the size of the force. Here the curve of the amplitude is different for every size of the force and for every direction of the sweep. A measurement must therefore say at which force and how it was made. FPGL (PDF p. 84) defines the frequency response of a nonlinear system as the ratio of the response at the frequency of the force to the force, obtained with a slow sweep, and warns that there can be several solutions.
