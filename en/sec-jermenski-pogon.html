Chapter 26 Belts as axially moving continua

26.9 Belt drive

So far we had a single moving string stretched between two eyelets (section 26.1), and in the sections up to 26.8 we took it apart into modes, a critical speed and finite elements. A belt in a machine, however, runs over several wheels, which we call pulleys (jermenice) because the belt runs over them. What of the computed results survives when the string is built into such a drive, and what does the model leave out? The section answers the first question with a theorem proved from the earlier sections, and the second by stating what was left out and where it is treated in the literature.

From history

According to the source, the first to treat the belt drive from the point of view of axially moving continua was Aiken, in a paper of 1878 titled "An Account of Some Experiments on Rigidity Produced by Centrifugal Force" (CHEN2005, PDF p. 4 and 21). This is the view of this chapter: every free part of a belt is a moving string. What Aiken's experiments showed is on none of the pages read, so we tell nothing about it. Mahalingam used the moving string in 1957 for the transverse vibration of power transmission chains, excited at both eyelets with the same frequency and a phase difference (CHEN2005, PDF p. 2 and 21). WM1990 counts chains and belts in the same class of systems as magnetic tapes, aerial cable tramways and band saws (WM1990, PDF p. 1). Ulsoy and co-workers first treated, in 1985, the coupled vibration of a belt and tensioner system (napenjalnik), modelling the belt as a moving string. A tensioner is a pulley with a moving centre on an arm that turns about a fixed point (CHEN2005, PDF p. 4), and the name says that it keeps the belt tense. They compared their model with measurements on an automotive accessory drive and found four principal mechanisms of transverse belt instability (CHEN2005, PDF p. 15 and 24). We use these four mechanisms at the end of the section to sort out what the chapter has covered and what it has not.

The story showed that a drive is made of spans, each a moving string of its own, and that there are several kinds of instability. We now build the drive from what we already know and say along the way what is an assumption.

We draw it as on a board. First we take two equal pulleys with axes at \(O_1\) and \(O_2\), which stay at rest, and give them a radius \(R\). Round both we stretch a closed band, the belt: on each pulley it embraces half of the circumference, and between the pulleys it lies straight in two spans. A span is the free part of the belt between two pulleys, a piece of belt that touches neither of them. The point where a span leaves a pulley is an eyelet as in section 26.1: there the belt still lies on the pulley and turns with it, so it cannot move transversely. The distance between the eyelets of one span is \(L\), equal to the distance of the axes because the pulleys are equal.

A motor turns the left pulley, so we call it driving (gonilna), the right one is driven (gnana). It turns clockwise, and the belt does not slip on it: a point of the belt has the speed of the rim, \(v = \omega R\) with the angular velocity \(\omega\) of the pulley (definition 8.77). Slip would need a theory of friction, which is in no source of the book, so no slip is an assumption. The top span runs from the driving pulley to the driven one, the bottom span back, both with the transport speed \(v\) of section 26.1.

Each span is a string with its own tension, since the belt has a different span on each side of a pulley. What is the force at the end of a span? A span pulls the belt lying on the pulley with a force that lies on the tangent along the span and starts at the eyelet, like the force of a neighbouring part on a piece of the string (derivation 13.1). On the figure these are the force \(T_1\) in the bottom span and the force \(T_2\) in the top one. Why should they differ? If they were equal, the two forces on the pulley would be parallel, equal in size and in the same direction, one above the axis and one below it at the same distance \(R\). Their moments about the axis would therefore cancel: the belt could then pass nothing from pulley to pulley. We compute the moment of the belt on the pulley from the law of angular momentum for a piece of belt, as we used it for a piece of a beam in section 13.5.3.

Lemma 26.43 Torque of the belt on a pulley (moment jermena na jermenici)

Let a belt run with constant speed \(v\) without slip over the arc of a pulley with centre \(O\) and radius \(R\). The pulley then has the angular velocity \(\omega = v/R\). Let the span arriving on the pulley have the tension force \(T_-\) and the span leaving it \(T_+\). The moment with which the pulley acts on the belt, about the axis through \(O\), is in the direction of motion of the belt

\[M_{\mathrm{O}} = (T_- - T_+)\,R , \tag{26.55}\]

and the belt acts on the pulley with the opposite moment. The power the pulley hands to the belt is \(P = M_{\mathrm{O}}\,\omega = (T_- - T_+)\,v\).

Proof

We must show that the friction of the pulley on the belt carries the difference of the span forces. We apply the law of angular momentum to a piece of belt that is a fixed set of particles, as in section 26.1.

  1. The piece is the set of particles that at time \(t_0\) occupies the whole arc on the pulley and a length \(a > 0\) of each of the two spans. Its length \(s\) is constant because the belt does not stretch (idealization 3 below estimates the error of this assumption as \(4\cdot 10^{-4}\)).
  2. Every particle of the piece has the speed \(v\) in the same sense of turning, in a span along a line at distance \(R\) from \(O\), on the arc along a circle of radius \(R\). The angular momentum of a point mass \(m\) about \(O\) is \(\vek r\times m\vek v\), of size \(m\,v\,R\) on both. The angular momentum of the piece is therefore \(\mu\,v\,R\,s\) in the sense of turning and stays constant as long as the piece has this form. Its time derivative is zero.
  3. The forces of the two spans at the ends of the piece lie on the lines of the spans at distance \(R\) from \(O\), so by definition 2.32 the sizes of their moments are \(R\,T_-\) and \(R\,T_+\). \(T_-\) pulls the piece back, against the motion, \(T_+\) forward, so the total moment in the direction of motion is \(-R\,T_- + R\,T_+\).
  4. The normal forces of the pulley on the arc point along the radius, where \(\vek r\parallel\vek F\), so their vector product is zero (definition 2.32). The friction forces are tangential. Their total moment about \(O\) we denote \(M_{\mathrm{O}}\), in the direction of motion.
  5. The sum of the moments on the piece is the derivative of its angular momentum, which is zero by step 2: \(-R\,T_- + R\,T_+ + M_{\mathrm{O}} = 0\). Equation (26.55) follows.
  6. The friction forces are tangential. The sum of their magnitudes, taken with the sign of the direction of motion, is \(M_{\mathrm{O}}/R\), because each acts at distance \(R\) from \(O\). Each moves its particle with speed \(v\) along its own direction, so the power is \(P = (M_{\mathrm{O}}/R)\,v = M_{\mathrm{O}}\,\omega\). By Newton's third law the belt acts on the pulley with the opposite moment.

Source: The proof is not from the sources. The books we have contain no theory of belt friction (a search for "capstan" and "belt friction" in books/txt had no hits), so the lemma is our own computation from the law of angular momentum.

The lemma says that the span forces differ exactly when the pulley gives or takes power. At the driving pulley \(M_{\mathrm{O}} > 0\) because it acts on the belt in the direction of its motion, so the span arriving on it is more tense than the one leaving it: \(T_- > T_+\). On the figure 26.27 this is the bottom span with the force \(T_1\), which we call the tight span (napeti razpon), and the top one with the smaller force \(T_2\) the slack span (ohlapni razpon). The word slack says that the belt there is less tense than in the other span. At the driven pulley it is the other way round: the belt acts on the pulley, so \(M_{\mathrm{O}} < 0\) and the span leaving it is the more tense.

A test on numbers. We choose the steel band \(0{,}9\times 50\) mm of example A of section 26.1 with mass \(\mu = 0{,}3510\) kg/m for the drive of figure 26.27. The dimensions of the band, the pulley radius \(R = 0{,}1\) m, the axis distance \(L = 1\) m and the stresses are our choice, because none of the sources gives belt data. Let the tight span have the stress \(\sigma_1 = 100\) MPa and the slack one \(\sigma_2 = 20\) MPa. With the cross-section \(A = 4{,}5\cdot 10^{-5}\) m\(^2\) the forces are \(T_1 = 4500\) N and \(T_2 = 900\) N. The moment with the lever arm \(R\) is \((T_1 - T_2)R = 3600\ \text{N}\cdot 0{,}1\ \text{m} = 360\) N m. At the speed \(v = 40\) m/s we have \(\omega = v/R = 400\) 1/s, that is 3820 revolutions per minute, and the power is \((T_1 - T_2)\,v = 144\) kW (num-09.out, part A).

An example that fails the requirement of the lemma is a drive without load. If the belt is mounted with the same force in both spans, \(T_- = T_+ = 2700\) N, then by equation (26.55) \(M_{\mathrm{O}} = 0\) and \(P = 0\). The pulleys turn but the belt carries nothing. The lemma also requires constant speed: during start-up the angular momentum of the piece is not constant (step 2) and equation (26.55) does not hold.

Now we can write the model. We state it with six idealizations and give each a measure where we can compute one.

  1. The axes of the pulleys are fixed and the pulleys turn uniformly: the pulleys do not excite the belt's vibration and the belt does not act back on them. According to the source this holds for a drive with fixed-centre pulleys, and for a drive with a tensioner, a pulley with a moving centre, it is only an approximation (CHEN2005, PDF p. 4).
  2. The point where a span leaves a pulley is an eyelet: the displacement of the span there is zero. A wave in the span is therefore reflected completely by the pulley. According to the source a wave at an attached component is partly transmitted and partly reflected (CHEN2005, PDF p. 4). How much a real drive would deviate we cannot say, because the sources give no measure.
  3. The same belt runs with the same speed \(v\) in all spans. The mass flow \(\mu v\) is constant along the belt, and the mass per length at a strain \(\varepsilon\) is smaller by the factor \(1/(1+\varepsilon)\). By Hooke's law (theorem 3.11) the spans have the strains \(\sigma_1/E = 5{,}10\cdot 10^{-4}\) and \(\sigma_2/E = 1{,}02\cdot 10^{-4}\) (\(E = 196\) GPa). The speeds in the spans differ by the factor \((1+\varepsilon_1)/(1+\varepsilon_2) - 1 = 4{,}1\cdot 10^{-4}\), at \(v = 40\) m/s by \(0{,}016\) m/s.
  4. The force in each span is constant along the span (CHEN2005, PDF p. 2, assumption 2) and the slope is small, \(|w_{\mathrm{x}}| \le 0{,}01\), as in definition 13.2.
  5. The weight of the belt is neglected, so the spans in the figure are straight. For the slack span \(\mu g L = 3{,}44\) N, which is \(0{,}38\) % of \(T_2\). The sag of a uniformly loaded taut string at rest is \(\mu g L^2/(8T_2) = 0{,}48\) mm. The string then has the shape of the parabola \(w = -4d\,x(L - x)/L^2\) with the sag \(d\) at the middle, so the slope at the eyelet is \(4d/L = 4\cdot 0{,}48\cdot 10^{-3}/1 = 0{,}0019\). When the belt runs, we add the weight \(-\mu g\) on the right of equation (26.5). A shape that does not change in time satisfies \((T_2 - \mu v^2)\,w_{\mathrm{xx}} = \mu g\), so it is the same parabola with \(T_2 - \mu v^2\) in place of \(T_2\). At \(v = 40\) m/s we have \(T_2 - \mu v^2 = 338{,}4\) N, a sag of \(1{,}27\) mm and a slope at the eyelet of \(0{,}0051\), which is still below the bound \(0{,}01\).
  6. A span is a string, not a beam. By theorem 26.37 the bending stiffness of the belt raises the critical speed of a span by the factor that is the square root of one plus the ratio of the buckling force of the band to the force of the span. With hinged ends the buckling force of our band is \(5{,}876\) N (example of section 26.7). The critical speed of the tight span therefore rises by \(0{,}065\) % (\(5{,}876/4500 = 0{,}0013\)), that of the slack span by \(0{,}33\) % (\(5{,}876/900 = 0{,}0065\)). With clamped ends the buckling force is \(23{,}503\) N and the shifts are \(0{,}26\) % and \(1{,}3\) %. The speed is below the critical speed of every span, \(v < c_{\mathrm{j}}\) (definition 26.20).
Notation. \(v\) is the transport speed as in section 26.1 and is not the deflection \(v\) of chapter 5. We number the spans with \(j\), \(T_{\mathrm{j}}\) is the force of the \(j\)-th span, not a kinetic energy. We write power as \(P\), which is not the compressive force of a column. The order of an excitation is denoted \(m\), so that it does not meet the span index.
Definition 26.44 Belt drive of strings (jermenski pogon z razponi)

A belt drive of strings is a system of a belt of mass \(\mu\) per unit length that runs with constant speed \(v\) over pulleys with fixed axes, in which each of the spans \(j = 1, \dots, N\) is a moving string (definition 26.3) of length \(L_{\mathrm{j}}\) between its eyelets, tension force \(T_{\mathrm{j}}\) and transverse displacement \(w_{\mathrm{j}}(x, t)\), \(x \in [0, L_{\mathrm{j}}]\). The eyelets of a span are its contact points with the pulleys, \(w_{\mathrm{j}}(0, t) = w_{\mathrm{j}}(L_{\mathrm{j}}, t) = 0\). The model holds with idealizations 1 to 6 above.

Source: CHEN2005 §2.4.2 (PDF p. 4): the transverse vibration of belt spans is an example of a moving string, and so is Ulsoy and co-workers' model (CHEN2005, PDF p. 15). The name and the notation are ours, and so are the measures in idealizations 3 and 5.

Idealizations 1 and 2 are the reason why we can treat the spans each by itself: no span has an unknown or a condition that it shares with another. What does that mean for the drive?

Theorem 26.45 Spans of a belt drive and the critical belt speed (razponi pogona in kritična hitrost jermena)

Let a belt drive of strings be given by definition 26.44 and let \(c_{\mathrm{j}} = \sqrt{T_{\mathrm{j}}/\mu}\) be the wave speed of span \(j\) (definition 13.4). For a span \(j\) with \(v < c_{\mathrm{j}}\) the modes of the span have the natural angular frequencies

\[\omega_{\mathrm{j},\mathrm{n}} = \frac{n\pi c_{\mathrm{j}}}{L_{\mathrm{j}}}\left(1 - \frac{v^2}{c_{\mathrm{j}}^2}\right) , \qquad n = 1, 2, \dots , \tag{26.56}\]

which are all positive. The drive has all its spans subcritical exactly when \(v < c_{\min} = \sqrt{T_{\min}/\mu}\), where \(T_{\min}\) is the smallest of the span forces. When the belt speed increases from zero, the span with the smallest force, the slack span, is the first to reach the critical speed.

Proof

We must show that the spans do not affect each other, that they get their frequencies from section 26.3, and that the subcriticality condition singles out the slackest span.

  1. By definition 26.44 span \(j\) is a moving string with its own displacement \(w_{\mathrm{j}}\), length \(L_{\mathrm{j}}\) and force \(T_{\mathrm{j}}\), with the same \(\mu\) and \(v\) for all spans. Its condition at the eyelets contains only \(w_{\mathrm{j}}\). The problems of different spans have no common unknown and no common condition, so we solve them each by itself.
  2. For span \(j\) with \(v < c_{\mathrm{j}}\) theorem 26.13 with \(c = c_{\mathrm{j}}\) and \(L = L_{\mathrm{j}}\) gives the modes and the frequencies (26.56).
  3. The frequency (26.56) is positive exactly when \(1 - v^2/c_{\mathrm{j}}^2 > 0\), that is for \(v < c_{\mathrm{j}}\). At \(v = c_{\mathrm{j}}\) the span is critical by definition 26.20.
  4. The root \(\sqrt{T_{\mathrm{j}}/\mu}\) increases with \(T_{\mathrm{j}}\), so \(v < c_{\mathrm{j}}\) for every \(j\) exactly when \(v < \min_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{j}} = \sqrt{T_{\min}/\mu}\).
  5. When \(v\) increases from zero, the first span at which \(v\) reaches \(c_{\mathrm{j}}\) is the one with the smallest \(c_{\mathrm{j}}\), so with the smallest force \(T_{\mathrm{j}}\).

Source: The theorem is not from a source, it follows from theorem 26.13. The source says only that the belt critical speed is one of four mechanisms of transverse instability (CHEN2005, PDF p. 15).

The theorem says that a drive is judged by its slackest span. We test it on the drive of this section. We shall draw the lowest frequency of the two spans against the speed. First the axes: horizontally the speed \(v\) in m/s, vertically the frequency \(f = \omega/(2\pi)\) in Hz. By equation (26.56) the lowest frequency of a span is \(f_1^{(j)} = \dfrac{c_{\mathrm{j}}}{2L}\left(1 - v^2/c_{\mathrm{j}}^2\right)\), a parabola that falls from \(c_{\mathrm{j}}/(2L)\) at \(v = 0\) to zero at \(v = c_{\mathrm{j}}\). For the tight span (\(j = 1\)) we have \(c_1 = \sqrt{T_1/\mu} = 113{,}23\) m/s and \(f_1^{(1)}\) falls from \(56{,}61\) Hz, for the slack one (\(j = 2\)) \(c_2 = 50{,}64\) m/s and the frequency falls from \(25{,}32\) Hz. The speeds \(c_1\) and \(c_2\) are the zeros of the parabolas. For \(v = 20, 30, 40\) and \(50\) m/s we get (num-09.out, part B):

\(v\) (m/s) \(v/c_1\) \(f_1^{(1)}\) (Hz) \(v/c_2\) \(f_1^{(2)}\) (Hz)
0 0 56,61 0 25,32
20 0,177 54,85 0,395 21,37
30 0,265 52,64 0,592 16,43
40 0,353 49,55 0,790 9,52
50 0,442 45,57 0,987 0,63

At \(v = 40\) m/s both frequencies are positive: \(49{,}55\) Hz for the tight and \(9{,}52\) Hz for the slack span. Span \(j = 2\) is at \(v/c_2 = 0{,}790\) near its critical speed and has only \(38\) % of its frequency at rest (\(9{,}52/25{,}32 = 0{,}376\)), while the tight span at \(v/c_1 = 0{,}353\) has \(0{,}875\) of its frequency at rest. In the slack span the two waves travel with the speeds \(c_2 + v = 90{,}64\) m/s with the running belt and \(c_2 - v = 10{,}64\) m/s against it (since \(c_2 - v > 0\), the wave against the flow still travels, but slowly). The critical speed of the slack span is \(c_2 = \sqrt{\sigma_2/\rho} = 50{,}64\) m/s and does not depend on the cross-section of the band, because \(\mu\) cancels. At this speed the tight span still has \(45{,}29\) Hz and \(v^2/c_1^2 = \sigma_2/\sigma_1 = 0{,}2\).

An example that fails the test. Let the slack span have only \(10\) MPa, with the same stress in the tight span. Then the critical speed \(c_2\) moves from \(50{,}64\) to \(35{,}81\) m/s, to the left of the vertical line \(v = 40\) m/s, and \(v/c_2 = 1{,}117\). The span is supercritical, and the model with two fixed eyelets claims nothing there (section 26.4). The tight span is meanwhile far below its critical speed, \(v/c_1 = 0{,}353\), its parabola is high at \(v = 40\) m/s. A test with the mean stress \(55\) MPa would give \(c = 83{,}97\) m/s and would wrongly let the drive pass. The theorem judges by the smallest force, not by the mean. A span at \(15\) MPa is just below the limit: \(c = 43{,}85\) m/s, \(v/c = 0{,}912\) and \(f_1 = 3{,}68\) Hz (num-09.out, part C).

The next mechanism that the source knows for a span is belt resonance. Sack determined, according to the source, the natural frequencies of a moving string from the resonance relation, that is from the condition that the frequency of the excitation equals the natural one (CHEN2005, PDF p. 2). In a drive one cause of excitation is kinematic because the belt is closed. Whatever is fixed to the belt, for instance a joint, returns to the same pulley after one circuit. The length of the closed belt is \(\Lambda = 2L + 2\pi R\), two straight spans and two half-circles. For \(L = 1\) m and \(R = 0{,}1\) m we have \(\Lambda = 2{,}6283\) m, at \(v = 40\) m/s a circuit takes \(65{,}7\) ms and repeats with the frequency \(15{,}22\) Hz. That a joint really excites the span is our assumption. If we accept it, the condition for resonance of order \(m\) is

\[\frac{m\,v}{\Lambda} = \frac{c}{2L}\left(1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}\right) \iff \frac{2mL}{\Lambda}\,\frac{v}{c} = 1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2} , \tag{26.57}\]

where \(m\) is an integer and \(c\) the wave speed of the span. The right-hand form shows that the solution \(v/c = -mL/\Lambda + \sqrt{(mL/\Lambda)^2 + 1}\) does not depend on the force of the span. The grey lines \(m\,v/\Lambda\) in figure 26.28 grow with \(v\) and the parabola falls, so for each \(m\) there is exactly one solution. For \(m = 1\) we get it at \(v/c = 0{,}690\), that is at \(78{,}07\) m/s for the tight and \(34{,}91\) m/s for the slack span, for \(m = 2\) at \(v/c = 0{,}496\), that is \(56{,}12\) and \(25{,}10\) m/s (num-09.out, part F). In a start-up to \(40\) m/s the slack span crosses the solutions \(m = 1\) to \(4\) (\(m = 4\) at \(15{,}15\) m/s, \(m = 3\) at \(19{,}04\) m/s), the tight one of these only \(m = 4\) at \(33{,}87\) m/s, because \(m = 3\) comes only at \(42{,}58\) m/s. Solutions with \(m > 4\) are at still lower speeds, for \(m = 5\) at \(27{,}95\) m/s in the tight and \(12{,}50\) m/s in the slack span, so both spans cross them. What happens there the equation does not say: the response to excitation is beyond this chapter, the source treats it with a Green's function (WM1990, PDF p. 3 and 4), which we do not derive.

Let us finally see what the chapter has covered of the four mechanisms that Ulsoy and co-workers distinguish according to the source (CHEN2005, PDF p. 15).

mechanism what it means where in the chapter
tensioner resonance (resonanca napenjalnika) the tensioner, a pulley with a moving centre, vibrates as a rigid body, the spans are its springs not covered, idealization 1
belt resonance (resonanca jermena) the frequency of the excitation matches a natural frequency of a span section 26.3, this section
Mathieu instabilities from tension variation (Mathieujeva nestabilnost) the tension varies periodically, so a coefficient of the equation varies not covered, the source mentions it (CHEN2005, PDF p. 11)
belt critical speed (kritična hitrost jermena) \(v = c_{\mathrm{j}}\) of a span section 26.4, theorem 26.45

The name Mathieu is carried by the equation that the source gets when it inserts the variation \(P_1\cos\Omega t\) of the tension about a mean \(P_0\) into a model with one mode (CHEN2005, PDF p. 11). Where the name comes from is not in the sources we have, so we do not state the origin of the name. The excitation here is parametric (parametrično): a coefficient of the equation varies, not an external force. This goes beyond the chapter.

The model leaves out more. Nonlinear and viscoelastic strings and the coupling with the rotation of the pulleys are treated elsewhere according to the source (CHEN2005, PDF p. 4, 14 and 15). Friction between the belt and the pulley and the centrifugal contribution to the belt force on a pulley are in no source of the book. So we do not compute with slip, and lemma 26.43 does not need these things, since it uses only the span forces and the speed.

The section has shown that a belt drive with pulleys of fixed axes is a set of separate moving strings and that the critical speed of the drive belongs to the slackest span. The lemma on the moment explained where a slack span comes from at all, and the theorem gave the criterion, tested on an example that the drive passes and on an example that it does not. What goes beyond the model is listed with its sources, and the connections with other chapters are in section 26.10.
