Chapter 1 Strain

1.12 Mathematics: functions without jumps

In section 1.11 we chose from every bounded sequence a subsequence that converges (Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem 1.57). When in the next section we look for the point where a function is largest, the theorem will choose points that converge to some point \(c\), and the values of the function at them will have to approach the value at \(c\). This is how functions whose graph has no jumps behave. With the words "without jumps" we also described both facts to which we left the remainder of a short step in any direction at the end of section 1.7.3. The first fact is about a function of one variable that has a derivative at every point between two numbers and no jump at the end points. The second assumes that the partial derivatives of the displacement change without jumps at points near \(P\): at points close enough to \(P\) they differ only a little from those at \(P\). So far we have understood the words "without jumps" only by feel, as a graph that we draw without lifting the pencil. In the proofs ahead of us we cannot work with such a feeling. So we must say precisely what a function without jumps is.

The route is as follows. First we write the definition for functions of one variable, with an allowed error and a band, as for the limit, and we try it on the displacement of the hanging bar. Then we hang a load at the middle of the bar and look at how the force in its cross sections jumps there. Then we describe the same property with sequences, for which we need rules for computing with limits of sequences, and with this description we show that sums and products of functions without jumps have no jumps either. Finally we carry everything over to functions of a point in space, such as the partial derivatives of the displacement, and check the second fact for the hanging bar.

1.12.1 Continuous function

The second fact already says what we want: the values at points close enough to a given point differ only a little from the value at that point. This is almost the idea of the limit from section 1.3.3, where \(g(h)\) was as close to the number \(d\) as we liked, as long as \(h\) was close enough to zero. We write it precisely with the same two letters: the allowed error \(\eta\) for the values and the band \(\delta\) around the point. The requirement differs from the limit in three things. We compare the values with the value \(f(c)\) at the point \(c\) itself, so the function must be defined at \(c\). We do not leave out the point \(x = c\), because there the condition holds by itself, \(|f(c) - f(c)| = 0 < \eta\). And we look only at points where the function is defined.

From history

The idea of no jumps received its precise form only in the nineteenth century. Bolzano, whom we met in section 1.11.4, held advanced views on functions without jumps too (CAJORI, PDF p. 383). Cauchy, who wrote down the theory of such functions in his textbook Cours d'analyse (CAJORI, PDF p. 385), was moreover the first to feel that one must investigate when the Fourier infinite sum of sines and cosines (section 1.3.1) really represents the function from which it arose (CAJORI, PDF p. 391-392). Dirichlet found his way unsatisfactory (CAJORI, PDF p. 392). In the fourth volume of Crelle's journal he was the first to treat this question thoroughly. He showed that the sum gives the value of the function everywhere except at points where the function has a jump, provided that the function does not become infinite, does not have infinitely many jumps and does not have infinitely many greatest and least values (CAJORI, PDF p. 392). Jumps thus became part of a theorem and not only part of a picture.

The definition that follows writes the idea of a function without jumps with inequalities: with a permitted error \(\eta\) for the values and with a strip \(\delta\) around the point.

Definition 1.58 Continuity (zveznost)

Let \(D\) be a set of real numbers and \(f\) a function whose input is a number \(x\) from \(D\) and whose output is a number \(f(x)\). The function \(f\) is continuous (zvezna) at the point \(c \in D\) if for every \(\eta > 0\) there is a \(\delta > 0\) such that

\[|f(x) - f(c)| < \eta \quad \text{for every } x \in D \text{ with } |x - c| < \delta . \tag{1.26}\]

The function \(f\) is continuous on the set \(D\) if it is continuous at every point of the set \(D\). We call this property continuity (zveznost).

Source: LEBL1 Def. 3.2.1 (PDF p. 122), with \(\eta\) in place of \(\epsilon\) and \(D\) in place of \(S\).

So continuity is a property of a function at one point, and continuity on a set is this property at all its points. Condition (1.26) says that the values at all points of the band from \(c - \delta\) to \(c + \delta\) lie within the allowed error around \(f(c)\), as figure 1.82 shows on the left. In general the band depends on the error \(\eta\) and also on the point \(c\) (LEBL1, PDF p. 122).

Why only \(x \in D\)? The displacement of the hanging bar, \(u(x) = a\,(2Lx - x^2)\), is defined at the points of the bar, that is, on the closed interval \([0, L]\). At the top, at \(c = 0\), there is no material above the point. Numbers \(x < 0\) are not points of the bar, and the displacement \(u(x)\) has no meaning for them. So at \(c = 0\) condition (1.26) checks only points below the top, as figure 1.82 shows on the right for the left end point of the interval on which a function is defined. Without the words "\(x \in D\)" the condition would speak about values that do not exist. In this way continuity on a closed interval also covers the two end points, which we can approach from one side only.

At an interior point, around which the function is defined on both sides, continuity is the same as the limit from definition 1.5 being equal to the value at the point. We wrote the definition of the limit only at the point \(0\), so we move the point \(c\) to \(0\): instead of \(f(x)\) we look at \(f(c + h)\), where \(h = x - c\) is the offset from \(c\).

Lemma 1.59 Continuity and the limit (zveznost in limita)

Let the function \(f\) have a domain \(D\) that contains all numbers \(x\) with \(|x - c| < r\) for some \(r > 0\). The function \(f\) is continuous at the point \(c\) if and only if

\[\lim_{\mathrm{h} \to 0} f(c + h) = f(c) ,\]

that is, if the function \(g(h) = f(c + h)\) has the limit \(f(c)\) at the point \(0\).

Proof

We must show that for every \(\eta > 0\) the same band \(\delta\) satisfies the continuity condition (1.26) if and only if it satisfies the condition of the definition of the limit 1.5 for the function \(g\) and the number \(d = f(c)\).

  1. For both conditions we may take a band \(\delta \le r\). If a condition holds for some band, it also holds for every narrower band, because a narrower band only checks some of the same points (section 1.3.3). So from now on we look only at bands \(\delta \le r\).
  2. The function \(g\) is defined for all \(h\) with \(|h| < r\), because then \(|(c + h) - c| = |h| < r\), and the number \(c + h\) is in \(D\). So the definition of the limit 1.5 applies to it as written, with the same \(r\).
  3. Let \(\delta \le r\). The numbers \(x \in D\) with \(|x - c| < \delta\) are exactly the numbers \(x = c + h\) with \(|h| < \delta\), because \(h = x - c\) and every such number is in \(D\) by assumption. For these numbers \(f(x) = g(h)\).
  4. So the continuity condition with the band \(\delta\) reads: \(|g(h) - f(c)| < \eta\) for every \(h\) with \(|h| < \delta\).
  5. Among these \(h\) is also \(h = 0\), where \(|g(0) - f(c)| = |f(c) - f(c)| = 0 < \eta\). There the condition holds for every \(\eta\).
  6. So the condition of step 4 holds if and only if \(|g(h) - f(c)| < \eta\) for every \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta\). This is the condition of the definition of the limit for the function \(g\) and the number \(d = f(c)\).
  7. So for every \(\eta > 0\) a band \(\delta \le r\) satisfies the continuity condition if and only if it satisfies the limit condition. Therefore \(f\) is continuous at \(c\) if and only if \(\lim_{\mathrm{h} \to 0} g(h) = f(c)\).

Source: LEBL1 Prop. 3.2.2, item (ii) (PDF pp. 122-123), with the limit at any point \(c\). The form with the limit at the point \(0\) is not from the source.

The limit tells what the values approach, and continuity tells in addition that they approach exactly the value at the point. A function can have a limit at a point that is not its value. Take the function \(g\) that equals \(0\) at every \(h \neq 0\) and equals \(1\) at the point \(0\). For every \(h \neq 0\) we have \(|g(h) - 0| = 0\), which is less than every error \(\eta\), so \(g\) has the limit \(0\) at the point \(0\). The definition of the limit does not look at the value \(g(0)\). This value, though, is \(1\), not \(0\), so by lemma 1.59 on continuity and the limit the function \(g\) is not continuous at the point \(0\) (LEBL1 Example 3.2.13, PDF p. 127).

We have defined continuity for functions whose input is one number. It is defined in the same way for functions whose input is a point of space, with the distance between points in place of the distance \(|x - c|\). We do this at the end of this section. A more general form is continuity of a function between sets in which a distance between the elements is given, metric spaces (LEBL1 Def. 7.5.1, PDF p. 288).

Let us try the definition on the displacement of the hanging bar. A steel bar of length \(L = 10\) m hangs under its own weight. Only the centre \(O\) of its top end is fixed, and the weight is carried by a force that pulls the top face up evenly, which is an idealization (figure 1.40). A point on the axis of the bar at depth \(x\) moves down by \(u(x) = a\,(2Lx - x^2)\), where \(a = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1}\). The domain is \(D = [0, L]\). We want to show that the displacement \(u\) is continuous at every point \(c \in [0, L]\). First we write the definition out for this case. We must show: for every \(\eta > 0\) there is a \(\delta > 0\) such that

\[|a\,(2Lx - x^2) - a\,(2Lc - c^2)| < \eta \quad \text{for every } x \in [0, L] \text{ with } |x - c| < \delta .\]
  1. We pick any error \(\eta > 0\). For it we must find a band \(\delta\).
  2. We arrange the difference by grouping the terms with \(2L\) and the terms with a square: \(u(x) - u(c) = a\,\big(2L\,(x - c) - (x^2 - c^2)\big)\).
  3. A difference of squares is a product, \(x^2 - c^2 = (x - c)(x + c)\). We take out the factor \(x - c\):
    \[u(x) - u(c) = a\,(x - c)\,(2L - x - c) .\]
  4. The absolute value of a product is the product of the absolute values (lemma 1.6, item 2), and \(a > 0\). So \(|u(x) - u(c)| = a\,|x - c|\,|2L - x - c|\).
  5. We bound the last factor. Since \(x\) and \(c\) are in \([0, L]\), we have \(0 \le x + c \le 2L\), so \(0 \le 2L - x - c \le 2L\), and \(|2L - x - c| \le 2L\). So by step 4
    \[|u(x) - u(c)| \le 2aL\,|x - c| .\]
  6. We choose the band so that the right side is smaller than \(\eta\): \(\delta = \eta / (2aL)\). This is a positive number.
  7. We check. For every \(x \in [0, L]\) with \(|x - c| < \delta\), step 5 gives \(|u(x) - u(c)| \le 2aL\,|x - c| < 2aL\,\delta = \eta\).
  8. We picked the error freely and found a band for it. So the displacement \(u\) is continuous at the point \(c\), and since \(c\) was any point of the interval, it is continuous on \([0, L]\).

Where did we need to look only at points of the bar? In step 5. If we allowed all numbers \(x\), the factor \(|2L - x - c|\) could be arbitrarily large, and the bound \(2L\) of step 5 would not hold.

What does the inequality of step 5 tell us? The factor \(a\,(2L - x - c)\) has a simple meaning. By (1.6), written for the point \(c\) and the step \(\Delta x = x - c\), the strain of the piece between \(c\) and \(x\) equals \(a\,(2L - 2c) - a\,(x - c) = a\,(2L - x - c)\). So step 3 says that the difference of the displacements of two points is the difference of their positions, \(x - c\), times the strain of the piece between them. The number \(2aL = 3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-6}\) is the strain at the top of the bar, a one-sided derivative of the displacement (definition 1.9), the largest in the bar (section 1.4). By step 5 no piece is stretched more, so the displacements of two points differ by at most \(2aL\) times the distance between them (figure 1.83). For the error \(\eta = 1\) µm \(= 10^{-6}\) m the band is

\[\delta = \frac{10^{-6}\ \text{m}}{3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-6}} = 0{,}2561\ \text{m} .\]

Two points of the bar that are closer than 25,61 cm move by amounts that differ by less than 1 µm. Here the band does not depend on the point \(c\), because the factor \(|2L - x - c|\) was bounded on the whole interval by the same number \(2L\).

1.12.2 A jump

What does a function look like that is not continuous at a point? Take again the steel bar of length \(L = 10\) m and diameter 10 mm that hangs under its own weight, and hang a load at its middle (figure 1.84, left). Through a small horizontal hole at depth \(L/2 = 5\) m we push a short pin and hang on each of its ends a block that weighs 10 N. The newton (N) is the unit of force, a body with a mass of 1 kg weighs \(9{,}81\) N, and we will meet it properly in chapter 2. Together the blocks weigh 20 N. Since they hang symmetrically, they do not bend the bar. The bar itself has a mass of \(6{,}126\) kg and weighs \(60{,}10\) N. The mass comes from the volume of the bar and the density of steel, the mass of one cubic metre of steel, which is 7800 kg (BOWER, Table 3.1, PDF p. 100), and the weight from the mass, \(6{,}126 \cdot 9{,}81\ \text{N} = 60{,}10\) N. We will write out the computation of the mass in chapter 2. How the top end is fixed does not matter here. All that matters is that the top end carries everything that hangs below it.

The model makes two simplifications. The pin presses on the material next to the hole, over a length of a few millimetres. We will imagine that it acts on a single cross section, the one at depth 5 m. This is an idealization. The hole also weakens the bar a little, which we neglect.

What force does the cross section at depth \(x\) carry? In section 1.2 we saw that every cross section carries the weight of everything that hangs below it. The part of the bar below the cross section, together with everything that hangs on it, is at rest, so the cross section pulls it up with a force equal to its weight. We denote by \(S(x)\) the force with which the cross section at depth \(x\) carries everything below it. In chapter 2 it will get a name and the symbol \(N(x)\). Below the cross section at depth \(x\) is a part of the bar of length \(L - x\). This is the fraction \((L - x)/L\) of the whole bar, so this part weighs \(60{,}10\ \text{N} \cdot (L - x)/L\). Below a cross section above the pin, where \(x < 5\) m, the blocks hang as well. Below a cross section under the pin, where \(x > 5\) m, they do not. So

\[S(x) = \begin{cases} 60{,}10\ \text{N} \cdot \dfrac{L - x}{L} + 20\ \text{N} , & 0 \le x \le 5\ \text{m} , \\ 60{,}10\ \text{N} \cdot \dfrac{L - x}{L} , & 5\ \text{m} < x \le 10\ \text{m} . \end{cases} \tag{1.27}\]

Where does the cross section at depth exactly 5 m belong? In the model the pin acts exactly on this cross section, so the model does not tell whether this cross section carries the blocks or not. With a real pin, which presses on a few millimetres of material, the question does not arise. We agree that it carries them, and so in (1.27) we put the point \(x = 5\) m into the first line. As we will see, the other choice would lead to the same conclusion.

At the top \(S(0) = 60{,}10\ \text{N} + 20\ \text{N} = 80{,}10\) N, at depth 5 m \(S(5\ \text{m}) = 30{,}05\ \text{N} + 20\ \text{N} = 50{,}05\) N, just below the pin the values are a little under \(30{,}05\) N, and at the lower end \(S(10\ \text{m}) = 0\) (figure 1.84, right). On each side of the pin the force decreases evenly, by \(60{,}10\ \text{N} / 10\ \text{m} = 6{,}010\) N per metre, because the weight of the bar is spread evenly along its length. As we go down past the depth of 5 m, though, the force drops at once by 20 N, the weight of the blocks.

Let us show with the definition of continuity 1.58 that \(S\) is not continuous at the point \(c = 5\) m. Continuity would require: for every \(\eta > 0\) there is a \(\delta > 0\) such that \(|S(x) - S(5\ \text{m})| < \eta\) for every \(x \in [0, L]\) with \(|x - 5\ \text{m}| < \delta\). To show that this fails, it is enough to find one error \(\eta\) for which no band \(\delta\) works. As the error we take half the jump, \(\eta = 10\) N.

  1. We pick the error \(\eta = 10\) N. We will show that no band works for it.
  2. Let \(\delta > 0\) be any band. Let \(t\) be half of the smaller of the numbers \(\delta\) and 5 m, and take the point below the pin \(x = 5\ \text{m} + t\). Since \(0 < t < \delta\), we have \(|x - 5\ \text{m}| = t < \delta\). Since \(t \le 2{,}5\) m, the point \(x\) lies between 5 m and \(7{,}5\) m, so in the bar.
  3. Since \(x > 5\) m, the second line of (1.27) applies:
    \[S(x) = 60{,}10\ \text{N} \cdot \frac{5\ \text{m} - t}{10\ \text{m}} = 30{,}05\ \text{N} - 6{,}010\ \frac{\text{N}}{\text{m}} \cdot t .\]
  4. The difference is
    \[S(5\ \text{m}) - S(x) = 50{,}05\ \text{N} - 30{,}05\ \text{N} + 6{,}010\ \frac{\text{N}}{\text{m}} \cdot t = 20\ \text{N} + 6{,}010\ \frac{\text{N}}{\text{m}} \cdot t .\]
    Since \(t > 0\), the difference is larger than 20 N, so \(|S(x) - S(5\ \text{m})| > 20\ \text{N} > 10\ \text{N} = \eta\).
  5. So in every band around 5 m there is a point of the bar at which \(S\) differs from \(S(5\ \text{m})\) by more than \(\eta\). We picked the band freely, so no band works for the error \(\eta = 10\) N, and \(S\) is not continuous at the point 5 m.

Why exactly half the jump? The band from \(40{,}05\) N to \(60{,}05\) N around \(S(5\ \text{m})\) does not reach the values below the pin, which are all under \(30{,}05\) N (figure 1.85). Any error up to 20 N would do, half only leaves a gap that can be seen. With the other choice, \(S(5\ \text{m}) = 30{,}05\) N, we would take the point above the pin, \(x = 5\ \text{m} - t\), where \(S(x) = 50{,}05\ \text{N} + 6{,}010\ \text{N/m} \cdot t\), and the difference would again be larger than 20 N.

At every other point \(c\), though, \(S\) is continuous. If \(c > 5\) m, we take a band \(\delta\) no larger than \(c - 5\) m. All points of the band are then below the pin, where the second line of (1.27) applies, and the difference is

\[|S(x) - S(c)| = 60{,}10\ \text{N} \cdot \frac{|x - c|}{10\ \text{m}} = 6{,}010\ \frac{\text{N}}{\text{m}} \cdot |x - c| .\]

If \(\delta\) is also at most \(\eta / (6{,}010\ \text{N/m})\), the difference is smaller than \(\eta\), as for the displacement. If \(c < 5\) m, we take \(\delta\) no larger than \(5\ \text{m} - c\) and argue in the same way with the first line, where the term 20 N cancels in the difference. So the function \(S\) has a single jump, at 5 m.

The jump comes from the idealization. A real pin presses on the material over a length of a few millimetres, and there the force drops by 20 N continuously, but very steeply. The idealization is useful because it gives a simple formula, and far from the pin it describes the force well. Mathematically, though, it makes the function discontinuous, and statements that require continuity may not be used at 5 m. The strain jumps too. For steel the elongation of a short piece is proportional to the force that pulls it (section 1.2). Without the blocks the strain at the top, a one-sided derivative of the displacement (definition 1.9), was \(2aL = 3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-6}\), when the top cross section carried \(60{,}10\) N. Just above the pin the cross section carries \(50{,}05\) N, so the strain there is \(3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-6} \cdot 50{,}05 / 60{,}10 = 3{,}251 \cdot 10^{-6}\). Just below the pin it carries \(30{,}05\) N, and the strain is \(3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-6} \cdot 30{,}05 / 60{,}10 = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6}\), the same as at the middle of the bar without the blocks, because below the pin nothing has changed. The displacement, however, does not jump, because the bar stays in one piece. Its graph has only a sharp corner at 5 m, where the slope changes at once.

1.12.3 Computing with limits of sequences

The proof that \(S\) is not continuous at 5 m had to find, for every band, a point that breaks the condition. For more complicated functions such work with \(\eta\) and \(\delta\) is long. A shorter way goes through sequences. We arrange points that approach \(c\) into a sequence and ask whether the values approach \(f(c)\). For this we need rules that give limits of sequences from known limits, without searching for a place \(N\) again every time. Take the strains of the top pieces of length \(1/n\) m of the hanging bar, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{n}} = 2aL - a/n\) (section 1.9.1). We showed their limit \(2aL\) in section 1.9.2 with a computation of its own, with \(\eta\) and \(N\). Could we have obtained it simply from the limits of the parts, the constant number \(2aL\) and the sequence \((1/n)\)? The next lemma says that we could. The idea is the same as in the proof of lemma 1.41 on the uniqueness of the limit of a sequence: when a sum of two errors must be small, we allow each of them half (figure 1.86).

Lemma 1.60 Limit laws for sequences (računanje z limitami zaporedij)

Let the sequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) converge to the number \(x\) and the sequence \((y_{\mathrm{n}})\) to the number \(y\), and let \(k\) be a number.

  1. The sequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}} + y_{\mathrm{n}})\) converges to \(x + y\).
  2. The sequence \((k\,x_{\mathrm{n}})\) converges to \(k\,x\).
  3. The sequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}}\,y_{\mathrm{n}})\) converges to \(x\,y\).
Proof

We must show all three items. In each we bound the difference between the term and the expected limit by the differences \(x_{\mathrm{n}} - x\) and \(y_{\mathrm{n}} - y\), which we can make as small as we like.

1. Sum.

  1. We pick any error \(\eta > 0\).
  2. Since \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) converges to \(x\), by the definition of the limit of a sequence 1.38 there is, for the error \(\eta/2\), a place \(N_1\) such that \(|x_{\mathrm{n}} - x| < \eta/2\) for every \(n \ge N_1\). We take half the error because in step 5 we will add two such errors.
  3. Since \((y_{\mathrm{n}})\) converges to \(y\), there is a place \(N_2\) such that \(|y_{\mathrm{n}} - y| < \eta/2\) for every \(n \ge N_2\).
  4. Let \(N\) be the larger of the numbers \(N_1\) and \(N_2\). For \(n \ge N\) both inequalities hold.
  5. We write the difference as a sum of differences, \((x_{\mathrm{n}} + y_{\mathrm{n}}) - (x + y) = (x_{\mathrm{n}} - x) + (y_{\mathrm{n}} - y)\). By the triangle inequality for numbers \(|a + b| \le |a| + |b|\) (lemma 1.6, item 1), for \(n \ge N\)
    \[|(x_{\mathrm{n}} + y_{\mathrm{n}}) - (x + y)| \le |x_{\mathrm{n}} - x| + |y_{\mathrm{n}} - y| < \tfrac{\eta}{2} + \tfrac{\eta}{2} = \eta .\]
  6. We picked the error freely, so \((x_{\mathrm{n}} + y_{\mathrm{n}})\) converges to \(x + y\).

2. Multiple.

  1. If \(k = 0\), then \(k\,x_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\) for every \(n\) and \(k\,x = 0\). The constant sequence \(0, 0, 0, \dots\) converges to \(0\) (section 1.9.2).
  2. Let \(k \neq 0\), and pick any error \(\eta > 0\). The number \(\eta/|k|\) is positive, so there is a place \(N\) such that \(|x_{\mathrm{n}} - x| < \eta/|k|\) for every \(n \ge N\). We divide the error by \(|k|\) because in step 3 it will be multiplied by \(|k|\).
  3. For \(n \ge N\) we have \(k\,x_{\mathrm{n}} - k\,x = k\,(x_{\mathrm{n}} - x)\). The absolute value of a product is the product of the absolute values (lemma 1.6, item 2), so
    \[|k\,x_{\mathrm{n}} - k\,x| = |k|\,|x_{\mathrm{n}} - x| < |k| \cdot \frac{\eta}{|k|} = \eta .\]
  4. We picked the error freely, so \((k\,x_{\mathrm{n}})\) converges to \(k\,x\).

3. Product.

  1. We write the difference so that in each part only one factor changes. We add and subtract \(x\,y_{\mathrm{n}}\) and take out the common factors:
    \[x_{\mathrm{n}} y_{\mathrm{n}} - x y = (x_{\mathrm{n}} y_{\mathrm{n}} - x\,y_{\mathrm{n}}) + (x\,y_{\mathrm{n}} - x y) = (x_{\mathrm{n}} - x)\,y_{\mathrm{n}} + x\,(y_{\mathrm{n}} - y) .\]
  2. By the triangle inequality for numbers and the rule for the absolute value of a product (lemma 1.6, items 1 and 2),
    \[|x_{\mathrm{n}} y_{\mathrm{n}} - x y| \le |x_{\mathrm{n}} - x|\,|y_{\mathrm{n}}| + |x|\,|y_{\mathrm{n}} - y| .\]
  3. The factor \(|y_{\mathrm{n}}|\) changes with \(n\), so we bound it by a single number. The convergent sequence \((y_{\mathrm{n}})\) is bounded by lemma 1.42 on the boundedness of a convergent sequence: there is a number \(B\) such that \(|y_{\mathrm{n}}| \le B\) for every \(n\). If \(B\) works, \(B + 1\) works too, so we may take \(B > 0\). We need it positive because in step 5 we will divide by \(B\). So by step 2
    \[|x_{\mathrm{n}} y_{\mathrm{n}} - x y| \le B\,|x_{\mathrm{n}} - x| + |x|\,|y_{\mathrm{n}} - y| .\]
  4. We pick any error \(\eta > 0\).
  5. Since \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) converges to \(x\), there is a place \(N_1\) such that \(|x_{\mathrm{n}} - x| < \eta/(2B)\) for every \(n \ge N_1\). For these \(n\) we have \(B\,|x_{\mathrm{n}} - x| < \eta/2\).
  6. Since \((y_{\mathrm{n}})\) converges to \(y\), there is a place \(N_2\) such that \(|y_{\mathrm{n}} - y| < \eta/(2|x| + 2)\) for every \(n \ge N_2\). The denominator \(2|x| + 2\) is positive also for \(x = 0\), while with the denominator \(2|x|\) we would divide by zero at \(x = 0\). For these \(n\)
    \[|x|\,|y_{\mathrm{n}} - y| \le |x| \cdot \frac{\eta}{2|x| + 2} = \frac{\eta}{2} \cdot \frac{|x|}{|x| + 1} < \frac{\eta}{2} ,\]
    because \(|x| < |x| + 1\) and so \(|x|/(|x| + 1) < 1\).
  7. Let \(N\) be the larger of the numbers \(N_1\) and \(N_2\). For \(n \ge N\), steps 3, 5 and 6 give \(|x_{\mathrm{n}} y_{\mathrm{n}} - x y| < \eta/2 + \eta/2 = \eta\).
  8. We picked the error freely, so \((x_{\mathrm{n}} y_{\mathrm{n}})\) converges to \(x y\).

Source: LEBL1 Prop. 2.2.5 (PDF pp. 63-64), the multiple through a constant sequence on PDF p. 65. The source proves the product with the bound \(K = \max\{|x|, |y|, \epsilon/3, 1\}\). The proof with the boundedness of the sequence is not from the source.

The difficulty with the product was that both factors change at once. Step 1 split it into two parts. In the first, the small difference \(x_{\mathrm{n}} - x\) is multiplied by the term \(y_{\mathrm{n}}\), which is bounded by \(B\), in the second, the small difference \(y_{\mathrm{n}} - y\) by the constant number \(x\). We will meet this trick, adding and subtracting an intermediate term, many times more.

A difference is a sum with a multiple, \(x_{\mathrm{n}} - y_{\mathrm{n}} = x_{\mathrm{n}} + (-1)\,y_{\mathrm{n}}\), so by items 1 and 2 it converges to \(x - y\). A similar rule holds for a quotient: the sequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}} / y_{\mathrm{n}})\) converges to \(x / y\) when \(y \neq 0\) and all \(y_{\mathrm{n}} \neq 0\) (LEBL1 Prop. 2.2.5, item (iv), PDF pp. 63-64). We do not prove this rule, because we will not need it in this book.

Let us try the lemma on the strains of the pieces. The constant sequence \(2aL, 2aL, \dots\) converges to \(2aL\). The sequence \((1/n)\) converges to \(0\) by lemma 1.39 on the limit of the sequence 1/n, so by item 2 with \(k = -a\) the sequence \((-a/n)\) converges to \(-a \cdot 0 = 0\). So by item 1, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{n}} = 2aL + (-a/n)\) converges to \(2aL + 0 = 2aL = 3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-6}\). This is the limit from section 1.9.2, this time without a new error \(\eta\) and without a new place \(N\). By item 3, for example, the sequence \((1/n^2)\), which is the product \((1/n) \cdot (1/n)\), converges to \(0 \cdot 0 = 0\).

1.12.4 Continuity through sequences

Now we can describe continuity with sequences. Let \(f\) be continuous at \(c\), and let the sequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) converge to \(c\). From some place on the terms \(x_{\mathrm{n}}\) are in the band \(\delta\) around \(c\), so their values are in the band \(\eta\) around \(f(c)\), and the values \(f(x_{\mathrm{n}})\) approach \(f(c)\) (figure 1.87). Perhaps surprisingly, the converse holds too: if \(f(x_{\mathrm{n}})\) approaches \(f(c)\) for every sequence that approaches \(c\), then \(f\) is continuous at \(c\).

Lemma 1.61 Continuity through sequences (zveznost z zaporedji)

Let \(f\) be a function defined on a set of real numbers \(D\), and \(c \in D\). The function \(f\) is continuous at the point \(c\) if and only if for every sequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) with terms in \(D\) that converges to \(c\), the sequence \((f(x_{\mathrm{n}}))\) converges to \(f(c)\).

Proof

We must show both directions.

Continuity implies that the values converge.

  1. Let \(f\) be continuous at \(c\), and let \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) be a sequence with terms in \(D\) that converges to \(c\). We pick any error \(\eta > 0\).
  2. By the definition of continuity 1.58 there is a band \(\delta > 0\) such that \(|f(x) - f(c)| < \eta\) for every \(x \in D\) with \(|x - c| < \delta\).
  3. Since \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) converges to \(c\), by the definition of the limit of a sequence 1.38 there is, for the error \(\delta\), a place \(N\) such that \(|x_{\mathrm{n}} - c| < \delta\) for every \(n \ge N\). Here the band \(\delta\) plays the role of the allowed error for the sequence.
  4. For \(n \ge N\) the term \(x_{\mathrm{n}}\) is in \(D\) and \(|x_{\mathrm{n}} - c| < \delta\), so by step 2 we have \(|f(x_{\mathrm{n}}) - f(c)| < \eta\). We picked the error freely, so \((f(x_{\mathrm{n}}))\) converges to \(f(c)\).

Convergence of the values implies continuity.

  1. Suppose that for every sequence with terms in \(D\) that converges to \(c\) the values converge to \(f(c)\). We argue by contradiction: suppose that \(f\) is not continuous at \(c\).
  2. Let us write out what this means. Continuity claims that for every error \(\eta\) there is a suitable band \(\delta\). If this is not true, there is at least one error \(\eta > 0\) for which no band works. That a band \(\delta\) does not work means that the condition \(|f(x) - f(c)| < \eta\) does not hold for all \(x \in D\) with \(|x - c| < \delta\), that is, that there is at least one such \(x\) for which \(|f(x) - f(c)| \ge \eta\).
  3. For every natural number \(n\) we use step 2 with the band \(\delta = 1/n\) and choose one such \(x\), which we denote by \(x_{\mathrm{n}}\). For it, \(x_{\mathrm{n}} \in D\), \(|x_{\mathrm{n}} - c| < 1/n\) and \(|f(x_{\mathrm{n}}) - f(c)| \ge \eta\). In this way we get a sequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) with terms in \(D\).
  4. The sequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) converges to \(c\). From \(|x_{\mathrm{n}} - c| < 1/n\) we get \(c - 1/n < x_{\mathrm{n}} < c + 1/n\). The sequences \((c - 1/n)\) and \((c + 1/n)\) converge to \(c\) by lemma 1.60 on the limit laws for sequences, items 1 and 2, because the constant sequence \(c, c, \dots\) converges to \(c\) and \((1/n)\) converges to \(0\) by lemma 1.39 on the limit of the sequence 1/n. Strict inequalities are also non-strict, so by the squeeze lemma 1.46 the sequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) also converges to \(c\).
  5. So by the assumption of step 1, \((f(x_{\mathrm{n}}))\) converges to \(f(c)\). For the error \(\eta\) of step 2 there is a place \(N\) such that \(|f(x_{\mathrm{n}}) - f(c)| < \eta\) for every \(n \ge N\), in particular for \(n = N\).
  6. By step 3, though, \(|f(x_{\mathrm{N}}) - f(c)| \ge \eta\). The two inequalities cannot both hold. So the assumption of step 1 that \(f\) is not continuous at \(c\) is false, and \(f\) is continuous at \(c\).

Source: LEBL1 Prop. 3.2.2, item (iii) (PDF pp. 122-123). The source proves the second direction by contrapositive, the proof by contradiction is not from the source.

The first direction translated the band \(\delta\) into a place \(N\): what holds in the band holds for all late terms. The second direction built from the bands \(1, \tfrac12, \tfrac13, \dots\) a sequence of numbers \(x_{\mathrm{n}}\) that approaches \(c\), while their values stay far from \(f(c)\).

The lemma gives a second way to the jump of the force \(S\) from section 1.12.2, in the bar that hangs under its own weight and has, at depth 5 m, a pin with two blocks that together weigh 20 N. Take the points below the pin \(x_{\mathrm{n}} = 5\ \text{m} + (1/n)\ \text{m}\), that is, 6 m, \(5{,}5\) m, \(5{,}333\) m and so on (figure 1.88, blue). All of them are in the bar, and the sequence converges to 5 m by lemma 1.60 on the limit laws for sequences. Since they are below the pin, the second line of (1.27) applies:

\[S(x_{\mathrm{n}}) = 60{,}10\ \text{N} \cdot \frac{5 - 1/n}{10} = 30{,}05\ \text{N} - \frac{6{,}010\ \text{N}}{n} .\]

By the same lemma the sequence \((S(x_{\mathrm{n}}))\) converges to \(30{,}05\) N, not to \(S(5\ \text{m}) = 50{,}05\) N. So by lemma 1.61 on continuity through sequences, \(S\) is not continuous at the point 5 m.

The points above the pin, \(x_{\mathrm{n}} = 5\ \text{m} - (1/n)\ \text{m}\), however, give by the first line of (1.27)

\[S(x_{\mathrm{n}}) = 60{,}10\ \text{N} \cdot \frac{5 + 1/n}{10} + 20\ \text{N} = 50{,}05\ \text{N} + \frac{6{,}010\ \text{N}}{n} ,\]

which converges to \(50{,}05\ \text{N} = S(5\ \text{m})\) (figure 1.88, grey). So a single sequence for which the values converge to \(f(c)\) does not prove continuity. The lemma requires all sequences (LEBL1 Example 3.2.10 and the remark after it, PDF p. 125).

A similar question was left open in section 1.9.2. The strains of the pieces of lengths \(1, \tfrac12, \tfrac13, \dots\) m at the top of the hanging bar approached the derivative, but one chosen sequence of lengths does not prove that the derivative exists. The converse, however, holds: when the derivative exists, the strains approach it for every sequence of lengths that goes to zero. The next lemma says this for the limit of any function at the point \(0\).

Lemma 1.62 Limit of a function along a sequence (limita funkcije vzdolž zaporedja)

Let the function \(g\) be defined for all \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < r\) for some \(r > 0\), and let \(\lim_{\mathrm{h} \to 0} g(h) = d\). If a sequence \((h_{\mathrm{n}})\) with terms \(0 < |h_{\mathrm{n}}| < r\) converges to \(0\), then the sequence \((g(h_{\mathrm{n}}))\) converges to \(d\). The same holds for the limit from the right, if \(g\) is defined for all \(h\) with \(0 < h < r\), \(\lim_{\mathrm{h} \to 0^+} g(h) = d\) and all terms satisfy \(0 < h_{\mathrm{n}} < r\), and for the limit from the left with terms \(-r < h_{\mathrm{n}} < 0\).

Proof

We must show that from some place on all values \(g(h_{\mathrm{n}})\) are closer to the number \(d\) than a given error.

  1. We pick any error \(\eta > 0\).
  2. By the definition of the limit 1.5 there is a band \(\delta > 0\) such that \(|g(h) - d| < \eta\) for every \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta\).
  3. Since \((h_{\mathrm{n}})\) converges to \(0\), by the definition of the limit of a sequence 1.38 there is, for the error \(\delta\), a place \(N\) such that \(|h_{\mathrm{n}} - 0| = |h_{\mathrm{n}}| < \delta\) for every \(n \ge N\).
  4. For \(n \ge N\) we have \(0 < |h_{\mathrm{n}}| < \delta\), because no term is zero. So by step 2, \(|g(h_{\mathrm{n}}) - d| < \eta\). We picked the error freely, so \((g(h_{\mathrm{n}}))\) converges to \(d\).
  5. For the limit from the right, step 2 gives by the definition of a one-sided limit 1.8 a band \(\delta > 0\) such that \(|g(h) - d| < \eta\) for every \(h\) with \(0 < h < \delta\). Step 3 is the same, and in step 4, for \(n \ge N\), the number \(h_{\mathrm{n}}\) is positive and smaller than \(\delta\), so again \(|g(h_{\mathrm{n}}) - d| < \eta\). For the limit from the left the terms are negative and the band holds for \(-\delta < h < 0\), and the conclusion is the same.

Source: LEBL1 Lemma 3.1.7 (PDF p. 116), one of its two directions, here for the limit at the point \(0\). By LEBL1 Def. 3.1.16 (PDF p. 119) a one-sided limit is the limit of the function restricted to the numbers on one side of the point, so the lemma holds for it too. We do not need the other direction.

The requirement that no term \(h_{\mathrm{n}}\) is zero is needed because the definition of the limit says nothing about the value at zero, and \(g\) may not even be defined there, like the difference quotient. Let us use the lemma on the hanging bar without the blocks, that is, on the steel bar of length \(L = 10\) m that hangs under its own weight only and whose point on the axis at depth \(x\) moves by \(u(x) = a\,(2Lx - x^2)\). Take the point at its middle, at \(x = 5\) m. By (1.6) the difference quotient there is

\[g(\Delta x) = \frac{u(5\ \text{m} + \Delta x) - u(5\ \text{m})}{\Delta x} = a\,(2L - 10\ \text{m}) - a\,\Delta x = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6} - a\,\Delta x\]

for every \(\Delta x \neq 0\), and its limit is the derivative \(u'(5\ \text{m}) = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6}\) (section 1.3.4). Take any sequence of lengths of pieces that are never zero, are all shorter than 5 m so that the pieces stay in the bar, and go to zero. By the lemma the strains of these pieces approach \(1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6}\). So it is, for example, for the lengths \(1/n\) m and for the lengths \(1/2^{n-1}\) m, which go to zero by corollary 1.47 on the limit of the powers of one half. For \(\Delta x = 1\) m the strain is \(1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6} - 0{,}1952 \cdot 10^{-6} = 1{,}757 \cdot 10^{-6}\), the first value at the middle in the table of section 1.2. In the same way the strains \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{n}} = 2aL - a/n\) at the top of the bar from section 1.9.1 had to approach \(2aL\). At the top the quotient of the displacement equals \(2aL - a\,\Delta x\), but only for positive \(\Delta x\), because there is no material above the top. By lemma 1.26 on the limit of a number plus a multiple the expression \(2aL - a\,\Delta x\) has the limit \(2aL\), so it also has the limit \(2aL\) from the right (section 1.3.3, after definition 1.8), and this is the one-sided derivative at the top. The lengths \(1/n\) m are positive, so the lemma in its form for the limit from the right says that the strains \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{n}}\) approach \(2aL\).

1.12.5 Sums, products and polynomials

With the lemma on continuity through sequences the rules for limits of sequences carry over to functions. The displacement of the hanging bar is built from simple parts, from numbers and from the variable \(x\), which we add and multiply: \(a\,(2Lx - x^2) = 2aL \cdot x + (-a) \cdot x \cdot x\). Let us give such functions a name.

Definition 1.63 Polynomial (polinom)

A polynomial (polinom) in one variable is a function whose input is a number \(x\) and whose output is the number

\[p(x) = b_0 + b_1 x + b_2 x^2 + \dots + b_{\mathrm{m}} x^m ,\]

where \(m\) is a natural number or zero and \(b_0, b_1, \dots, b_{\mathrm{m}}\) are given numbers, the coefficients (koeficienti) of the polynomial. A polynomial in three variables is a function whose input is three numbers \(x\), \(y\), \(z\) and whose output is a sum of finitely many terms, each of which is the product of a given number and finitely many factors, each of which is \(x\), \(y\) or \(z\).

Source: LEBL1 Prop. 3.2.4 (PDF p. 123) for one variable, Example 7.5.3 (PDF p. 288) for two variables, where the source adds that the same holds for more variables. The description of the terms by their chosen factors is not from the source.

The power \(x^m\) means the product of \(m\) factors \(x\), so \(x^1 = x\) and \(x^2 = x \cdot x\). For \(x^0\) we take \(1\), so that the first term of a polynomial is just the number \(b_0\). The displacement of the hanging bar, \(u(x) = 2aL\,x - a\,x^2\), is a polynomial in one variable with \(m = 2\) and the coefficients \(b_0 = 0\), \(b_1 = 2aL\) and \(b_2 = -a\). A constant number is a polynomial too, with the single term \(b_0\). All three components of the field of the hanging bar (1.18) are polynomials in three variables. The component

\[u_{\mathrm{y}} = -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\,y = (-0{,}6\,aL) \cdot y + 0{,}6\,a \cdot x \cdot y\]

is a sum of two terms: the product of the number \(-0{,}6\,aL\) with the factor \(y\), and the product of the number \(0{,}6\,a\) with the factors \(x\) and \(y\). The force \(S\) of (1.27), on the other hand, is a polynomial on each side of the pin separately, but not on the whole interval \([0, L]\).

Corollary 1.64 Sum and product of continuous functions (vsota in produkt zveznih funkcij)
  1. Let the functions \(f\) and \(g\), defined on a set of real numbers \(D\), be continuous at the point \(c \in D\), and let \(k\) be a number. Then the functions \(f + g\), \(k f\) and \(f g\), whose values at \(x\) are \(f(x) + g(x)\), \(k\,f(x)\) and \(f(x)\,g(x)\), are also continuous at the point \(c\).
  2. Every polynomial in one variable is continuous at every point \(c \in \mathbb R\).
Proof

We must show both items. The first follows from the lemma on continuity through sequences and the rules for limits of sequences, the second from the first by induction.

1. Sum, multiple and product.

  1. Let \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) be a sequence with terms in \(D\) that converges to \(c\). By lemma 1.61 on continuity through sequences, first direction, \((f(x_{\mathrm{n}}))\) converges to \(f(c)\) and \((g(x_{\mathrm{n}}))\) to \(g(c)\).
  2. By lemma 1.60 on the limit laws for sequences, items 1, 2 and 3, the sequences \((f(x_{\mathrm{n}}) + g(x_{\mathrm{n}}))\), \((k\,f(x_{\mathrm{n}}))\) and \((f(x_{\mathrm{n}})\,g(x_{\mathrm{n}}))\) converge to \(f(c) + g(c)\), \(k\,f(c)\) and \(f(c)\,g(c)\).
  3. These are the values of the functions \(f + g\), \(k f\) and \(f g\) at the terms \(x_{\mathrm{n}}\) and at the point \(c\). We chose the sequence freely, so by lemma 1.61, second direction, these functions are continuous at \(c\).

2. Polynomials.

  1. A constant function, which has the same value \(b\) at every \(x\), is continuous at every point \(c\). For every \(\eta > 0\) and every \(x\) we have \(|b - b| = 0 < \eta\), so every band works, for example \(\delta = 1\).
  2. The function whose value at \(x\) is \(x\) is continuous at every point \(c\). The condition \(|x - c| < \eta\) holds for every \(x\) with \(|x - c| < \delta\) if we take \(\delta = \eta\).
  3. The function \(x^m\) is continuous at \(c\) for every natural number \(m\). We show this with the principle of induction 1.44. For \(m = 1\) this is step 2. If \(x^m\) is continuous at \(c\), then \(x^{m+1} = x^m \cdot x\) is the product of two functions continuous at \(c\) and is continuous by item 1.
  4. So by item 1 every term \(b_{\mathrm{m}} x^m\), a multiple of the function \(x^m\), is continuous at \(c\), and by step 1 so is the constant term \(b_0\).
  5. A polynomial with \(m = 0\) is the constant function \(b_0\), continuous by step 1. For \(m \ge 1\) we use induction on \(m\). For \(m = 1\), \(b_0 + b_1 x\) is the sum of two functions continuous at \(c\) and is continuous by item 1. If every polynomial with powers up to \(x^m\) is continuous, a polynomial with powers up to \(x^{m+1}\) is the sum of such a polynomial and the term \(b_{\mathrm{m}+1} x^{m+1}\), which is continuous by step 4. By item 1 the sum is continuous. By the principle of induction every polynomial is continuous.

Source: LEBL1 Prop. 3.2.5 (PDF p. 124), where the proof is left to the reader, and Prop. 3.2.4 (PDF pp. 123-124), which treats polynomials directly with the rules for limits of sequences. The proof by induction is not from the source.

The continuity of the displacement of the hanging bar now follows without computation. The displacement \(u(x) = 2aL\,x - a\,x^2\) is a polynomial, so it is continuous at every point \(c\). When we look at it only on the interval \([0, L]\), it stays continuous at every point \(c \in [0, L]\), because condition (1.26) then checks only some of the same points. The direct computation with the definition in section 1.12.1, though, told us more. It gave the band \(\delta = \eta / (2aL)\), which works for all points at once, and showed that the displacements of two points differ by at most the largest strain times the distance. The lemma on continuity through sequences says only that a band exists, not how large it is.

1.12.6 Continuity in space

The second fact from the start of the section is about the partial derivatives of the displacement. These are functions of three variables: their input is the three coordinates of a point, their output one number. A point \(Q\) is close to a point \(P\) when the distance between them is small. We will write the distance between the points \(P\) and \(Q\), the length of the vector \(\overrightarrow{PQ}\) (definition 1.17), briefly as \(|PQ|\), as in section 1.7.3. What we wrote for numbers with the distance \(|x - c|\), we write for points with the distance \(|PQ|\). Let us start with sequences.

Definition 1.65 Limit of a sequence of points (limita zaporedja točk)

A sequence of points (zaporedje točk) is a function whose input is a natural number \(n\) and whose output is a point of space \(P_{\mathrm{n}}\). The sequence of points \((P_{\mathrm{n}})\) converges to the point \(P\) if the sequence of distances \((|P_{\mathrm{n}} P|)\) converges to \(0\). The point \(P\) is then the limit of the sequence, and we write \(\lim_{\mathrm{n} \to \infty} P_{\mathrm{n}} = P\).

Source: LEBL1 Def. 7.3.1 and 7.3.2 (PDF p. 274), for sets in which a distance is given.

Let us write the condition with the definition of the limit of a sequence 1.38. Since \(\big||P_{\mathrm{n}} P| - 0\big| = |P_{\mathrm{n}} P|\), it reads: for every \(\eta > 0\) there is a place \(N\) such that \(|P_{\mathrm{n}} P| < \eta\) for every \(n \ge N\). The set of all points that are closer to \(P\) than \(\eta\) is called the ball (krogla) with centre \(P\) and radius \(\eta\). So from the place \(N\) on, all points of the sequence are in this ball. A simple example are the points \(P_{\mathrm{n}} = (1/n,\ 2/n,\ 2/n)\), with coordinates in metres. Their distance from the origin \(O\) is

\[|P_{\mathrm{n}} O| = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n^2} + \frac{4}{n^2} + \frac{4}{n^2}} = \sqrt{\frac{9}{n^2}} = \frac{3}{n} ,\]

because \(3/n\) is a nonnegative number whose square is \(9/n^2\) (theorem 1.36 on the existence of square roots, uniqueness). By lemma 1.60 on the limit laws for sequences, item 2, the sequence \((3/n)\) converges to \(3 \cdot 0 = 0\), so the sequence of points \((P_{\mathrm{n}})\) converges to \(O\).

These points show something more: each coordinate, \(1/n\), \(2/n\) and \(2/n\), converges to the corresponding coordinate of the point \(O\), to zero. Is this always equivalent to the convergence of the points? The distance and the differences of the coordinates are the sides of a right triangle (figure 1.89). The hypotenuse is not shorter than either leg and not longer than the sum of the legs. So when one goes to zero, so does the other.

Lemma 1.66 Convergence coordinate by coordinate (konvergenca po koordinatah)

Let \(P = (x_{\mathrm{P}}, y_{\mathrm{P}}, z_{\mathrm{P}})\) be a point and \((P_{\mathrm{n}})\) a sequence of points with coordinates \(P_{\mathrm{n}} = (x_{\mathrm{n}}, y_{\mathrm{n}}, z_{\mathrm{n}})\). The sequence of points \((P_{\mathrm{n}})\) converges to \(P\) if and only if the sequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) converges to \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\), the sequence \((y_{\mathrm{n}})\) to \(y_{\mathrm{P}}\) and the sequence \((z_{\mathrm{n}})\) to \(z_{\mathrm{P}}\).

Proof

We must show both directions. First we compare the distance with the differences of the coordinates, then we use the squeeze lemma.

  1. We write \(s_{\mathrm{x}} = |x_{\mathrm{n}} - x_{\mathrm{P}}|\), \(s_{\mathrm{y}} = |y_{\mathrm{n}} - y_{\mathrm{P}}|\) and \(s_{\mathrm{z}} = |z_{\mathrm{n}} - z_{\mathrm{P}}|\). By (1.8) and (1.10)
    \[|P_{\mathrm{n}} P|^2 = (x_{\mathrm{n}} - x_{\mathrm{P}})^2 + (y_{\mathrm{n}} - y_{\mathrm{P}})^2 + (z_{\mathrm{n}} - z_{\mathrm{P}})^2 = s_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + s_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + s_{\mathrm{z}}^2 ,\]
    because the square of a number equals the square of its absolute value.
  2. Since the squares \(s_{\mathrm{y}}^2\) and \(s_{\mathrm{z}}^2\) are not negative, \(s_{\mathrm{x}}^2 \le |P_{\mathrm{n}} P|^2\). The numbers \(s_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(|P_{\mathrm{n}} P|\) are not negative, so by lemma 1.28 that a larger nonnegative number has a larger square, \(s_{\mathrm{x}} \le |P_{\mathrm{n}} P|\). In the same way \(s_{\mathrm{y}} \le |P_{\mathrm{n}} P|\) and \(s_{\mathrm{z}} \le |P_{\mathrm{n}} P|\).
  3. We get the square of a sum of three numbers by multiplying out the brackets \((s_{\mathrm{x}} + s_{\mathrm{y}} + s_{\mathrm{z}})(s_{\mathrm{x}} + s_{\mathrm{y}} + s_{\mathrm{z}})\):
    \[(s_{\mathrm{x}} + s_{\mathrm{y}} + s_{\mathrm{z}})^2 = s_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + s_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + s_{\mathrm{z}}^2 + 2\,s_{\mathrm{x}} s_{\mathrm{y}} + 2\,s_{\mathrm{x}} s_{\mathrm{z}} + 2\,s_{\mathrm{y}} s_{\mathrm{z}} .\]
    The last three terms are not negative, so \((s_{\mathrm{x}} + s_{\mathrm{y}} + s_{\mathrm{z}})^2 \ge |P_{\mathrm{n}} P|^2\), and by the same lemma \(|P_{\mathrm{n}} P| \le s_{\mathrm{x}} + s_{\mathrm{y}} + s_{\mathrm{z}}\).
  4. The sequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) converges to \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\) if and only if the sequence \((s_{\mathrm{x}})\) converges to \(0\). By the definition of the limit of a sequence 1.38 both claims require the same, \(|x_{\mathrm{n}} - x_{\mathrm{P}}| < \eta\) for every \(n \ge N\), because \(|s_{\mathrm{x}} - 0| = s_{\mathrm{x}} = |x_{\mathrm{n}} - x_{\mathrm{P}}|\). The same holds for \(y\) and \(z\).
  5. Let \((P_{\mathrm{n}})\) converge to \(P\), that is, \(|P_{\mathrm{n}} P|\) to \(0\). By step 2, \(0 \le s_{\mathrm{x}} \le |P_{\mathrm{n}} P|\) for every \(n\). So by the squeeze lemma 1.46 with the lower sequence \(0, 0, \dots\) and the upper sequence \((|P_{\mathrm{n}} P|)\), \(s_{\mathrm{x}}\) converges to \(0\), and by step 4 \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) converges to \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\). The same holds for \(y\) and \(z\).
  6. Conversely, let \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\), \((y_{\mathrm{n}})\) and \((z_{\mathrm{n}})\) converge to \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\), \(y_{\mathrm{P}}\) and \(z_{\mathrm{P}}\). By step 4, \(s_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(s_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(s_{\mathrm{z}}\) go to \(0\), and by lemma 1.60 on the limit laws for sequences, item 1, used twice, their sum goes to \(0 + 0 + 0 = 0\).
  7. By step 3, \(0 \le |P_{\mathrm{n}} P| \le s_{\mathrm{x}} + s_{\mathrm{y}} + s_{\mathrm{z}}\) for every \(n\). So by the squeeze lemma \(|P_{\mathrm{n}} P|\) converges to \(0\), and \((P_{\mathrm{n}})\) converges to \(P\).

Source: LEBL1 Prop. 7.3.9 (PDF p. 276), for points with \(n\) coordinates. The source proves the second direction with the error \(\epsilon/\sqrt n\) for each coordinate, the bound by the sum is not from the source.

Now let us write continuity for a function of a point. The band \(\delta\) around the number \(c\) becomes a ball of radius \(\delta\) around the point \(P\) (figure 1.90).

Definition 1.67 Continuous function of a point in space (zvezna funkcija točke v prostoru)

Let \(D\) be a set of points of space and \(f\) a function whose input is a point \(Q\) from \(D\), given by three coordinates, and whose output is a number \(f(Q)\). The function \(f\) is continuous at the point \(P \in D\) if for every \(\eta > 0\) there is a \(\delta > 0\) such that

\[|f(Q) - f(P)| < \eta \quad \text{for every point } Q \in D \text{ with } |PQ| < \delta . \tag{1.28}\]

The function \(f\) is continuous on the set \(D\) if it is continuous at every point of the set \(D\).

Source: LEBL1 Def. 7.5.1 (PDF p. 288), for functions between sets in which a distance is given.

The set \(D\) is, for example, the set of all points of a bar. As with numbers, the condition checks only points of \(D\), so at the surface of a body it looks only at points on one side, in the body. Continuity is defined in the same way for functions whose input is a point of a plane, that is, two coordinates, or a group of \(n\) coordinates, and more generally still for functions between sets in which a distance is given (LEBL1 Def. 7.5.1, PDF p. 288). The definition of the limit of a sequence of points 1.65 has such a general form too (LEBL1 Def. 7.3.2, PDF p. 274).

For functions of a point the same lemma on sequences holds as for functions of a number, with the same proof.

Lemma 1.68 Continuity through sequences of points (zveznost z zaporedji točk)

Let \(f\) be a function defined on a set of points \(D\), and \(P \in D\). The function \(f\) is continuous at the point \(P\) (definition 1.67) if and only if for every sequence of points \((Q_{\mathrm{n}})\) with terms in \(D\) that converges to \(P\), the sequence \((f(Q_{\mathrm{n}}))\) converges to \(f(P)\).

Proof

The proof is the proof of lemma 1.61 on continuity through sequences, with the distance \(|PQ|\) in place of the distance \(|x - c|\). Let us write it out.

Continuity implies that the values converge.

  1. Let \(f\) be continuous at \(P\), and let the sequence of points \((Q_{\mathrm{n}})\) with terms in \(D\) converge to \(P\). We pick any error \(\eta > 0\).
  2. By definition 1.67 there is a \(\delta > 0\) such that \(|f(Q) - f(P)| < \eta\) for every point \(Q \in D\) with \(|PQ| < \delta\).
  3. By the definition of the limit of a sequence of points 1.65 the distances \(|Q_{\mathrm{n}} P|\) converge to \(0\), so for the error \(\delta\) there is a place \(N\) such that \(|Q_{\mathrm{n}} P| < \delta\) for every \(n \ge N\). The distance \(|P Q_{\mathrm{n}}|\) equals \(|Q_{\mathrm{n}} P|\), because the vectors \(\overrightarrow{PQ_{\mathrm{n}}}\) and \(\overrightarrow{Q_{\mathrm{n}} P}\) are opposite and so have the same length.
  4. So for \(n \ge N\) step 2 gives \(|f(Q_{\mathrm{n}}) - f(P)| < \eta\). We picked the error freely, so \((f(Q_{\mathrm{n}}))\) converges to \(f(P)\).

Convergence of the values implies continuity.

  1. Suppose that the values converge to \(f(P)\) for every sequence of points from \(D\) that converges to \(P\). We argue by contradiction: suppose that \(f\) is not continuous at \(P\).
  2. As in step 2 of the second direction of the proof of lemma 1.61, there is an error \(\eta > 0\) such that for every band \(\delta > 0\) there is a point \(Q \in D\) with \(|PQ| < \delta\) and \(|f(Q) - f(P)| \ge \eta\).
  3. For every natural number \(n\) we use step 2 with the band \(\delta = 1/n\) and choose such a point \(Q_{\mathrm{n}}\). For it, \(Q_{\mathrm{n}} \in D\), \(|P Q_{\mathrm{n}}| < 1/n\) and \(|f(Q_{\mathrm{n}}) - f(P)| \ge \eta\).
  4. The distances are trapped, \(0 \le |Q_{\mathrm{n}} P| < 1/n\). By the squeeze lemma 1.46 with the lower sequence \(0, 0, \dots\) and the upper sequence \((1/n)\), which converges to \(0\) by lemma 1.39 on the limit of the sequence 1/n, the distances converge to \(0\), and \((Q_{\mathrm{n}})\) converges to \(P\).
  5. So by the assumption of step 1, \((f(Q_{\mathrm{n}}))\) converges to \(f(P)\), and there is a place \(N\) with \(|f(Q_{\mathrm{N}}) - f(P)| < \eta\). This contradicts step 3. So \(f\) is continuous at \(P\).

Source: LEBL1 Prop. 7.5.2 (PDF p. 288).

Corollary 1.69 Continuity of sums, products and polynomials in space (zveznost vsot, zmnožkov in polinomov v prostoru)
  1. Let the functions \(f\) and \(g\), defined on a set of points \(D\), be continuous at the point \(P \in D\) (definition 1.67), and let \(k\) be a number. Then the functions \(f + g\), \(k f\) and \(f g\) are also continuous at \(P\).
  2. Every polynomial in three variables \(x\), \(y\), \(z\) is, as a function of the point \((x, y, z)\), continuous at every point of space.
Proof

We must show both items. We prove the first as item 1 of corollary 1.64 on the sum and product of continuous functions, and the second from the first by induction.

1. Sum, multiple and product.

  1. Let the sequence of points \((Q_{\mathrm{n}})\) with terms in \(D\) converge to \(P\). By lemma 1.68 on continuity through sequences of points, first direction, \((f(Q_{\mathrm{n}}))\) converges to \(f(P)\) and \((g(Q_{\mathrm{n}}))\) to \(g(P)\).
  2. By lemma 1.60 on the limit laws for sequences, items 1, 2 and 3, the sequences \((f(Q_{\mathrm{n}}) + g(Q_{\mathrm{n}}))\), \((k\,f(Q_{\mathrm{n}}))\) and \((f(Q_{\mathrm{n}})\,g(Q_{\mathrm{n}}))\) converge to \(f(P) + g(P)\), \(k\,f(P)\) and \(f(P)\,g(P)\).
  3. We chose the sequence freely, so by lemma 1.68, second direction, the functions \(f + g\), \(k f\) and \(f g\) are continuous at \(P\).

2. Polynomials.

  1. The function that assigns to a point \(Q\) its coordinate \(x_{\mathrm{Q}}\) is continuous at every point \(P\). If a sequence of points \((Q_{\mathrm{n}})\) converges to \(P\), then by lemma 1.66 on convergence coordinate by coordinate their \(x\) coordinates converge to \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\), and by lemma 1.68 the function is continuous at \(P\). The same holds for the coordinates \(y\) and \(z\).
  2. A constant function is continuous at every point, because the difference of its values is always \(0 < \eta\).
  3. A term of a polynomial is the product of a number \(b\) and \(m\) factors, each of which is \(x\), \(y\) or \(z\). By induction on \(m\) we show that it is continuous at \(P\). For \(m = 1\) the term is the product of a constant function and one coordinate, continuous by steps 1, 2 and item 1. A term with \(m + 1\) factors is the product of a term with \(m\) factors and one coordinate, continuous by item 1. A term without factors is a constant number, continuous by step 2.
  4. A polynomial is a sum of finitely many terms. By induction on the number of terms and by item 1 for the sum, it is continuous at \(P\): a sum of one term is the term itself, and when we add one more continuous term to a sum of continuous terms, the new sum is continuous by item 1.

Source: LEBL1 Example 7.5.3 (PDF p. 288), for two variables, where the proof uses the rules for limits of sequences. The proof by induction on factors and terms is not from the source.

Now we can check the second fact from the start of the section for the hanging bar. Recall: a steel bar of length \(L = 10\) m hangs under its own weight, fixed only at the centre \(O\) of its top end, and the weight is carried by a force that pulls the top face up evenly. The axis \(x\) points vertically down from \(O\), the axes \(y\) and \(z\) lie horizontally, and the displacement field is (1.18). We computed all nine partial derivatives in section 1.7.2. The row gives the component of the displacement, the column the variable we differentiate by:

by \(x\) by \(y\) by \(z\)
\(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) \(2a\,(L - x)\) \(-0{,}6\,a\,y\) \(-0{,}6\,a\,z\)
\(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) \(0{,}6\,a\,y\) \(-0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\) \(0\)
\(u_{\mathrm{z}}\) \(0{,}6\,a\,z\) \(0\) \(-0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\)

Each of them is a polynomial in three variables. For example \(2a\,(L - x) = 2aL + (-2a) \cdot x\) is the sum of the constant number \(2aL\) and a term with the factor \(x\), \(-0{,}6\,a\,y\) is a single term with the factor \(y\), and \(0\) is a constant number. So by corollary 1.69 on the continuity of sums, products and polynomials in space, item 2, all of them are continuous at every point of space, in particular at every point of the bar. So the partial derivatives of the hanging bar change without jumps, as the second fact assumes.

How close must a point be? Take the point \(P\) at depth \(x_{\mathrm{P}} = 5\) m, 4 mm to the right of the axis in the plane \(z = 0\), as in section 1.7.2, and the partial derivative \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial y = -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\), which at \(P\) equals \(-5{,}856 \cdot 10^{-7}\). For a point \(Q\) at depth \(x_{\mathrm{Q}}\) the difference is

\[\Big| -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x_{\mathrm{Q}}) + 0{,}6\,a\,(L - x_{\mathrm{P}}) \Big| = 0{,}6\,a\,|x_{\mathrm{Q}} - x_{\mathrm{P}}| \le 0{,}6\,a\,|PQ| ,\]

where we used that the difference of one coordinate is not larger than the distance (step 2 of the proof of lemma 1.66 on convergence coordinate by coordinate). Let the difference be smaller than one percent of the value at \(P\), that is, than \(\eta = 5{,}856 \cdot 10^{-9}\). It is enough that

\[|PQ| < \frac{5{,}856 \cdot 10^{-9}}{0{,}6\,a} = \frac{5{,}856 \cdot 10^{-9}}{1{,}1712 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1}} = 0{,}05\ \text{m} .\]

At all points of the bar closer to \(P\) than 5 cm the partial derivative differs from its value at \(P\) by less than one percent. Why exactly 5 cm? The partial derivative is proportional to the distance \(L - x\) to the lower end of the bar, which at \(P\) is 5 m, and one percent of 5 m is 5 cm.

Why does the definition require closeness in all directions at once, that is, a ball, and not only along the axes? Partial derivatives look at a function only along lines in the directions of the axes. A function, though, can be continuous along every axis and still not be continuous as a function of a point. Take the function of a point

\[f(x, y, z) = \frac{x\,y}{x^2 + y^2}\]

when \(x\) and \(y\) are not both zero, and \(f = 0\) on the axis \(z\), where \(x = y = 0\) (LEBL1, PDF p. 288, for two variables). On the axis \(x\) we have \(y = 0\), on the axis \(y\) we have \(x = 0\), so \(f\) is zero on all three axes. Along each axis it is constant, so continuous there, and all three partial derivatives at the origin \(O\) exist and are zero. On the line \(y = x\) in the plane \(z = 0\), however,

\[f(x, x, 0) = \frac{x \cdot x}{x^2 + x^2} = \frac{x^2}{2x^2} = \frac12 \quad \text{for every } x \neq 0 .\]

The points \(Q_{\mathrm{n}} = (1/n,\ 1/n,\ 0)\) converge to \(O\) by lemma 1.66 on convergence coordinate by coordinate, but the values \(f(Q_{\mathrm{n}}) = \tfrac12\) do not converge to \(f(O) = 0\). So by lemma 1.68 on continuity through sequences of points, \(f\) is not continuous at \(O\) (figure 1.91). Every ball around \(O\), however small, contains points of the line \(y = x\), where the value is \(\tfrac12\). The example shows two things. First, a function can have all its partial derivatives at a point and still not be continuous there. So the existence of the partial derivatives of the displacement does not by itself guarantee that the displacement changes without jumps near \(P\). Second, a function that is continuous along every axis need not be continuous as a function of a point. That is why the second fact requires the partial derivatives of the displacement to be continuous as functions of a point, that is, to differ only a little from their values at \(P\) at all points of a small enough ball around \(P\), not only at points on the lines through \(P\) in the directions of the axes.

Now we know precisely what a function without jumps is, and we can check continuity by the definition, with sequences and with the rules for sums and products. For the hanging bar we have also checked that its partial derivatives are continuous, as the second fact requires. The next section shows that a continuous function on a closed interval is largest somewhere and smallest somewhere. The proof will look for the point where the function is largest as the limit of a subsequence given by the Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem 1.57, and lemma 1.61 on continuity through sequences will tell us that the function really is largest at that point.
