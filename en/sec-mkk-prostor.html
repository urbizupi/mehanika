Chapter 15 Continuum finite elements

15.10 Solid elements: the tetrahedron and the brick

A vessel for compressed gas has the shape of a thick spherical shell, of the kind we solved exactly in chapter 11 (theorem 11.28): steel with \(E = 196\) GPa and \(\nu = 0{,}3\), inner radius \(a = 40\) mm, outer radius \(b = 60\) mm and pressure \(p = 30\) MPa on the inner surface. The exact solution has a single displacement \(u_{\mathrm{R}}(a) = 6{,}6864\ \mu\)m on the inner surface. But what about a body for which there is no solution, for example a bearing housing or an engine head? There we must cut the body into elements, not in a plane but in space. In sections 15.1 to 15.9 we learned all the ingredients in the plane: coordinates with areas, the element with a linear field, the isoparametric map, the Gauss rules, the patch test and locking. Now we carry them into space and test them on exactly this shell, to see how close we come to the Lamé solution.

In the plane the basic pieces were the triangle and the quadrilateral. In space two other pieces replace them. The first is the tetrahedron (tetraeder, from the Greek words for four and face: it has four triangular faces), which in space is what the triangle is in the plane: with it we can fill every body with flat faces. The second is the brick (opeka, hexahedron, "six-face", six quadrilateral faces), the image of a cube, which the isoparametric map bends and stretches, just as it does a square in the plane. Let us begin with the tetrahedron.

15.10.1 Volume coordinates and the tetrahedron

For the triangle we fixed a point by ratios of areas (definition 15.3). We do the same with volumes. Imagine a tetrahedron with nodes \(\vek x_1, \dots, \vek x_4\) and a point \(P\) in it (figure 15.38, left). The point \(P\) cuts the tetrahedron, with segments to the four nodes, into four smaller tetrahedra. The one opposite node \(1\) has volume \(V_1\), and comparing it with the volume \(V\) of the whole tetrahedron gives a number between \(0\) and \(1\) that tells how close to node \(1\) the point \(P\) lies. There are four such numbers and their sum is \(1\), because the four pieces add up to the whole. Before we name them we must prove that this is well defined, that is, that the volume \(V\) of the tetrahedron is really different from zero and that the numbers really determine the point.

Definition 15.66 Volume coordinates (prostorninske koordinate)

Let \(\vek x_1, \vek x_2, \vek x_3, \vek x_4\) be four points in space that do not lie in one plane, that is \(\det\mat A \ne 0\) for the matrix \(\mat A\) with columns \(\vek a = \vek x_2 - \vek x_1\), \(\vek b = \vek x_3 - \vek x_1\), \(\vek c = \vek x_4 - \vek x_1\). The volume coordinates (prostorninske koordinate) of a point \(\vek x\) are the numbers \(L_1, L_2, L_3, L_4\) for which

\[L_1 + L_2 + L_3 + L_4 = 1 , \qquad L_1\vek x_1 + L_2\vek x_2 + L_3\vek x_3 + L_4\vek x_4 = \vek x . \tag{15.51}\]

The tetrahedron (tetraeder) with nodes \(\vek x_1, \dots, \vek x_4\) is the set of points in which all four coordinates are nonnegative, \(L_{\mathrm{i}} \ge 0\). The coordinate \(L_{\mathrm{i}}\) is always written with an index, because \(L\) elsewhere in the book is a length. The volume of the tetrahedron is \(V = |\det\mat A|/6\).

Source: ONATE1 §8.6, equations (8.57) to (8.59) (PDF p. 300), where the \(L_{\mathrm{i}}\) are quotients of volumes. The form above with the equations (15.51) is mine, so that the coordinates exist also outside the tetrahedron.

We shall justify the name "volume" with the next lemma: the coordinate \(L_{\mathrm{i}}\) really is a ratio of volumes, as in the figure. The lemma also says that every point gets exactly one quadruple of numbers and that the coordinates are linear functions of the point.

Lemma 15.67 Properties of the volume coordinates (lastnosti prostorninskih koordinat)

Let \(\vek x_1, \dots, \vek x_4\) be as in definition 15.66. Then:

  1. For every point \(\vek x\) there is exactly one quadruple \((L_1, \dots, L_4)\) that satisfies the equations (15.51).
  2. Every coordinate is a polynomial of degree one in \(\vek x\), \(L_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek x) = c_{\mathrm{i}} + \vek g_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot\vek x\), with vectors \(\vek g_2 = \vek b\times\vek c/\det\mat A\), \(\vek g_3 = \vek c\times\vek a/\det\mat A\), \(\vek g_4 = \vek a\times\vek b/\det\mat A\) and \(\vek g_1 = -\vek g_2 - \vek g_3 - \vek g_4\). The gradient of a coordinate is therefore a constant vector.
  3. \(L_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek x_{\mathrm{j}}) = \delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\), and the coordinate \(L_{\mathrm{i}}\) is zero exactly in the plane of the face opposite node \(i\).
  4. For a point in the tetrahedron \(L_{\mathrm{i}} = V_{\mathrm{i}}/V\), where \(V_{\mathrm{i}}\) is the volume of the tetrahedron obtained when in \(\vek x_1, \dots, \vek x_4\) the node \(i\) is replaced by the point \(\vek x\).
  5. The tetrahedron has volume \(V = |\det\mat A|/6\), as the definition claims.
Proof

We must show that the system of equations (15.51) has a single solution, write it down and read off the volumes. The first equation is used to eliminate \(L_1\).

  1. From \(L_1 = 1 - L_2 - L_3 - L_4\) and the second equation (15.51) it follows that \(\vek x - \vek x_1 = L_2\vek a + L_3\vek b + L_4\vek c\), that is \(\mat A\,(L_2, L_3, L_4)^\T = \vek x - \vek x_1\).
  2. Since \(\det\mat A \ne 0\), by theorem 2.54 on the inverse matrix with the determinant, \(\mat A\) has an inverse with rows \(\vek b\times\vek c/\det\mat A\), \(\vek c\times\vek a/\det\mat A\), \(\vek a\times\vek b/\det\mat A\). So the solution is unique, \(L_2 = \vek g_2\cdot(\vek x - \vek x_1)\), \(L_3 = \vek g_3\cdot(\vek x - \vek x_1)\), \(L_4 = \vek g_4\cdot(\vek x - \vek x_1)\) and \(L_1 = 1 - L_2 - L_3 - L_4\). These are polynomials of degree one, with gradients \(\vek g_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(\vek g_1 = -\vek g_2 - \vek g_3 - \vek g_4\). This proves points 1 and 2.
  3. At \(\vek x = \vek x_{\mathrm{j}}\) we have \(\vek x_{\mathrm{j}} - \vek x_1 = \vek a\), \(\vek b\) or \(\vek c\) (or \(\vek 0\)), so by point 4 of lemma 2.31 (\(\vek a\cdot(\vek a\times\vek b) = 0\)) the scalar products \(\vek g_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot\vek a\) and so on equal \(\delta\), and \(\det\mat A = \vek a\cdot(\vek b\times\vek c)\). Thus \(L_2(\vek x_2) = \vek g_2\cdot\vek a = 1\), \(L_2(\vek x_3) = \vek g_2\cdot\vek b = 0\) and \(L_2(\vek x_4) = 0\), similarly for \(L_3\) and \(L_4\), and \(L_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek x_1) = 0\) for \(i = 2, 3, 4\), so \(L_1(\vek x_1) = 1\). The equation \(L_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\) is the equation of the plane through the three nodes that are not \(\vek x_{\mathrm{i}}\) (these are the nodes with \(L_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\) by the first part and \(\vek g_{\mathrm{i}} \ne \vek 0\)).
  4. For \(i = 2\) insert into the volume of the tetrahedron \(\vek x_1, \vek x, \vek x_3, \vek x_4\) the columns \(\vek x - \vek x_1\), \(\vek b\), \(\vek c\): its determinant is \((\vek x - \vek x_1)\cdot(\vek b\times\vek c) = L_2\det\mat A\). By point 5, then, \(V_2 = |L_2|\,V\), and in the tetrahedron \(L_2 \ge 0\). For \(i = 3, 4\) the computation is the same. For \(i = 1\) we renumber the nodes: the definition does not depend on the numbering.
  5. Let \(T_0\) be the tetrahedron with nodes \((0,0,0)\), \((1,0,0)\), \((0,1,0)\), \((0,0,1)\). By definition 4.20 for a body between graphs its volume is \(\int_0^1\int_0^{1-\xi}\int_0^{1-\xi-\eta}1\,\mathrm{d}\zeta\,\mathrm{d}\eta\,\mathrm{d}\xi = \tfrac16\). The map \(\vek\xi \mapsto \vek x_1 + \mat A\vek\xi\) takes \(T_0\) onto the tetrahedron with coordinates \(L_2 = \xi\), \(L_3 = \eta\), \(L_4 = \zeta\) and \(L_1 = 1 - \xi - \eta - \zeta\), that is, onto the set \(L_{\mathrm{i}} \ge 0\). By theorem 4.58 on the volume under a linear map, the volume of the image is \(|\det\mat A|\cdot\tfrac16\).

Source: ONATE1 §8.6 (PDF pp. 300-302): the definition of the coordinates as ratios of volumes and \(\sum L_{\mathrm{i}} = 1\). The proof with a system of equations and with the theorem on the inverse matrix is not from the source.

Example 15.68 A steel tetrahedron (jeklen tetraeder)

Take the tetrahedron with nodes \((0, 0, 0)\), \((40, 0, 0)\), \((10, 30, 0)\) and \((10, 10, 30)\) mm. The base face \(1\,2\,3\) is a triangle of area \(600\) mm² (the area of a triangle was computed in section 15.1), the height to node \(4\) is \(30\) mm, so \(V = 600\cdot 30/3 = 6000\) mm³. The determinant is \(\det\mat A = 36\,000\) mm³, six times the volume, as point 5 of the lemma requires (SymPy and NumPy, num-10.out). The point \(P = (15, 10, 6)\) mm cuts the tetrahedron into pieces with \(V_1 = 1650\), \(V_2 = 1550\), \(V_3 = 1600\), \(V_4 = 1200\) mm³, which add up to \(6000\) mm³, and

\[L_1 = 0{,}275 , \quad L_2 = 0{,}2583 , \quad L_3 = 0{,}2667 , \quad L_4 = 0{,}2 ,\]

as given also by the solution of the system (15.51) (check \(\sum L_{\mathrm{i}}\vek x_{\mathrm{i}} = (15, 10, 6)\) mm). The point is near the centroid, so all four are near \(1/4\). The smallest is \(L_4\), because, measured by the height of the tetrahedron, \(P\) is nearest to the face \(1\,2\,3\) opposite node \(4\): it is only \(6\) of \(30\) mm above it, and \(L_4 = 6/30 = 0{,}2\).

The gradients of the coordinates (unit 1/mm) are by point 2 of lemma 15.67

\[\vek g_1 = \begin{bmatrix} -0{,}025 \\ -0{,}025 \\ -0{,}01667 \end{bmatrix} , \quad \vek g_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0{,}025 \\ -0{,}00833 \\ -0{,}00556 \end{bmatrix} , \quad \vek g_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0{,}03333 \\ -0{,}01111 \end{bmatrix} , \quad \vek g_4 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0{,}03333 \end{bmatrix} ,\]

the sum is \(\vek 0\), since \(\sum L_{\mathrm{i}} = 1\) is constant. For \(\vek g_4\) only the third component is different from zero, \(1/30\) mm⁻¹: \(L_4\) grows from \(0\) on the plane \(z = 0\) to \(1\) at the height \(30\) mm.

The name is justified. Now we make a finite element out of the tetrahedron, as we made one out of two nodes for the bar element (definition 7.1).

Definition 15.69 The four-node tetrahedron (tetraeder s štirimi vozlišči, T4)

The element T4 is a tetrahedron with nodes \(\vek x_1, \dots, \vek x_4\) (definition 15.66) and with three displacements \(u_{\mathrm{x}}, u_{\mathrm{y}}, u_{\mathrm{z}}\) at each node, in all \(12\) degrees of freedom, collected in a column \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\). The shape functions (definition 7.2) are the volume coordinates, \(N_{\mathrm{i}} = L_{\mathrm{i}}\), and the field of displacements in the element is

\[\vek u(\vek x) = \sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{4} L_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek x)\,\vek u_{\mathrm{i}} . \tag{15.52}\]

The vector \(\vek u_{\mathrm{i}}\) is the displacement of node \(i\).

Source: ONATE1 §8.6 (PDF p. 301): for the four-node tetrahedron the volume coordinates coincide with the shape functions. COOK section 6.5 (PDF pp. 231-232) knows the same element as a special case of the isoparametric 3D element.

Two questions are the same as for the triangle. Can the field (15.52) show the simple states, for example a translation and a stretch, that we know? And do neighbouring elements agree on a common face, so that no crack appears in the body? The answer yes to both is given by the next lemma.

Lemma 15.70 The field of the T4 element (polje elementa T4)

Let \(\vek u\) be the field (15.52) on a T4 element.

  1. The field is affine, \(\vek u(\vek x) = \vek c + \mat W\vek x\), and the gradient \(\mat W\) is constant. Every affine field \(\vek c + \mat W\vek x\) is on the element equal to the field (15.52) with nodal values \(\vek u_{\mathrm{i}} = \vek c + \mat W\vek x_{\mathrm{i}}\).
  2. If two T4 elements have a common face with three nodes, their fields agree on the face, because there they depend only on the three common nodal values.
Proof

We must show that the field is a polynomial of degree one, that the affine fields are contained and that the value on a face is determined by three nodes.

  1. By point 2 of lemma 15.67 every \(L_{\mathrm{i}}\) is a polynomial of degree one, so the sum (15.52), a linear combination of them, is also a polynomial of degree one in \(\vek x\).
  2. Let \(\vek u_{\mathrm{i}} = \vek c + \mat W\vek x_{\mathrm{i}}\). Then \(\sum L_{\mathrm{i}}\vek u_{\mathrm{i}} = \vek c\sum L_{\mathrm{i}} + \mat W\sum L_{\mathrm{i}}\vek x_{\mathrm{i}} = \vek c + \mat W\vek x\) by the equations (15.51).
  3. On the face opposite node \(i\) we have \(L_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\) (point 3 of lemma 15.67), so the field there is \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}\ne \mathrm{i}}L_{\mathrm{j}}\vek u_{\mathrm{j}}\). The remaining three coordinates are on the plane of the face the area coordinates of the triangle (definition 15.3), determined by the three nodes and the point. Two elements that share these three nodes have on the face the same coordinates and the same nodal values, so the same field.

Source: The proof is not from the source. ONATE1 §8.6 (PDF pp. 300-301) gives the coordinates and the linear interpolation.

Point 2 of the lemma says that the field is continuous across a face, so the field on a mesh of tetrahedra is a piecewise smooth field (section 15.2): for T4 the theorems for the Ritz method hold, as proved in that section. The condition "three common nodes" is not small. If a node of one tetrahedron lies in the interior of a face of another, the two do not share it and the fields on the face are not determined by the same numbers, so in general they differ and a jump appears, as for the hanging node of triangles (section 15.1).

The tetrahedron has one more condition, which we already required in definition 15.66: the four nodes must not lie in a plane. Let us see what happens when we approach this limit. In example 15.68 we lower node \(4\) from the height \(30\) mm to \(h = 3\) mm. The gradient \(\vek g_4\) is then \((0, 0, 1/h) = (0, 0, 0{,}3333)\) mm⁻¹, ten times larger, and as \(h \to 0\) it grows beyond all bounds, because \(L_4\) rises from \(0\) to \(1\) over an ever shorter height. The third components of the other three gradients are \(-1/(2h)\), \(-1/(6h)\) and \(-1/(3h)\) (SymPy) and grow in the same way. The stiffness matrix, which we derive below, is the volume \(V = 200\,h\) mm³ times a product of two matrices made of the gradients, so its entries grow like \(1/h\): a very flat tetrahedron is useless. At \(h = 0\) we have \(\det\mat A = 0\) and the coordinates do not exist.

Heat conduction on the tetrahedron

First we look at a scalar field, the temperature, as for the triangle (section 15.3): one unknown per node, no \(3 \times 3\) blocks. The functional of the heat problem is \(\frac12\int k\,|\nabla T|^2\,\mathrm{d}V\) plus the load terms (chapter 14), \(k\) is the thermal conductivity. What do we get if \(T\) is the field (15.52) with nodal temperatures \(T_{\mathrm{i}}\)?

Lemma 15.71 Conduction matrix of the tetrahedron (prevodnostna matrika tetraedra)

Let \(\mat G\) be the \(3 \times 4\) matrix with columns \(\vek g_1, \dots, \vek g_4\) from lemma 15.67, \(k\) the thermal conductivity and \(V\) the volume of the element. The temperature \(T = \sum L_{\mathrm{i}} T_{\mathrm{i}}\) has in the element a constant gradient \(\nabla T = \mat G\vek T_{\mathrm{e}}\) and

\[\frac12\int_{\text{element}} k\,|\nabla T|^2\,\mathrm{d}V = \frac12\,\vek T_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek T_{\mathrm{e}} , \qquad \mat k_{\mathrm{e}} = k\,V\,\mat G^\T\mat G . \tag{15.53}\]

The matrix \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) is symmetric and positive semidefinite, the sum of every row is zero, and \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\vek T = \vek 0\) exactly for constant temperatures \(T_{\mathrm{i}} = \text{const}\).

Proof

We must show that the integral can be computed as a product and that the matrix has the listed properties.

  1. By point 2 of lemma 15.67 \(\nabla L_{\mathrm{i}} = \vek g_{\mathrm{i}}\) is constant, so \(\nabla T = \sum T_{\mathrm{i}}\vek g_{\mathrm{i}} = \mat G\vek T_{\mathrm{e}}\).
  2. The integrand \(k\,|\mat G\vek T_{\mathrm{e}}|^2\) is constant in the element, so its integral over a set of volume \(V\) equals the value times \(V\) (lemma 4.32, point 1): \(\tfrac12 k V\,\vek T_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat G^\T\mat G\vek T_{\mathrm{e}}\). This is the equation (15.53).
  3. The matrix is symmetric, since \((\mat G^\T\mat G)^\T = \mat G^\T\mat G\). For arbitrary \(\vek T_{\mathrm{e}}\), \(\vek T_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\vek T_{\mathrm{e}} = kV\,|\mat G\vek T_{\mathrm{e}}|^2 \ge 0\). So the matrix is positive semidefinite, and by lemma 7.16 \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\vek T_{\mathrm{e}} = \vek 0\) exactly when \(\mat G\vek T_{\mathrm{e}} = \vek 0\).
  4. The sum of the coordinates is \(1\), so \(\sum\vek g_{\mathrm{i}} = \vek 0\), that is \(\mat G(1, 1, 1, 1)^\T = \vek 0\). Hence \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\vek 1 = \vek 0\): the sum of every row is zero. Conversely, if \(\mat G\vek T_{\mathrm{e}} = \vek 0\), the gradient of the field \(T\) is zero, and the field is affine (lemma 15.70), so constant.

Source: The form after ONATE1 §4.3 (PDF pp. 155-168) for the triangle, the same with three components of the gradient. COOK section 12.2 (PDF pp. 473-475). The proof is not from the source.

For the tetrahedron of example 15.68 at \(k = 1\) W/(mm K) the matrix (unit W/K) is

\[\mat k_{\mathrm{e}} = \begin{bmatrix} 9{,}167 & -1{,}944 & -3{,}889 & -3{,}333 \\ -1{,}944 & 4{,}352 & -1{,}296 & -1{,}111 \\ -3{,}889 & -1{,}296 & 7{,}407 & -2{,}222 \\ -3{,}333 & -1{,}111 & -2{,}222 & 6{,}667 \end{bmatrix} ,\]

with eigenvalues \(0\), \(5{,}719\), \(9{,}193\) and \(12{,}681\) (num-10.out). One eigenvalue is zero, since the kernel of the matrix is a single direction, constant temperature. The field \(T = 20 + 0{,}5\,x - 0{,}2\,y + 0{,}1\,z\) has at the nodes the values \(20\), \(40\), \(19\) and \(26\), and \(\mat G\vek T_{\mathrm{e}} = (0{,}5, -0{,}2, 0{,}1)\) K/mm is exactly its gradient: the element shows every linear temperature distribution.

For the triangle the edges where the body gives heat to its surroundings are segments. For the tetrahedron they are faces, which have an area. For the convection terms we need integrals of the products \(L_{\mathrm{i}}L_{\mathrm{j}}\) over a face, which are given by the formula for area coordinates (lemma 15.9): \(\int L_{\mathrm{i}}L_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{d}A = A/12\) for \(i \ne j\) and \(\int L_{\mathrm{i}}^2\,\mathrm{d}A = A/6\), where \(A\) is the area of the face and \(i\) and \(j\) are nodes of that face (the coordinate of the fourth node is zero on the face). The convection matrix of each face is assembled from them, as given for the triangle in chapter 14. The spatial problem (AK 9) is thus the same as the planar one: the same element matrix \(kV\mat G^\T\mat G\), only the vectors \(\vek g_{\mathrm{i}}\) have three components, and the assembly by theorem 7.13 is the same.

Stiffness matrix of the tetrahedron

In elasticity the unknowns are three displacements at a node, and the strain is a symmetric tensor with six components. In Voigt notation (definition 3.18, order \(xx\), \(yy\), \(zz\), \(yz\), \(xz\), \(xy\) with engineering shear strains \(\gamma = 2\varepsilon\)) the small strain tensor (definition 1.115) is linear in the displacements: \(\{\varepsilon\} = \mat B\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\). For T4 the \(6 \times 12\) matrix \(\mat B\) has blocks with three columns each

\[\mat B_{\mathrm{i}} = \begin{bmatrix} g_{\mathrm{ix}} & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & g_{\mathrm{iy}} & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & g_{\mathrm{iz}} \\ 0 & g_{\mathrm{iz}} & g_{\mathrm{iy}} \\ g_{\mathrm{iz}} & 0 & g_{\mathrm{ix}} \\ g_{\mathrm{iy}} & g_{\mathrm{ix}} & 0 \end{bmatrix} , \qquad \mat B = \begin{bmatrix} \mat B_1 & \mat B_2 & \mat B_3 & \mat B_4 \end{bmatrix} , \tag{15.54}\]

where \(g_{\mathrm{ix}}, g_{\mathrm{iy}}, g_{\mathrm{iz}}\) are the components of the vector \(\vek g_{\mathrm{i}}\). Since the \(\vek g_{\mathrm{i}}\) are constant, \(\mat B\) is a constant matrix, and the strain in the element is constant: the element is the spatial relative of the constant-strain triangle (section 15.4).

The material matrix \(\mat C\) of an isotropic material in Voigt order (theorem 3.11, lemma 3.19) has the value \(\lambda + 2\mu\) on the diagonal of the upper \(3 \times 3\) block, \(\lambda\) off the diagonal and \(\mu\) in the remaining diagonal (ONATE1 writes this matrix \(\mat D\), we keep \(\mat C\) from chapter 3). For steel with \(E = 196\) GPa and \(\nu = 0{,}3\) we have \(\lambda = 113{,}08\) GPa, \(\mu = 75{,}38\) GPa and \(\lambda + 2\mu = 263{,}85\) GPa.

Lemma 15.72 Stiffness matrix of the tetrahedron (togostna matrika tetraedra)

Let \(\mat C\) be the material matrix of an isotropic linearly elastic material with \(E > 0\) and \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\), and \(V\) the volume of the T4 element. The strain energy of the element is \(\tfrac12\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) with

\[\mat k_{\mathrm{e}} = V\,\mat B^\T\mat C\,\mat B . \tag{15.55}\]

The matrix \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) is symmetric and positive semidefinite and \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\vek d = \vek 0\) exactly for six independent rigid motions \(\vek u = \vek a + \vek\omega\times\vek x\), at the nodes \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = (\vek a + \vek\omega\times\vek x_{\mathrm{i}})_{\mathrm{i}}\) with arbitrary \(\vek a\) and \(\vek\omega\). Of the twelve columns of the matrix, \(12 - 6 = 6\) are independent.

Proof

We must show that the energy gives the written matrix, and then find the vectors for which the energy is zero.

  1. The strain energy density is \(\tfrac12\{\varepsilon\}^\T\mat C\{\varepsilon\}\) (definition 4.85) and for an isotropic material with the given constants it is positive for every nonzero strain (theorem 3.48). Since \(\{\varepsilon\} = \mat B\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) is constant, the energy of the element by lemma 4.32, point 1, equals \(\tfrac12 V\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat B^\T\mat C\mat B\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\).
  2. The matrix \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) is symmetric, because \(\mat C\) is symmetric. For every \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\), \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = V\{\varepsilon\}^\T\mat C\{\varepsilon\} \ge 0\), and it is zero exactly when \(\{\varepsilon\} = \vek 0\) (point 1). By lemma 7.16 the kernel of \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) is the set of all \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) with \(\mat B\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = \vek 0\).
  3. The field of the element is affine, \(\vek u = \vek c + \mat W\vek x\) (lemma 15.70) and \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) determines it uniquely, since the four nodes are not coplanar. The strain is the symmetric part \(\tfrac12(\mat W + \mat W^\T)\) of the matrix \(\mat W\) (definition 1.115), and is zero exactly for a skew-symmetric \(\mat W\). Such a \(\mat W\) has three independent numbers, and we write it as \(\mat W\vek x = \vek\omega\times\vek x\). The kernel is thus \(\{\vek c + \vek\omega\times\vek x\}\) with six parameters \(\vek c\) and \(\vek\omega\) and has dimension \(6\).

Source: ONATE1 §8.3 and §8.6 (PDF pp. 284-286, 300-306) and COOK section 6.5 (PDF pp. 231-232) for the form \(\mat B^\T\mat C\mat B\,V\). The proof about the kernel is not from the source.

For the tetrahedron of example 15.68 the \(12 \times 12\) matrix \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) has exactly six eigenvalues that, compared with the largest (\(4{,}7\cdot 10^9\) N/m), are zero up to rounding (up to \(10^{-6}\) N/m), and the remaining six lie between \(9{,}9\cdot 10^8\) and \(4{,}7\cdot 10^9\) N/m. For three translations \(\vek a\) and three rotations \(\vek\omega\), \(\|\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\vek d\|/(\|\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\|\,\|\vek d\|) \sim 10^{-17}\). For the affine field \(\vek u = \mat W\vek x\) with \(\mat W = 10^{-3}\begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 & 0\\ 0 & -1 & 3\\ 1 & 0 & 2\end{bmatrix}\) and nodal displacements \(\vek u_{\mathrm{i}} = \mat W\vek x_{\mathrm{i}}\), \(\mat B\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) gives the strain \((1, -1, 2, 3, 1, 2)\cdot 10^{-3}\), as given directly by \(\tfrac12(\mat W + \mat W^\T)\) with \(\gamma = 2\varepsilon\), for example \(\gamma_{\mathrm{yz}} = W_{\mathrm{yz}} + W_{\mathrm{zy}} = (3 + 0)\cdot 10^{-3}\) (num-10.out).

Load on the tetrahedron

As for the bar (definition 7.7) we move the load to the nodes with the same requirement: the work stays the same. For T4 two integrals of the shape functions are needed.

Lemma 15.73 Consistent forces of the tetrahedron (konsistentne sile tetraedra)

Let a T4 element have volume \(V\).

  1. A constant body force \(\vek b\) (N/m³) gives every node the force \(\vek b\,V/4\).
  2. A constant surface force \(\vek t\) (N/m²) on a face of area \(A\) gives each of the three nodes of that face the force \(\vek t\,A/3\), and the fourth node zero.
Proof

We must show \(\int L_{\mathrm{i}}\,\mathrm{d}V = V/4\) and \(\int L_{\mathrm{i}}\,\mathrm{d}A = A/3\).

  1. The consistent force is \(\vek f_{\mathrm{i}} = \int\vek b\,L_{\mathrm{i}}\,\mathrm{d}V\) (definition 7.7 in three dimensions). By theorem 4.59 on the change of variables with the affine map \(\vek\xi \mapsto \vek x_1 + \mat A\vek\xi\) from the proof of lemma 15.67 (point 5), \(\int L_2\,\mathrm{d}V = |\det\mat A|\int_{\mathrm{T}_0}\xi\,\mathrm{d}V = 6V\cdot\tfrac1{24} = V/4\), since \(\int_{\mathrm{T}_0}\xi = \tfrac1{24}\) (iterated integral, SymPy). The same for \(L_3\) and \(L_4\), and from \(L_1 = 1 - L_2 - L_3 - L_4\) it follows that \(\int L_1 = V - \tfrac34V = V/4\).
  2. On a face one coordinate is zero, the remaining three are the area coordinates of the face, and by lemma 15.9 with \(a = 1\), \(\int L_{\mathrm{i}}\,\mathrm{d}A = 1!\cdot 2A/3! = A/3\).

Source: ONATE1 §8.3.5 (PDF pp. 286-288) for the notion of "equivalent nodal force", the formula follows from the computation above.

Our steel tetrahedron of density \(7850\) kg/m³ weighs \(\rho\,g\,V = 0{,}462\) N (\(V = 6\cdot 10^{-6}\) m³), and each of the four nodes receives \(0{,}1155\) N downwards. If a pressure of \(1\) MPa acts on the face \(1\,2\,3\), that is a force of \(600\) N on the area \(600\) mm², each of the nodes \(1\), \(2\), \(3\) receives \(200\) N. A point force acts directly on a node. An initial strain due to temperature (chapter 3, definition 3.13) gives the force \(V\mat B^\T\mat C\{\varepsilon_0\}\) with \(\{\varepsilon_0\} = \alpha\Delta T\,(1, 1, 1, 0, 0, 0)^\T\), as for the bar (lemma 7.49) and in the plane (section 15.4).

15.10.2 The brick with eight nodes

The tetrahedron has constant strain, which in bending is too stiff, as we saw for the triangle and the cantilever. A better piece is the image of a cube. Let \([-1, 1]^3\) be a cube with natural coordinates \(\xi\), \(\eta\), \(\zeta\) (figure 15.39, left). Its nodes are numbered \(1\) to \(8\) and lie at the corners \((\xi_{\mathrm{i}}, \eta_{\mathrm{i}}, \zeta_{\mathrm{i}}) \in \{-1, 1\}^3\), the lower quadrilateral \(1\) to \(4\) at \(\zeta = -1\) and the upper \(5\) to \(8\) at \(\zeta = 1\). The axes are the directions of increasing coordinates. For each node we make a function that is \(1\) at it and \(0\) at the other seven: a product of three linear functions, one in each coordinate.

Definition 15.74 The eight-node brick (opeka z osmimi vozlišči, H8)

Let \(\vek x_1, \dots, \vek x_8\) be nodes numbered like the corners of the cube \([-1, 1]^3\). The shape functions (definition 7.2) are

\[N_{\mathrm{i}}(\xi, \eta, \zeta) = \tfrac18\,(1 + \xi_{\mathrm{i}}\xi)(1 + \eta_{\mathrm{i}}\eta)(1 + \zeta_{\mathrm{i}}\zeta) , \qquad i = 1, \dots, 8 , \tag{15.56}\]

with \(\xi_{\mathrm{i}}, \eta_{\mathrm{i}}, \zeta_{\mathrm{i}} \in \{-1, 1\}\) the coordinates of node \(i\) in the cube. The geometry and the displacement field follow the same recipe (isoparametric element, definition 15.30):

\[\Phi(\xi, \eta, \zeta) = \sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{8}N_{\mathrm{i}}\,\vek x_{\mathrm{i}} , \qquad \vek u = \sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{8}N_{\mathrm{i}}\,\vek u_{\mathrm{i}} , \tag{15.57}\]

and \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) has \(24\) numbers. The brick (hexahedron) is the image \(\Phi([-1, 1]^3)\) if \(\Phi\) is a curved box (definition 4.65).

Source: ONATE1 §8.4 (PDF pp. 288-289), COOK section 6.5 (PDF pp. 231-232), equation (6.5-2) for the Jacobian matrix.

The functions (15.56) have the same properties as for the quadrilateral (section 15.5), and the proof is the same in three coordinates. In short: \(N_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek\xi_{\mathrm{j}}) = \delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\), the sum of the functions is the product \(\tfrac12[(1 - \xi) + (1 + \xi)]\cdot\tfrac12[(1 - \eta) + (1 + \eta)]\cdot\tfrac12[(1 - \zeta) + (1 + \zeta)] = 1\), and therefore the field (15.57) contains every affine field on every brick (lemma 15.35). On the face \(\zeta = 1\) the four functions with \(\zeta_{\mathrm{i}} = -1\) are zero, so the map and the field there are determined by the four nodes of the face, and two bricks with a common face of four nodes agree on it. The condition that all four nodes are common is necessary here too.

For the quadrilateral the Jacobian determinant was linear and its sign could be seen already at the corners. Here it is not so.

Lemma 15.75 Jacobian determinant of the brick (Jacobijeva determinanta opeke)

Let \(J = \det\mat F_\Phi\) be the determinant of the matrix of derivatives \(\partial\Phi_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial\xi_{\mathrm{j}}\) of the map (15.57).

  1. \(J\) is a polynomial of degree at most \(2\) in each of the coordinates \(\xi\), \(\eta\), \(\zeta\).
  2. If the nodes are the corners of a parallelepiped, numbered so that \(\det\mat A > 0\) for the matrix \(\mat A\) of the edges from node \(1\) (in the proof), \(J\) is constant, equal to \(V/8\), where \(V\) is the volume of the parallelepiped.
  3. If \(\Phi\) is a curved box, the volume of the brick is \(V = \int_{[-1,1]^3}J\,\mathrm{d}V = \sum_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}\in\{1,2\}}w_{\mathrm{i}}w_{\mathrm{j}}w_{\mathrm{k}}\,J(\xi_{\mathrm{i}}, \xi_{\mathrm{j}}, \xi_{\mathrm{k}})\) with the Gauss rule of two points in each coordinate.
Proof

We must show the degree estimate, the value for a parallelepiped and the exactness of the rule.

  1. The column \(\partial\Phi/\partial\xi\) contains the functions \(\tfrac18\xi_{\mathrm{i}}(1 + \eta_{\mathrm{i}}\eta)(1 + \zeta_{\mathrm{i}}\zeta)\), which are of degree \(0\) in \(\xi\) and \(1\) in \(\eta\) and \(\zeta\). Similarly the columns \(\partial\Phi/\partial\eta\) and \(\partial\Phi/\partial\zeta\) are of degree \(0\) in their own coordinate and \(1\) in the other two. Every term of the expansion of the determinant (definition 2.50) is a product of one element from each column, so it is in \(\xi\) of degree at most \(0 + 1 + 1 = 2\), the same in \(\eta\) and \(\zeta\).
  2. For a parallelepiped \(\vek x_{\mathrm{i}} = \vek x_1 + \tfrac12[(1 + \xi_{\mathrm{i}})\vek a + (1 + \eta_{\mathrm{i}})\vek b + (1 + \zeta_{\mathrm{i}})\vek c]\) with edges \(\vek a, \vek b, \vek c\), and \(\Phi(\vek\xi) = \vek x_1 + \tfrac12\mat A\,(\vek\xi + \vek 1)\) is a linear map with matrix of derivatives \(\tfrac12\mat A\). Its determinant is \(\det\mat A/8\), and by theorem 4.58 the volume of the parallelepiped is \(|\det\mat A|\).
  3. By theorem 4.59, \(V = \int J\,\mathrm{d}V\). By theorem 4.9 the integral of a polynomial \(\xi^p\eta^q\zeta^r\) is a product of three one-dimensional integrals, and for \(p, q, r \le 2 \le 3\) each is computed exactly by the Gauss rule with two points (lemma 7.25, \(q = 2\) gives exactness up to degree \(3\)).

Source: COOK section 6.5 (PDF pp. 231-232) for the Jacobian matrix. The degree estimate and the proof are not from the source.

Example 15.76 An irregular brick (an irregular brick)

Take a brick with nodes (mm): \((0, 0, 0)\), \((40, 0, 0)\), \((46, 30, 0)\), \((-6, 30, 0)\), \((4, 4, 20)\), \((36, 2, 20)\), \((38, 26, 20)\), \((2, 28, 20)\) (figure 15.39, right). The map \(\Phi\) takes the cube to the brick: corner \(i\) of the cube goes to the node \(\vek x_{\mathrm{i}}\) with the same label. The lower face is a trapezoid, the upper a smaller irregular quadrilateral. The Jacobian determinant has at the corners the values \(3000\), \(3000\), \(3900\), \(3900\), \(1910\), \(1930\), \(2170\), \(2150\) mm³, at the centre \(2700\) mm³, and on a grid of \(21^3\) points the smallest \(1910\) and the largest \(3900\) mm³: it is positive everywhere. The volume by the rule with two points in each coordinate is \(21\,720\) mm³, by the rule with five points in each coordinate also \(21\,720\) mm³, as point 3 of the lemma requires. For the quadrilateral we got the area as \(4J\) at the centre. Here \(8J(0, 0, 0)\) would give \(21\,600\) mm³, \(0{,}55\) % too little: \(J\) is quadratic in \(\zeta\) (SymPy), so its value at the centre is not the average. For a box \(40 \times 30 \times 20\) mm, \(J = 3000\) mm³ everywhere, and indeed \(40\cdot 30\cdot 20/8 = 3000\).

We still have to decide when the map \(\Phi\) is a curved box, that is, when it is injective and has a positive Jacobian determinant in a neighbourhood of the cube (definition 4.65). For the quadrilateral (theorem 15.32) a check at the corners was enough. For the brick, where \(J\) is quadratic, it is not. Let us first see what goes wrong.

Example 15.77 A brick that is fine at the corners and folded inside (opeka, ki je v ogliščih v redu in znotraj prepognjena)

Let a brick have nodes (mm) \((-4, -3, 4)\), \((8, 8, 0)\), \((20, 10, 7)\), \((2, 12, -5)\), \((-19, 8, 20)\), \((31, 8, 13)\), \((16, 25, 13)\), \((-3, 20, 15)\). We found it by a systematic search over random perturbations of a cube of side \(20\) mm (num-10.py), and it is strongly distorted. The Jacobian determinant is positive at all eight corners: \(416{,}625\), \(68{,}875\), \(97{,}75\), \(1040{,}25\), \(1232{,}25\), \(1723{,}375\), \(337{,}75\) and \(433{,}625\) mm³ (exact, SymPy). But on the edge \(2\,3\) (\(\xi = 1\), \(\zeta = -1\)) we have \(J(\eta) = 137{,}34\,\eta^2 + 14{,}44\,\eta - 54{,}03\) mm³, which has zeros at \(\eta = -0{,}682\) and \(\eta = 0{,}577\) and is negative between them, lowest \(-54{,}4\) mm³ at \(\eta = -0{,}053\) (figure 15.40). The axis \(\eta\) leads from one corner to the other, the dashed line would join the two positive values at the corners, as a linear \(J\) would for the quadrilateral. But the curve \(J\) is a quadratic parabola and falls below zero. The map folds on this edge: the brick is fine at the corners, but inside it overlaps itself. We can check this with numbers. The point of the cube \(\xi = 0{,}9\), \(\eta = 0\), \(\zeta = -0{,}9\) with \(J = -29{,}5\) mm³ and the point \(\xi = 0{,}678\), \(\eta = 0{,}373\), \(\zeta = -0{,}977\) with \(J = 55{,}0\) mm³ are mapped to the same point \((13{,}676, 9{,}155, 3{,}796)\) mm (Newton's method, NumPy), so \(\Phi\) is not injective.

A sufficient condition that we can prove is a requirement on the symmetric part of the Jacobian matrix. Its advantage is that it too can be checked on a grid of points and that it does not speak only about the corners.

Lemma 15.78 A sufficient condition for a curved box (zadosten pogoj za ukrivljen kvader)

Let \(\Phi\) be a polynomial map from space to space and \(K = [-1, 1]^3\). If the symmetric part \(\tfrac12(\mat F_\Phi + \mat F_\Phi^\T)\) of the matrix of derivatives is positive definite at every point of the cube \(K\), then there is an open convex set \(D \supset K\) on which \(\Phi\) is injective and \(J_\Phi > 0\). Hence \(\Phi(K)\) is a curved box (definition 4.65), and we may use theorem 4.59.

Proof

We must show that the condition holds on a neighbourhood of the cube, then that \(J_\Phi > 0\) and that \(\Phi\) is injective.

  1. Let \(\mat S(\vek x) = \tfrac12(\mat F_\Phi + \mat F_\Phi^\T)\) at the point \(\vek x\) and let \(m(\vek x)\) be the smallest value of the number \(\vek v\cdot\mat S(\vek x)\vek v\) over unit vectors \(\vek v\). It exists by theorem 1.78, since the unit sphere is closed and bounded, and by the hypothesis \(m(\vek x) > 0\) for \(\vek x \in K\). For a unit \(\vek v\) we have \(|v_{\mathrm{i}}v_{\mathrm{j}}| \le 1\), so in passing from \(\vek x\) to \(\vek x'\) the number \(\vek v\cdot\mat S\vek v\), and with it \(m\), changes at most by the sum \(s(\vek x, \vek x')\) of the absolute values of the changes of the nine entries of \(\mat S\). The entries are polynomials, so \(m\) is continuous and has on \(K\) a smallest value \(m_{\mathrm{K}} > 0\) (theorem 1.78).
  2. The entries of \(\mat S\) are continuous on the closed and bounded set of points at a distance at most \(1\) from \(K\), so they are uniformly continuous there (theorem 4.6). So there is an \(\epsilon \in (0, 1)\) with \(s(\vek x, \vek x') < m_{\mathrm{K}}/2\) whenever \(|\vek x - \vek x'| < \epsilon\). Let \(D\) be the set of points at a distance less than \(\epsilon\) from \(K\). For \(\vek x \in D\) some \(\vek x' \in K\) is closer than \(\epsilon\), so \(m(\vek x) > m(\vek x') - m_{\mathrm{K}}/2 \ge m_{\mathrm{K}}/2 > 0\). The set \(D\) is open. It is convex because \(K\) is convex: if \(\vek x\) and \(\vek y\) are closer than \(\epsilon\) to the points \(\vek x'\) and \(\vek y'\) of \(K\), then \((1 - t)\vek x + t\vek y\) is closer than \(\epsilon\) to the point \((1 - t)\vek x' + t\vek y' \in K\).
  3. Let \(\vek x \in D\) and \(\mat F = \mat F_\Phi(\vek x)\). For \(s \in [0, 1]\) let \(\mat F_{\mathrm{s}} = (1 - s)\mat I + s\mat F\). The symmetric part of \(\mat F_{\mathrm{s}}\) is \((1 - s)\mat I + s\,\mathrm{sym}\,\mat F\), positive definite, so \(\mat F_{\mathrm{s}}\vek v \ne \vek 0\) for \(\vek v \ne \vek 0\) (otherwise \(\vek v\cdot\mat F_{\mathrm{s}}\vek v = 0\)). By lemma 2.53 on the zero determinant, \(\det\mat F_{\mathrm{s}} \ne 0\) for every \(s\). The function \(s \mapsto \det\mat F_{\mathrm{s}}\) is continuous and equals \(1\) at \(s = 0\), so it stays positive up to \(s = 1\): \(J_\Phi(\vek x) > 0\).
  4. Let \(\vek p \ne \vek q\) be in \(D\). The segment between them lies in \(D\), because \(D\) is convex. The function \(s \mapsto \Phi(\vek q + s(\vek p - \vek q))\) has by the chain rule the derivative \(\mat F_\Phi(\ldots)(\vek p - \vek q)\), and by the fundamental theorem of calculus \(\Phi(\vek p) - \Phi(\vek q) = \int_0^1\mat F_\Phi(\vek q + s(\vek p - \vek q))(\vek p - \vek q)\,\mathrm{d}s\). The scalar product with \(\vek p - \vek q\) is the integral of a quadratic form with a positive definite matrix, that is, the integral of a function that is positive for every \(s\) and continuous in \(s\), and is therefore \(> 0\). Hence \(\Phi(\vek p) \ne \Phi(\vek q)\).

Source: The proof is not from the source. For quadrilaterals and bricks the same problem is treated by COOK section 6.11 (PDF pp. 249-251) and ONATE1 (PDF pp. 210-216), which mention a condition on the Jacobian matrix without proof.

The condition is not necessary. The rotation of the cube by a quarter turn about the \(\zeta\) axis, \(\Phi(\xi, \eta, \zeta) = (-\eta, \xi, \zeta)\), is injective and has \(J = 1\), but the symmetric part of its matrix of derivatives is \(\mathrm{diag}(0, 0, 1)\) and is not positive definite. A rotation \(\mat Q\) changes neither injectivity nor the Jacobian determinant (\(\det\mat Q = 1\)), so we may check the condition for \(\mat Q\Phi\) with any rotation \(\mat Q\), which helps with bricks that are rotated in space. For the brick of example 15.76 the smallest eigenvalue of the symmetric part of the Jacobian matrix on a grid of \(21^3\) points is \(9{,}2\) mm, positive (checked on the grid and not for all points, so not a proof). For the brick of example 15.77 the determinant is negative on the edge \(2\,3\), so the condition fails (the lemma would give \(J > 0\)), and the map, as we saw, is not injective.

The strain of a brick needs the inverse of the Jacobian matrix, because \(\partial N_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial\vek x = \mat F_\Phi^{-\T}\,\partial N_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial\vek\xi\) (chain rule). The matrix \(\mat B\) is therefore a rational function of \(\xi\), \(\eta\), \(\zeta\), and the stiffness matrix

\[\mat k_{\mathrm{e}} = \int_{[-1,1]^3}\mat B^\T\mat C\,\mat B\,J\,\mathrm{d}V \approx \sum_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}}w_{\mathrm{i}}w_{\mathrm{j}}w_{\mathrm{k}}\,\mat B^\T\mat C\,\mat B\,J\Big|_{(\xi_\mathrm{i}, \xi_\mathrm{j}, \xi_\mathrm{k})} , \tag{15.58}\]

where the integral is over the cube (\(J > 0\), theorem 4.59) and the sum is the \(2 \times 2 \times 2\) Gauss rule (theorem 15.41, a product by Fubini). For a general brick this is not exact, because the integrand is not a polynomial. For a parallelepiped \(\mat F_\Phi\) is constant, \(\mat B\) is a polynomial, and the rule is exact (lemma 15.75, point 2, and degree \(\le 2\) in each coordinate, ONATE1 PDF p. 313 speaks of rational functions in \(\mat G\)). For the brick of example 15.76, with the \(2\times 2\times 2\) rule the matrix \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) (\(24 \times 24\)) has exactly six zero eigenvalues (the rigid motions), with the \(1\times 1\times 1\) rule eighteen, twelve of them spurious modes without strain (the chapter on reduction, section 15.6). Such a reduced integration of the brick is usable only with a correction, which is not the business of this section.

The load is as for the quadrilateral (definition 7.7): a point force goes to a node, a body force \(\vek b\) gives \(\vek f_{\mathrm{i}} = \vek b\int N_{\mathrm{i}}J\,\mathrm{d}V\), and for a box \(40\times 30\times 20\) mm with \(\int_{[-1,1]^3}N_{\mathrm{i}}\,\mathrm{d}V = 1\) and \(J = 3000\) mm³ every node receives \(\vek b\,V/8\) (by lemma 15.75, point 2). A surface load on a face needs the normal of the face, which is given by the cross product of the columns of the matrix of derivatives (equation (4.37)), and the \(2 \times 2\) numerical integration on the face (ONATE1 §8.3.5, PDF pp. 286-288).

15.10.3 The patch in space

Do the brick and the tetrahedron pass the patch test (preizkus z zaplato, theorem 15.47)? For T4 the answer is direct: the field of the element is affine and continuous, the field on the patch is therefore from the family, and the theorem suffices. For the brick the difficulty is the integration. The theorem assumes the exact energy, but we use the \(2\times 2\times 2\) rule on a rational integrand. The next lemma shows that for a field whose strain is constant there is no error at all.

Lemma 15.79 The 2 × 2 × 2 rule is exact for an affine field (pravilo 2 × 2 × 2 je natančno za afino polje)

Let \(\vek u\) be an affine field \(\vek c + \mat W\vek x\) and \(\vek w\) any field (15.57) on a brick with \(J > 0\). Then the integrand \(\{\varepsilon(\vek u)\}^\T\mat C\{\varepsilon(\vek w)\}J\) is a polynomial of degree at most \(2\) in each coordinate, and the \(2\times 2\times 2\) rule gives the exact integral.

Proof

We must show that \(J\,\mat B\) is a polynomial of degree \(\le 2\) in each coordinate.

  1. The strain of an affine field is constant, \(\{\varepsilon(\vek u)\} = \vek\varepsilon_0\) (lemma 15.70, point 1, holds for every \(\vek x\)), so the integrand is \(\vek\varepsilon_0^\T\mat C\,\mat B\,\vek d_{\mathrm{w}}\,J\).
  2. By the chain rule \(\mat B\) is made of \(\partial N_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}} = (\mat F_\Phi^{-\T}\,\partial N_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial\vek\xi)_{\mathrm{j}}\). By theorem 2.54, \(\mat F_\Phi^{-1} = \mathrm{adj}\,\mat F_\Phi/J\) with the cross products of the columns in the rows, so \(J\,\partial N_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}} = (\mathrm{adj}\,\mat F_\Phi)^\T_{\mathrm{jl}}\,\partial N_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial\xi_{\mathrm{l}}\) (sum over \(l\)), a polynomial.
  3. The cross product of two columns, for example \(\partial\Phi/\partial\eta\times\partial\Phi/\partial\zeta\), has by point 1 of lemma 15.75 degree \(\le 2\) in \(\xi\) and \(\le 1\) in \(\eta\) and \(\zeta\). The corresponding \(\partial N_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial\xi\) has degree \(0\) in \(\xi\) and \(1\) in \(\eta\) and \(\zeta\). The product therefore has degree \(\le 2\) in each coordinate. The same holds for the other two cross products and their derivatives.
  4. So the integrand is a polynomial of degree at most \(2\) in each coordinate. By theorem 4.9 and lemma 7.25 (\(q = 2\)) the rule is exact.

Source: The proof is not from the source. For a field with non-constant strain the integrand is rational (ONATE1 PDF p. 313), and the rule is not exact.

Lemma 15.79 together with theorem 15.47 predicts that the brick passes the test. Let us test it by computation. The patch is eight bricks from the cube \([0, 100]^3\) mm with \(27\) nodes and a step of \(50\) mm, the middle node is moved by \((7, -4, 6)\) mm, so that all bricks are distorted. All nodes except the central one get the displacements of the affine field \(\mat W\vek x\) with \(\mat W = 10^{-3}\begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 & 0{,}5\\ 0 & -1 & 1{,}5\\ 0{,}5 & 0 & 2\end{bmatrix}\). The central node is in equilibrium without forces and must get the exact value of the field. It gets \((177{,}0, 38{,}0, 140{,}5)\ \mu\)m, as the field gives, with a relative error of \(1{,}6\cdot 10^{-16}\) (rounding). For T4 the same: four tetrahedra around an interior node give an error of \(3\cdot 10^{-17}\) (num-10.out). The test is a necessary condition, not a sufficient one: a brick would pass it even if it had other faults, and so the result does not mean convergence on all assemblies (section 15.7).

15.10.4 The shell in an octant

Now let us return to the vessel from the beginning of the section. We load the thick spherical shell \(40 \le R \le 60\) mm with an internal pressure \(p = 30\) MPa. The shell and the pressure do not change if we reflect them in the plane \(x = 0\). The mirror image of the solution therefore solves the same boundary value problem. The shell is supported nowhere, so by theorem 11.4 (point 2) it differs from the solution at most by a small rigid displacement and has the same stresses (point 1). The problem is linear, so the average of the solution and its mirror image is also a solution with the same stresses, and for it on the plane \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = -u_{\mathrm{x}}\), that is \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\). We do the same with the planes \(y = 0\) and \(z = 0\) (symmetry in general is treated in chapter 19). So an octant suffices (figure 15.41) with the condition that the displacement normal to each of the three planes is zero: \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\) at \(x = 0\) and so on. This is the model we compute: a linearly elastic steel shell without body forces, held only by the symmetry. We describe the mesh by a number \(n\): we divide the outer surface like the three faces of the cube \([0, 1]^3\), each into \(n \times n\) squares, and project them radially onto the sphere. For \(n = 2\) the grid lines are at \(0\), \(\tfrac12\) and \(1\), and with one layer of bricks between the radii \(a\) and \(b\) we get \(3\cdot 2\cdot 2 = 12\) bricks. The axes \(x\), \(y\), \(z\) are dashed up to the outer surface, the arrows continue them outside it, the thickness runs from \(a\) to \(b\) along the \(z\) axis. The mesh of tetrahedra has \(24\) tetrahedra in each brick: each of the six faces is divided by its centre into four triangles, and these are joined to the centre of the brick. The common faces of neighbours are divided in the same way, so the field is continuous.

The exact solution (theorem 11.28 with \(p_{\mathrm{a}} = p\), \(p_{\mathrm{b}} = 0\)): \(u_{\mathrm{R}}(a) = 6{,}6864\ \mu\)m, \(u_{\mathrm{R}}(b) = 4{,}0602\ \mu\)m and the stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{RR}}(a) = -30{,}00\) MPa (the pressure on the inner surface), \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}(a) = 33{,}95\) MPa and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}(b) = 18{,}95\) MPa. The hoop stress changes across the thickness by a factor of \(1{,}79\). As the measure we choose the strain energy of the octant. With \(\vek d\) the solution, the weak form with \(\vek w = \vek u\) (definition 6.6) gives \(2U = \ell(\vek u)\), so \(U_{\mathrm{h}} = \tfrac12\vek f^\T\vek d\), and for the exact solution \(U = \tfrac12\,p\,u_{\mathrm{R}}(a)\,\pi a^2/2 = 0{,}25207\) J, since the pressure acts on an octant of the sphere with area \(4\pi a^2/8\) and \(u_{\mathrm{R}}(a)\) is constant on it. Note: the mesh does not capture the sphere exactly, since the faces of the bricks are curved only bilinearly and the faces of the tetrahedra are flat. The exact hoop stress changes across the wall by a factor of \(1{,}79\), while the tetrahedron has by lemma 15.72 a constant strain, so every tetrahedron has a single stress and describes the course across the wall only by steps, like the triangle in the cantilever (section 15.4). 15.10.5 A point force on a body

The last example is a trap. A steel cube of \(100\) mm (\(E = 196\) GPa, \(\nu = 0{,}3\)), the lower face clamped, at the middle of the upper face a force of \(1\) kN downwards, at a node of a mesh of bricks (\(n\) bricks along an edge, \(n\) even). How large is the displacement under the force as we refine the mesh?

\(n\) side of a brick unknowns displacement under the force (\(\mu\)m) displacement times side (\(\mu\)m mm)
\(2\) \(50{,}00\) mm \(54\) \(0{,}1646\) \(8{,}23\)
\(4\) \(25{,}00\) mm \(300\) \(0{,}3141\) \(7{,}85\)
\(6\) \(16{,}67\) mm \(882\) \(0{,}4730\) \(7{,}88\)
\(8\) \(12{,}50\) mm \(1944\) \(0{,}6322\) \(7{,}90\)

The displacement does not converge: it grows, and the product of the displacement with the side of a brick is nearly constant. The reason lies in a law we know: stress is force per area. Let \(h\) be the side of a brick. The force \(F\) acts on a node, and the bricks next to it carry it through faces whose area is of order \(h^2\). The stress next to the node is therefore of order \(F/h^2\), the strain of order \(F/(E\,h^2)\), and the displacement over the depth of one brick \(h\) is of order \(F/(E\,h)\). The displacement therefore grows like \(1/h\), which the last column of the table shows. The exact model of a body in which a force acts at one point has no finite displacement under the force: the Boussinesq solution for a force \(F\) normal to the flat surface of a half-space, which we shall mention for contacts (chapter 25), has on the surface at the distance \(R\) from the force the vertical displacement \(u_{\mathrm{z}} = F(1 - \nu)/(2\pi\mu R)\), where \(\mu\) is the shear modulus (SADD example 13.4, equation (13.4.20) at \(z = 0\), PDF p. 415). As \(R \to 0\) it grows beyond all bounds like \(1/R\), just like our estimate \(F/(E\,h)\) as \(h \to 0\). Finite elements are therefore loaded with a force on a face of finite size, or the values under a point force are not read.
