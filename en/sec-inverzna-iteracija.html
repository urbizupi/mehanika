Chapter 21 Modal reduction and eigenvalue solvers

21.6 Inverse iteration (inverzna iteracija)

The previous sections shrank a large model to a reduced model of order a few dozen, for example the cantilever with \(32\) coordinates to \(8\) (section 21.5). The reduced eigenproblem is small but it is still an eigenproblem, and for a large model that reduction did not shrink enough we must compute the eigenvalues too. Chapter 10 postponed this task (section 10.7.7), because the computation with a full matrix used there costs of the order of \(n^3\) operations and is not suitable for thousands of unknowns. This section shows the simplest procedure that finds one eigenvalue and its mode without a full matrix and without the characteristic polynomial: take any shape, repeat a static computation on it, and the shape approaches the first mode by itself. The next two sections build subspace iteration and the Lanczos procedure from it. The procedure is called iteration because it repeats (iterates) the same step, and inverse because the step contains the inverse of the stiffness matrix, which we never compute but replace by solving a system with the factorization of lemma 7.47. The section first does one step on a frame, then proves that the steps converge and how fast, shows what happens when the start has no first mode, adds higher modes by orthogonalization, and finally reaches them from another starting point, a shift.

21.6.1 One step on the frame

Take the frame with three floors from section 10.8 (equation (10.21)): the floors have the mass \(\bar m = 500\) kg, the storeys the stiffness \(k = 501\,760\) N/m. We compute in units in which \(k\) and \(\bar m\) equal \(1\), so the matrices are

\[\mat K = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 & 0 \\ -1 & 2 & -1 \\ 0 & -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} , \qquad \mat M = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} ,\]

and the eigenvalues are \(0{,}198062\), \(1{,}554958\) and \(3{,}246980\) in the unit \(k/\bar m = 1003{,}52\) 1/s\(^2\). Let the starting shape be \(\vek x_0 = (1,\ 1,\ 1)^\T\), all floors displaced equally. If the frame vibrates in this shape, an inertia force proportional to \(m_{\mathrm{i}} x_{\mathrm{i}}\) acts on floor \(i\), and all floors carry the same force. Ask: what is the static shape of the frame when these forces really act? It is the solution of the system \(\mat K\vek y_1 = \mat M\vek x_0\). The inverse is

\[\mat K^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 2 \\ 1 & 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\]

(check by multiplication, \(\mat K\mat K^{-1} = \mat I\)), so \(\vek y_1 = (3,\ 5,\ 6)^\T\).

Read the figure in parts. On the left is the frame with its fixing in the ground and the starting shape, where all three floors are displaced by the same amount. In the middle is the force on each floor \(\bar m\,x_{0,\mathrm{i}}\), three equal orange arrows. On the right is the static response to these forces: the lowest column carries all three forces and bends most, the highest only one. So the first floor moves by \(3\), the second by \(2\) more and the third by only \(1\) more, and the shape \((3, 5, 6)\) is no longer \((1, 1, 1)\). Normalize it with the top floor, \(\vek x_1 = (0{,}5,\ 0{,}8333,\ 1)^\T\). The Rayleigh quotient (definition 10.25) of the starting shape is \(R(\vek x_0) = 1/3\), of the new one \(R(\vek x_1) = 0{,}200000\), which is already only \(1{,}0\) % above the true value \(0{,}198062\) (by theorem 10.26 never below it). Repeat the step five more times and every time the shape comes nearer to the first mode: after the sixth step \(\vek x_6 = (0{,}445044,\ 0{,}801939,\ 1)^\T\) and \(R = 0{,}19806226\). Let us write down what we did.

Definition 21.26 Inverse iteration (inverzna iteracija)

Let \(\mat K\) and \(\mat M\) be symmetric positive definite matrices of order \(n\) and \(\vek x_0 \ne \vek 0\) a starting column. Inverse iteration is the sequence of columns \(\vek x_{\mathrm{k}}\) obtained by solving, in step \(k+1\), the system

\[\mat K\,\vek y_{\mathrm{k}+1} = \mat M\,\vek x_{\mathrm{k}} \tag{21.42}\]

and setting \(\vek x_{\mathrm{k}+1} = \vek y_{\mathrm{k}+1}/t_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) with a nonzero number \(t_{\mathrm{k}+1}\), for example such that the last component equals \(1\). The estimate of the eigenvalue after \(k\) steps is the Rayleigh quotient \(R(\vek x_{\mathrm{k}})\).

Source: CK section 15.2.1 (PDF pp. 489-490, equations 15.7 to 15.10): an equation of the form \(\vek u_{\mathrm{s}+1} = \mat D\,\vek u_{\mathrm{s}}\) with the dynamical matrix \(\mat D = \mat K^{-1}\mat M\) and a "scaling factor" that makes the largest element equal to \(1\). The estimate by the Rayleigh quotient is in CK example 15.1 (PDF pp. 491-492).

The number \(t_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) changes neither the shape of the column nor the quotient, since \(R(t\,\vek x) = R(\vek x)\) for every \(t \ne 0\) (numerator and denominator are multiplied by \(t^2\)). So in the proofs we write simply \(\vek x_{\mathrm{k}} = (\mat K^{-1}\mat M)^k\vek x_0\) up to a number.

21.6.2 Why the shape converges

What does the frame actually do during the iteration? The expansion theorem (corollary 10.14) writes every column by modes, \(\vek x_0 = \sum_{\mathrm{r}} c_{\mathrm{r}}\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) with \(c_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\,\vek x_0\), so it is enough to see what one step does to one mode. From \(\mat K\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\mat M\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) follows \(\mat K^{-1}\mat M\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}/\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\). In every step mode \(r\) is multiplied by \(1/\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\), so the mode with the smallest eigenvalue grows most. Figure 21.19 shows both sides of this claim on the frame.

On the axes of the left picture the floor number is vertical and the displacement horizontal, and every shape is scaled so that the displacement of the top floor is \(1\). We see how the starting shape, vertical above the first floor, goes over into the first and second iterate, which cling to the exact first mode (orange). The right picture has on its axes the steps \(k\) horizontally and, vertically on a logarithmic scale, the ratio \(c_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}}/c_{1,\mathrm{k}}\), where \(c_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}}\) is the coefficient of mode \(r\) in the iterate \(\vek x_{\mathrm{k}}\). On it the second mode and the third mode fall along a straight line, which means the content of each decreases by a constant ratio in every step. The starting shape \((1,1,1)\) has the coefficients \(c_{\mathrm{r}} = 1{,}655971,\ 0{,}473952,\ 0{,}182018\), ratios \(c_2/c_1 = 0{,}2862\) and \(c_3/c_1 = 0{,}1099\), and after one step \(0{,}0365\) and \(0{,}0067\). The ratios by steps are computed by the theorem.

Theorem 21.27 Convergence of inverse iteration (konvergenca inverzne iteracije)

Let \(\mat K\), \(\mat M\) be symmetric positive definite, \(\lambda_1 < \lambda_2 \le \dots \le \lambda_{\mathrm{n}}\) the eigenvalues of the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\) with \(\mat M\)-orthonormal columns \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) (theorem 10.13) and \(\vek x_{\mathrm{k}} = t_{\mathrm{k}}(\mat K^{-1}\mat M)^k\vek x_0\) with nonzero numbers \(t_{\mathrm{k}}\). Let the start \(\vek x_0 = \sum_{\mathrm{r}} c_{\mathrm{r}}\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) have a part of the first mode, \(c_1 \ne 0\). Denote \(d_{\mathrm{r}}^{(k)} = (c_{\mathrm{r}}/c_1)(\lambda_1/\lambda_{\mathrm{r}})^k\) for \(r \ge 2\) and the shape error by \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{k}} = \bigl(\sum_{\mathrm{r}\ge 2}(d_{\mathrm{r}}^{(k)})^2\bigr)^{1/2}\). Then:

  1. The column \(\vek x_{\mathrm{k}}\) equals, up to a number, \(\vek\varphi_1 + \sum_{\mathrm{r}\ge2}d_{\mathrm{r}}^{(k)}\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\), and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{k}} \le (\lambda_1/\lambda_2)^k\,\varepsilon_0\). The shape converges to the first mode.
  2. \(0 \le R(\vek x_{\mathrm{k}}) - \lambda_1 \le (\lambda_1/\lambda_2)^{2k}\sum_{\mathrm{r}\ge2}(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} - \lambda_1)(c_{\mathrm{r}}/c_1)^2\).
Proof

We must show how the coefficients of the modes change with the steps (point 1) and where this leads for the quotient (point 2).

  1. By the expansion theorem \((\mat K^{-1}\mat M)\sum_{\mathrm{r}} c_{\mathrm{r}}\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = \sum_{\mathrm{r}} (c_{\mathrm{r}}/\lambda_{\mathrm{r}})\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\), because \(\mat K^{-1}\mat M\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}/\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) (multiply \(\mat K\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\mat M\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) by \(\mat K^{-1}\)). After \(k\) steps the coefficient of mode \(r\) is \(c_{\mathrm{r}}/\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}^k\).
  2. Divide by the coefficient of the first mode \(c_1/\lambda_1^k \ne 0\). The coefficient of mode \(r\) becomes \((c_{\mathrm{r}}/c_1)(\lambda_1/\lambda_{\mathrm{r}})^k = d_{\mathrm{r}}^{(k)}\) and that of the first becomes \(1\). This is the first claim of point 1.
  3. Since \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} \ge \lambda_2\) for \(r \ge 2\), we have \(\lambda_1/\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} \le \lambda_1/\lambda_2 < 1\) and \(|d_{\mathrm{r}}^{(k)}| \le (\lambda_1/\lambda_2)^k|c_{\mathrm{r}}/c_1|\). Squaring and summing over \(r \ge 2\) gives \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{k}} \le (\lambda_1/\lambda_2)^k\varepsilon_0\). The right side falls toward zero, so the shape approaches \(\vek\varphi_1\) (figure 21.19, right).
  4. The Rayleigh quotient is independent of scale, so we compute it with the shares \(1, d_2, \dots, d_{\mathrm{n}}\). By equation (10.31), \(R(\vek x_{\mathrm{k}}) = (\lambda_1 + \sum_{\mathrm{r}\ge2}\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} d_{\mathrm{r}}^2)/(1 + \sum_{\mathrm{r}\ge2}d_{\mathrm{r}}^2)\) and
    \[R(\vek x_{\mathrm{k}}) - \lambda_1 = \frac{\sum_{\mathrm{r}\ge2}(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} - \lambda_1)\,d_{\mathrm{r}}^2}{1 + \sum_{\mathrm{r}\ge2}d_{\mathrm{r}}^2} .\]
    The numerator is nonnegative because \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} \ge \lambda_1\), so the difference is \(\ge 0\).
  5. The denominator is \(\ge 1\), so the difference is at most \(\sum_{\mathrm{r}\ge2}(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} - \lambda_1)d_{\mathrm{r}}^2\). By step 3, \(d_{\mathrm{r}}^2 \le (\lambda_1/\lambda_2)^{2k}(c_{\mathrm{r}}/c_1)^2\), which gives the upper bound of point 2.

Source: CK section 15.2.2 (PDF pp. 493-494): proof of convergence by expansion in modes, the speed depends on the ratios \(\lambda_1/\lambda_{\mathrm{i}}\), especially \(\lambda_1/\lambda_2\), and on the content of the starting column. The bound for the shape error and the quotient in this notation was added by the book.

Test the bounds on the frame. For \(\vek x_0 = (1,1,1)^\T\) we have \(\lambda_1/\lambda_2 = 0{,}12737\) and its square \(0{,}01622\), the sum in point 2 with the starting shares \(c_2/c_1 = 0{,}2862\) and \(c_3/c_1 = 0{,}1099\) is \(0{,}2862^2\cdot1{,}3569 + 0{,}1099^2\cdot3{,}0489 = 0{,}148\) (in the unit \(k/\bar m\)). The ratios of the shape error approach \(\lambda_1/\lambda_2 = 0{,}12737\), which is the drop of the blue straight line on the right of the figure in one step, the ratios of the quotient approach its square \(0{,}01622\), as the theorem predicts, and the bound of point 2 at \(k = 6\) is \(0{,}148\cdot0{,}01622^6 = 2{,}7\cdot10^{-12}\), the actual value \(2{,}0\cdot10^{-12}\), so below the bound. The meaning is that the quotient gains twice as many digits as the shape: \(-\log_{10}0{,}12737 = 0{,}895\) digits of shape and \(1{,}790\) digits of quotient per step. The frequency from the quotient by steps \(k = 0\) to \(3\) is \(18{,}2895\), \(14{,}1670\), \(14{,}0993\), \(14{,}0982\) 1/s, the exact one is \(\omega_1 = \sqrt{0{,}198062 \cdot 1003{,}52\ \text{1/s}^2} = 14{,}0982\) 1/s (\(f_1 = 2{,}2438\) Hz). After two steps the frequency is right to four digits (relative error \(7{,}5\cdot10^{-5}\)), while the shape still has the error \(\varepsilon_2 = 4{,}7\cdot10^{-3}\).

This quotient is a Ritz one (definition 10.41): every iterate is one Ritz shape \(\mat\Psi = (\vek x_{\mathrm{k}})\) of order \(m = 1\) and theorem 10.42 guarantees \(R(\vek x_{\mathrm{k}}) \ge \lambda_1\) in every step, which is the left part of point 2.

When is the speed bad? At \(\lambda_1/\lambda_2 = 0{,}99\) we need \(229{,}1\) steps for one digit of shape error, for the frame \(1{,}12\) steps. The smallness of the ratio is therefore essential. On the frame every step reduces the shape error to about \(0{,}13\) of the previous one, while with a dense spectrum, where the first two eigenvalues are nearly equal, the iteration stalls. On bar \(C\) of section 10.9.4 (steel, \(16\) elements, the start is the static shape under the force at the end) \(\lambda_1/\lambda_2 = 0{,}11040\) and its square \(0{,}012188\). The relative errors of the frequency against \(f_1 = 1253{,}70\) Hz of the model with \(16\) elements are, by steps \(0\) to \(3\), \(1{,}02\cdot10^{-1}\), \(6{,}41\cdot10^{-4}\), \(7{,}44\cdot10^{-6}\), \(9{,}01\cdot10^{-8}\), their ratios \(0{,}0063\), \(0{,}0116\), \(0{,}0121\) approach \(0{,}0122\). The start from the static shape is sensible because the first mode already dominates it: it gives \(81{,}12\) % of the displacement of the end (section 10.9.4), and its \(c_1^2\) is \(98{,}55\) % of the sum \(\sum_{\mathrm{r}} c_{\mathrm{r}}^2\). So the first step already hits well.

From history

The procedure in this form is older than computers. Vianello used it in 1898 for the buckling of a strut and Stodola in 1904 for the vibration of a rotating shaft (CK, PDF p. 489). CK writes that it is perhaps the most effective procedure for hand computation of a few eigenvalues and modes of a small system, for example with \(N \le 5\) degrees of freedom (CK, PDF p. 489). History is here because it shows that the procedure computes one lowest value and shape without the characteristic polynomial, as hand computation needed. The rotating shaft comes in chapter 24.

21.6.3 The cost of one step

Step (21.42) means solving a system with the matrix \(\mat K\), which in finite elements is banded (definition 7.40). The factorization \(\mat K = \mat L\mat D\mat L^\T\) (lemma 7.47) is done only once, then every step needs only two substitutions, forward with \(\mat L\) and backward with \(\mat L^\T\) (and division by \(\mat D\)). For order \(n = 10\,000\) and half bandwidth \(b = 100\) the factorization is by equation (7.44) at most \(b(b+1)(3n - 2b - 1)/6 = 5{,}02\cdot10^7\) multiplications, a full matrix by equation (7.43) \(n^3/6 = 1{,}67\cdot10^{11}\), so about \(3300\) times more. Each substitution has at most \(b\) terms in every row, the two together about \(2nb = 2{,}0\cdot10^6\) multiplications, and the product \(\mat M\vek x_{\mathrm{k}}\) with a banded \(\mat M\) another about \(2{,}0\cdot10^6\). The factorization is therefore worth about \(25\) pairs of substitutions (forward and backward) or \(12{,}5\) whole steps. At speed \(0{,}13\) per step, as on the frame, about \(11\) steps are needed for ten digits of the shape (\(10/0{,}895 = 11{,}2\)). They cost \(11 \cdot 4{,}0\cdot10^6 = 4{,}4\cdot10^7\) multiplications, somewhat less than the factorization itself. The whole iteration therefore costs less than two factorizations, about \(1800\) times less than the factorization of the full matrix.

21.6.4 What does not work: a start without the first mode

The theorem requires \(c_1 \ne 0\). What happens if \(c_1 = 0\)? Take \(\vek x_0 = \vek\varphi_2 + \vek\varphi_3\), which is \(\mat M\)-orthogonal to \(\vek\varphi_1\) up to \(8{,}3\cdot10^{-17}\), that is, up to rounding. Then by step 1 of the proof of the theorem the coefficient of the first mode is zero in all steps, and the iterate stays in the plane of modes \(2\) and \(3\). Its quotient goes toward \(\lambda_2 = 1{,}554958\), since the third mode decreases in the ratio \(\lambda_2/\lambda_3 = 0{,}4789\) per step.

In the figure the horizontal axis counts the steps \(k\) and the vertical one shows the quotient \(R(\vek x_{\mathrm{k}})\). The grey dashed lines are the eigenvalues. No curve reaches the highest, \(\lambda_3\), because the quotient is a weighted mean of the eigenvalues (equation (10.31)) and the share of the third mode falls in every step. The ordinary start is the blue curve, which hits \(\lambda_1\) after a few steps. The start without the first mode (orange) first goes toward \(\lambda_2\): \(R = 2{,}400969\) at \(k = 0\), \(1{,}870613\) at \(k = 1\), \(1{,}556031\) at \(k = 5\), \(1{,}554959\) at \(k = 10\) and \(1{,}554947\) at \(k = 15\). Then it falls: \(1{,}512998\) at \(k = 17\), \(0{,}655418\) at \(k = 18\), \(0{,}209164\) at \(k = 19\), \(0{,}198244\) at \(k = 20\) and \(0{,}198062 = \lambda_1\) from \(k = 22\) on. Why? Rounding in every step returns a small part of the first mode into the iterate, and this part is multiplied in a step against the second by the ratio \(\lambda_2/\lambda_1 = 7{,}851\). From \(8{,}3\cdot10^{-17}\) to a size of order \(1\) it needs \(\ln(1/8{,}3\cdot10^{-17})/\ln 7{,}851 = 17{,}97\) steps, and indeed the orange curve breaks at \(k = 17\) to \(19\). Computing the second mode is therefore impossible with a good start alone: the first mode must be removed in every step, not only at the beginning.

21.6.5 Higher modes: removing those already found

How do we remove the first mode? Once \(\vek\varphi_1\) is known, we subtract from any column \(\vek x\) its parallel component. Figure 21.21 shows this in the plane of the first two modes of the frame.

The axes \(\eta_1\) and \(\eta_2\) are the coefficients of the column at the first and second mode, \(\eta_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\,\vek x\) (corollary 10.14). Divide the column \(\vek x\) into the part along the first mode and the remainder, which is perpendicular to it, as the parallel line to the axis shows. We write this in general.

Lemma 21.28 Removing the found modes and iteration of a higher mode (\(\mat M\)-Gram-Schmidt sweeping, \(\mat M\)-Gram-Schmidtova ortogonalizacija)

Let \(\vek\varphi_1, \dots, \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{p}}\) be the first \(\mat M\)-orthonormal eigencolumns (theorem 10.13) and

\[\mat S_{\mathrm{p}} = \mat I - \sum_{\mathrm{s}=1}^p\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\mat M . \tag{21.43}\]

Then:

  1. For \(\vek x = \sum_{\mathrm{r}} c_{\mathrm{r}}\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) we have \(\mat S_{\mathrm{p}}\vek x = \sum_{\mathrm{r}>\mathrm{p}}c_{\mathrm{r}}\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\), and \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\mat M\,\mat S_{\mathrm{p}}\vek x = 0\) for \(s \le p\).
  2. If \(\lambda_{\mathrm{p}+1} < \lambda_{\mathrm{p}+2}\) and \(c_{\mathrm{p}+1} \ne 0\), the iteration \(\vek x_{\mathrm{k}+1} = t\,\mat S_{\mathrm{p}}\mat K^{-1}\mat M\,\vek x_{\mathrm{k}}\) with the start \(\mat S_{\mathrm{p}}\vek x_0\) (and nonzero numbers \(t\)) satisfies theorem 21.27 with \(\lambda_{\mathrm{p}+1}\) in place of \(\lambda_1\) and \(\lambda_{\mathrm{p}+2}\) in place of \(\lambda_2\): the shape of \(\vek x_{\mathrm{k}}\) converges to \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{p}+1}\) with the ratio \(\lambda_{\mathrm{p}+1}/\lambda_{\mathrm{p}+2}\) per step and the quotient to \(\lambda_{\mathrm{p}+1}\) with its square.
Proof

We must show what the matrix \(\mat S_{\mathrm{p}}\) does to the expansion, and then that the steps of the iteration keep the zeros.

  1. By the expansion theorem \(c_{\mathrm{s}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\mat M\,\vek x\). So \(\mat S_{\mathrm{p}}\vek x = \vek x - \sum_{\mathrm{s}\le \mathrm{p}}c_{\mathrm{s}}\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}} = \sum_{\mathrm{r}>\mathrm{p}}c_{\mathrm{r}}\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\).
  2. The coefficient of mode \(s \le p\) in \(\mat S_{\mathrm{p}}\vek x\) is zero, so \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\mat M\,\mat S_{\mathrm{p}}\vek x = 0\). This is the first point.
  3. Let \(\vek x_{\mathrm{k}}\) have coefficients \(a_{\mathrm{r}}^{(k)}\) that are zero for \(r \le p\). The step \(\mat K^{-1}\mat M\) divides them by \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) by step 1 of theorem 21.27, and \(\mat S_{\mathrm{p}}\) sets them to zero again for \(r \le p\). After \(k\) steps the coefficient of mode \(r > p\) is thus \(c_{\mathrm{r}}/\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}^k\) up to a number, the others are zero.
  4. The proof of theorem 21.27 from step 2 on (division by the coefficient of mode \(p+1\), estimates with \(\lambda_{\mathrm{p}+1}/\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} \le \lambda_{\mathrm{p}+1}/\lambda_{\mathrm{p}+2}\)) stays the same, because the sum runs only over \(r \ge p+2\).

Source: CK section 15.2.3 (PDF pp. 494-496, equations 15.16 to 15.22): removal by Gram-Schmidt, the "sweeping matrix" \(\mat S_1\) in example 15.2, removal in every step because rounding reintroduces the first mode. The notation with \(\mat M\)-orthonormal columns and the bounds are ours.

On the frame with the start \((1,1,1)\) and removal of \(\vek\varphi_1\) in every step the quotient by steps \(k = 0\) to \(7\) is \(1{,}772437\), \(1{,}610318\), \(1{,}567983\), \(1{,}557963\), \(1{,}555648\), \(1{,}555116\), \(1{,}554994\), \(1{,}554966\) and the errors against \(\lambda_2 = 1{,}554958\) are \(2{,}175\cdot10^{-1}\), \(5{,}536\cdot10^{-2}\), \(1{,}302\cdot10^{-2}\), \(3{,}005\cdot10^{-3}\), \(6{,}901\cdot10^{-4}\), \(1{,}583\cdot10^{-4}\), \(3{,}631\cdot10^{-5}\), \(8{,}327\cdot10^{-6}\). The ratios of successive errors \(0{,}2546\), \(0{,}2353\), \(0{,}2307\), \(0{,}2297\), \(0{,}2294\), \(0{,}2294\), \(0{,}2293\) approach the square of the ratio, \((\lambda_2/\lambda_3)^2 = 0{,}22934\), as the lemma says. The speed here is worse than for the first mode (\(0{,}016\)), because the ratio \(\lambda_2/\lambda_3 = 0{,}479\) is closer to \(1\) than \(\lambda_1/\lambda_2 = 0{,}127\).

21.6.6 What does not work: removal only at the start

The lemma says we must remove the mode in every step. What if we remove it only at the start? In practice \(\vek\varphi_1\) is only approximate. Take \(\vek\varphi_1\) from four steps of inverse iteration with the start \((1,1,1)\), which has a shape error \(7{,}5\cdot10^{-5}\), and remove the first mode with it. The remainder then still has \(|c_1| = 7{,}1\cdot10^{-5}\). If we remove only at the start, this part is multiplied by \(\lambda_2/\lambda_1 = 7{,}851\) per step, and by steps \(k = 0, 2, 4, 6\) we have \(|c_1| = 7{,}1\cdot10^{-5}\), \(4{,}7\cdot10^{-3}\), \(2{,}8\cdot10^{-1}\), \(1{,}0\). The quotient by steps \(k = 0, 2, \dots, 12\) is \(1{,}772531\), \(1{,}567959\), \(1{,}451422\), \(0{,}202342\), \(0{,}198063\), \(0{,}198062\), \(0{,}198062\) and the iteration ends at the first mode, as with the start without the first mode. If we remove in every step, \(|c_1| = 7{,}5\cdot10^{-5}\) stays for all steps and the quotient goes toward \(1{,}554958\) (\(1{,}554994\) at \(k = 6\), \(1{,}554960\) at \(k = 8\) and \(1{,}554958\) from \(k = 10\) on). Removal in every step therefore holds even when \(\vek\varphi_1\) is only approximate.

21.6.7 The shift: other modes with the same method

Removal needs all the lower modes. Is it possible to reach the mode we need without them? Instead of the matrix \(\mat K\) take a matrix that has its smallest eigenvalue where we search. Figure 21.22 shows this on the frame.

At the top, without a shift, the highest bar is at \(\lambda_1\) (amplification \(5{,}049\) against \(0{,}643\) and \(0{,}308\)). Below, with a shift \(\lambda_0 = 1{,}4\), the highest bar is at \(\lambda_2\) (\(6{,}453\) against \(0{,}832\) and \(0{,}541\)), because the distance \(|\lambda_2 - \lambda_0| = 0{,}155\) is the smallest. The amplification of mode \(r\) is the reciprocal of this distance.

Definition 21.29 Spectrum shift (premik spektra)

Let \(\mat K\), \(\mat M\) be as in definition 21.26 and \(\lambda_0\) a real number that is not an eigenvalue of the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\). Inverse iteration with the shift \(\lambda_0\) solves in every step

\[(\mat K - \lambda_0\mat M)\,\vek y_{\mathrm{k}+1} = \mat M\,\vek x_{\mathrm{k}} . \tag{21.44}\]

Source: CK section 15.2.4 (PDF p. 497, equations 15.23 to 15.25): "let \(\mu\) be a number in the vicinity of the frequency of interest", in this book written \(\lambda_0\) because \(\mu\) means the absorber ratio.

The name is because all eigenvalues are shifted by \(\lambda_0\): \((\mat K - \lambda_0\mat M)\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = (\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} - \lambda_0)\mat M\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\), the spectrum moves along the axis downward by \(\lambda_0\). The eigenvalues of the shifted pair are \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} - \lambda_0\), and their absolute values are the distances below the lower axis of the figure. The matrix \(\mat K - \lambda_0\mat M\) is invertible: if \((\mat K - \lambda_0\mat M)\vek v = \vek 0\) for \(\vek v \ne \vek 0\), then \(\mat K\vek v = \lambda_0\mat M\vek v\) and by corollary 10.14 \(\lambda_0\) would be an eigenvalue. For \(\lambda_0 > \lambda_1\) it is no longer positive definite, so lemma 7.47 does not hold for it and the factorization may need adapting (row exchange), which we do not prove here.

Corollary 21.30 Inverse iteration with a shift (inverzna iteracija s premikom)

Let \(\lambda_0\) be as in definition 21.29 and let \(\lambda_{\mathrm{j}}\) be the only eigenvalue nearest to \(\lambda_0\): \(|\lambda_{\mathrm{j}} - \lambda_0| < |\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} - \lambda_0|\) for every \(r \ne j\). Let \(c_{\mathrm{j}} \ne 0\) and \(\rho = |\lambda_{\mathrm{j}} - \lambda_0|/\min_{\mathrm{r}\ne \mathrm{j}}|\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} - \lambda_0| < 1\). Then the shape of the iterates converges to \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\), the shape error \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{k}}\), defined as in theorem 21.27 with mode \(j\) in place of the first, satisfies \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{k}} \le \rho^k\varepsilon_0\), and

\[|R(\vek x_{\mathrm{k}}) - \lambda_{\mathrm{j}}| \le \rho^{2k}\sum_{\mathrm{r}\ne \mathrm{j}}|\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} - \lambda_{\mathrm{j}}|\,(c_{\mathrm{r}}/c_{\mathrm{j}})^2 .\]
Proof

We must show that the proof of theorem 21.27 stayed the same with another number in the denominator.

  1. Denote \(\mu_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}} - \lambda_0\). From \((\mat K - \lambda_0\mat M)\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = \mu_{\mathrm{r}}\mat M\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) follows \((\mat K - \lambda_0\mat M)^{-1}\mat M\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}/\mu_{\mathrm{r}}\) (equation (21.44), \(\mu_{\mathrm{r}} \ne 0\)).
  2. After \(k\) steps the coefficient of mode \(r\) is \(c_{\mathrm{r}}/\mu_{\mathrm{r}}^k\) up to a number. Divide by the coefficient of mode \(j\) and obtain \(d_{\mathrm{r}}^{(k)} = (c_{\mathrm{r}}/c_{\mathrm{j}})(\mu_{\mathrm{j}}/\mu_{\mathrm{r}})^k\) for \(r \ne j\), with \(|\mu_{\mathrm{j}}/\mu_{\mathrm{r}}| \le \rho < 1\). As in step 3 of theorem 21.27, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{k}} \le \rho^k\varepsilon_0\).
  3. By equation (10.31), \(R(\vek x_{\mathrm{k}}) - \lambda_{\mathrm{j}} = \sum_{\mathrm{r}\ne \mathrm{j}}(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} - \lambda_{\mathrm{j}})d_{\mathrm{r}}^2/(1 + \sum_{\mathrm{r}\ne \mathrm{j}}d_{\mathrm{r}}^2)\). The denominator is \(\ge 1\) and \(d_{\mathrm{r}}^2 \le \rho^{2k}(c_{\mathrm{r}}/c_{\mathrm{j}})^2\), which gives the bound. The sign of the difference is now not determined, because \(\lambda_{\mathrm{j}}\) is no longer the lowest, so the bound is for the absolute value.

Source: CK section 15.2.4 (PDF p. 497): "a proof similar to that in Eqs. 15.11 through 15.15 can be used to show that inverse iteration applied to the shifted eigenproblem [...] produces convergence to the \(\lambda_{\mathrm{i}}\) closest to the shift value". The derivation of the bounds is ours.

Test on the frame. At \(\lambda_0 = 1{,}4\) the distances are \(|\mu_{\mathrm{r}}| = 1{,}201938,\ 0{,}154958,\ 1{,}84698\), the nearest is \(\lambda_2\), and \(\rho = 0{,}154958/1{,}201938 = 0{,}12892\). The quotients by steps \(1\) to \(5\) are \(1{,}32773\), \(1{,}55041\), \(1{,}55488\), \(1{,}55496\), \(1{,}55496\) and the errors \(2{,}27\cdot10^{-1}\), \(4{,}55\cdot10^{-3}\), \(7{,}60\cdot10^{-5}\), \(1{,}26\cdot10^{-6}\), \(2{,}10\cdot10^{-8}\) with the ratios \(0{,}0200\), \(0{,}0167\), \(0{,}0166\), \(0{,}0166\) against \(\rho^2 = 0{,}01662\). The shift therefore reaches the second mode without removing the first, and the speed is nearly that for the first mode without a shift (\(0{,}0166\) against \(0{,}0162\)), because the ratio of distances \(\rho = 0{,}1289\) is nearly the ratio \(\lambda_1/\lambda_2 = 0{,}1274\). At \(\lambda_0 = 3{,}0\) the distances are \(2{,}802,\ 1{,}445,\ 0{,}247\) and the iteration goes to \(\lambda_3 = 3{,}246980\) with \(\rho = 0{,}17092\) (\(\rho^2 = 0{,}02921\)): quotients \(2{,}0\), \(3{,}22222\), \(3{,}24658\), \(3{,}24697\). The shift is near \(\lambda_3\) against the spacing of the eigenvalues: the distance \(0{,}247\) is \(0{,}17\) of the distance to the second nearest. A good choice of shift therefore has \(\rho \ll 1\), which means \(|\lambda_{\mathrm{j}} - \lambda_0|\) must be several times smaller than every other distance.

The condition of a unique nearest eigenvalue is essential. Take \(\lambda_0 = 0{,}876510\), the midpoint between \(\lambda_1\) and \(\lambda_2\), where both distances are \(0{,}678448\). Then modes \(1\) and \(2\) are amplified equally and the iteration converges to neither: the coefficients of the first two modes in the normalized iterate are, by steps, \((-0{,}961, 0{,}275)\), \((0{,}961, 0{,}275)\), \((-0{,}961, 0{,}275)\), ... (the third decays), the sign of the first oscillates, and the quotient goes toward \(0{,}300797\). This is not an eigenvalue but the weighted mean \((\lambda_1c_1^2 + \lambda_2c_2^2)/(c_1^2 + c_2^2)\) for \(c = (0{,}961398,\ 0{,}27516)\), which are the coefficients \(c_1 = 1{,}655971\) and \(c_2 = 0{,}473952\) of the start divided by \(\sqrt{c_1^2 + c_2^2}\). The corollary does not apply here, because the nearest eigenvalue is not unique: the ratio is \(\rho = 1\), so the bound \(\rho^{2k}\sum_{\mathrm{r}\ne \mathrm{j}}|\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} - \lambda_{\mathrm{j}}|(c_{\mathrm{r}}/c_{\mathrm{j}})^2\) does not decrease with the steps.

For a system with rigid-body modes \(\mat K\) is singular and the inverse does not exist. A small shift removes the singularity (CK, PDF p. 497), because \(\mat K - \lambda_0\mat M\) becomes invertible. For other procedures for such systems CK refers to another source (PDF p. 497), we do not treat them.

The section showed that one eigenvalue and its mode can be found with one factorization and the repetition of a static computation, and that the speed is determined by the ratio \(\lambda_1/\lambda_2\) (or with removal \(\lambda_{\mathrm{p}+1}/\lambda_{\mathrm{p}+2}\), with a shift the ratio of distances). The speed may be good or bad, and it leads us to the question of whether we should not improve several columns at once and in every step take the best combinations by a Ritz step. This is done by subspace iteration in the next section.

We do not treat the QR method, Householder, Jacobi, Sturm sequences and the determinant search: they are in CK sections 15.1 and 15.4 (PDF pp. 487-489 and 501-506) and are not proved in this book.
