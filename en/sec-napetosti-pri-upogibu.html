Chapter 5 Beam theory (teorija nosilcev)

5.5 Stress and stiffness in bending

We now have two parts of the beam. From equilibrium we know the internal forces in every cross section, the axial force \(N\), the shear force \(V\) and the bending moment \(M\) (section 5.2). From kinematics we know the strain, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = u' - y\,v''\) (theorem 5.15), where \(u(x)\) and \(v(x)\) are the displacements of the points of the axis. The link between them is missing. How large is the stress in the fibre at height \(y\) above the axis, and how are \(N\) and \(M\) related to the displacements \(u\) and \(v\)? We need the answer because we shall compute the deflection of the beam from \(M(x)\), and from the same stress check whether the steel carries the load. All calculations will be done on the beams of section 5.1.3, a flat steel bar of width \(b = 20\) mm and height \(h = 40\) mm, standing on its edge.

The path is short. From the strain and Hooke's law we get the stress, where we must say what we assume about the other stress components. We then add the stress over the cross section, as lemma 5.9 and corollary 5.19 say, and obtain \(N\) and \(M\) in terms of the displacements. This shows where in the cross section the axis must be placed. Finally we invert the relation and compute the stress from \(N\) and \(M\). The addendum at the end of the section shows what changes if the beam is heated unevenly.

5.5.1 From strain to stress

The question is how large the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) is in the fibre (definition 1.24) that lies along the axis \(x\) at height \(y\). Hooke's law for an isotropic material (theorem 3.11) links the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) with all three normal strains at once. For \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) we would therefore need \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\) as well as \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), or else the stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\). What do we know about them?

The beam is thin. The upper and lower faces are free, except where the distributed load \(q\) acts on the upper one, and the side faces are entirely free. The stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) is therefore zero on the lower face, on the upper face by the condition on that face it equals the pressure of the load, and in between it takes values between the two. Let us check how large it is. The simply supported beam of section 5.1.3 carries \(q_0 = 1\) kN/m over a width \(b = 20\) mm, which is a pressure \(q_0/b = 50\) kPa. The stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) in the same beam will reach \(93{,}75\) MPa (we compute it later in this section), so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) is at most \(0{,}05\,\%\) of that value. The same holds for \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\), since the side faces are free. The cross section \(20 \times 40\) mm is not a state of plane stress, so this is only an approximation. The elastic calculation shows how good it is. For a beam under a uniform load, SADD (PDF p. 175, plane stress) finds that the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) at the outer fibres differs from the elementary value by \(w/5\), where \(w\) is the pressure of the load on the upper face, whatever the dimensions of the beam. For our beam this gives a difference \(w/5 = 10\) kPa, which is \(0{,}011\,\%\) of the largest stress.

We therefore take a step which we call assumption 6 (the numbers of the assumptions are the same as in the table that collects them at the end of the chapter): in each fibre the stress is uniaxial, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\). The shear stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) does not follow from Hooke's law, as we shall see below, but from equilibrium (in the next section). For the normal stress of a fibre, however, what we learned in the tensile test holds. The fibre is in the same state as the bar of the test, in which the only nonzero stress component is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), so by lemma 3.6 on uniaxial stress we have \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) (equation (3.6)). The material is here linearly elastic, homogeneous and isotropic (definitions 3.3, 3.5, 3.4) and the stresses are below the yield strength (definition 2.7). This is assumption 7. With the strain \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = u' - y\,v''\) from theorem 5.15 we obtain

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(x, y) = E\,\big(u'(x) - y\,v''(x)\big) . \tag{5.21}\]

The stress is a linear function of the height \(y\). The meaning of the two terms is the same as for the strain. The term \(E\,u'\) is a stress that stretches all fibres of the cross section equally, and the term \(-E\,y\,v''\) is proportional to the height and therefore stretches one side of the cross section and shortens the other (figure 5.32). For \(v'' < 0\), as at the clamp of a cantilever, the upper fibre is stretched and the lower compressed, for \(v'' > 0\) it is the other way round.

Let us test this on the cantilever of section 5.1.3. Later in this section we shall derive that its curvature at the clamp is \(v''(0) = -0{,}02392\) 1/m (radius of curvature \(41{,}8\) m). At the clamp we therefore have \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = -y\,v''(0)\) equal to \(+4{,}78\cdot 10^{-4}\) at the top of the cross section (\(y = 20\) mm) and \(-4{,}78\cdot 10^{-4}\) at the bottom. By equation (5.21) with \(E = 196\) GPa the stress is \(\pm 93{,}75\) MPa, \(43\,\%\) of the yield strength \(220\) MPa.

Assumption 6 has a cost that we must state. Assumption 4 of section 5.3.2 says that the fibres on a normal keep their length, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0\). By lemma 3.6, however, a fibre in uniaxial stress has the transverse strain \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = -\nu\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/E\), which is not zero if \(\nu \neq 0\). Both statements hold together only for \(\nu = 0\). The model is therefore not entirely consistent and stays so in every book that uses it. CM (PDF p. 309) writes explicitly in the derivation that in the relation \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = (2\mu + \lambda)\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) he took \(\nu = 0\), but not in the relation \(2\mu = 2G = E/(1 + \nu)\). What would happen if, instead of assumption 6, we kept \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\)? Hooke's law (theorem 3.11) would give \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = (\lambda + 2\mu)\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\,(1 - \nu)/\big((1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)\big)\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), for \(\nu = 0{,}3\) this factor is \(1{,}346\) and the stress would be \(35\,\%\) larger. That would hold only if someone prevented the cross section from changing its shape. The side faces are free, so the fibre contracts transversally when it is stretched, as in the tensile test. The upper fibre at the clamp of the cantilever stretches along its length by \(4{,}78\cdot 10^{-4}\) and contracts transversally by \(\nu \cdot 4{,}78\cdot 10^{-4} = 1{,}43\cdot 10^{-4}\) of its width, which for a width of \(20\) mm is \(2{,}9\) µm. This is unimportant for the displacements we compute, so we neglect the transverse strains. The stress of the fibre is therefore taken from the uniaxial state (assumption 6), and the displacements from the kinematics of the beam. That the model works well in spite of this inconsistency is already shown by the comparison with the elastic solution above: for a slender beam the difference in stress is \(0{,}011\,\%\).

One more contradiction. By Hooke's law \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\) gives \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 2G\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\), so the shear force \(V = \int \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,dA\) would have nothing to arise from. At the same time, by the equilibrium of a piece of the beam (corollary 5.11), \(V = -M'\) is not zero if the moment changes. This is an inconsistency which EP (p. 74) and CM (PDF p. 309) call a flaw of the Euler-Bernoulli model. Both books resolve it in the same way: they do not compute the shear stress from Hooke's law but from equilibrium, and the shear force from \(V = -M'\). We shall do the same in the next section. For now we are interested only in the normal stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\).

5.5.2 Neutral axis

From history

In calculating the breaking force Mariotte took the same point of rotation of the cross section as Galileo, at the edge of the cross section (TIMO, PDF p. 31). We met Coulomb and his memoir of 1773 in section 1.1. As a member of the military engineering corps he spent nine years in charge of construction works on the island of Martinique, which led him to the mechanical properties of materials, and his memoir also covered bending (TIMO, PDF p. 55). He first tested sandstone. The strength in shear was equal to the strength in tension, and bars for bending, 1 inch deep, 2 inches wide and 9 inches long, broke at a force of 20 pounds (TIMO, PDF p. 56-57).

In his theory he considered a rectangular cantilever and its cross section, and concluded that the fibres of the upper part of the cross section are in tension and those of the lower part are compressed (TIMO, PDF p. 57). He resolved the forces in the fibres into a horizontal and a vertical component and wrote three equations of statics: the sum of the horizontal forces across the cross section is zero, the sum of the vertical forces equals the applied load, and the moment of all fibre forces about a chosen axis equals the moment of the load about the same axis (TIMO, PDF p. 57-58). He noticed that these equations do not depend on how the forces in the fibres depend on the elongations (TIMO, PDF p. 58). He could therefore use them for a material that follows Hooke's law up to fracture and for a perfectly rigid material. For the first he obtained the equation \(S h/6 = Q l\), where \(S\) is the strength of the bar in tension, \(h\) its depth, \(l\) the arm of the force \(Q\) and \(Q\) the breaking force (TIMO, PDF p. 58). For a perfectly rigid material with uniformly distributed forces he obtained \(S h/2 = Q l\) (TIMO, PDF p. 58). The breaking force calculated from this equation was somewhat larger than the measured one, so he concluded that the point of rotation cannot lie at the edge but inside the cross section, where part of the cross section is in compression (TIMO, PDF p. 58). Coulomb applied statics correctly and had a clear picture of the distribution of forces over the cross section (TIMO, PDF p. 58). Engineers understood his results only after more than forty years (TIMO, PDF p. 78).

After the death of his uncle Gauthey in 1807, Navier edited and published the three volumes of his unfinished work on bridges and canals, the second volume in 1813 (TIMO, PDF p. 79). In a note on page 18 of the second volume he gave the complete theory of bending of prismatic bars (TIMO, PDF p. 79). He did not know Coulomb's work. He thought, as Mariotte and Jacob Bernoulli had done, that the position of the line in the cross section where the fibres neither lengthen nor shorten does not matter, and for that line he took the tangent to the edge of the cross section on the concave side (TIMO, PDF p. 79). In the first printed edition of his book on the strength of materials in 1826 he assumed that the bar bends in the plane in which the forces act, so his calculation holds only for beams with a plane of symmetry loaded in that plane, and that the cross sections remain plane during bending (TIMO, PDF p. 81-82). With the three equations of statics he concluded that this line passes through the centroid of the cross section (TIMO, PDF p. 82). We see the reason from the first equation. The force in a fibre is proportional to its distance from the line, and in bending without an axial force the sum of all longitudinal forces across the cross section must be zero. This holds exactly when the line passes through the centroid, since the centroid is the point about which the contributions of area on both sides balance.

Let us return to the question of the section: where in the cross section lie the fibres that neither lengthen nor shorten in bending, and how large is the stress elsewhere.

So far we have left open where in the cross section the axis of the beam lies, the line from which we measure the height \(y\) and on which the displacements \(u\) and \(v\) are defined. Up to now we took cross sections with a symmetry, where it lies at mid-height. But what if the cross section is not symmetric, like the letter T? Let us see what happens if we choose the axis anywhere. Let \(y\) be measured from some line in the cross section, let \(S = \int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,dA\) be the first moment of area (statični moment prereza) about it (definition 5.20), and \(I_0 = \int_{\mathrm{A}} y^2\,dA\) the second moment of area about it. If we insert the stress (5.21) in \(N\) and \(M\) of lemma 5.9, we get \(N = E\,(A\,u' - S\,v'')\) and \(M = E\,(I_0\,v'' - S\,u')\). For \(S \neq 0\) the axial and bending deformations are coupled. An axial force would also bend the beam, and a moment would stretch it. For \(S = 0\) they separate. By the definition of the centroid (definition 5.21) and by the first step of the proof of lemma 5.22, the first moment about a line at height \(c\) above the original origin equals \(S_{\mathrm{z}} - cA = A\,(y_{\mathrm{T}} - c)\), that is the area times the distance of the centroid from the line. This is zero exactly when \(c = y_{\mathrm{T}}\), so when the line passes through the centroid. Therefore \(S = 0\) exactly when the line passes through the centroid, and we place the axis of the beam through the centroids of the cross sections (assumption 8). With this we complete definition 5.1, which placed the axis at mid-height for cross sections with symmetry: there mid-height is exactly the centroid.

Lemma 5.33 Separation of axial and bending parts (osna in upogibna togost se ločita)

Let a beam be made of a linearly elastic, homogeneous, isotropic material with modulus of elasticity \(E\), let the stress in the fibres satisfy equation (5.21) (assumption 6) and let \(y\) be the height above a line \(\ell\) in a cross section of area \(A\) which is a rectangle or a union of rectangles. Let \(S = \int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,dA\) and \(I_0 = \int_{\mathrm{A}} y^2\,dA\). Then

\[N = E\,\big(A\,u' - S\,v''\big) , \qquad M = E\,\big(I_0\,v'' - S\,u'\big) . \tag{5.22}\]

If \(\ell\) passes through the centroid of the cross section, then \(S = 0\), \(I_0 = I\) is the second moment of area of the cross section (definition 5.26) and

\[N = EA\,u' , \qquad M = EI\,v'' . \tag{5.23}\]
Proof

We insert the stress (5.21) in the expressions for \(N\) and \(M\) of lemma 5.9 and corollary 5.19 and compute the integrals over the cross section.

  1. By lemma 5.9 for a rectangular cross section and by corollary 5.19 for a union of rectangles, \(N = \int_{\mathrm{A}} \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,dA\) and \(M = -\int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,dA\). For \(y\) measured from \(\ell\), \(M\) is the moment about \(\ell\).
  2. By equation (5.21), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\,(u' - y\,v'')\). On a cross section at fixed \(x\) the quantities \(u'\) and \(v''\) are constants, since they are functions of \(x\) only, and \(E\) is constant by homogeneity. Hence by linearity of the integral over the cross section (section 5.4.1) we can take them out.
  3. The first integral is \(N = E\,u' \int_{\mathrm{A}} dA - E\,v'' \int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,dA = E\,(A\,u' - S\,v'')\).
  4. The second integral is \(M = -E\,u' \int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,dA + E\,v'' \int_{\mathrm{A}} y^2\,dA = E\,(I_0\,v'' - S\,u')\).
  5. If \(\ell\) passes through the centroid, then by lemma 5.22 the first moment is \(S = 0\). The integral \(\int_{\mathrm{A}} y^2\,dA\) is by definition 5.26 the second moment of area \(I\), since \(\ell\) is an axis through the centroid. With this the equations (5.23) are proved.

Source: EP (2.5.8) to (2.5.11) (pp. 74-75, there \(A_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(B_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(D_{\mathrm{xx}}\) for an \(E\) that varies over the cross section). The sign of \(M\) is opposite to that of EP by D1. CM (PDF p. 310, before (7.3.30)).

What does the theorem say if the modulus of elasticity is not constant over the cross section? Then the integrals acquire weights, \(A_{\mathrm{xx}} = \int E\,dA\), \(B_{\mathrm{xx}} = \int y\,E\,dA\) and \(D_{\mathrm{xx}} = \int y^2 E\,dA\) (EP, (2.5.9), p. 74), and the centroid must be computed with the weight \(E\). This is the case of composite beams of several materials, for example a bimetallic strip. We stay with one material.

Now we can name the line where the stress vanishes.

Definition 5.34 Neutral axis (nevtralna os)

The neutral axis of a cross section of the beam at \(x\) is the set of points of the cross section at which \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0\). If \(v''(x) \neq 0\), it is the line at height \(y_{\mathrm{n}} = u'/v''\), parallel to the axis \(z\).

Source: SADD (PDF p. 577) calls the neutral axis (nevtralna os) the axis through the centroid on which the stress in bending is zero. The general form with \(y_{\mathrm{n}} = u'/v''\) is derived here.

We get the height \(y_{\mathrm{n}}\) from equation (5.21), \(E\,(u' - y\,v'') = 0\). If the axis passes through the centroid and \(N = 0\), then by lemma 5.33 \(u' = N/(EA) = 0\) and \(y_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\): the neutral axis is the axis of the beam itself, the line through the centroids. This happens for the beams of section 5.1.3, since \(N = 0\) for them. If \(N \neq 0\), then by the lemma \(u' = N/(EA)\) and \(v'' = M/(EI)\), so \(y_{\mathrm{n}} = N\,I/(A\,M)\) and the neutral axis is no longer on the axis: it moves towards the side that the moment compresses.

For a cross section with a symmetry the centroid is at mid-height, so the neutral axis is at mid-height. For the letter T it is not. Take the T-section of example 5.25: web \(20 \times 60\) mm, flange \(80 \times 20\) mm on top, \(80\) mm high in all, area \(A = 2800\) mm². The centroid was computed there as the average of the centroids of the two rectangles weighted by area (lemma 5.23). It is at \(y_{\mathrm{T}} = 370/7 = 52{,}86\) mm from the bottom edge and \(27{,}14\) mm from the top edge, so not at mid-height (\(40\) mm). The second moment of area about the centroidal axis is by theorem 5.28 the sum of the contributions of the two rectangles, each being its own moment (lemma 5.27) plus \(A\,d^2\) for the distance \(d\) of its centroid from the centroid of the cross section:

\[I = \frac{20 \cdot 60^3}{12} + 1200\,\big(52{,}86 - 30\big)^2 + \frac{80 \cdot 20^3}{12} + 1600\,\big(70 - 52{,}86\big)^2 = 1{,}510\cdot 10^{6}\ \text{mm}^4 .\]

Let \(N = 0\) and \(M = 500\) N m, as in the largest cross sections of all three beams of section 5.1.3. By the lemma \(u' = 0\) and \(v'' = M/(EI)\), so by (5.21) \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -M\,y/I\) with the height \(y\) above the centroid. At the top (\(y = 27{,}14\) mm) it is \(-9{,}0\) MPa, compression, at the bottom (\(y = -52{,}86\) mm) \(+17{,}5\) MPa, tension. At mid-height (\(y = 40 - 52{,}86 = -12{,}86\) mm) the stress is \(+4{,}3\) MPa and not zero (figure 5.33). The bottom, which is farther from the centroid, has a \(1{,}95\) times larger stress than the top, because the stress is proportional to the distance from the neutral axis and the ratio of the distances is \(52{,}86 : 27{,}14 = 1{,}95\).

5.5.3 Bending moment and curvature

From history

Galileo and Mariotte investigated the strength of beams, not their deflection (TIMO, PDF p. 34). Toward the end of the seventeenth century the calculus with infinitely small quantities was developing fast. Jacob Bernoulli (1654 to 1705) and his brother sought applications for it in examples from mechanics and physics (TIMO, PDF p. 33). One of these examples was the shape into which an elastic bar bends (TIMO, PDF p. 33-34).

Jacob Bernoulli followed Mariotte in the position of the axis of rotation of the cross section and took a cantilever of rectangular cross section with depth \(h\) and width \(b\), loaded by \(P\) at the free end (TIMO, PDF p. 34). The cross section turns about the axis \(A\), and the elongation of the fibres between two neighbouring cross sections is proportional to the distance from \(A\). By Hooke's law the resultant of the forces in the fibres of the cross section equals \(\tfrac12 m\,\Delta\,\mathrm{d}s\,b h/\mathrm{d}s\), where \(\mathrm{d}s\) is the length of a small piece of the bar, \(\Delta\,\mathrm{d}s\) the elongation of the farthest fibre and \(m\) a constant depending on the material (TIMO, PDF p. 34). Its moment about \(A\) must equal the moment \(P x\) of the load about the same axis (TIMO, PDF p. 34). Since the ratio of the fibre elongation to the length \(\mathrm{d}s\) equals \(h\) divided by the radius of curvature \(r\) of the bar, he obtained an equation of the form \(P x = C/r\), where \(C = m b h^3/3\) is a constant determined by the material and the cross section (TIMO, PDF p. 35). Because of the wrong position of the axis of rotation, its expression was wrong. The general form is correct, however: the curvature of the bent bar at each point is proportional to the bending moment at that point (TIMO, PDF p. 35).

Euler accepted this statement of Bernoulli without discussion (TIMO, PDF p. 38-39). He called the constant \(C\) the absolute elasticity. He did not explain its physical meaning, only wrote that it depends on the properties of the material and that for rectangular bars it is proportional to the width and to the square of the depth (TIMO, PDF p. 40). That square was a mistake, since \(C\) is proportional to the third power of the depth, as Bernoulli's expression shows (TIMO, PDF p. 40-41). Navier reached the same relation differently in his book of 1826. From plane cross sections and statics he derived that the curvature equals the moment divided by the product of the modulus \(E\) (the ratio of the load per unit of cross-sectional area to the elongation per unit of length) and the second moment of area \(I\) of the cross section about the line without elongation (TIMO, PDF p. 82). For small deflections he wrote it as \(E I\,\mathrm{d}^2 y/\mathrm{d}x^2 = M\) (TIMO, PDF p. 83). What Bernoulli and Euler wrote with the constant \(C\) is the product \(E I\) in Navier, and in him each part of the constant got its meaning: \(E\) describes the material, \(I\) the cross section.

Let us return to the question of the section: how the moment and the stress in a beam are connected with bending, that is, with the curvature of its axis. Navier's equation is the answer that we shall now derive from our assumptions.

Lemma 5.33 says what happens when the stress is known. Let us turn the question round: for known \(N\) and \(M\) from equilibrium, what is the stress? We solve both equations (5.23) for \(u'\) and \(v''\) and insert into (5.21). Let us state the result as a theorem.

Theorem 5.35 Stress and deformation in bending (napetost in deformacija pri upogibu)

Let a beam have its axis through the centroids of the cross sections, let the displacements satisfy theorem 5.15, let the fibres be in uniaxial stress (assumption 6) and let the material be linearly elastic, homogeneous and isotropic with modulus of elasticity \(E\). Let \(A\) be the area and \(I\) the second moment of area of the cross section about the axis \(z\) through the centroid, and \(N\) and \(M\) the internal force and moment of definition 2.5, lemma 5.9 and corollary 5.19. Then

\[N = EA\,u' , \qquad M = EI\,v'' , \tag{5.24}\]

and the stress in the cross section at \(x\) at height \(y\) above the axis is

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \frac{N}{A} - \frac{M\,y}{I} . \tag{5.25}\]
Proof

The relations (5.24) are proved by lemma 5.33, then we invert them and insert into the stress.

  1. By lemma 5.33, for an axis through the centroid \(N = EA\,u'\) and \(M = EI\,v''\). These are the equations (5.24).
  2. The area \(A\) is positive. The second moment of area \(I = \int_{\mathrm{A}} y^2\,dA\) is the integral of a function that is nonnegative and zero only on the line \(y = 0\) of area zero, so \(I > 0\). Also \(E > 0\). Hence we may divide and get \(u' = N/(EA)\) and \(v'' = M/(EI)\).
  3. We insert into the stress (5.21): \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\,\big(N/(EA) - y\,M/(EI)\big) = N/A - M\,y/I\).

Source: EP (2.5.12), (2.5.15), (2.5.16) (pp. 74-75, in EP \(M_{\text{EP}} = \int z\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,dA = -M\) and \(z\) is our \(y\), so \(M\) in (2.5.12) and (2.5.16) has the opposite sign to that in (5.24) and (5.25)), SADD (D.6) to (D.8) (PDF pp. 577-578, \(M_{\text{SADD}} = M\), \(\sigma = -My/I\) and \(d^2v/dx^2 = M/(EI)\) for \(v\) upward), ONATE2 (1.5) (PDF p. 34, \(M = EI\,d^2w/dx^2\), the same sign as ours), CM (7.3.30), (7.3.31) (PDF p. 310, \(M_{\text{CM}} = -M\)). The translation of signs between the sources was checked with SymPy from our definitions.

The theorem says two things. First, \(M = EI\,v''\) means that the moment is proportional to the curvature \(\kappa = v''\) (definition 5.16), with the property of the cross section and the material \(EI\) as the coefficient. Second, the stress is the sum of a uniform part \(N/A\) and a part that is linear in \(y\) and proportional to \(M\).

Definition 5.36 Axial and bending stiffness (osna in upogibna togost)

The product \(EA\) of the modulus of elasticity and the area of the cross section is the axial stiffness of the beam, and the product \(EI\) of the modulus of elasticity and the second moment of area of the cross section about the axis \(z\) through the centroid is the bending stiffness. Their units are N and N m².

Source: EP (2.5.9), (2.5.10) (p. 74, there \(A_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and \(D_{\mathrm{xx}}\)), CM (PDF p. 310). The names axial and bending stiffness were promised by section 3.7 of chapter 3.

Why do we call \(EI\) a stiffness? From \(M = EI\,v''\) we have \(EI = M/v''\): this is the moment that we need for a unit of curvature. Take the beam of section 5.1.3, where \(E = 196\) GPa and \(I = bh^3/12 = 1{,}0667\cdot 10^{-7}\) m⁴. We get \(EI = 20\,906{,}7\) N m². A curvature of \(1\) 1/m, that is a circular arc of radius \(1\) m, would need a moment of \(20\,907\) N m. The curvature at the clamp of the cantilever, \(0{,}0239\) 1/m, corresponds to a moment of \(500\) N m. The axial stiffness \(EA = 1{,}568\cdot 10^{8}\) N is the force that would stretch the bar by \(100\,\%\) (\(\varepsilon = 1\)), if the material could bear it. For \(\varepsilon = 10^{-3}\) the force is \(156{,}8\) kN. Why \(I\) is decisive is shown by \(h^3\) in \(I = bh^3/12\) (lemma 5.27): if we double the height, \(EI\) grows eightfold, while \(EA\) grows only twofold for the same use of material.

Let us test the theorem on both beams of section 5.1.3. For the cantilever, clamped at \(x = 0\), with a force of \(500\) N downward at the end \(x = L = 1\) m, by section 5.2.2 we have \(N = 0\) and \(M(x) = -F\,(L - x)\), so at the clamp \(M(0) = -500\) N m. The curvature at the clamp is \(v''(0) = M(0)/(EI) = -500/20\,906{,}7 = -0{,}02392\) 1/m, the radius of curvature \(41{,}8\) m. The stress at the top of the cross section (\(y = h/2 = 20\) mm) by (5.25) is

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -\frac{M\,y}{I} = \frac{500\ \text{N m} \cdot 0{,}020\ \text{m}}{1{,}0667\cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^4} = 93{,}75\ \text{MPa} ,\]

tension, and at the bottom it is \(-93{,}75\) MPa, compression (figure 5.34). This is \(43\,\%\) of the yield strength, so we are in the region where linear elasticity holds. The tension is at the top because at the clamp \(v'' < 0\) and the upper fibres stretch (figure 5.32). For the simply supported beam with the uniform load \(q_0 = 1\) kN/m the largest moment at mid-span is \(M = +500\) N m, equal in magnitude, but the stress has the opposite sign: on the upper side it is \(-93{,}75\) MPa, compression, and on the lower \(+93{,}75\) MPa, tension. The same number is no coincidence. For all three beams we chose the load so that the largest moment is the same (section 5.2.2), and by the theorem the stress depends only on the moment and the cross section.

What does \(M = EI\,v''\) mean on a piece of the beam? Figure 5.35 shows a piece of the beam with moments on both faces. The moment on the right face is the moment that the part of the beam to the right of it exerts on the piece, that is the moment of the stresses on the face with outward normal \(+\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) (lemma 5.9), and on the left face it is equal and opposite. When \(M > 0\), the beam bends as the figure shows: \(v'' > 0\), the fibres at the bottom are stretched and at the top compressed. If we reverse the moment, the curvature reverses.

Why is the cross section in the shape of the letter I so popular? Take the cross section of section 5.4.3: height \(200\) mm, flanges \(100 \times 10\) mm, web \(180 \times 6\) mm, \(A = 3080\) mm², \(I = 2{,}098\cdot 10^{7}\) mm⁴. The same moment \(500\) N m gives at the outer edge by (5.25) the stress \(500 \cdot 0{,}100/(2{,}098\cdot 10^{-5}) = 2{,}38\) MPa, at the junction of web and flange \(2{,}14\) MPa. A rectangle of the same area, \(20 \times 154\) mm, would have \(I = 6{,}087\cdot 10^{6}\) mm⁴, so \(3{,}45\) times less, and a stress of \(6{,}32\) MPa. Material far from the axis carries the most moment, because \(y^2\) grows with distance, so the I-section is \(3{,}45\) times stiffer for the same area (figure 5.36).

We proved the theorem from the model. Is the model really accurate for bending? Let us check it for pure bending (section 5.3.1), where the argument from symmetry showed that the cross sections stay plane. The exact elastic solution for pure bending of a straight beam with a rectangular cross section (SADD, Example 8.2, PDF pp. 171-172, plane stress) gives a stress that equals the elementary one, and a displacement of the axis that is also equal (SADD (8.1.22), (8.1.23)). In pure bending the elementary stress (5.25) is therefore exact. The difference between the model and the elastic solution appears only under shear loading, which the next section treats.

5.5.4 Temperature and bending

For the uniaxial state we have already seen that a freely heated piece stretches without stress (theorem 3.15). A beam, however, can be heated unevenly: for example, if the sun heats the upper flange of a bridge beam and the lower one stays in the shade. What happens then?

Hooke's law with temperature, in the uniaxial form, for a fibre in which \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) (assumption 6), is \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/E + \alpha\,\Delta T\), where \(\alpha\) is the coefficient of thermal expansion (first row of equation (3.16)). EP (2.4.19), p. 70, writes the same relation in general form with the stiffness matrix. Let us denote the change of temperature by \(\theta(x, y)\) and assume that it is linear over the height, \(\theta = \theta_{\mathrm{m}}(x) + g(x)\,y\), where \(\theta_{\mathrm{m}}\) is the change of temperature on the axis and \(g\) the temperature gradient over the height, in K/m. A linear one is the simplest distribution with a gradient. How the temperature is really distributed over a body is treated by chapter 14.

Theorem 5.37 Bending caused by temperature (upogib zaradi temperature)

Let the assumptions of theorem 5.35 hold for the beam, let \(E\) and \(\alpha\) not depend on temperature and let the change of temperature be \(\theta(x, y) = \theta_{\mathrm{m}}(x) + g(x)\,y\). Then

\[N = EA\,(u' - \alpha\,\theta_{\mathrm{m}}) , \qquad M = EI\,(v'' + \alpha\,g) , \tag{5.26}\]

and the stress is still \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = N/A - M\,y/I\). If \(N = M = 0\), then \(v'' = -\alpha\,g\), \(u' = \alpha\,\theta_{\mathrm{m}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0\).

Proof

The procedure is as for lemma 5.33, except that the thermal part is subtracted in the stress.

  1. In the uniaxial state, by Hooke's law with temperature, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/E + \alpha\,\theta\) (theorem 3.15, first row of equation (3.16), for \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\)), so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} - \alpha\,\theta)\).
  2. With \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = u' - y\,v''\) (theorem 5.15) we have \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\,\big(u' - \alpha\,\theta_{\mathrm{m}} - y\,(v'' + \alpha\,g)\big)\).
  3. By lemma 5.9 and corollary 5.19, \(N = \int_{\mathrm{A}} \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,dA\). From step 2, \(N = E\,(u' - \alpha\,\theta_{\mathrm{m}})\,A - E\,(v'' + \alpha\,g) \int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,dA\), and since the axis passes through the centroid, the integral is zero (lemma 5.22). This is the first equation (5.26).
  4. By the same lemma, \(M = -\int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,dA = -E\,(u' - \alpha\,\theta_{\mathrm{m}}) \int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,dA + E\,(v'' + \alpha\,g) \int_{\mathrm{A}} y^2\,dA = EI\,(v'' + \alpha\,g)\). This is the second equation.
  5. From step 3, \(u' - \alpha\,\theta_{\mathrm{m}} = N/(EA)\), from step 4, \(v'' + \alpha\,g = M/(EI)\). We insert into step 2: \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = N/A - M\,y/I\). For \(N = M = 0\) we have \(u' = \alpha\,\theta_{\mathrm{m}}\) and \(v'' = -\alpha\,g\).

Source: The relation of stress and strain: EP (2.4.19) (p. 70). The result for the beam is not from the sources, it is derived from theorem 3.15, checked with SymPy.

The theorem shows that the stress is still expressed by \(N\) and \(M\) as before. The temperature only shifts the zeros of the axial and bending deformation: the beam is free of stress when \(v'' = -\alpha\,g\), not when \(v'' = 0\). The curvature \(-\alpha g\) we call the free curvature, the curvature that the beam takes when nothing restrains it.

Let us test this on our cantilever. Let the upper edge be \(20\) K warmer than the lower, \(\theta = g\,y\) with \(g = 20/0{,}040 = 500\) K/m and \(\theta_{\mathrm{m}} = 0\) (on the axis the temperature does not change). The free curvature is \(v'' = -\alpha g = -15\cdot 10^{-6} \cdot 500 = -7{,}5\cdot 10^{-3}\) 1/m. The upper fibre has the free strain \(\alpha \cdot 10\ \text{K} = 1{,}5\cdot 10^{-4}\), the lower \(-1{,}5\cdot 10^{-4}\). The cantilever is clamped at \(x = 0\) (\(v(0) = v'(0) = 0\)) and has no load. Equilibrium (section 5.2) gives \(N = M = 0\) along the whole length. Theorem 5.37 therefore gives \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0\) everywhere, and the beam bends: \(v'' = -\alpha g\), \(v'(x) = -\alpha g\,x\) and \(v(x) = -\alpha g\,x^2/2\). The free end at \(x = L = 1\) m falls by \(\alpha g L^2/2 = 3{,}75\) mm. The upper fibres are longer, so the cantilever bends downward. There is no stress, because nothing restrains the bending.

Now let us clamp the same beam at both ends (\(v = v' = 0\) at \(x = 0\) and \(x = L\), and the axis cannot stretch). Without load over the span \(V' = -q = 0\) and \(M' = -V\), so \(M\) is a linear function of \(x\), and by theorem 5.37 \(v'' = M/(EI) - \alpha g\) is linear. Integrating a linear function twice gives a cubic polynomial \(v\). Since \(v = v' = 0\) at both ends gives it double zeros at \(x = 0\) and \(x = L\), it would have to be divisible by the polynomial of fourth degree \(x^2 (x - L)^2\), so it is zero and \(v'' = 0\). Similarly \(u(0) = u(L) = 0\) gives \(u' = 0\). By theorem 5.37 we then have \(N = 0\) and \(M = EI\,\alpha g = 20\,906{,}7 \cdot 7{,}5\cdot 10^{-3} = 156{,}8\) N m. The stress is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -M\,y/I = -E\,\alpha\,g\,y\), at the top \(-E\alpha\,\Delta T/2 = -29{,}4\) MPa (compression, the warm fibre would like to stretch) and at the bottom \(+29{,}4\) MPa, which is \(13\,\%\) of the yield strength. The clamps must carry a moment of \(156{,}8\) N m, without any external load. For comparison: if the beam clamped at both ends were heated evenly by \(20\) K (\(\theta_{\mathrm{m}} = 20\) K, \(g = 0\)), the stress would be \(-E\alpha\theta_{\mathrm{m}} = -58{,}8\) MPa, twice as much as for a temperature difference of \(20\) K over the height, where \(\theta\) at the edges is \(\pm 10\) K (figure 5.37).

What have we learned in this section? The stress in a beam is a linear function of the height above the centroidal axis, and has the form (5.25). The moment is proportional to the curvature, and the constant of proportionality is the bending stiffness \(EI\). On the steel bar \(20 \times 40\) mm at \(M = 500\) N m we got \(93{,}75\) MPa, \(43\,\%\) of the yield strength, and with an I-section of equal area only \(2{,}38\) MPa. The next section shows how the beam carries the shear force \(V\).
