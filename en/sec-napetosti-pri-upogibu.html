Chapter 5 Beam theory (teorija nosilcev)

5.5 Stress and stiffness in bending

We now have two parts of the beam. From equilibrium we know the internal forces in every cross section, the axial force \(N\), the shear force \(V\) and the bending moment \(M\) (section 5.2). From kinematics we know the strain, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = u' - y\,v''\) (theorem 5.15), where \(u(x)\) and \(v(x)\) are the displacements of the points of the axis. The link between them is missing. How large is the stress in the fibre at height \(y\) above the axis, and how are \(N\) and \(M\) related to the displacements \(u\) and \(v\)? We need the answer because we shall compute the deflection of the beam from \(M(x)\), and from the same stress check whether the steel carries the load. All calculations will be done on the beams of section 5.1.3, a flat steel bar of width \(b = 20\) mm and height \(h = 40\) mm, standing on its edge.

The path is short. From the strain and Hooke's law we get the stress, where we must say what we assume about the other stress components. We then add the stress over the cross section, as lemma 5.9 and corollary 5.19 say, and obtain \(N\) and \(M\) in terms of the displacements. This shows where in the cross section the axis must be placed. Finally we invert the relation and compute the stress from \(N\) and \(M\). The addendum at the end of the section shows what changes if the beam is heated unevenly.

5.5.1 From strain to stress

The question is how large the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) is in the fibre (definition 1.25) that lies along the axis \(x\) at height \(y\). Hooke's law for an isotropic material (theorem 3.19) links the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) with all three normal strains at once. For \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) we would therefore need \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\) as well as \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), or else the stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\), which press or pull the fibre crosswise. What do we know about them?

The beam is thin. The upper and lower faces are free, except where the distributed load \(q\) acts on the upper one, and the side faces are entirely free. The stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) is therefore zero on the lower face, on the upper face by the condition on that face it equals the pressure of the load, and in between it takes values between the two. Let us check how large it is. The simply supported beam of section 5.1.3 carries \(q_0 = 1\) kN/m. Every metre of the beam therefore carries \(1\) kN, spread over the upper edge of the cross section, which is \(b = 20\) mm wide, and that is a pressure \(q_0/b = 50\) kPa. Take the small square at the top of midspan, where the bending moment is largest. The longitudinal stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) compresses it there with \(93{,}75\) MPa (we compute it later in this section), the transverse \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) with only \(50\) kPa, drawn much too long in the figure:

\[\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}} \le \frac{0{,}05\ \text{MPa}}{93{,}75\ \text{MPa}} = 0{,}05\,\% .\]

The same holds for \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\), since the side faces are free.

That \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) are small does not yet say how \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) changes if we set them to zero. The elastic calculation says that. For a beam under a uniform load, SADD (PDF p. 175, plane stress) finds that the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) at the outer fibres differs from the elementary value by \(w/5\), where \(w\) is the pressure of the load on the upper face, whatever the dimensions of the beam. Our cross section \(20 \times 40\) mm is not thin in the direction \(z\), so carrying the plane stress solution over to it is only an estimate. With \(w = q_0/b\) it gives a difference \(w/5 = 10\) kPa, and against the largest stress \(93{,}75\) MPa this is \(0{,}011\,\%\). The difference grows, however, with the square of the ratio \(h/L\). The largest stress of a simply supported beam of span \(L\) is, as we shall derive later, \(3wL^2/(4h^2)\), so the ratio is

\[\frac{w/5}{3wL^2/(4h^2)} = \frac{4}{15}\Big(\frac{h}{L}\Big)^2 .\]

For our beam with \(L/h = 2\ \text{m}/40\ \text{mm} = 50\) this is \(0{,}011\,\%\), for a short beam with \(L/h = 2\) already \(6{,}7\,\%\).

In the figure both beams have the same height \(h\) and differ in the span \(L\). The slender beam with \(L/h = 50\) has a small difference of \(0{,}011\,\%\), the short beam with \(L/h = 2\) already \(6{,}7\,\%\), because the difference \(w/5\) stays the same while the largest stress \(3wL^2/(4h^2)\) is much smaller at the shorter span.

We therefore take a step which we call assumption 6 (the numbers of the assumptions are the same as in the table that collects them at the end of the chapter): in each fibre the stress is uniaxial, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\). It is right for slender beams such as ours, where the neglected part changes \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) by \(0{,}011\,\%\). It does not hold for short and deep beams, where the error grows to several percent, and right next to a point load or a support, where the force presses on a small area and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) is large there. The shear stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) does not follow from Hooke's law, as we shall see below, but from equilibrium (in the next section). For the normal stress of a fibre, however, what we learned in the tensile test holds. The fibre is in the same state as the bar of the test, in which the only nonzero stress component is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), so by lemma 3.12 on uniaxial stress we have \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) (equation (3.15)). The factor \(E\) holds because the fibre is not held laterally: for a piece whose lateral strains are prevented the stiffness is different (lemma 3.14). The material is here linearly elastic, homogeneous and isotropic (definitions 3.9, 3.11, 3.10) and the stresses are below the yield strength (definition 2.7). This is assumption 7, which we check on the cantilever below. With the strain \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = u' - y\,v''\) from theorem 5.15 we obtain

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(x, y) = E\,\big(u'(x) - y\,v''(x)\big) . \tag{5.21}\]

The stress is a linear function of the height \(y\). We see why on a short piece of the beam. The piece before bending has the length \(\Delta x\) and the height \(h\), and the height \(y\) is measured upwards. Let us bend it as the cantilever is bent at the clamp, with \(u' = 0\) and \(v'' < 0\). The cross sections stay straight, so their extensions meet at the point \(O\), the centre of curvature. The axis keeps the length \(\Delta x\) and lies on a circle of radius \(R\), where \(1/R = -v''\) is the curvature. The upper fibre lies on a circle of radius \(R + h/2\) and is therefore longer, the lower one on a circle of radius \(R - h/2\) and is shorter.

Let us straighten all three fibres and lay them with their left ends together: the upper, the middle and the lower, each grey dashed before bending and orange after it. The vertical grey dashed line shows where the fibres ended before bending. Arcs of the same angle have lengths in the ratio of their radii, so the upper fibre lengthens by \(\Delta x \cdot \frac{h/2}{R}\), the middle one keeps its length, and the lower one shortens by just as much. The difference divided by the length \(\Delta x\) is the strain. At the top it is \(h/(2R) = -\frac{h}{2}\,v''\), on the axis zero, at the bottom the opposite, and in between, by the same ratio of radii, it grows in proportion to the height. This is the straight line in the middle, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = -y\,v''\). The stress is the strain multiplied by \(E\) and therefore has the same shape, \(-E\,\frac{h}{2}\,v''\) at the top and \(E\,\frac{h}{2}\,v''\) at the bottom. The term \(E\,u'\) in equation (5.21) would add the same stress to all fibres and only shift the line sideways. For \(v'' < 0\), as at the clamp of a cantilever, the upper fibre is stretched and the lower compressed, for \(v'' > 0\) it is the other way round.

Let us test this on the cantilever of section 5.1.3. Later in this section we shall derive that its curvature at the clamp is \(v''(0) = -0{,}02392\) 1/m, so the radius of curvature is \(R = 41{,}8\) m, strongly shortened in the figure. At the clamp \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = -y\,v''(0)\), at the top and bottom of the cross section, at \(y = \pm 20\) mm,

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \pm 0{,}02\ \text{m} \cdot 0{,}02392\ \text{m}^{-1} = \pm 4{,}78\cdot 10^{-4} ,\]

plus at the top and minus at the bottom. By equation (5.21) with \(E = 196\) GPa the stress at the top and at the bottom is \(\pm 93{,}75\) MPa, \(43\,\%\) of the yield strength \(220\) MPa. This checks assumption 7. Linear elasticity is right here because all stresses are below the yield strength. The stress at the edge is proportional to the moment, so the edge fibre would reach the yield strength at the moment \(500\ \text{N m} \cdot 220/93{,}75 = 1{,}17\) kN m. At a larger moment the outer fibres would stay permanently lengthened or shortened after unloading (definition 2.7) and equation (5.21) would no longer hold there. Homogeneity fails for beams of several materials, which we mention in section 5.5.2.

Assumption 6 has a cost that we must state. As we saw in section 5.3.4, assumption (b) of section 5.3.2 (assumption 4 in the table at the end of the chapter) requires that the fibres on a normal keep their length, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0\). By lemma 3.12, however, a fibre in uniaxial stress has the transverse strain \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = -\nu\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/E\), which is not zero if \(\nu \neq 0\). Both statements hold together only for \(\nu = 0\), so the model is not entirely consistent. What would happen if, instead of assumption 6, we kept \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\)? Hooke's law (theorem 3.19) would give \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = (\lambda + 2\mu)\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\,(1 - \nu)/\big((1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)\big)\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), for \(\nu = 0{,}3\) this factor is \(1{,}346\) and the stress would be \(35\,\%\) larger. That would hold only if someone prevented the cross section from changing. The side faces are free, so the fibre contracts transversally when it is stretched, as in the tensile test. The upper fibre at the clamp of the cantilever stretches along its length by \(4{,}78\cdot 10^{-4}\) and contracts transversally by \(\nu \cdot 4{,}78\cdot 10^{-4} = 1{,}43\cdot 10^{-4}\) of its width, so the cross section narrows at the top by \(2{,}9\) µm, by \(1{,}4\) µm on each side. This is unimportant for the displacements we compute, so we neglect the transverse strains. The stress of the fibre is therefore taken from the uniaxial state (assumption 6), and the displacements from the kinematics of the beam. That the model works well in spite of this inconsistency is already shown by the comparison with the elastic solution above: for a slender beam the difference in stress is \(0{,}011\,\%\).

One more contradiction, which we also met in section 5.3.4. By Hooke's law \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\) gives \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 2G\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\), so the shear force \(V = \int \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,dA\) would have nothing to arise from. The equilibrium of a short piece of the beam, however, requires \(V = -M'\) (corollary 5.11), which is not zero if the moment changes. EP (p. 74) and CM (PDF p. 309) call this a flaw of the Euler-Bernoulli model and do not compute shear stresses from Hooke's law. We shall do the same in the next section: shear stresses from equilibrium, the shear force from \(V = -M'\). For now we are interested only in the normal stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\).

5.5.2 Neutral axis

From history

In calculating the breaking force Mariotte took the same point of rotation of the cross section as Galileo, at the edge of the cross section (TIMO, PDF p. 31). We met Coulomb and his memoir of 1773 in section 1.1. As a member of the military engineering corps he spent nine years in charge of construction works on the island of Martinique, which led him to the mechanical properties of materials, and his memoir also covered bending (TIMO, PDF p. 55). He first tested sandstone. The strength in shear was equal to the strength in tension, and bars for bending, 1 inch deep, 2 inches wide and 9 inches long, broke at a force of 20 pounds (TIMO, PDF p. 56-57).

In his theory he considered a rectangular cantilever and its cross section, and concluded that the fibres of the upper part of the cross section are in tension and those of the lower part are compressed (TIMO, PDF p. 57). He resolved the forces in the fibres into a horizontal and a vertical component and wrote three equations of statics: the sum of the horizontal forces across the cross section is zero, the sum of the vertical forces equals the applied load, and the moment of all fibre forces about a chosen axis equals the moment of the load about the same axis (TIMO, PDF p. 57-58). He noticed that these equations do not depend on how the forces in the fibres depend on the elongations (TIMO, PDF p. 58). He could therefore use them for a material that follows Hooke's law up to fracture and for a perfectly rigid material. For the first he obtained the equation \(S h/6 = Q l\), where \(S\) is the strength of the bar in tension, \(h\) its depth, \(l\) the arm of the force \(Q\) and \(Q\) the breaking force (TIMO, PDF p. 58). For a perfectly rigid material with uniformly distributed forces he obtained \(S h/2 = Q l\) (TIMO, PDF p. 58). The breaking force calculated from this equation was somewhat larger than the measured one, so he concluded that the point of rotation cannot lie at the edge but inside the cross section, where part of the cross section is in compression (TIMO, PDF p. 58). Coulomb applied statics correctly and had a clear picture of the distribution of forces over the cross section (TIMO, PDF p. 58). Engineers understood his results only after more than forty years (TIMO, PDF p. 78).

After the death of his uncle Gauthey in 1807, Navier edited and published the three volumes of his unfinished work on bridges and canals, the second volume in 1813 (TIMO, PDF p. 79). In a note on page 18 of the second volume he gave the complete theory of bending of prismatic bars (TIMO, PDF p. 79). He did not know Coulomb's work. He thought, as Mariotte and Jacob Bernoulli had done, that the position of the line in the cross section where the fibres neither lengthen nor shorten does not matter, and for that line he took the tangent to the edge of the cross section on the concave side (TIMO, PDF p. 79). In the first printed edition of his book on the strength of materials in 1826 he assumed that the bar bends in the plane in which the forces act, so his calculation holds only for beams with a plane of symmetry loaded in that plane, and that the cross sections remain plane during bending (TIMO, PDF p. 81-82). With the three equations of statics he concluded that this line passes through the centroid of the cross section (TIMO, PDF p. 82). The reason lies in the first equation, the sum of the horizontal forces, and we show it below on figure 5.82: in bending without an axial force the push and the pull in the cross section must balance, and this happens exactly with the line through the centroid.

Let us return to the question of the section: where in the cross section lie the fibres that neither lengthen nor shorten in bending, and how large is the stress elsewhere.

So far we have left open where in the cross section the axis of the beam lies, the line from which we measure the height \(y\) and on which the displacements \(u\) and \(v\) are defined. Up to now we took cross sections that are also symmetric about the horizontal axis \(z\), where the axis of the beam lies at mid-height. But what if the cross section is not symmetric about the axis \(z\), like the letter T (figure 5.82)?

Take the T-section of example 5.25, with the flange \(80 \times 20\) mm on top and the web \(20 \times 60\) mm below it, and bend it with a moment that shortens the upper fibres and stretches the lower ones. By equation (5.21) the stress is linear in the height, and its slope \(-E\,v''\) is given by the curvature. Let the stress change by \(0{,}331\) MPa with every millimetre of height, which we obtain below for the moment \(500\) N m. Let us first guess that the line of zero stress is at mid-height, \(40\) mm from the bottom. Above it the whole flange and the upper part of the web are compressed, below it only the lower part of the web is stretched. The stress is equally large at the top and at the bottom, \(0{,}331 \cdot 40 = 13{,}2\) MPa. Stress added up over an area gives a force, and the flange is four times as wide as the web, so the same stress on it gives four times the force. The upper part therefore pushes on the cross section with \(17{,}2\) kN, the lower part pulls on it with \(5{,}3\) kN. The forces are drawn on the right, as if we looked at the cross section from the side: the push ends at the cross section, the pull starts at it.

Push and pull must balance. Why? No force acts on the beam along its axis, so the axial force \(N\) is zero. The cut-off part of the beam is held in the direction \(x\) only by the cross section, and since the cut-off part is at rest, by Newton's law the sum of the forces on it in the direction \(x\) is zero (section 5.2). With the line at mid-height, however, a push of \(17{,}2 - 5{,}3 = 11{,}9\) kN is left over, which would shorten the beam. Such a state is not possible at \(N = 0\).

So let us move the line upwards, towards the flange, as the lower part of the figure shows. The compressed part gets smaller, the stretched part larger. At the height \(52{,}86\) mm from the bottom the push and the pull are equal, both \(9{,}25\) kN. Exactly at this height lies the centroid of the cross section, which we computed in example 5.25. This is no coincidence. A small piece of the cross section carries a force proportional to its area and to its distance from the line, above as push and below as pull. Push and pull therefore balance exactly when the sum of the areas of the pieces, multiplied by their distances from the line, is zero, which is the first moment of area \(\int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,dA = 0\) about the line. By definition 5.21 this means that the line passes through the centroid.

We can see the shift of the line in another way too. The same curvature with the line higher means that we added the same pull to all fibres. At mid-height the stress was zero, now it is \(0{,}331 \cdot 12{,}86 = 4{,}26\) MPa, and just as much is added everywhere. A uniform \(4{,}26\) MPa on the whole area \(1200 + 1600 = 2800\) mm² is \(11{,}9\) kN, exactly the excess of push. This is the term \(E\,u'\) in equation (5.21).

Push and pull, equally large and opposite, lie at a distance of \(54{,}1\) mm. They have no net force, but they have the moment \(9{,}25\ \text{kN} \cdot 54{,}1\ \text{mm} = 500\) N m, exactly the bending moment of the cross section. The bending moment is therefore the couple of push and pull in the cross section.

The calculation shows the same for every cross section. Let \(y\) be measured from some line in the cross section, let \(S = \int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,dA\) be the first moment of area (statični moment prereza) about it (definition 5.20), and \(I_0 = \int_{\mathrm{A}} y^2\,dA\) the second moment of area about it. If we insert the stress (5.21) in \(N\) and \(M\) of lemma 5.9, we get \(N = E\,(A\,u' - S\,v'')\) and \(M = E\,(I_0\,v'' - S\,u')\). For \(S \neq 0\) the axial and bending deformations are coupled. An axial force would also bend the beam, and a moment would stretch or shorten it, as with the line at mid-height. For \(S = 0\) they separate. By the definition of the centroid (definition 5.21) and by the first step of the proof of lemma 5.22, the first moment about a line at height \(c\) above the original origin equals \(S_{\mathrm{z}} - cA = A\,(y_{\mathrm{T}} - c)\), that is the area times the distance of the centroid from the line. This is zero exactly when \(c = y_{\mathrm{T}}\), so when the line passes through the centroid. Therefore \(S = 0\) exactly when the line passes through the centroid, and we place the axis of the beam through the centroids of the cross sections (assumption 8). This is not an approximation but a choice that does not spoil the results: with it we only separate the axial and the bending part. With this we complete definition 5.1, which placed the axis at mid-height for cross sections with symmetry: there mid-height is exactly the centroid.

Lemma 5.33 Separation of axial and bending parts (osna in upogibna togost se ločita)

Let a beam be made of a linearly elastic, homogeneous, isotropic material with modulus of elasticity \(E\), let the stress in the fibres satisfy equation (5.21) (assumption 6) and let \(y\) be the height above a line \(\ell\) in a cross section of area \(A\) which is a rectangle or a union of rectangles, with \(u\) and \(v\) the displacements of the points of \(\ell\). Let \(S = \int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,dA\) and \(I_0 = \int_{\mathrm{A}} y^2\,dA\). Then

\[N = E\,\big(A\,u' - S\,v''\big) , \qquad M = E\,\big(I_0\,v'' - S\,u'\big) . \tag{5.22}\]

Here \(M\) is the moment of the stress about a point of the line \(\ell\) (step 1 of the proof), which equals the bending moment of definition 5.8 when \(\ell\) passes through the centroid or when \(N = 0\), since in general \(M = M_{\mathrm{T}} - (y_{\mathrm{T}} - c)\,N\), where \(M_{\mathrm{T}}\) is the moment about the centroid and \(c\) the height of \(\ell\). If \(\ell\) passes through the centroid of the cross section, then \(S = 0\), \(I_0 = I\) is the second moment of area of the cross section (definition 5.26) and

\[N = EA\,u' , \qquad M = EI\,v'' . \tag{5.23}\]
Proof

We insert the stress (5.21) in the expressions for \(N\) and \(M\) of lemma 5.9 and corollary 5.19 and compute the integrals over the cross section.

  1. By corollary 5.19 with the point \(C\) on \(\ell\) (a rectangle is a figure made of rectangles, and so is a union of rectangles), \(N = \int_{\mathrm{A}} \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,dA\), the sum of push and pull, and \(M = -\int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,dA\), their moment. For \(y\) measured from \(\ell\), \(M\) is the moment about \(\ell\).
  2. By equation (5.21), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\,(u' - y\,v'')\). At fixed \(x\) the quantities \(u'\), \(v''\) and \(E\) are constants over the cross section, so by linearity of the integral (section 5.4.1) we take them out.
  3. The first integral is \(N = E\,u' \int_{\mathrm{A}} dA - E\,v'' \int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,dA = E\,(A\,u' - S\,v'')\).
  4. The second integral is \(M = -E\,u' \int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,dA + E\,v'' \int_{\mathrm{A}} y^2\,dA = E\,(I_0\,v'' - S\,u')\).
  5. If \(\ell\) passes through the centroid, then by lemma 5.22 the first moment is \(S = 0\), as with the line through the centroid of the T-section. The integral \(\int_{\mathrm{A}} y^2\,dA\) is by definition 5.26 the second moment of area \(I\), since \(\ell\) is an axis through the centroid. With this the equations (5.23) are proved.

Source: EP (2.5.8) to (2.5.11) (pp. 74-75, there \(A_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(B_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(D_{\mathrm{xx}}\) for an \(E\) that varies over the cross section). The sign of \(M\) is opposite to that of EP by D1. CM (PDF p. 310, before (7.3.30)).

What does lemma 5.33 say if the modulus of elasticity is not constant over the cross section? Then the integrals acquire weights, \(A_{\mathrm{xx}} = \int E\,dA\), \(B_{\mathrm{xx}} = \int y\,E\,dA\) and \(D_{\mathrm{xx}} = \int y^2 E\,dA\) (EP, (2.5.9), p. 74), and the centroid must be computed with the weight \(E\), so the weighted centroid moves from the geometric one towards the stiff layer. This is the case of composite beams of several materials, for example a bimetallic strip, where steel has more than twice the \(E\) of aluminium. We stay with one material.

Now we can name the line where the stress vanishes.

Definition 5.34 Neutral axis (nevtralna os)

The neutral axis of a cross section of the beam at \(x\) is the set of points of the cross section at which \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0\). If \(v''(x) \neq 0\), it consists of the points of the cross section on the line at height \(y_{\mathrm{n}} = u'/v''\), parallel to the axis \(z\). If this line misses the cross section (at a large axial force), the cross section has no neutral axis.

Source: SADD (PDF p. 577) calls the neutral axis (nevtralna os) the axis through the centroid on which the stress in bending is zero. The general form with \(y_{\mathrm{n}} = u'/v''\) is derived here.

We get the height \(y_{\mathrm{n}}\) from equation (5.21), \(E\,(u' - y\,v'') = 0\). If the axis passes through the centroid and \(N = 0\), then by lemma 5.33 \(u' = N/(EA) = 0\) and \(y_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\): the neutral axis is the axis of the beam itself, the line through the centroids. This happens for the beams of section 5.1.3, since \(N = 0\) for them. If \(N \neq 0\), then by the lemma \(u' = N/(EA)\) and \(v'' = M/(EI)\), so \(y_{\mathrm{n}} = N\,I/(A\,M)\) and the neutral axis is no longer on the axis. The uniform stress \(N/A\) moves it, just as in the T-section the uniform pull of \(4{,}26\) MPa moved the line of zero stress from mid-height to the centroid. For a tensile axial force it moves towards the side that the moment compresses, for a compressive one towards the opposite side.

Let us also compute the stresses in the T-section. The centroid is at \(y_{\mathrm{T}} = 370/7 = 52{,}86\) mm from the bottom edge and \(27{,}14\) mm from the top edge, so not at mid-height (\(40\) mm). The second moment of area about the centroidal axis is by theorem 5.28 the sum of the contributions of the web and the flange, each being its own moment (lemma 5.27) plus \(A\,d^2\) for the distance \(d\) of its centroid from the centroid of the cross section. The centroid of the web is at the height \(30\) mm, the centroid of the flange at \(70\) mm, so the distances are \(52{,}86 - 30\) and \(70 - 52{,}86\) mm:

\[I = \frac{20 \cdot 60^3}{12} + 1200\,\big(52{,}86 - 30\big)^2 + \frac{80 \cdot 20^3}{12} + 1600\,\big(70 - 52{,}86\big)^2 = 1{,}510\cdot 10^{6}\ \text{mm}^4 .\]

Let \(N = 0\) and \(M = 500\) N m, as \(|M|\) in the largest cross sections of both beams under all three loads of section 5.1.3 (for the cantilever \(M = -500\) N m and the stresses change sign). By the lemma \(u' = 0\) and \(v'' = M/(EI)\), so by (5.21) \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -M\,y/I\) with the height \(y\) above the centroid. The stress therefore changes by \(M/I = 500\,000\ \text{N mm}/(1{,}510\cdot 10^{6}\ \text{mm}^4) = 0{,}331\) MPa with every millimetre of height, as we took above. At the top (\(y = 27{,}14\) mm) it is \(-9{,}0\) MPa, compression, at the bottom (\(y = -52{,}86\) mm) \(+17{,}5\) MPa, tension. At mid-height (\(y = 40 - 52{,}86 = -12{,}86\) mm) the stress is \(+4{,}3\) MPa and not zero. The bottom, which is farther from the centroid, has a \(1{,}95\) times larger stress than the top, because the stress is proportional to the distance from the neutral axis and the ratio of the distances is \(52{,}86 : 27{,}14 = 1{,}95\).

5.5.3 Bending moment and curvature

From history

Galileo and Mariotte investigated the strength of beams, not their deflection (TIMO, PDF p. 34). Toward the end of the seventeenth century the calculus with infinitely small quantities was developing fast. Jacob Bernoulli (1654 to 1705) and his brother sought applications for it in examples from mechanics and physics (TIMO, PDF p. 33). One of these examples was the shape into which an elastic bar bends (TIMO, PDF p. 33-34).

Jacob Bernoulli followed Mariotte in the position of the axis of rotation of the cross section and took a cantilever of rectangular cross section with depth \(h\) and width \(b\), loaded by \(P\) at the free end (TIMO, PDF p. 34). The cross section turns about the axis \(A\), and the elongation of the fibres between two neighbouring cross sections is proportional to the distance from \(A\). By Hooke's law the resultant of the forces in the fibres of the cross section equals \(\tfrac12 m\,\Delta\,\mathrm{d}s\,b h/\mathrm{d}s\), where \(\mathrm{d}s\) is the length of a small piece of the bar, \(\Delta\,\mathrm{d}s\) the elongation of the farthest fibre and \(m\) a constant depending on the material (TIMO, PDF p. 34). Its moment about \(A\) must equal the moment \(P x\) of the load about the same axis (TIMO, PDF p. 34). Since the ratio of the fibre elongation to the length \(\mathrm{d}s\) equals \(h\) divided by the radius of curvature \(r\) of the bar, he obtained an equation of the form \(P x = C/r\), where \(C = m b h^3/3\) is a constant determined by the material and the cross section (TIMO, PDF p. 35). Because of the wrong position of the axis of rotation, its expression was wrong. The general form is correct, however: the curvature of the bent bar at each point is proportional to the bending moment at that point (TIMO, PDF p. 35).

Euler accepted this statement of Bernoulli without discussion (TIMO, PDF p. 38-39). He called the constant \(C\) the absolute elasticity. He did not explain its physical meaning, only wrote that it depends on the properties of the material and that for rectangular bars it is proportional to the width and to the square of the depth (TIMO, PDF p. 40). That square was a mistake, since \(C\) is proportional to the third power of the depth, as Bernoulli's expression shows (TIMO, PDF p. 40-41). Navier reached the same relation differently in his book of 1826. From plane cross sections and statics he derived that the curvature equals the moment divided by the product of the modulus \(E\) (the ratio of the load per unit of cross-sectional area to the elongation per unit of length) and the second moment of area \(I\) of the cross section about the line without elongation (TIMO, PDF p. 82). For small deflections he wrote it as \(E I\,\mathrm{d}^2 y/\mathrm{d}x^2 = M\) (TIMO, PDF p. 83). What Bernoulli and Euler wrote with the constant \(C\) is the product \(E I\) in Navier, and in him each part of the constant got its meaning: \(E\) describes the material, \(I\) the cross section.

Let us return to the question of the section: how the moment and the stress in a beam are connected with bending, that is, with the curvature of its axis. Navier's equation is the answer that we shall now derive from our assumptions.

Lemma 5.33 computes \(N\) and \(M\) from known \(u'\) and \(v''\). Let us turn the question round: for known \(N\) and \(M\) from equilibrium, what is the stress? We solve both equations (5.23) for \(u'\) and \(v''\) and insert into (5.21). Let us state the result as a theorem.

Theorem 5.35 Stress and deformation in bending (napetost in deformacija pri upogibu)

Let a beam have its axis through the centroids of the cross sections, let the displacements satisfy theorem 5.15, let the fibres be in uniaxial stress (assumption 6) and let the material be linearly elastic, homogeneous and isotropic with modulus of elasticity \(E\). Let \(A\) be the area and \(I\) the second moment of area of the cross section about the axis \(z\) through the centroid, and \(N\) and \(M\) the internal force and moment of definition 2.5, lemma 5.9 and corollary 5.19. Then

\[N = EA\,u' , \qquad M = EI\,v'' , \tag{5.24}\]

and the stress in the cross section at \(x\) at height \(y\) above the axis is

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \frac{N}{A} - \frac{M\,y}{I} . \tag{5.25}\]
Proof

The relations (5.24) are proved by lemma 5.33, which sums the forces and moments of the strips of the cross section, then we invert them and insert into the stress.

  1. By lemma 5.33, which adds up the forces and moments of the strips of the cross section, for an axis through the centroid \(N = EA\,u'\) and \(M = EI\,v''\). These are the equations (5.24).
  2. The area \(A\) is positive. The second moment of area \(I = \int_{\mathrm{A}} y^2\,\mathrm{d}A\) is the integral of a function that is nonnegative and zero only on the line \(y = 0\) of area zero, so \(I > 0\): all the blue bars of the contributions point the same way and none is subtracted. Also \(E > 0\). Hence we may divide and get \(u' = N/(EA)\) and \(v'' = M/(EI)\).
  3. We insert into the stress (5.21): \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\,\big(N/(EA) - y\,M/(EI)\big) = N/A - M\,y/I\).

Source: EP (2.5.12), (2.5.15), (2.5.16) (pp. 74-75, in EP \(M_{\text{EP}} = \int z\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}A = -M\) and \(z\) is our \(y\), so \(M\) in (2.5.12) and (2.5.16) has the opposite sign to that in (5.24) and (5.25)), SADD (D.6) to (D.8) (PDF pp. 577-578, \(M_{\text{SADD}} = M\), \(\sigma = -My/I\) and \(d^2v/dx^2 = M/(EI)\) for \(v\) upward), ONATE2 (1.5) (PDF p. 34, \(M = EI\,d^2w/dx^2\), the same sign as ours), CM (7.3.30), (7.3.31) (PDF p. 310, \(M_{\text{CM}} = -M\)). The translation of signs between the sources was checked with SymPy from our definitions.

The theorem says two things. First, \(M = EI\,v''\) means that the moment is proportional to the curvature \(\kappa = v''\) (definition 5.16), with the property of the cross section and the material \(EI\) as the coefficient. Second, the stress is the sum of a uniform part \(N/A\), the same at all points of the cross section, and a part \(-M\,y/I\), which is linear in \(y\) and proportional to \(M\). Where does \(I\) come from? The stress in the strip of the cross section at height \(y\) is \(E\,(u' - y\,v'')\) by (5.21). Its constant part \(E\,u'\) adds nothing to the moment. For a rectangle we see this on pairs of strips: the strip at height \(y\) and the strip at height \(-y\) carry the same force with opposite lever arms. For every cross section the sum of the lever arms weighted by area is \(\int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\mathrm{d}A = 0\), because the axis passes through the centroid. The other part of the stress, \(-E\,y\,v''\), is proportional to \(y\), so the force the strip carries because of it is proportional to \(y\) too. This force has the lever arm \(y\) to the axis, so the strip contributes to the moment with \(y^2\) (the blue bars in figure 5.70). The sum of the contributions of all strips is \(E\,v''\,I\).

Figure 5.85 draws the two parts for the cross section of our bar, \(20 \times 40\) mm with \(A = 800\) mm² and \(I = 1{,}0667\cdot 10^{5}\) mm⁴, in which let \(N = 20\) kN and \(M = -500\) N m. The uniform part is \(20\,000\ \text{N}/800\ \text{mm}^2 = 25\) MPa. The linear part is the same as at the clamp of the cantilever below, \(+93{,}75\) MPa at the top and \(-93{,}75\) MPa at the bottom. The sum is \(25 + 93{,}75 = 118{,}75\) MPa at the top and \(25 - 93{,}75 = -68{,}75\) MPa at the bottom. The straight line stays a straight line, it only shifts by \(25\) MPa toward tension. So the stress is no longer zero on the axis but where the linear part cancels the uniform one: from \(N/A - M\,y/I = 0\) we get \(y = N I/(M A) = -5{,}33\) mm, below the axis. For \(N = 0\) the zero is on the axis.

What does \(M = EI\,v''\) mean on a piece of the beam? Let us draw it (figure 5.86).

Let us cut a straight piece of length \(\Delta x\) out of the beam. We draw it grey dashed, as it is before loading. On its right face the part of the beam to the right of it acts with the moment \(M\). This is the moment of the stresses on the face with outward normal \(+\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) (lemma 5.9), for \(M > 0\) turned counterclockwise. On the left face the moment is equally large and opposite, so clockwise, otherwise the piece would begin to turn. The moments turn the two faces toward each other at the top and apart at the bottom. We draw the piece after loading in orange. The fibre at the bottom is now longer than \(\Delta x\), the fibre at the top shorter, and the axis has kept the length \(\Delta x\) (section 5.5.2). The extended faces meet at the centre of curvature \(O\) above the piece, and along the radius the distance from \(O\) to the axis is \(1/v''\). This is \(v'' > 0\). If we reverse the moment, the curvature reverses too: \(O\) is then below the piece and the upper fibres are the longer ones.

Definition 5.36 Axial and bending stiffness (osna in upogibna togost)

The product \(EA\) of the modulus of elasticity and the area of the cross section is the axial stiffness of the beam, and the product \(EI\) of the modulus of elasticity and the second moment of area of the cross section about the axis \(z\) through the centroid is the bending stiffness. Their units are N and N m².

Source: EP (2.5.9), (2.5.10) (p. 74, there \(A_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and \(D_{\mathrm{xx}}\)), CM (PDF p. 310). The names axial and bending stiffness were promised by section 3.7 of chapter 3.

Why do we call \(EI\) a stiffness? A stiff thing is one that is hard to bend. From \(M = EI\,v''\) we have \(EI = M/v''\): this is the moment that we need for a unit of curvature. The larger \(EI\) is, the less the piece curves under the same moment. Take the beam of section 5.1.3, where \(E = 196\) GPa and \(I = bh^3/12 = 1{,}0667\cdot 10^{-7}\) m⁴. We get \(EI = 20\,906{,}7\) N m². Two cases:

\[v'' = 1\ \text{1/m}:\quad M = 20\,907\ \text{N m} , \qquad\qquad v'' = 0{,}0239\ \text{1/m}:\quad M = 500\ \text{N m} .\]

The first is a circular arc of radius \(1\) m. A steel bar cannot be bent into it without yielding: by (5.21) the edge of the cross section would have the stress \(E\,(h/2)\,v'' = 196\ \text{GPa} \cdot 0{,}020\ \text{m} \cdot 1\ \text{1/m} = 3920\) MPa, almost eighteen times the yield strength \(220\) MPa. The second is, in magnitude, the curvature at the clamp of our cantilever, which we compute below, an arc of radius \(41{,}8\) m at the moment \(500\) N m. Over the length \(1\) m it departs from the tangent by only \(12\) mm. The axial stiffness \(EA = 1{,}568\cdot 10^{8}\) N is the force that would stretch the bar by \(100\,\%\) (\(\varepsilon = 1\)), if the material could bear it. For \(\varepsilon = 10^{-3}\) the force is \(156{,}8\) kN.

Why \(I\) is decisive is shown by \(h^3\) in \(I = bh^3/12\) (lemma 5.27). Let us double the height: we draw the piece of height \(2h\) on the right, under the same moment \(M\). There are twice as many strips of the cross section and each is twice as far from the axis, so it counts four times as much (the contribution \(y^2\)). The stiffness \(EI\) grows \(2 \cdot 4 = 8\) times and the curvature is eight times smaller: the right piece is almost straight in the figure. For our bar the radius at the clamp would grow from \(41{,}8\) m to \(334{,}5\) m. The axial stiffness \(EA\), like the use of material, grows only twofold.

Let us test the theorem on both beams of section 5.1.3, first on the cantilever (figure 5.88).

The cantilever is clamped at \(x = 0\), the axis \(x\) runs along it and \(y\) upward. The force \(F = 500\) N acts downward at the end \(x = L = 1\) m. By section 5.2.2 we have \(N = 0\) and \(M(x) = -F\,(L - x)\), so in the cut at the clamp \(M(0) = -500\) N m. The curvature at the clamp is \(v''(0) = M(0)/(EI) = -500/20\,906{,}7 = -0{,}02392\) 1/m. The minus sign says that the centre of curvature \(O\) is below the beam: the cantilever bends downward, which we draw in orange and strongly magnified. The radius of curvature is \(1/0{,}02392 = 41{,}8\) m, almost \(42\) lengths of the beam, so the figure cannot show it to scale. The stress at the top of the cross section (\(y = h/2 = 20\) mm) by (5.25) is

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -\frac{M\,y}{I} = \frac{500\ \text{N m} \cdot 0{,}020\ \text{m}}{1{,}0667\cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^4} = 93{,}75\ \text{MPa} ,\]

tension, and at the bottom it is \(-93{,}75\) MPa, compression. The guide lines carry the heights \(y = \pm 20\) mm of the two edges of the cross section over to the diagram: the stress is a straight line with its zero on the axis, with \(+93{,}75\) MPa at the top and \(-93{,}75\) MPa at the bottom. This is \(43\,\%\) of the yield strength \(220\) MPa, so we are in the region where linear elasticity holds, on which the theorem stands. The edge of the cross section would reach the yield strength at the force \(500 \cdot 220/93{,}75 = 1173\) N. Above it the material at the edge yields, the stress there no longer grows in proportion to \(y\) and (5.25) no longer holds. The tension is at the top because at the clamp \(v'' < 0\) and the upper fibres stretch.

For the simply supported beam with the uniform load \(q_0 = 1\) kN/m the largest moment at mid-span is \(M = +500\) N m, equal in magnitude, but the stress has the opposite sign: on the upper side it is \(-93{,}75\) MPa, compression, and on the lower \(+93{,}75\) MPa, tension. The sign turns because at \(M > 0\) also \(v'' > 0\), and then the lower fibres are the longer ones. The same number is no coincidence. For all three loadings of the two beams we chose the load so that the largest moment is the same in magnitude (section 5.2.2), and by the theorem the stress depends only on the moment and the cross section.

Why is the cross section in the shape of the letter I so popular? Let us compare it with a rectangle of the same area under the same moment (figure 5.89).

Take the I-section of section 5.4.3: height \(200\) mm, flanges \(100 \times 10\) mm, web \(180 \times 6\) mm, \(A = 3080\) mm², \(I = 2{,}098\cdot 10^{7}\) mm⁴. The moment \(500\) N m gives in it by (5.25) a linear stress, which is \(500 \cdot 0{,}100/(2{,}098\cdot 10^{-5}) = 2{,}38\) MPa at the outer edge and \(2{,}14\) MPa at the junction of web and flange. A rectangle of the same area, \(20 \times 154\) mm, would have \(I = 6{,}087\cdot 10^{6}\) mm⁴, so \(3{,}45\) times less, and a stress of \(6{,}32\) MPa at the edge. Material far from the axis carries the most moment, because \(y^2\) grows with distance, so the I-section is \(3{,}45\) times stiffer for the same area. The stress at the edge is \(2{,}65\) times smaller and not \(3{,}45\) times, because the edge of the I-section is farther from the axis (\(100\) mm against \(77\) mm) and by (5.25) the stress is proportional to \(y/I\).

We proved the theorem from the model. Is the model really accurate for bending? Let us check it for pure bending (section 5.3.1), on the middle part of four-point bending, where the moment is constant and there is no shear force. There the argument from symmetry showed that the cross sections stay plane. The exact elastic solution for pure bending of a straight beam with a rectangular cross section (SADD, Example 8.2, PDF pp. 171-172, plane stress) gives a stress that equals the elementary one, and a displacement of the axis that is also equal (SADD (8.1.22), (8.1.23)). In pure bending the elementary stress (5.25) is therefore exact when the end moments act as a linearly distributed stress, and otherwise away from the ends (SADD, PDF p. 171). The difference between the model and the elastic solution appears only under shear loading, which the next section treats.

5.5.4 Temperature and bending

For the uniaxial state we have already seen that a freely heated piece stretches without stress (theorem 3.23). A beam, however, can be heated unevenly: for example, if the sun heats the upper flange of a bridge beam and the lower one stays in the shade, so that the upper part is warmer and the lower colder. What happens then?

Hooke's law with temperature, in the uniaxial form, for a fibre in which \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) (assumption 6), is \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/E + \alpha\,\Delta T\), where \(\alpha\) is the coefficient of thermal expansion (first row of equation (3.31)). EP (2.4.19), p. 70, writes the same relation in general form with the stiffness matrix. Let us denote the change of temperature by \(\theta(x, y)\) and assume that it is linear over the height, \(\theta = \theta_{\mathrm{m}}(x) + g(x)\,y\), where \(\theta_{\mathrm{m}}\) is the change of temperature on the axis and \(g\) the temperature gradient over the height, in K/m, the slope of the line \(\theta(y)\). A linear one is the simplest distribution with a gradient. What changes when it is not linear we show at the end of the section. How the temperature is really distributed over a body is treated by chapter 14.

Let us draw what the temperature does in one cross section. The vertical axis is the height \(y\) above the axis of the beam, on the horizontal axis is the strain of a fibre. A fibre that nothing holds would stretch by \(\alpha\,\theta\) because of the temperature. Since \(\theta\) is linear over the height, these values lie on the orange line. The cross section, however, stays plane, so the actual strain of the fibres \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = u' - y\,v''\) is a line too, the blue one. The stress of a fibre is \(E\) times the horizontal difference between the two lines, since stress arises only from the part of the stretch that the temperature does not give by itself. The difference of two lines is again a line, so the stress is linear over the height, as without temperature. We now write this precisely.

Theorem 5.37 Bending caused by temperature (upogib zaradi temperature)

Let the assumptions of theorem 5.35 hold for the beam, let \(E\) and \(\alpha\) not depend on temperature and let the change of temperature be \(\theta(x, y) = \theta_{\mathrm{m}}(x) + g(x)\,y\). Then

\[N = EA\,(u' - \alpha\,\theta_{\mathrm{m}}) , \qquad M = EI\,(v'' + \alpha\,g) , \tag{5.26}\]

and the stress is still \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = N/A - M\,y/I\). If \(N = M = 0\), then \(v'' = -\alpha\,g\), \(u' = \alpha\,\theta_{\mathrm{m}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0\).

Proof

The procedure is as for lemma 5.33, except that the thermal part is subtracted in the stress.

  1. In the uniaxial state, by Hooke's law with temperature, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/E + \alpha\,\theta\) (theorem 3.23, first row of equation (3.31), for \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\)), so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} - \alpha\,\theta)\). The part \(\alpha\,\theta\) is the stretch of a free piece, which needs no stress, and only the difference from it gives stress.
  2. With \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = u' - y\,v''\) (theorem 5.15) and \(\theta = \theta_{\mathrm{m}} + g\,y\) we have \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\,\big(u' - \alpha\,\theta_{\mathrm{m}} - y\,(v'' + \alpha\,g)\big)\). The first part is the difference on the axis, the second is proportional to \(y\).
  3. By lemma 5.9 and corollary 5.19, \(N = \int_{\mathrm{A}} \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}A\). From step 2, \(N = E\,(u' - \alpha\,\theta_{\mathrm{m}})\,A - E\,(v'' + \alpha\,g) \int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\mathrm{d}A\), and since the axis passes through the centroid, the integral is zero (lemma 5.22): a strip above the axis and the mirrored strip below it contribute equal and opposite amounts. This is the first equation (5.26).
  4. By the same lemma, \(M = -\int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}A = -E\,(u' - \alpha\,\theta_{\mathrm{m}}) \int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\mathrm{d}A + E\,(v'' + \alpha\,g) \int_{\mathrm{A}} y^2\,\mathrm{d}A = EI\,(v'' + \alpha\,g)\). The first integral is zero as in step 3, the second is \(I\), the sum of the contributions \(y^2\) of the strips. This is the second equation.
  5. From step 3, \(u' - \alpha\,\theta_{\mathrm{m}} = N/(EA)\), from step 4, \(v'' + \alpha\,g = M/(EI)\). We insert into step 2: \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = N/A - M\,y/I\). For \(N = M = 0\) we have \(u' = \alpha\,\theta_{\mathrm{m}}\) and \(v'' = -\alpha\,g\), so \(u' - y\,v'' = \alpha\,\theta\) and the two lines coincide.

Source: The relation of stress and strain: EP (2.4.19) (p. 70). The result for the beam is not from the sources, it is derived from theorem 3.23, checked with SymPy.

The theorem shows that the stress is still expressed by \(N\) and \(M\) as before. The temperature only shifts the zeros of the axial and bending deformation: the beam is free of stress when \(v'' = -\alpha\,g\), not when \(v'' = 0\). The curvature \(-\alpha g\) we call the free curvature, the curvature that the beam takes when nothing restrains it.

Let us test this on our cantilever (figure 5.91).

Let the upper edge be \(20\) K warmer than the lower: the upper edge \(+10\) K, the lower \(-10\) K, so \(\theta = g\,y\) with \(g = 20/0{,}040 = 500\) K/m and \(\theta_{\mathrm{m}} = 0\) (on the axis the temperature does not change). For steel we take \(\alpha = 15\cdot 10^{-6}\) 1/K (section 3.3). Let us first look at a short free piece, which we draw grey dashed before heating. The upper fibre has the free strain \(\alpha \cdot 10\ \text{K} = 1{,}5\cdot 10^{-4}\), the lower \(-1{,}5\cdot 10^{-4}\): the upper edge lengthens and the lower shortens, and the piece gets the outline of a trapezium, drawn in orange. Such trapezia, laid one beside the other, make an arc. This is the free curvature \(v'' = -\alpha g = -15\cdot 10^{-6} \cdot 500 = -7{,}5\cdot 10^{-3}\) 1/m.

The cantilever is clamped at \(x = 0\) (\(v(0) = v'(0) = 0\)) and has no load. Equilibrium (section 5.2) gives \(N = M = 0\) along the whole length. Theorem 5.37 therefore gives \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0\) everywhere, and the beam bends out of its straight position with \(v'' = -\alpha g\), so \(v'(x) = -\alpha g\,x\) and \(v(x) = -\alpha g\,x^2/2\). The free end at \(x = L = 1\) m falls by \(\alpha g L^2/2 = 3{,}75\) mm. The upper fibres are longer, so the cantilever bends downward. There is no stress, because nothing restrains the bending.

Now let us clamp the same beam at both ends (\(v = v' = 0\) at \(x = 0\) and \(x = L\), and the axis cannot stretch). Without load over the span \(V' = -q = 0\) and \(M' = -V\), so \(M\) is a linear function of \(x\), and by theorem 5.37 \(v'' = M/(EI) - \alpha g\) is linear. Integrating a linear function twice with \(v(0) = v'(0) = 0\) gives \(v = a\,x^2 + b\,x^3\). The conditions at the right end are \(v(L) = a L^2 + b L^3 = 0\) and \(v'(L) = 2 a L + 3 b L^2 = 0\). The first gives \(a = -b L\), the second is then \(b L^2 = 0\), so \(b = 0\) and \(a = 0\): \(v = 0\) everywhere, the beam stays straight and \(v'' = 0\). Without axial load \(N\) is constant along the beam (section 5.2.3), so for \(\theta_{\mathrm{m}} = 0\) the derivative \(u' = N/(EA)\) is constant too, and since \(u(0) = u(L) = 0\), we have \(u' = 0\). By theorem 5.37 we then have \(N = 0\) and \(M = EI\,\alpha g = 20\,906{,}7 \cdot 7{,}5\cdot 10^{-3} = 156{,}8\) N m. With this moment the clamps must act on the ends of the beam, without any external load: the beam would like to bend as the cantilever above, and the clamps bend it back into the straight position. The stress is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -M\,y/I = -E\,\alpha\,g\,y\), at the top \(-E\alpha\,\Delta T/2 = -29{,}4\) MPa (compression, the warm fibre would like to stretch and cannot) and at the bottom \(+29{,}4\) MPa, which is \(13\,\%\) of the yield strength. For comparison: if the beam clamped at both ends were heated evenly by \(20\) K (\(\theta_{\mathrm{m}} = 20\) K, \(g = 0\)), the stress over the whole height would be \(-E\alpha\theta_{\mathrm{m}} = -58{,}8\) MPa, twice as much as for a temperature difference of \(20\) K over the height, where \(\theta\) at the edges is only \(\pm 10\) K.

The theorem assumed two things. First, \(E\) and \(\alpha\) do not depend on temperature. This is an assumption of theorem 3.23 and it holds for moderate changes of temperature, such as our \(20\) K. For very large changes we would have to take \(E\) and \(\alpha\) as functions of temperature, and then they could not stand in front of the integrals in steps 3 and 4 of the proof. What does the assumption do to the result? The moment of the clamps \(M = EI\,\alpha g\) and the stress \(-E\,\alpha\,g\,y\) are proportional to the product \(E\alpha\). If this product changed by \(1\,\%\) over our \(20\) K, \(156{,}8\) N m would change by \(1{,}6\) N m and \(29{,}4\) MPa by \(0{,}3\) MPa. The conclusion that a free beam has no stress, however, needs \(\alpha\) independent of temperature. Otherwise the free strain is the integral of \(\alpha\) over the temperature (COOK §2.10, PDF p. 66), which for a linear \(\theta\) is no longer linear in \(y\), and even a free beam gets a stress like the one the example below shows. Second, the change of temperature is linear over the height. This assumption decides the conclusion that a free beam has no stress. Let us see what happens without it (figure 5.92).

Let both edges be \(10\) K warmer than the axis, \(\theta = 10\ \text{K}\cdot(2y/h)^2\): the curve of the temperature is a parabola with its zero on the axis and with \(10\) K at the edges. The cross sections stay plane, so the strain \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = u' - y\,v''\) is still linear in \(y\), while the free thermal strain \(\alpha\theta\) is not. A linear function cannot fit a parabola everywhere, and the stress must cover the difference: by step 1 of the proof \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\,(u' - y\,v'' - \alpha\,\theta)\). The beam is free, so \(N = 0\) and \(M = 0\). We insert this stress into \(N\) and \(M\) as in steps 3 and 4 of the proof:

\[N = EA\,(u' - \alpha\,\bar\theta) , \qquad M = EI\,v'' + E\,\alpha \int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\theta\,\mathrm{d}A ,\]

where \(\bar\theta = \frac{1}{A}\int_{\mathrm{A}} \theta\,\mathrm{d}A\) is the mean of the temperature over the cross section, here \(10/3 = 3{,}33\) K. The last integral is zero: the parabola has the same temperature at the height \(y\) and at the height \(-y\), but the product \(y\,\theta\) has opposite signs, so the contributions of the two strips cancel. The condition \(M = 0\) therefore gives \(v'' = 0\), and the condition \(N = 0\) gives \(u' = \alpha\,\bar\theta\). So the beam stretches as a whole by the mean temperature, stays straight, and in it there is the stress

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -E\,\alpha\,(\theta - \bar\theta) : \qquad -19{,}6\ \text{MPa at the edges} , \qquad +9{,}8\ \text{MPa on the axis} .\]

The edges are warmer than the mean, they would like to stretch more than the cross section does and are therefore in compression, the middle is cooler than the mean and is in tension. The stress is \(9\,\%\) of the yield strength, although nothing holds the beam. This stress gives neither a force nor a moment, since \(N\) and \(M\) are zero. So the equations (5.26) hold here too if we take the mean \(\bar\theta\) for \(\theta_{\mathrm{m}}\), here \(3{,}33\) K, and the number \(\frac1I\int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\theta\,\mathrm{d}A\) for \(g\), here \(0\). For a linear \(\theta\) these two numbers are exactly the change on the axis and the slope, because \(\int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\mathrm{d}A = 0\). At the free ends the stress must be zero, so this distribution does not hold right at the ends. Since it gives neither a force nor a moment on the end face, by Saint-Venant's principle (figure 2.16) this changes the state only near the ends (SADD §5.6, PDF p. 114). The conclusion of the theorem that a free beam has no stress, however, holds only when \(\theta\) is linear in \(y\).

What have we learned in this section? The stress in a beam without a change of temperature, or with one that is linear over the height, is a linear function of the height above the centroidal axis and has the form (5.25), because the cross sections stay plane and every fibre follows Hooke's law. Without a change of temperature the moment is proportional to the curvature, and the constant of proportionality is the bending stiffness \(EI\): a piece twice as high bends eight times less under the same moment. On the steel bar \(20 \times 40\) mm at \(M = 500\) N m we got \(93{,}75\) MPa, \(43\,\%\) of the yield strength. With an I-section of the same area as a \(20 \times 154\) mm rectangle we would have \(2{,}38\) MPa instead of \(6{,}32\) MPa. An unevenly heated beam bends freely without stress, and stress arises only when the supports prevent the bending or when the temperature is not linear over the height.
