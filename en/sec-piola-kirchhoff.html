Chapter 2 Stress and equilibrium

2.13 Stress referred to the original shape

When we defined the stress in a bar in section 2.2, we divided the axial force by the area of the cross section before loading. We also wrote that for steel it does not matter which area we take, but for rubber it does. The traction vector of section 2.5, however, is the force per unit area of a cut in the body as it is under load. The area we divide by there is therefore the area after loading. For steel the two areas differ by less than a tenth of a per cent, so we have not noticed the difference so far. For the rubber band that we stretch from 10 cm to 15 cm (section 1.1), however, they differ by a third. Then we must say which area the force is referred to.

From history

The equations of equilibrium at every interior point of a body were derived by Gabrio Piola in 1833 in his memoir La Meccanica de' corpi naturalmente estesi (TP1, PDF p. 434 and 435). He wanted to direct the attention of mathematicians to the principles of Lagrange's Mécanique analytique and, following Lagrange's example, he treated bodies that return to their original shape after unloading (TP1, PDF p. 435). In 1852 Kirchhoff published in the proceedings of the Vienna Academy of Sciences a memoir on the equilibrium of such a body under displacements that are not infinitely small, relying on the way indicated by Saint-Venant (TP2B, PDF p. 60). As variables he took the coordinates of the point after the displacement, not the displacements themselves, and wrote the equations of equilibrium in the usual form (TP2B, PDF p. 60). The stresses in them, however, did not have the usual meaning, for they meant forces across faces that were parallel to the coordinate planes before loading and no longer are afterwards (TP2B, PDF p. 60). So in his words these nine stresses are no longer equal in pairs (TP2B, PDF p. 60).

Let us return to the stress per area before loading: its nine components are no longer equal in pairs. We proved theorem 2.36 on the symmetry of stress by the equilibrium of a small cube with faces in the coordinate planes. Faces that were parallel to these planes before loading are no longer perpendicular to each other afterwards, and such a proof does not hold for them. The stress on faces from the initial position we shall meet in definition 2.82.

The goal of this section is to write the stress also per unit area before loading, at every point of a body and on every face, not only in a bar. The route has four steps. First we compute how the area and the normal of a small piece of a face change in a deformation. Then we divide the force that acts across such a piece after loading by its area before loading. We get two new measures of stress, which books name after Piola and Kirchhoff. Next we split the deformation gradient into a stretch and a rotation and use the split to define one more measure, named after Biot. Finally we compare all the measures on the rubber band and on the steel bar and say when the difference between them matters.

We compute in the following model. We look at a point of a body that is in equilibrium after loading. At this point we know the deformation gradient \(\mat F = \mat I + \mat H\) (definition 1.110), that is, the matrix that by lemma 1.111 on a short fibre after loading maps the vector of a short fibre before loading to its vector after loading, up to a remainder that is small compared with the length of the fibre. We also know the stress components \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ab}}\) at this point, computed in the body after loading. They form the stress matrix \(\ten\sigma\) (definition 2.41), whose row \(a\) is the traction vector on the face with the normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{a}}\). By theorem 2.36 on the symmetry of stress, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ab}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{ba}}\), so the matrix \(\ten\sigma\) is symmetric. We assume three more things.

  1. The determinant \(J = \det\mat F\) is positive, as CM also assumes (PDF p. 126). By lemma 2.55 on the change of volume, \(J\) is the ratio of the volume of a small piece after loading to its volume before. With \(J = 0\) the piece would be squashed into a surface without volume, which matter cannot do. With \(J < 0\) it would turn into its mirror image, and by corollary 2.58 on a positive Jacobian it would have to be flattened on the way during gradual loading (section 2.10.5). We will need a nonzero determinant for the inverse matrix \(\mat F^{-1}\), and a positive one for the outward normal to stay outward and for the split of the deformation gradient into a stretch and a rotation in section 2.13.4.
  2. The pieces of faces we look at are small. So we may describe a face after loading with the deformation gradient at the point, just like fibres. For a linear displacement field, as for the band and for the sheared cube below, this description is exact.
  3. The coordinate system is the same before and after loading, as everywhere in the book (CM §3.2.1, PDF p. 112). It is right-handed (definition 2.29), because we will use the vector product.

We do not assume, however, that the strains are small. Everything that follows holds also for a band stretched by half its length.

2.13.1 Two areas

Let us start with an example. The rubber band of section 1.1 is \(L_0 = 10\) cm long before loading. Its cross section is a rectangle 2 mm wide and 1 mm thick, with the area \(A_0 = 2\ \text{mm}^2\). The index 0 means that a quantity refers to the shape before loading, as with the length \(L_0\) in section 1.6.2. We lay the axis \(x\) along the band, with the origin at its centre, the axis \(y\) along the width and the axis \(z\) along the thickness. We pull the band at both ends along the axis \(x\) until it is 15 cm long. The stretch (razmerje dolžin) of section 1.1, the new length divided by the old one, is then

\[\lambda = \frac{15\ \text{cm}}{10\ \text{cm}} = 1{,}5 .\]

Let the force with which we pull be 10 N at this length. We chose this number for the example. The real force depends on the kind of rubber, but all the stresses we will compute are proportional to it. The band has the volume \(100\ \text{mm} \cdot 2\ \text{mm}^2 = 200\ \text{mm}^3\) and rubber has a density of at most \(0{,}91\) Mg/m³ (BOWER, Table 3.1, PDF p. 100), so the band weighs less than \(0{,}002\) N. We therefore neglect its weight. As for the bar in section 2.1, the axial force in every cross section equals the force at the end, \(N = 10\) N.

How does the cross section change? We assume three things. First, rubber strongly resists a change of volume (BOWER §3.5, PDF p. 121). The ratio of the relative decrease of thickness to the relative increase of length is \(0{,}49\) for rubber (BOWER, Table 3.1, PDF p. 100), very close to the value \(0{,}5\) for a material that keeps its volume exactly (section 1.6). In the model we therefore assume that the band keeps its volume exactly. This is an idealization, real rubber changes its volume a little. Second, rubber responds in the same way in all directions across the band, so the width and the thickness decrease by the same factor \(k\). Third, the band is stretched equally everywhere. Close to the ends, where we hold it, this is not true, but far from them it almost is, by Saint-Venant's principle (section 2.2). We get the factor \(k\) from the volume, which is the area of the cross section times the length.

  1. The length increases by the factor \(\lambda\), the width and the thickness decrease by the factor \(k\), so the area of the cross section decreases by the factor \(k^2\) and the volume changes by the factor \(\lambda k^2\).
  2. The volume stays the same, so \(\lambda k^2 = 1\) and
    \[k = \frac{1}{\sqrt\lambda} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1{,}5}} = 0{,}8165 .\]
  3. After loading the band is \(2 \cdot 0{,}8165 = 1{,}633\) mm wide and \(0{,}8165\) mm thick, and the area of its cross section is
    \[A = k^2 A_0 = \frac{A_0}{\lambda} = \frac{2\ \text{mm}^2}{1{,}5} = 1{,}333\ \text{mm}^2 .\]

The thickness of the band has thus decreased by \(1 - 0{,}8165 = 0{,}1835\) of its value, and the length has increased by \(0{,}5\) of its value. The ratio is \(0{,}1835/0{,}5 = 0{,}367\), not \(0{,}5\). The number \(0{,}5\) of section 1.6 holds only for small stretches. At \(\lambda = 1{,}001\) we would have \(k = 1/\sqrt{1{,}001} = 0{,}9995\), the thickness would decrease by \(0{,}0005\) of its value, and the ratio would be \(0{,}0005/0{,}001 = 0{,}5\). For a larger stretch the ratio is smaller, because \(k = 1/\sqrt\lambda\) decreases more and more slowly as \(\lambda\) grows. The number \(0{,}49\) for rubber also holds only for small stretches, since BOWER gives it for a model in which the strain is proportional to the stress and which works only for small shape changes (BOWER §3.2.2, PDF p. 97). So for the band we started from the volume, not from the ratio \(0{,}49\).

In section 2.2 we wrote the area \(A = A_0/\lambda\) as \(A/1{,}5 = 0{,}667\,A\), where \(A\) was the area before loading. Now we divide the axial force by both areas. A megapascal is a newton per square millimetre (section 2.2), so

\[\frac{N}{A_0} = \frac{10\ \text{N}}{2\ \text{mm}^2} = 5\ \text{MPa} , \qquad \frac{N}{A} = \frac{10\ \text{N}}{1{,}333\ \text{mm}^2} = 7{,}5\ \text{MPa} .\]

The second value is exactly \(1{,}5\) times the first, because the area after loading is \(\lambda = 1{,}5\) times smaller. Both numbers are a force per unit area, only per a different area (figure 2.87). Following BOWER, in section 2.2 we called the first the nominal stress and the second the true stress.

Which of the two numbers does the traction vector describe? Definition 2.20 divides the force by the area of a patch in the body as it is under load, that is, by the area after loading. The traction vector on a cross section of the band is therefore \(7{,}5\) MPa in the direction of the axis \(x\), and the component \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) is \(7{,}5\) MPa. On the faces along the band there are no forces, as for the bar in section 2.6.6, so all the other stress components are zero. The stress described by the traction vector and the components \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ab}}\) is called the Cauchy stress (Cauchyjeva napetost). CM stresses that it is measured per unit area of the body after loading (CM §4.4.1, PDF p. 193), and BOWER that it is the true stress of introductory strength of materials courses (BOWER, PDF p. 71).

For large deformations, then, every piece of a body has two shapes that we must keep apart: the shape before loading and the shape after it. CM calls the shape of the body to which the description of the deformation is referred the reference configuration (referenčna konfiguracija). Usually this is the shape before loading, but it can be any other known shape (CM §3.2.2, PDF p. 112). The shape after loading CM calls the current or deformed configuration (trenutna konfiguracija) (CM §3.2.1, PDF p. 111). We stay with the words before loading and after loading. A patch that has the area \(\Delta A_0\) and the unit normal \(\vek n_0\) before loading has the area \(\Delta A\) and the unit normal \(\vek n\) after loading. Quantities without an index refer to the shape after loading, like the area \(\Delta A\) and the normal \(\vek n\) in definition 2.20, where the patch was a piece of a cut in the loaded body.

Let us also write the deformation gradient of the band. A point at the distance \(x\) from the centre along the band moves to the distance \(1{,}5\,x\), and a point at the distance \(y\) from the axis of the band in the direction of the width moves to the distance \(0{,}8165\,y\). The same holds in the direction of the thickness. The displacement field of the band is therefore

\[u_{\mathrm{x}} = 0{,}5\,x , \qquad u_{\mathrm{y}} = -0{,}1835\,y , \qquad u_{\mathrm{z}} = -0{,}1835\,z ,\]

where \(-0{,}1835 = 0{,}8165 - 1\). The only nonzero partial derivatives are \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial x = 0{,}5\), \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial y = -0{,}1835\) and \(\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}/\partial z = -0{,}1835\), so

\[\mat F = \mat I + \mat H = \begin{bmatrix} 1{,}5 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0{,}8165 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0{,}8165 \end{bmatrix} .\]

The determinant of a diagonal matrix is the product of its diagonal elements, because in the six-term formula (2.51) only the term with the diagonal remains. So \(J = 1{,}5 \cdot 0{,}8165^2 = \lambda \cdot (1/\sqrt\lambda)^2 = 1\): the band keeps its volume, as we assumed. The field is linear, so the difference of the displacements of two points is exactly \(\mat H\) times the difference of their positions, with no remainder, and \(\mat F\) maps the vector of every fibre exactly to its vector after loading.

2.13.2 How a small face changes

For the band we got the area of the cross section after loading from the width and the thickness, and the normal of the cross section stayed \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). How do the area and the normal change in general? A first guess would be that the normal moves like a fibre, so that after loading it points in the direction of the vector \(\mat F\vek n_0\). The sheared cube shows that this is not so.

Let us return to the steel cube of section 1.6.2. Its edge is 10 mm, its bottom face is glued to the table, and we pushed its top face by 10 µm in the direction of the axis \(x\). The axis \(z\) points vertically up. The displacement field is (1.16), \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = 0{,}001\,z\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\). The only nonzero partial derivative is \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial z = 0{,}001\), so \(\mat H\) has a single nonzero element, \(0{,}001\) in row \(x\) and column \(z\), and

\[\mat F = \mat I + \mat H = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0{,}001 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} .\]

Let us look at two faces of the cube (figure 2.88).

  1. The top face has the outward normal \(\vek n_0 = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) before loading. All its points move by the same vector, by 10 µm in the direction of the axis \(x\), so it stays horizontal, and its normal after loading is again \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\). The vector \(\mat F\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), however, is the third column of the matrix \(\mat F\), that is \(0{,}001\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} + \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\). It leans in the direction of the push, like the vertical edge \(AD\), which is a fibre in the direction \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\).
  2. The right face has the outward normal \(\vek n_0 = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) before loading. A fibre in the direction \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) does not change, because the first column of the matrix \(\mat F\) is just \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), so \(\mat F\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). The face, however, tilts, because its point at the height \(z\) moves by \(0{,}001\,z\) in the direction of the axis \(x\). It tilts like the right vertical edge \(BC\), which lies in it. The normal after loading is therefore no longer \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) but leans downwards, perpendicular to the tilted edge.

On the top face, then, the fibre along the normal tilted and the normal did not, and on the right face it was just the other way round. The normal does not move like a fibre. Why? A fibre along the normal does not lie in the face. The face is made of the fibres that lie in it, and the normal after loading is the unit vector perpendicular to these fibres after loading. So we will describe a face by two fibres that lie in it.

As in section 2.5, we take for the patch a small square or, more generally, a parallelogram spanned in the face by the vectors \(\vek a\) and \(\vek b\) of two short fibres that are not parallel (figure 2.89). By item 4 of lemma 2.31 on the properties of the vector product, the vector \(\vek a\times\vek b\) is perpendicular to \(\vek a\) and to \(\vek b\), and its length is the area of the parallelogram (section 2.8.1). So

\[\vek a\times\vek b = \Delta A_0\,\vek n_0 ,\]

where \(\Delta A_0\) is the area of the patch before loading and \(\vek n_0\) its unit normal. We choose the order of the fibres so that \(\vek a\times\vek b\) points to the desired side of the face, for example out of the part of the body we are looking at. After loading the fibres become approximately \(\mat F\vek a\) and \(\mat F\vek b\) by lemma 1.111 on a short fibre after loading, and the patch becomes approximately the parallelogram spanned by these two vectors. Its area \(\Delta A\) and its unit normal \(\vek n\) are therefore determined by

\[(\mat F\vek a)\times(\mat F\vek b) = \Delta A\,\vek n .\]

For a linear displacement field this is exact. For other fields the patch after loading differs from the parallelogram by the remainders of theorem 1.88 on the change of a field over a short step, which are small compared with the side of the patch. This is a simplification. To show that even then the ratio of the areas approaches the ratio of the areas of the parallelograms as the patch shrinks, we would need the area of a curved surface, which we have not defined yet. CM computes in the same way as we do, with small parallelograms (CM (3.3.22)-(3.3.24), PDF p. 127).

We must now express the vector \((\mat F\vek a)\times(\mat F\vek b)\) through the vector \(\vek a\times\vek b\). The idea is this. We add a third short fibre \(\vek c\) that does not lie in the face (figure 2.89). The fibres \(\vek a\), \(\vek b\) and \(\vek c\) span a parallelepiped whose base is our patch. Its volume is the area of the base times the height, and the height is the projection of the fibre \(\vek c\) onto the normal of the patch (lemma 2.47 on the volume of a parallelepiped). If \(\vek c\) points to the side into which \(\vek n_0\) points, the volume before loading is therefore \((\vek a\times\vek b)\cdot\vek c\), after loading it is \(\big((\mat F\vek a)\times(\mat F\vek b)\big)\cdot(\mat F\vek c)\), and by item 1 of lemma 2.55 on the change of volume the second is \(J\) times the first. This holds for every fibre \(\vek c\), and that is enough to determine the vector \((\mat F\vek a)\times(\mat F\vek b)\).

For the computation we need two tools. The first tells us how to move a matrix from one vector of a scalar product to the other. Let \(\mat M\) be a \(3\times 3\) matrix and \(\vek c\) and \(\vek v\) vectors. The indices \(i\) and \(j\) run over \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\). By definition 1.96 the component \(i\) of the vector \(\mat M\vek c\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} M_{\mathrm{ij}}\,c_{\mathrm{j}}\), and by definition 2.8 of the scalar product

\[(\mat M\vek c)\cdot\vek v = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \Big(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} M_{\mathrm{ij}}\,c_{\mathrm{j}}\Big) v_{\mathrm{i}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{j}} \Big(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} M_{\mathrm{ij}}\,v_{\mathrm{i}}\Big) = \vek c\cdot(\mat M^\T\vek v) . \tag{2.78}\]

In the middle step we only added the same nine products \(M_{\mathrm{ij}}\,c_{\mathrm{j}}\,v_{\mathrm{i}}\) in a different order. The sum \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} M_{\mathrm{ij}}\,v_{\mathrm{i}}\) is the component \(j\) of the vector \(\mat M^\T\vek v\), because the element in row \(j\) and column \(i\) of the transposed matrix is \(M_{\mathrm{ij}}\) (definition 1.100).

The second tool is the inverse of the matrix \(\mat F^\T\). Since \(\det\mat F = J \neq 0\), the matrix \(\mat F\) has an inverse \(\mat F^{-1}\) (theorem 2.54). By lemma 1.117 on the inverse of the transposed matrix, the matrix \(\mat F^\T\) therefore also has an inverse, and it is \((\mat F^{-1})^\T\). As in section 1.17.7, we write it in short as \(\mat F^{-\T}\).

Lemma 2.81 Nanson's formula (Nansonova formula)

Let \(\mat F\) be a real \(3\times 3\) matrix with the determinant \(J = \det\mat F \neq 0\). Then for any two vectors \(\vek a\) and \(\vek b\)

\[(\mat F\vek a)\times(\mat F\vek b) = J\,\mat F^{-\T}\,(\vek a\times\vek b) . \tag{2.79}\]

Let \(\mat F\) be the deformation gradient at a point of a body with \(J > 0\), and let short non-parallel fibres with the vectors \(\vek a\) and \(\vek b\) span before loading a parallelogram with the area \(\Delta A_0\) and the unit normal \(\vek n_0\), so that \(\vek a\times\vek b = \Delta A_0\,\vek n_0\). Let the vectors \(\mat F\vek a\) and \(\mat F\vek b\) span a parallelogram with the area \(\Delta A\) and the unit normal \(\vek n\), so that \((\mat F\vek a)\times(\mat F\vek b) = \Delta A\,\vek n\). Then

\[\vek n\,\Delta A = J\,\mat F^{-\T}\,\vek n_0\,\Delta A_0 . \tag{2.80}\]
Proof

We must show the equality (2.79), from which (2.80) follows by substitution. We use an arbitrary third vector \(\vek c\) and determinants, which are volumes of parallelepipeds with a sign (lemma 2.47).

  1. Let \(\vek c\) be any vector. We write \(\det(\vek c, \vek a, \vek b)\) for the determinant of the matrix with the columns \(\vek c\), \(\vek a\) and \(\vek b\). By definition 2.50 of the determinant, \(\det(\vek c, \vek a, \vek b) = \vek c\cdot(\vek a\times\vek b)\).
  2. By the same definition
    \[\det(\mat F\vek c, \mat F\vek a, \mat F\vek b) = (\mat F\vek c)\cdot\big((\mat F\vek a)\times(\mat F\vek b)\big) .\]
  3. By item 1 of lemma 2.55 on the change of volume, \(\det(\mat F\vek c, \mat F\vek a, \mat F\vek b) = \det\mat F\,\det(\vek c, \vek a, \vek b) = J\,\det(\vek c, \vek a, \vek b)\). Write \(\vek v = (\mat F\vek a)\times(\mat F\vek b)\). From steps 1 and 2
    \[(\mat F\vek c)\cdot\vek v = J\,\vek c\cdot(\vek a\times\vek b) .\]
  4. We rewrite the left side by (2.78), \((\mat F\vek c)\cdot\vek v = \vek c\cdot(\mat F^\T\vek v)\). By the rules for the scalar product (lemma 2.12) therefore
    \[\vek c\cdot\big(\mat F^\T\vek v - J\,(\vek a\times\vek b)\big) = 0 .\]
  5. This holds for every vector \(\vek c\). For \(\vek c = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) it says that the component \(x\) of the vector \(\mat F^\T\vek v - J\,(\vek a\times\vek b)\) is zero, and for \(\vek c = \vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\vek c = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) the same for the components \(y\) and \(z\). So \(\mat F^\T\vek v = J\,(\vek a\times\vek b)\).
  6. We multiply both sides by \(\mat F^{-\T}\), the inverse of the matrix \(\mat F^\T\), as lemma 1.117 on the inverse of the transposed matrix says. On the left \(\mat F^{-\T}(\mat F^\T\vek v) = (\mat F^{-\T}\mat F^\T)\,\vek v = \mat I\vek v = \vek v\) remains, by item 1 of lemma 1.99 on the properties of the matrix product and by lemma 1.106 on multiplication by the identity matrix, on the right \(J\,\mat F^{-\T}(\vek a\times\vek b)\), because the number \(J\) may be moved in front of the matrix (lemma 1.97 on the linearity of the product with a matrix). This is (2.79).
  7. Into (2.79) we substitute \(\vek a\times\vek b = \Delta A_0\,\vek n_0\) and \((\mat F\vek a)\times(\mat F\vek b) = \Delta A\,\vek n\) and move the number \(\Delta A_0\) in front of the matrix by the same lemma. We get (2.80).

Source: CM (3.3.25) (PDF p. 127), BOWER §2.2.4 (PDF p. 72). The proof with a third vector is not from the sources, CM leaves it to Problem 3.16.

Equation (2.80) is called Nanson's formula (CM, PDF p. 194). It says two things. The area of the patch is multiplied in the deformation by the length of the vector \(J\,\mat F^{-\T}\vek n_0\), and after loading the normal points in the direction of the vector \(\mat F^{-\T}\vek n_0\), not in the direction \(\mat F\vek n_0\). Why precisely \(\mat F^{-\T}\)? The normal must be perpendicular to every fibre of the face after loading. Let \(\vek a\) be a fibre in the face, so \(\vek n_0\cdot\vek a = 0\). After loading this fibre is \(\mat F\vek a\), and by (2.78) with the matrix \(\mat M = \mat F^{-\T}\), whose transpose is \(\mat F^{-1}\),

\[(\mat F^{-\T}\vek n_0)\cdot(\mat F\vek a) = \vek n_0\cdot(\mat F^{-1}\mat F\vek a) = \vek n_0\cdot\vek a = 0 .\]

The matrix \(\mat F^{-\T}\) thus exactly undoes the effect of the matrix \(\mat F\) on the fibres of the face, and the vector \(\mat F^{-\T}\vek n_0\) stays perpendicular to them. The sign is right too. If a fibre \(\vek c\) points to the side of the face into which \(\vek n_0\) points, then \(\vek n_0\cdot\vek c > 0\). By step 3 of the proof, \(\Delta A\,\vek n\cdot(\mat F\vek c) = J\,\Delta A_0\,\vek n_0\cdot\vek c > 0\), because \(J > 0\), so the fibre \(\mat F\vek c\) points to the side into which \(\vek n\) points. The outward normal stays outward.

Let us test the formula. For the band, \(\mat F^{-1}\) is the diagonal matrix with the reciprocals of the diagonal of the matrix \(\mat F\), \(1/1{,}5 = 0{,}6667\) and \(1/0{,}8165 = 1{,}2247\), because the product of two diagonal matrices multiplies the corresponding diagonal elements and gives \(\mat I\). Since it is diagonal, \(\mat F^{-\T} = \mat F^{-1}\). For the cross section \(\vek n_0 = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(J = 1\), so

\[\vek n\,\Delta A = 1 \cdot \mat F^{-\T}\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\,\Delta A_0 = 0{,}6667\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\,\Delta A_0 .\]

The normal stays \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and the area decreases to \(\Delta A_0/1{,}5\), as we computed from the width and the thickness.

For the sheared cube \(J = \det\mat F = 1\), because \(\mat F\) has only zeros below the diagonal and in the six-term formula (2.51) only the product of the diagonal remains. The inverse is \(\mat F^{-1} = \mat I - \mat H\), because \((\mat I + \mat H)(\mat I - \mat H) = \mat I - \mat H\mat H = \mat I\) and likewise \((\mat I - \mat H)(\mat I + \mat H) = \mat I - \mat H\mat H = \mat I\). The product \(\mat H\mat H\) is the zero matrix: the only nonzero element of \(\mat H\) is in row \(x\) and column \(z\), and row \(z\) is zero. So \(\mat F^{-\T} = \mat I - \mat H^\T\), with the element \(-0{,}001\) in row \(z\) and column \(x\).

  1. For the top face \(\vek n_0 = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), and \(\mat F^{-\T}\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) is the third column of the matrix \(\mat F^{-\T}\), just \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\). The normal stays \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), the area does not change. This agrees with figure 2.88.
  2. For the right face \(\vek n_0 = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(\mat F^{-\T}\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}} - 0{,}001\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), the first column of the matrix \(\mat F^{-\T}\). The normal leans downwards, perpendicular to the tilted edge \(BC\), and the area is multiplied by the length of this vector, \(\sqrt{1 + 0{,}001^2} = 1{,}0000005\). The right face is spanned by a fibre in the direction \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\), which does not change, and by the edge \(BC\), which like the edge \(AD\) gets longer by \(5 \cdot 10^{-7}\) of its length (the table in section 1.6.2). So the area does increase by the factor \(1{,}0000005\).

2.13.3 The Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensors

Now we can divide the force that acts across a patch after loading by the area of the patch before loading. Take a patch that has the area \(\Delta A_0\) and the unit normal \(\vek n_0\) before loading and the area \(\Delta A\) and the unit normal \(\vek n\) after loading (figure 2.90).

  1. The part of the body on the side into which \(\vek n\) points acts on the patch with the force \(\Delta\vek F\). By definition 2.20, for a small patch \(\Delta\vek F \approx \vek t(\vek n)\,\Delta A\), where \(\vek t(\vek n)\) is the traction vector after loading.
  2. By Cauchy's formula in the form (2.24), the component \(b\) of the vector \(\vek t(\vek n)\) is \(t_{\mathrm{b}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xb}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yb}}\,n_{\mathrm{y}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zb}}\,n_{\mathrm{z}}\). Since \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ab}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{ba}}\), this is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{bx}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{by}}\,n_{\mathrm{y}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{bz}}\,n_{\mathrm{z}}\), row \(b\) of the matrix \(\ten\sigma\) times the vector \(\vek n\) (definition 1.96). So \(\vek t(\vek n) = \ten\sigma\,\vek n\), which is (2.42).
  3. We substitute this into step 1. The number \(\Delta A\) may be moved to the vector \(\vek n\) (lemma 1.97 on the linearity of the product with a matrix), so \(\Delta\vek F \approx \ten\sigma\,(\vek n\,\Delta A)\).
  4. By Nanson's formula (2.80), \(\vek n\,\Delta A = J\,\mat F^{-\T}\vek n_0\,\Delta A_0\). When we substitute it and multiply the matrices in the same order (items 1 and 2 of lemma 1.99 on the properties of the matrix product), we get \(\Delta\vek F \approx J\,\ten\sigma\,\mat F^{-\T}\,\vek n_0\,\Delta A_0\).
  5. We divide by the area before loading:
    \[\frac{\Delta\vek F}{\Delta A_0} \approx J\,\ten\sigma\,\mat F^{-\T}\,\vek n_0 . \tag{2.81}\]

The left side is the force after loading per unit area before loading. The right side does not depend on the size of the patch, only on its normal before loading, and it depends on it through the matrix \(J\,\ten\sigma\,\mat F^{-\T}\), just as the traction vector depends on the normal \(\vek n\) through the matrix \(\ten\sigma\). We denote the right side by \(\vek t_0(\vek n_0)\). The index 0 says that the force is referred to the area before loading and that \(\vek n_0\) is the normal before loading. The force \(\Delta\vek F\) itself, however, is the real force after loading and points in the direction in which it points after loading. CM calls the vector \(\vek t_0\) the pseudo stress vector (psevdo vektor napetosti) (CM (4.4.2)-(4.4.4), PDF pp. 193-194).

How do we get nine components from this? For the Cauchy stress, row \(a\) of the matrix \(\ten\sigma\) is the traction vector on the face with the normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{a}}\) (definition 2.25). We do the same here.

  1. For \(\vek n_0 = \vek e_{\mathrm{a}}\) we have \(\vek t_0(\vek e_{\mathrm{a}}) = J\,\ten\sigma\,\mat F^{-\T}\vek e_{\mathrm{a}}\). A matrix times \(\vek e_{\mathrm{a}}\) gives its column \(a\), so this is column \(a\) of the matrix \(J\,\ten\sigma\,\mat F^{-\T}\).
  2. Column \(a\) of a matrix is row \(a\) of its transpose (definition 1.100). By item 3 of lemma 1.101 on transposing a product, used twice,
    \[(J\,\ten\sigma\,\mat F^{-\T})^\T = J\,(\mat F^{-\T})^\T\,\ten\sigma^\T = J\,\mat F^{-1}\,\ten\sigma ,\]
    because \(\ten\sigma^\T = \ten\sigma\) and because transposing twice gives back the original matrix, \((\mat F^{-\T})^\T = \mat F^{-1}\).

Row \(a\) of the matrix \(J\,\mat F^{-1}\ten\sigma\) is therefore the force after loading per unit area before loading on the face that had the normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{a}}\) before loading. We give this matrix a name.

Definition 2.82 First Piola-Kirchhoff stress (prvi Piola-Kirchhoffov tenzor napetosti)

Let \(\ten\sigma\) be the symmetric stress matrix after loading at a point of a body (definition 2.41), \(\mat F\) the deformation gradient and \(J = \det\mat F > 0\). The first Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor (prvi Piola-Kirchhoffov tenzor napetosti) at this point is the matrix

\[\mat P = J\,\mat F^{-1}\,\ten\sigma . \tag{2.82}\]

Its element \(P_{\mathrm{ab}}\) is the component \(b\) of the force after loading per unit area before loading on the face that had the unit normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{a}}\) before loading (section 2.13.3).

Source: CM §4.4.2, (4.4.8) (PDF pp. 193-194), BOWER §2.2.4 (PDF pp. 71-72).

The bold \(\mat P\) is a matrix, not a point. Since \(\mat P^\T = J\,\ten\sigma\,\mat F^{-\T}\), we can write (2.81) as \(\vek t_0(\vek n_0) = \mat P^\T\vek n_0\), with the components

\[t_{0\mathrm{b}}(\vek n_0) = P_{\mathrm{xb}}\,n_{0\mathrm{x}} + P_{\mathrm{yb}}\,n_{0\mathrm{y}} + P_{\mathrm{zb}}\,n_{0\mathrm{z}} .\]

This is the same form as Cauchy's formula (2.24): the first index names the face, the second the component of the force. Here, however, the order of the indices really matters, because \(\mat P\) is in general not symmetric (CM, PDF p. 194). We will see this on the turned band in section 2.13.5. BOWER defines the same matrix by the same product \(J\,\mat F^{-1}\ten\sigma\) and calls it the nominal stress (nominalna napetost), but denotes it by the letter \(S\), which we use for another tensor (BOWER, PDF p. 71). CM, however, writes the transposed matrix, \(\mat P = J\,\ten\sigma\cdot\mat F^{-\T}\) (CM (4.4.8), PDF p. 194). CM also points out that \(\mat P\) is a two-point tensor (dvotočkovni tenzor), which connects a point of the body before loading with the same point after loading (CM, PDF p. 194). In our notation the first index refers to the face before loading and the second to the direction of the force after loading. In CM's notation \(\mathbf P = P_{\mathrm{iI}}\,\hat{\mathbf e}_{\mathrm{i}}\,\hat{\mathbf E}_{\mathrm{I}}\) (CM (4.4.5), PDF p. 194) it is the other way round.

Is \(\mat P\) really a tensor in the sense of definition 2.45? Let us turn the axes with an orthogonal matrix \(\mat Q\), the same for both shapes of the body. The stress components change to \(\bar{\ten\sigma} = \mat Q\,\ten\sigma\,\mat Q^\T\) (theorem 2.42), and the deformation gradient to \(\bar{\mat F} = \mat I + \bar{\mat H} = \mat Q\,\mat F\,\mat Q^\T\), because by lemma 2.43 on the displacement gradient in turned axes \(\bar{\mat H} = \mat Q\,\mat H\,\mat Q^\T\), and \(\mat Q\,\mat I\,\mat Q^\T = \mat Q\mat Q^\T = \mat I\). The determinant does not change, because by theorem 2.52 on the determinant of a product \(\det\bar{\mat F} = \det\mat Q\,\det\mat F\,\det\mat Q^\T = \det\mat F\), since \(\det\mat Q\,\det\mat Q^\T = \det(\mat Q\mat Q^\T) = 1\). The inverse of the matrix \(\bar{\mat F}\) is \(\mat Q\,\mat F^{-1}\mat Q^\T\), because \(\mat Q\mat F\mat Q^\T\mat Q\mat F^{-1}\mat Q^\T = \mat Q\mat F\mat F^{-1}\mat Q^\T = \mat I\) and \(\mat Q\mat F^{-1}\mat Q^\T\mat Q\mat F\mat Q^\T = \mat Q\mat F^{-1}\mat F\mat Q^\T = \mat I\). Therefore

\[\bar{\mat P} = J\,\mat Q\,\mat F^{-1}\mat Q^\T\,\mat Q\,\ten\sigma\,\mat Q^\T = \mat Q\,(J\,\mat F^{-1}\ten\sigma)\,\mat Q^\T = \mat Q\,\mat P\,\mat Q^\T ,\]

which is the rule of the definition of a tensor.

Let us test the definition on the band. There \(J = 1\), \(\mat F^{-1}\) has the diagonal \(0{,}6667\), \(1{,}2247\), \(1{,}2247\), and the only nonzero component of the Cauchy stress is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 7{,}5\) MPa. The product \(\mat F^{-1}\ten\sigma\) multiplies row \(x\) of the matrix \(\ten\sigma\) by \(0{,}6667\), so the only nonzero component is

\[P_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0{,}6667 \cdot 7{,}5\ \text{MPa} = 5\ \text{MPa} = \frac{N}{A_0} .\]

This is the nominal stress. The stress in a bar of definition 2.6, the axial force divided by the area of the cross section before loading, is therefore the component \(P_{\mathrm{xx}}\) of the first Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor, not a component of the Cauchy stress. CM also calls the first Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor the nominal stress tensor (CM, PDF p. 193). In section 2.6.6 we wrote \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma = N/A\) for the bar. Exactly speaking, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) is the force per area after loading, but for the steel bar the two areas differ by less than a tenth of a per cent, as we will compute in section 2.13.6, so the equation was accurate enough.

The force \(\Delta\vek F\) and the vector \(\vek t_0(\vek n_0)\) point in the direction after loading. For the band, which only stretches, this does not matter. When a piece of the body also turns in the deformation, however, the force acting on it turns with it, and \(\mat P\) changes, although the material is stretched no more than before. So books define one more measure, in which they carry the force back to the shape before loading in the same way as a fibre. A fibre with the vector \(\vek a\) before loading has the vector \(\mat F\vek a\) after loading. Conversely, a vector \(\vek v\) after loading belongs to the fibre with the vector \(\mat F^{-1}\vek v\) before loading, because \(\mat F\) maps it to \(\mat F\mat F^{-1}\vek v = \vek v\). We apply the same rule to the force. The vector \(\mat F^{-1}\Delta\vek F\) (figure 2.90, blue) is the force as it would be before loading if the deformation mapped it like a fibre (CM (4.4.9), PDF p. 194). This is a fictitious force, since no force acts on the body before loading (BOWER, PDF p. 73).

  1. By (2.81) the fictitious force per unit area before loading is
    \[\frac{\mat F^{-1}\Delta\vek F}{\Delta A_0} \approx J\,\mat F^{-1}\,\ten\sigma\,\mat F^{-\T}\,\vek n_0 .\]
Definition 2.83 Second Piola-Kirchhoff stress (drugi Piola-Kirchhoffov tenzor napetosti)

Let \(\ten\sigma\) be the symmetric stress matrix after loading at a point of a body (definition 2.41), \(\mat F\) the deformation gradient and \(J = \det\mat F > 0\). The second Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor (drugi Piola-Kirchhoffov tenzor napetosti) at this point is the matrix

\[\mat S = J\,\mat F^{-1}\,\ten\sigma\,\mat F^{-\T} . \tag{2.83}\]

Source: CM §4.4.3, (4.4.9)-(4.4.12) (PDF pp. 194-195), BOWER §2.2.4 (PDF pp. 71-73), where it is called the material stress (materialna napetost).

By step 8, \(\mat S\vek n_0\) is the fictitious force \(\mat F^{-1}\Delta\vek F\) per unit area before loading on the patch with the normal \(\vek n_0\) before loading. The three measures of stress are related.

Lemma 2.84 Relations between the stress measures (zveze med merami napetosti)

Let \(\ten\sigma\) be the symmetric stress matrix after loading at a point of a body, \(\mat F\) the deformation gradient with \(J = \det\mat F > 0\), \(\mat P\) the first and \(\mat S\) the second Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor. Then:

  1. \(\mat S\) is symmetric, \(\mat S^\T = \mat S\),
  2. \(\mat P = \mat S\,\mat F^\T\),
  3. \(\ten\sigma = \dfrac{1}{J}\,\mat F\,\mat P = \dfrac{1}{J}\,\mat F\,\mat S\,\mat F^\T\).
Proof

We show all three claims with the definitions 2.82 and 2.83 and the rules for the matrix product.

1. Symmetry.

  1. By item 2 of lemma 1.101 on transposing a product the number \(J\) stays in front of the transposed matrix, and by item 3, applied to a product of three matrices, \(\mat S^\T = J\,(\mat F^{-\T})^\T\,\ten\sigma^\T\,(\mat F^{-1})^\T\).
  2. Transposing twice gives back the original matrix, so \((\mat F^{-\T})^\T = \mat F^{-1}\). The matrix \((\mat F^{-1})^\T\) is \(\mat F^{-\T}\) by our notation, and \(\ten\sigma^\T = \ten\sigma\). So \(\mat S^\T = J\,\mat F^{-1}\,\ten\sigma\,\mat F^{-\T} = \mat S\).

2. The first tensor from the second.

  1. By the definition, \(\mat S\,\mat F^\T = J\,\mat F^{-1}\,\ten\sigma\,\mat F^{-\T}\,\mat F^\T\).
  2. The matrix \(\mat F^{-\T}\) is the inverse of the matrix \(\mat F^\T\), as lemma 1.117 on the inverse of the transposed matrix says, so \(\mat F^{-\T}\mat F^\T = \mat I\), and \(J\,\mat F^{-1}\ten\sigma = \mat P\) remains.

3. The Cauchy stress.

  1. We multiply \(\mat P = J\,\mat F^{-1}\ten\sigma\) from the left by \(\mat F\). Since \(\mat F\mat F^{-1} = \mat I\), we get \(\mat F\,\mat P = J\,\ten\sigma\), and since \(J > 0\), \(\ten\sigma = \mat F\,\mat P/J\).
  2. By item 2, \(\mat F\,\mat P = \mat F\,\mat S\,\mat F^\T\), so also \(\ten\sigma = \mat F\,\mat S\,\mat F^\T/J\).

Source: CM (4.4.8), (4.4.11), (4.4.12) (PDF pp. 194-195) in CM's notation, where \(\mat P\) is transposed, BOWER (PDF pp. 71-72).

For the band \(\mat F^{-\T} = \mat F^{-1}\), and the product \(\mat F^{-1}\ten\sigma\,\mat F^{-1}\) multiplies \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) twice by \(0{,}6667\). The only nonzero component of the second Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor is therefore

\[S_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0{,}6667 \cdot 7{,}5\ \text{MPa} \cdot 0{,}6667 = 3{,}333\ \text{MPa} = \frac{N}{\lambda A_0} .\]

The fictitious force is \(\lambda\) times shorter than the real one, because \(\mat F^{-1}\) shortens every vector in the direction of the axis \(x\) by the factor \(1{,}5\), and it is divided by the area before loading. Item 2 of the lemma checks out: \(S_{\mathrm{xx}}\,F_{\mathrm{xx}} = 3{,}333 \cdot 1{,}5 = 5 = P_{\mathrm{xx}}\).

Why such a fictitious force? By item 1 of lemma 2.84 \(\mat S\) is symmetric, while \(\mat P\) in general is not, as we will see on the turned band. In section 2.13.5 we will also show that \(\mat S\) does not change when the deformed body is merely turned as a rigid body. The Green-Lagrange strain tensor \(\mat E = \tfrac12(\mat F^\T\mat F - \mat I)\) of definition 1.112 does not change under such a turn either. A turn with an orthogonal matrix \(\mat Q\) changes \(\mat F\) into \(\mat Q\mat F\), and \((\mat Q\mat F)^\T\mat Q\mat F = \mat F^\T\mat Q^\T\mat Q\,\mat F = \mat F^\T\mat F\). This is why for large rotations books write the laws of a material with the pair \(\mat S\) and \(\mat E\) (BOWER §3.4, PDF pp. 118-119). Why precisely these two belong together, BOWER shows with the work that the stresses do on a unit of volume before loading (BOWER §2.4.2, PDF pp. 83-84). In chapter 3 we will compute the work of deformation only for small strains and small rotations, where the differences between the stress measures do not matter (section 2.13.6). To separate the rotation from the stretch, however, we need a new theorem.

2.13.4 Stretch and rotation

The deformation gradient describes two things at once: how much the fibres stretch and how they turn. In section 1.17.6 we turned a square plate as a whole about the axis \(z\), with the numbers \(c = 0{,}6\) and \(s = 0{,}8\), for which \(c^2 + s^2 = 1\). The displacement field \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = -0{,}4\,x - 0{,}8\,y\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = 0{,}8\,x - 0{,}4\,y\), \(u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\) turns every fibre in the plane \(xy\) without changing its length, but its deformation gradient

\[\mat F = \begin{bmatrix} 0{,}6 & -0{,}8 & 0 \\ 0{,}8 & 0{,}6 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\]

is far from the identity matrix. In every deformation we would like to separate the stretch from the rotation. How?

The length of a fibre after loading is determined by the matrix \(\mat F^\T\mat F\). By (2.78) with \(\mat M = \mat F\), \(\vek c = \vek a\) and \(\vek v = \mat F\vek a\), the square of the length of a fibre after loading is

\[|\mat F\vek a|^2 = (\mat F\vek a)\cdot(\mat F\vek a) = \vek a\cdot(\mat F^\T\mat F\,\vek a) .\]

A rotation does not change lengths, so the matrix \(\mat F^\T\mat F\) does not see the rotation, it sees only the stretch. The element in row \(k\) and column \(l\) of the matrix \(\mat F^\T\mat F\) is row \(k\) of the matrix \(\mat F^\T\) times column \(l\) of the matrix \(\mat F\), that is, the scalar product of the columns \(k\) and \(l\) of the matrix \(\mat F\). For the rigid rotation above, therefore, \(\mat F^\T\mat F = \mat I\), because the first two columns have the length \(\sqrt{0{,}36 + 0{,}64} = 1\) and are perpendicular, \(0{,}6 \cdot (-0{,}8) + 0{,}8 \cdot 0{,}6 = 0\), and the third column is \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), a unit vector perpendicular to both. The matrix \(\mat F^\T\mat F\) is symmetric, so by theorem 2.61 on the principal directions of a symmetric matrix it has three mutually perpendicular eigenvectors. From them we will build a matrix \(\mat U\) that only stretches the fibres along these three directions, and we will show that the rest, \(\mat R = \mat F\mat U^{-1}\), does not change lengths.

Theorem 2.85 Polar decomposition (polarni razcep)

Let \(\mat F\) be a real \(3\times 3\) matrix with \(\det\mat F > 0\). Then there is exactly one pair of matrices \(\mat R\) and \(\mat U\) such that

\[\mat F = \mat R\,\mat U , \tag{2.84}\]

where \(\mat R\) is an orthogonal matrix with \(\det\mat R = 1\) and \(\mat U\) is a symmetric matrix whose eigenvalues are all positive. Every eigenvector of the matrix \(\mat F^\T\mat F\) with the eigenvalue \(\mu\) is an eigenvector of the matrix \(\mat U\) with the eigenvalue \(\sqrt\mu\), and these are all the eigenvalues of the matrix \(\mat U\). The matrix \(\mat U\) is the only symmetric matrix with positive eigenvalues for which \(\mat U^2 = \mat F^\T\mat F\).

Proof

We must show that such a pair exists, that there is only one, that \(\mat U\) has the stated eigenvectors and eigenvalues, and that \(\mat U\) is the only such matrix whose square is \(\mat F^\T\mat F\). The indices \(k\) and \(l\) in the proof count eigenvectors and run over \(1\), \(2\), \(3\). We denote the matrix \(\mat F^\T\mat F\) by \(\mat C\).

1. Existence.

  1. The matrix \(\mat C\) is symmetric. By items 1 and 3 of lemma 1.101 on transposing a product, \(\mat C^\T = \mat F^\T(\mat F^\T)^\T = \mat F^\T\mat F = \mat C\), because transposing twice gives back the original matrix.
  2. By theorem 2.61 on the principal directions of a symmetric matrix there are mutually perpendicular unit vectors \(\vek n_1\), \(\vek n_2\), \(\vek n_3\) and real numbers \(\mu_1\), \(\mu_2\), \(\mu_3\) such that \(\mat C\vek n_{\mathrm{k}} = \mu_{\mathrm{k}}\,\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\).
  3. Every number \(\mu_{\mathrm{k}}\) is positive. The vector \(\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\) is a unit vector, so \(\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\vek n_{\mathrm{k}} = 1\), and by (2.78)
    \[\mu_{\mathrm{k}} = \vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot(\mu_{\mathrm{k}}\,\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}) = \vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot(\mat F^\T\mat F\,\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}) = (\mat F\vek n_{\mathrm{k}})\cdot(\mat F\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}) = |\mat F\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}|^2 .\]
    The vector \(\mat F\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\) is not the zero vector, because \(\det\mat F \neq 0\) and \(\vek n_{\mathrm{k}} \neq \vek 0\) (lemma 2.53 on the zero determinant). So \(\mu_{\mathrm{k}} > 0\).
  4. By theorem 1.36 on the existence of square roots, \(\lambda_{\mathrm{k}} = \sqrt{\mu_{\mathrm{k}}}\) is a positive number with \(\lambda_{\mathrm{k}}^2 = \mu_{\mathrm{k}}\).
  5. Let \(\mat Q\) be the matrix whose rows are \(\vek n_1^\T\), \(\vek n_2^\T\) and \(\vek n_3^\T\). The element in row \(k\) and column \(l\) of the matrix \(\mat Q\mat Q^\T\) is the scalar product \(\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\vek n_{\mathrm{l}}\), which is \(1\) for \(k = l\) and \(0\) otherwise, so \(\mat Q\mat Q^\T = \mat I\). By theorem 2.52 on the determinant of a product and lemma 2.51 on the properties of the determinant, \((\det\mat Q)^2 = \det\mat Q\,\det\mat Q^\T = \det\mat I = 1\), so \(\mat Q\) has an inverse (theorem 2.54). Multiplying the equation \(\mat Q\mat Q^\T = \mat I\) from the left by \(\mat Q^{-1}\) we get \(\mat Q^\T = \mat Q^{-1}\), and therefore \(\mat Q^\T\mat Q = \mat I\). The matrix \(\mat Q\) is orthogonal (definition 2.39).
  6. The components of the vector \(\mat Q\vek n_1\) are the scalar products \(\vek n_1\cdot\vek n_1 = 1\), \(\vek n_2\cdot\vek n_1 = 0\) and \(\vek n_3\cdot\vek n_1 = 0\), so \(\mat Q\vek n_1 = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). In the same way \(\mat Q\vek n_2 = \vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\mat Q\vek n_3 = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\). Conversely, \(\mat Q^\T\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = \vek n_1\), \(\mat Q^\T\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} = \vek n_2\) and \(\mat Q^\T\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = \vek n_3\), because the columns of the matrix \(\mat Q^\T\) are the rows of the matrix \(\mat Q\).
  7. Let \(\mat\Lambda\) be the diagonal matrix with the diagonal \(\lambda_1\), \(\lambda_2\), \(\lambda_3\) and \(\mat U = \mat Q^\T\mat\Lambda\,\mat Q\). By items 3 and 1 of the lemma on transposing a product, \(\mat U^\T = \mat Q^\T\mat\Lambda^\T(\mat Q^\T)^\T = \mat Q^\T\mat\Lambda^\T\mat Q = \mat U\), because a diagonal matrix equals its transpose. The matrix \(\mat U\) is symmetric.
  8. By step 6, \(\mat U\vek n_1 = \mat Q^\T\mat\Lambda\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = \mat Q^\T(\lambda_1\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = \lambda_1\vek n_1\), and in the same way \(\mat U\vek n_2 = \lambda_2\vek n_2\) and \(\mat U\vek n_3 = \lambda_3\vek n_3\). The vectors \(\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\) are eigenvectors of the matrix \(\mat U\) with the eigenvalues \(\lambda_{\mathrm{k}}\).
  9. The matrix \(\mat U\) has no other eigenvalues. Let \(\mat U\vek v = \alpha\vek v\) with \(\vek v \neq \vek 0\), and let \(\vek w = \mat Q\vek v\). The vector \(\vek w\) is not the zero vector, because \(\vek w = \vek 0\) would give \(\vek v = \mat Q^\T\vek w = \vek 0\). From \(\mat Q\mat U = \mat Q\mat Q^\T\mat\Lambda\,\mat Q = \mat\Lambda\,\mat Q\) it follows that \(\mat\Lambda\vek w = \mat Q\mat U\vek v = \alpha\,\vek w\). The components of this equation are \(\lambda_1 w_{\mathrm{x}} = \alpha w_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\lambda_2 w_{\mathrm{y}} = \alpha w_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\lambda_3 w_{\mathrm{z}} = \alpha w_{\mathrm{z}}\). At least one component of the vector \(\vek w\) is not zero, so \(\alpha\) equals one of the numbers \(\lambda_{\mathrm{k}}\). All the eigenvalues of the matrix \(\mat U\) are therefore positive.
  10. \(\mat U^2 = \mat C\). The element in row \(k\) and column \(l\) of the matrix \(\mat Q\mat C\mat Q^\T\) is \(\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot(\mat C\vek n_{\mathrm{l}}) = \mu_{\mathrm{l}}\,\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\vek n_{\mathrm{l}}\), which is \(\mu_{\mathrm{k}}\) for \(k = l\) and \(0\) otherwise. So \(\mat Q\mat C\mat Q^\T = \mat\Lambda^2\), the diagonal matrix with the diagonal \(\mu_1\), \(\mu_2\), \(\mu_3\). Multiplying this equation from the left by \(\mat Q^\T\) and from the right by \(\mat Q\), we get by step 5 \(\mat C = \mat Q^\T\mat\Lambda^2\mat Q\). On the other hand, also by step 5, \(\mat U^2 = \mat Q^\T\mat\Lambda\,\mat Q\mat Q^\T\mat\Lambda\,\mat Q = \mat Q^\T\mat\Lambda^2\mat Q\).
  11. Let the diagonal matrix \(\mat\Lambda^{-1}\) have the diagonal \(1/\lambda_1\), \(1/\lambda_2\), \(1/\lambda_3\). The matrix \(\mat Q^\T\mat\Lambda^{-1}\mat Q\) is the inverse of the matrix \(\mat U\), because \(\mat U\,\mat Q^\T\mat\Lambda^{-1}\mat Q = \mat Q^\T\mat\Lambda\,\mat\Lambda^{-1}\mat Q = \mat Q^\T\mat Q = \mat I\), and the same in the reverse order. \(\mat U^{-1}\) is symmetric too, for the same reason as in step 7.
  12. Let \(\mat R = \mat F\,\mat U^{-1}\). Then \(\mat R\,\mat U = \mat F\,\mat U^{-1}\mat U = \mat F\).
  13. By item 3 of the lemma on transposing a product, the symmetry of the matrix \(\mat U^{-1}\) and step 10,
    \[\mat R^\T\mat R = (\mat U^{-1})^\T\mat F^\T\mat F\,\mat U^{-1} = \mat U^{-1}\,\mat U^2\,\mat U^{-1} = \mat I .\]
  14. By the theorem on the determinant of a product and the lemma on the properties of the determinant, \(\det\mat C = \det\mat F^\T\det\mat F = (\det\mat F)^2\). From \(\mat C = \mat Q^\T\mat\Lambda^2\mat Q\) and \(\det\mat Q^\T\det\mat Q = 1\) (step 5), \(\det\mat C = \det\mat\Lambda^2 = \mu_1\mu_2\mu_3\), because the determinant of a diagonal matrix is the product of its diagonal. In the same way \(\det\mat U = \lambda_1\lambda_2\lambda_3\). This number is positive, and its square is \(\mu_1\mu_2\mu_3 = (\det\mat F)^2\). The number \(\det\mat F\) is also positive with this square, so by the uniqueness in the theorem on the existence of square roots \(\det\mat U = \det\mat F\). From \(\det\mat F = \det\mat R\,\det\mat U\) it follows that \(\det\mat R = 1\).
  15. Since \(\det\mat R \neq 0\), the matrix \(\mat R\) has an inverse (theorem 2.54). Multiplying the equation \(\mat R^\T\mat R = \mat I\) from the right by \(\mat R^{-1}\) we get \(\mat R^\T = \mat R^{-1}\), and therefore \(\mat R\mat R^\T = \mat I\). Together with step 13, \(\mat R\) is orthogonal.

2. The eigenvectors of the matrix \(\mat C\).

  1. Let \(\mat C\vek m = \mu\,\vek m\) with \(\vek m \neq \vek 0\). By step 3 of item 1, repeated for \(\vek m/|\vek m|\), \(\mu > 0\). Let \(\alpha = \sqrt\mu\).
  2. Let \(\vek w = \mat Q\vek m\). By step 5 of item 1, \(\vek m = \mat Q^\T\vek w = w_{\mathrm{x}}\vek n_1 + w_{\mathrm{y}}\vek n_2 + w_{\mathrm{z}}\vek n_3\), because a matrix times a vector is the sum of its columns multiplied by the components of the vector.
  3. On the left side of the equation \(\mat C\vek m = \alpha^2\vek m\) we have, by lemma 1.97 on the linearity of the product with a matrix and by step 2 of item 1, \(w_{\mathrm{x}}\mu_1\vek n_1 + w_{\mathrm{y}}\mu_2\vek n_2 + w_{\mathrm{z}}\mu_3\vek n_3\), on the right \(\alpha^2 w_{\mathrm{x}}\vek n_1 + \alpha^2 w_{\mathrm{y}}\vek n_2 + \alpha^2 w_{\mathrm{z}}\vek n_3\). The scalar product of both sides with \(\vek n_1\) gives \(w_{\mathrm{x}}\mu_1 = \alpha^2 w_{\mathrm{x}}\), because \(\vek n_1\) is perpendicular to \(\vek n_2\) and \(\vek n_3\). In the same way the scalar products with \(\vek n_2\) and with \(\vek n_3\) give \(w_{\mathrm{y}}\mu_2 = \alpha^2 w_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(w_{\mathrm{z}}\mu_3 = \alpha^2 w_{\mathrm{z}}\).
  4. If \(w_{\mathrm{x}} \neq 0\), then \(\mu_1 = \alpha^2\), and since \(\lambda_1\) and \(\alpha\) are positive, \(\lambda_1 = \alpha\) by the uniqueness of square roots. So the equality \(w_{\mathrm{x}}\lambda_1 = w_{\mathrm{x}}\alpha\) holds in both cases, for \(w_{\mathrm{x}} \neq 0\) and for \(w_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\). In the same way \(w_{\mathrm{y}}\lambda_2 = w_{\mathrm{y}}\alpha\) and \(w_{\mathrm{z}}\lambda_3 = w_{\mathrm{z}}\alpha\).
  5. By step 8 of item 1 and by step 4,
    \[\mat U\vek m = w_{\mathrm{x}}\lambda_1\vek n_1 + w_{\mathrm{y}}\lambda_2\vek n_2 + w_{\mathrm{z}}\lambda_3\vek n_3 = \alpha\,(w_{\mathrm{x}}\vek n_1 + w_{\mathrm{y}}\vek n_2 + w_{\mathrm{z}}\vek n_3) = \alpha\,\vek m .\]
    The vector \(\vek m\) is an eigenvector of the matrix \(\mat U\) with the eigenvalue \(\alpha = \sqrt\mu\). That \(\mat U\) has no other eigenvalues we showed in step 9 of item 1.

3. Uniqueness.

  1. Let \(\mat F = \mat R'\mat U'\), where \(\mat R'\) is orthogonal with determinant \(1\) and \(\mat U'\) symmetric with positive eigenvalues. By item 3 of the lemma on transposing a product, \(\mat C = \mat U'^\T\mat R'^\T\mat R'\,\mat U' = \mat U'^2\), because \(\mat U'^\T = \mat U'\) and \(\mat R'^\T\mat R' = \mat I\).
  2. By the theorem on principal directions, \(\mat U'\) has mutually perpendicular unit eigenvectors \(\vek m_1\), \(\vek m_2\), \(\vek m_3\) with eigenvalues \(\alpha_1\), \(\alpha_2\), \(\alpha_3\), which are positive by assumption. So \(\mat C\vek m_{\mathrm{k}} = \mat U'(\mat U'\vek m_{\mathrm{k}}) = \alpha_{\mathrm{k}}^2\,\vek m_{\mathrm{k}}\).
  3. By item 2, \(\vek m_{\mathrm{k}}\) is an eigenvector of the matrix \(\mat U\) with the eigenvalue \(\sqrt{\alpha_{\mathrm{k}}^2} = \alpha_{\mathrm{k}}\), so \(\mat U\vek m_{\mathrm{k}} = \alpha_{\mathrm{k}}\vek m_{\mathrm{k}} = \mat U'\vek m_{\mathrm{k}}\) for \(k = 1, 2, 3\).
  4. Let \(\mat Q'\) be the matrix with the rows \(\vek m_1^\T\), \(\vek m_2^\T\), \(\vek m_3^\T\). By the same argument as in step 5 of item 1 it is orthogonal. The columns of the matrix \(\mat U\mat Q'^\T\) are \(\mat U\vek m_{\mathrm{k}}\), and the columns of the matrix \(\mat U'\mat Q'^\T\) are \(\mat U'\vek m_{\mathrm{k}}\), so by step 3 \(\mat U\mat Q'^\T = \mat U'\mat Q'^\T\). Multiplying both sides from the right by \(\mat Q'\) we get \(\mat U = \mat U'\), because \(\mat Q'^\T\mat Q' = \mat I\).
  5. Therefore also \(\mat R' = \mat F\,\mat U'^{-1} = \mat F\,\mat U^{-1} = \mat R\).
  6. Steps 2 to 4 used only that \(\mat U'\) is symmetric with positive eigenvalues and that \(\mat U'^2 = \mat C\). So \(\mat U\) is the only such matrix whose square is \(\mat C = \mat F^\T\mat F\), which is the last claim of the theorem.

Source: CM §3.9.2, (3.9.1), (3.9.9), (3.9.10) (PDF pp. 161-163), BOWER §2.1.13-2.1.14 (PDF pp. 54-56). The proof is not from the sources. CM only states the theorem and computes \(\mat U\) as in item 1.

Books call the matrix \(\mat U\) the right stretch tensor (desni tenzor raztezanja) and the matrix \(\mat R\) the rotation tensor (tenzor zasuka) (CM, PDF p. 161, BOWER §2.1.13, PDF p. 54). The eigenvalues \(\lambda_1\), \(\lambda_2\), \(\lambda_3\) of the matrix \(\mat U\) are the principal stretches (glavna razmerja dolžin) (CM, PDF p. 163), and we will call its eigenvectors \(\vek n_1\), \(\vek n_2\), \(\vek n_3\) the stretch directions (smeri raztezanja). What does the theorem say? The split \(\mat F\vek a = \mat R\,(\mat U\vek a)\) divides the deformation of a fibre into two steps (CM, PDF p. 162, BOWER, PDF pp. 55-56).

  1. The matrix \(\mat U\) stretches a fibre in the stretch direction \(\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\) by the factor \(\lambda_{\mathrm{k}}\) and does not turn it, because \(\mat U\vek n_{\mathrm{k}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{k}}\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\). A small cube with edges along the stretch directions becomes a box with edges along the same directions.
  2. The matrix \(\mat R\) then turns everything without changing any length. By (2.78) and \(\mat R^\T\mat R = \mat I\), indeed, \(|\mat R\vek v|^2 = \vek v\cdot(\mat R^\T\mat R\,\vek v) = \vek v\cdot\vek v = |\vek v|^2\) for every vector \(\vek v\).

Every deformation of a small piece of a body is thus a stretch in three mutually perpendicular directions followed by a rotation. CM also gives the split in the reverse order, first a rotation and then a stretch (CM (3.9.1), PDF p. 161), which we will not need.

Let us test the theorem on the deformation that first stretches a square by the factor \(1{,}5\) in the direction of the axis \(x\) and then turns it like the rigid rotation above (figure 2.91). Its deformation gradient is

\[\mat F = \begin{bmatrix} 0{,}9 & -0{,}8 & 0 \\ 1{,}2 & 0{,}6 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} ,\]

the first column of the rigid rotation multiplied by \(1{,}5\), the other two columns unchanged. We cannot read this off the matrix itself. Let us compute the split by the steps of the proof.

  1. The element in row \(k\) and column \(l\) of the matrix \(\mat F^\T\mat F\) is, as we saw above, the scalar product of the columns \(k\) and \(l\) of the matrix \(\mat F\). The first column has the squared length \(0{,}81 + 1{,}44 = 2{,}25\), the second \(0{,}64 + 0{,}36 = 1\), the third \(1\). The scalar product of the first two is \(0{,}9 \cdot (-0{,}8) + 1{,}2 \cdot 0{,}6 = 0\), and the third column is perpendicular to both. So \(\mat F^\T\mat F\) is the diagonal matrix with the diagonal \(2{,}25\), \(1\), \(1\).
  2. The eigenvectors are \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), with the eigenvalues \(2{,}25\), \(1\), \(1\). The principal stretches are their roots, \(1{,}5\), \(1\) and \(1\).
  3. \(\mat U\) is the diagonal matrix with the diagonal \(1{,}5\), \(1\), \(1\), and \(\mat U^{-1}\) the diagonal matrix with the diagonal \(1/1{,}5\), \(1\), \(1\).
  4. \(\mat R = \mat F\,\mat U^{-1}\) divides the first column of the matrix \(\mat F\) by \(1{,}5\) and leaves the other two, and we get exactly the matrix of the rigid rotation, with the columns \(0{,}6\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} + 0{,}8\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(-0{,}8\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} + 0{,}6\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\). Its determinant is \(0{,}6 \cdot 0{,}6 - (-0{,}8) \cdot 0{,}8 = 1\).

The split found the stretch and the rotation from which we built the deformation. For the rigid rotation alone, however, \(\mat F^\T\mat F = \mat I\), so \(\mat U = \mat I\) and \(\mat R = \mat F\): there is no stretch. The small strain tensor of definition 1.115, on the other hand, has \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = c - 1 = -0{,}4\) for the same rotation, as we saw in section 1.17.6. The split separates the rotation from the stretch exactly, even for a large rotation.

In this example the stretch directions were just the axes. In general they are not. Take again the sheared cube of section 1.6.2: a steel cube with the edge 10 mm, its bottom face glued to the table, whose top face we pushed by 10 µm in the direction of the axis \(x\). Its deformation gradient has a single off-diagonal element, \(0{,}001\) in row \(x\) and column \(z\). The matrix \(\mat F^\T\mat F\) therefore has the elements \(1\), \(1\) and \(1{,}000001\) on the diagonal and \(0{,}001\) in row \(x\), column \(z\) and in row \(z\), column \(x\). We computed the split with NumPy. The principal stretches are \(1{,}0005\), \(1\) and \(0{,}9995\). The stretch direction with the stretch \(1{,}0005\) is \(0{,}70693\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} + 0{,}70728\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), almost the diagonal \(AC\) of the front face, whose unit vector is \(0{,}70711\,(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} + \vek e_{\mathrm{z}})\). The stretch direction with the stretch \(0{,}9995\) is perpendicular to it, almost the diagonal \(BD\), and the stretch direction with the stretch \(1\) is the axis \(y\). More precisely, the stretches are \(1{,}0005001\) and \(0{,}9995001\), so the strains in the stretch directions, \(\lambda_{\mathrm{k}} - 1\), are \(+5{,}001 \cdot 10^{-4}\) and \(-4{,}999 \cdot 10^{-4}\), to four places the same as the strains of the diagonals \(AC\) and \(BD\) in the table of section 1.6.2. The rotation tensor is

\[\mat R = \begin{bmatrix} c & 0 & s \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ -s & 0 & c \end{bmatrix} , \qquad s = 0{,}0005 , \quad c = 0{,}999999875 ,\]

rounded to the places shown, with \(c^2 + s^2 = 1\). The matrix \(\mat R\) maps the vertical unit vector \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) to its third column, \(s\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} + c\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), which leans in the direction of the push. So the sheared cube stretches in the direction of the diagonal \(AC\), shrinks in the direction of the diagonal \(BD\), and then turns slightly as a whole about the axis \(y\) in the direction of the push (figure 2.92). This is the simple shear of section 1.6.2, taken apart into a stretch and a rotation.

The numbers of the cube show one more thing. The matrix \(\mat U - \mat I\) has the elements \(5 \cdot 10^{-4}\) in row \(x\), column \(z\) and in row \(z\), column \(x\), and on the diagonal only \(-1{,}25 \cdot 10^{-7}\) and \(3{,}75 \cdot 10^{-7}\). This is almost exactly the small strain tensor of the cube, which has \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zx}} = \tfrac12 \cdot 0{,}001 = 5 \cdot 10^{-4}\) and zeros elsewhere. The matrix \(\mat R - \mat I\) has \(s = 5 \cdot 10^{-4}\) in row \(x\), column \(z\) and \(-5 \cdot 10^{-4}\) in row \(z\), column \(x\), and on the diagonal \(c - 1 = -1{,}25 \cdot 10^{-7}\) in rows \(x\) and \(z\). This is almost exactly the small rotation tensor of definition 1.116, \(\omega_{\mathrm{xz}} = \tfrac12\,(0{,}001 - 0) = 5 \cdot 10^{-4}\) and \(\omega_{\mathrm{zx}} = -5 \cdot 10^{-4}\). This is no coincidence. Let \(\ten\varepsilon = \tfrac12(\mat H + \mat H^\T)\) be the small strain tensor and \(\ten\omega = \tfrac12(\mat H - \mat H^\T)\) the small rotation tensor.

  1. We multiply out the brackets: \(\mat F^\T\mat F = (\mat I + \mat H^\T)(\mat I + \mat H) = \mat I + \mat H + \mat H^\T + \mat H^\T\mat H\).
  2. In the same way \((\mat I + \ten\varepsilon)^2 = \mat I + 2\ten\varepsilon + \ten\varepsilon^2 = \mat I + \mat H + \mat H^\T + \ten\varepsilon^2\).
  3. The two matrices differ only in the last term, \(\mat H^\T\mat H\) against \(\ten\varepsilon^2\). The elements of both are sums of products of two partial derivatives. For partial derivatives of the order \(0{,}001\) these products are of the order \(0{,}000001\), a thousand times smaller than the elements of the matrix \(\mat H\). By the rule of section 1.6.2 we may drop them next to the first-order terms.
  4. By theorem 2.85 on the polar decomposition, \(\mat U\) is the only symmetric matrix with positive eigenvalues whose square is \(\mat F^\T\mat F\). The matrix \(\mat I + \ten\varepsilon\) is symmetric, has eigenvalues close to \(1\), and its square differs from \(\mat F^\T\mat F\) only by such products. So we expect \(\mat U \approx \mat I + \ten\varepsilon\), up to terms of the order of the square of the partial derivatives. The numbers of the cube confirm it, since such a term is exactly \(-1{,}25 \cdot 10^{-7} = -0{,}001^2/8\), and we prove an exact estimate below.
  5. Since \((\mat I + \ten\varepsilon)(\mat I - \ten\varepsilon) = \mat I - \ten\varepsilon^2\), we have \(\mat U^{-1} \approx \mat I - \ten\varepsilon\), and \(\mat R = \mat F\,\mat U^{-1} \approx (\mat I + \mat H)(\mat I - \ten\varepsilon) = \mat I + \mat H - \ten\varepsilon - \mat H\ten\varepsilon \approx \mat I + \ten\omega\), when we drop the product \(\mat H\ten\varepsilon\).

In steps 3 to 5 we dropped terms and only expected them to be small. How small are they? For the cube they were of the order \(0{,}001^2\), the square of the largest partial derivative. Let us show that this is so for every displacement gradient with small elements: if all partial derivatives of the displacement are at most \(m\) in absolute value, then \(\mat U\) differs from \(\mat I + \ten\varepsilon\), and \(\mat R\) from \(\mat I + \ten\omega\), in no element by more than a fixed number times \(m^2\). The idea is this. The matrices \(\mat U\) and \(\mat S = \mat I + \ten\varepsilon\) are symmetric, and by steps 1 and 2 their squares differ only by products of partial derivatives, by the matrix \(\mat H^\T\mat H - \ten\varepsilon^2\). If we write the difference of the squares in the eigendirections of both matrices, the difference of the matrices is the difference of the squares divided by the sum of the eigenvalues, which is close to \(2\). Then we get the rotation from \(\mat R\,\mat U = \mat F\).

Lemma 2.86 Stretch and rotation for small partial derivatives (raztezanje in zasuk pri majhnih parcialnih odvodih)

Let \(\mat F = \mat I + \mat H\) be the deformation gradient at a point of a body with \(\det\mat F > 0\), \(\mat F = \mat R\,\mat U\) its polar decomposition from theorem 2.85, \(\ten\varepsilon = \tfrac12(\mat H + \mat H^\T)\) the small strain tensor and \(\ten\omega = \tfrac12(\mat H - \mat H^\T)\) the small rotation tensor. Let \(m\) be a number with \(0 < m \le \tfrac16\) such that \(|H_{\mathrm{ij}}| \le m\) for all indices \(i\) and \(j\). Then for all \(i\) and \(j\)

\[|U_{\mathrm{ij}} - I_{\mathrm{ij}} - \varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}| \le \frac{9\,m^2}{1 - 3m} \le 18\,m^2 , \qquad |R_{\mathrm{ij}} - I_{\mathrm{ij}} - \omega_{\mathrm{ij}}| \le 91\,m^2 . \tag{2.85}\]
Proof

We must show both estimates. The indices \(i\) and \(j\) run over \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\), while the indices \(k\) and \(l\) count eigenvectors and run over \(1\), \(2\) and \(3\).

1. Product with a unit vector.

  1. Let \(\mat A\) be a \(3 \times 3\) matrix whose elements are at most \(m\) in absolute value, and \(\vek v\) a unit vector. The vector \((|v_{\mathrm{x}}|, |v_{\mathrm{y}}|, |v_{\mathrm{z}}|)\) has the length \(|\vek v| = 1\), and the vector \((1, 1, 1)\) the length \(\sqrt3\). By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality (corollary 2.15) their scalar product is therefore \(|v_{\mathrm{x}}| + |v_{\mathrm{y}}| + |v_{\mathrm{z}}| \le \sqrt3\).
  2. The component \(i\) of the vector \(\mat A\vek v\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} A_{\mathrm{ij}}\,v_{\mathrm{j}}\). By the triangle inequality for numbers and the rule for the absolute value of a product (lemma 1.6) it is at most \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} |A_{\mathrm{ij}}|\,|v_{\mathrm{j}}| \le m\sqrt3\) in absolute value. So \(|\mat A\vek v|^2 \le 3 \cdot 3m^2 = 9m^2\), and by lemma 1.28, that a larger nonnegative number has a larger square, \(|\mat A\vek v| \le 3m\).
  3. The elements of the matrices \(\ten\varepsilon\) and \(\ten\omega\) are at most \(m\) in absolute value, since for instance \(|\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}| = \tfrac12\,|H_{\mathrm{ij}} + H_{\mathrm{ji}}| \le \tfrac12\,(m + m)\). So step 2 holds for \(\mat A = \mat H\) and for \(\mat A = \ten\varepsilon\).

2. Eigenvalues.

  1. The matrix \(\mat S = \mat I + \ten\varepsilon\) is symmetric. By theorem 2.61 on the principal directions of a symmetric matrix it has mutually perpendicular unit eigenvectors \(\vek w_1\), \(\vek w_2\), \(\vek w_3 = \vek w_1\times\vek w_2\) with eigenvalues \(\kappa_1\), \(\kappa_2\), \(\kappa_3\). The vector \(\vek w_{\mathrm{l}}\) is a unit vector, so \(\kappa_{\mathrm{l}} = \vek w_{\mathrm{l}}\cdot(\mat S\vek w_{\mathrm{l}}) = 1 + \vek w_{\mathrm{l}}\cdot(\ten\varepsilon\,\vek w_{\mathrm{l}})\). By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and step 2, \(|\vek w_{\mathrm{l}}\cdot(\ten\varepsilon\,\vek w_{\mathrm{l}})| \le |\ten\varepsilon\,\vek w_{\mathrm{l}}| \le 3m\), so \(\kappa_{\mathrm{l}} \ge 1 - 3m\).
  2. By the proof of theorem 2.85 on the polar decomposition (steps 2, 3, 4 and 8 of item 1) the matrix \(\mat U\) has mutually perpendicular unit eigenvectors \(\vek n_1\), \(\vek n_2\), \(\vek n_3 = \vek n_1\times\vek n_2\) with positive eigenvalues \(\lambda_1\), \(\lambda_2\), \(\lambda_3\), for which \(\lambda_{\mathrm{k}}^2 = |\mat F\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}|^2\).
  3. By item 3 of the triangle inequality for vectors (corollary 2.16), used for the vectors \(\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(-\mat H\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\), and by step 2, \(|\mat F\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}| = |\vek n_{\mathrm{k}} + \mat H\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}| \ge |\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}| - |\mat H\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}| \ge 1 - 3m\). The number \(1 - 3m\) is not smaller than \(\tfrac12\), since \(m \le \tfrac16\). Both numbers \(\lambda_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(1 - 3m\) are positive and \(\lambda_{\mathrm{k}}^2 \ge (1 - 3m)^2\), so by lemma 1.28 \(\lambda_{\mathrm{k}} \ge 1 - 3m\).
  4. Steps 4 and 6 give \(\lambda_{\mathrm{k}} + \kappa_{\mathrm{l}} \ge 2\,(1 - 3m) \ge 1\) for all \(k\) and \(l\).

3. Difference of the squares.

  1. By theorem 2.85, \(\mat U^2 = \mat F^\T\mat F\). As in steps 1 and 2 before the lemma, \(\mat F^\T\mat F = \mat I + \mat H + \mat H^\T + \mat H^\T\mat H\) and \(\mat S^2 = \mat I + \mat H + \mat H^\T + \ten\varepsilon^2\). The matrix \(\mat D = \mat U^2 - \mat S^2\) is therefore \(\mat H^\T\mat H - \ten\varepsilon^2\).
  2. The element \((i, j)\) of the matrix \(\mat H^\T\mat H\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} H_{\mathrm{ki}}\,H_{\mathrm{kj}}\), a sum of three products, each at most \(m^2\) in absolute value. The same holds for \(\ten\varepsilon^2\) by step 3. By the triangle inequality for numbers, therefore, \(|D_{\mathrm{ij}}| \le 3m^2 + 3m^2 = 6m^2\).

4. Difference of the matrices in the eigendirections.

  1. Let \(\mat X = \mat U - \mat S\). The matrix \(\mat U\) is symmetric, so by (2.60) \(\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot(\mat U\vek w_{\mathrm{l}}) = \vek w_{\mathrm{l}}\cdot(\mat U\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}) = \lambda_{\mathrm{k}}\,(\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\vek w_{\mathrm{l}})\). Moreover \(\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot(\mat S\vek w_{\mathrm{l}}) = \kappa_{\mathrm{l}}\,(\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\vek w_{\mathrm{l}})\). So \(\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot(\mat X\vek w_{\mathrm{l}}) = (\lambda_{\mathrm{k}} - \kappa_{\mathrm{l}})\,(\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\vek w_{\mathrm{l}})\).
  2. The matrix \(\mat U^2 = \mat F^\T\mat F\) is symmetric, and \(\mat U^2\vek n_{\mathrm{k}} = \mat U(\lambda_{\mathrm{k}}\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}) = \lambda_{\mathrm{k}}^2\,\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\) by lemma 1.97 on the linearity of the product with a matrix. In the same way \(\mat S^2\vek w_{\mathrm{l}} = \kappa_{\mathrm{l}}^2\,\vek w_{\mathrm{l}}\). As in step 10, therefore,
    \[\begin{aligned} \vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot(\mat D\vek w_{\mathrm{l}}) &= (\lambda_{\mathrm{k}}^2 - \kappa_{\mathrm{l}}^2)\,(\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\vek w_{\mathrm{l}}) = (\lambda_{\mathrm{k}} + \kappa_{\mathrm{l}})(\lambda_{\mathrm{k}} - \kappa_{\mathrm{l}})\,(\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\vek w_{\mathrm{l}}) \\ &= (\lambda_{\mathrm{k}} + \kappa_{\mathrm{l}})\,\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot(\mat X\vek w_{\mathrm{l}}) . \end{aligned}\]
  3. By step 7, \(\lambda_{\mathrm{k}} + \kappa_{\mathrm{l}} \ge 2\,(1 - 3m) > 0\), so
    \[\big(\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot(\mat X\vek w_{\mathrm{l}})\big)^2 \le \frac{\big(\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot(\mat D\vek w_{\mathrm{l}})\big)^2}{4\,(1 - 3m)^2} .\]

5. Sum of the squares of the elements.

  1. The vectors \(\vek n_1\), \(\vek n_2\), \(\vek n_3\) are the unit vectors of the axes of a turned coordinate system, as we saw after theorem 2.61 on the principal directions of a symmetric matrix. By lemma 1.21 on a vector as a sum of steps along the axes, used in these axes, and by lemma 2.38 on the components of a vector in turned axes, every vector is \(\vek a = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} (\vek a\cdot\vek n_{\mathrm{k}})\,\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\), and as in step 7 of the proof of corollary 2.63, \(|\vek a|^2 = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} (\vek a\cdot\vek n_{\mathrm{k}})^2\). The same holds for \(\vek w_1\), \(\vek w_2\), \(\vek w_3\).
  2. Let \(\mat M\) be a \(3 \times 3\) matrix. By step 13 for the vector \(\mat M\vek w_{\mathrm{l}}\), by (1.10), by (2.78) and by step 13 for the vector \(\mat M^\T\vek e_{\mathrm{i}}\),
    \[\begin{aligned} \sum_{\mathrm{k}}\sum_{\mathrm{l}} \big(\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot(\mat M\vek w_{\mathrm{l}})\big)^2 &= \sum_{\mathrm{l}} |\mat M\vek w_{\mathrm{l}}|^2 = \sum_{\mathrm{l}}\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \big(\vek e_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot(\mat M\vek w_{\mathrm{l}})\big)^2 \\ &= \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{l}} \big((\mat M^\T\vek e_{\mathrm{i}})\cdot\vek w_{\mathrm{l}}\big)^2 \\ &= \sum_{\mathrm{i}} |\mat M^\T\vek e_{\mathrm{i}}|^2 = \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} M_{\mathrm{ij}}^2 , \end{aligned}\]
    where at the end we used that the vector \(\mat M^\T\vek e_{\mathrm{i}}\) has the components \(M_{\mathrm{ix}}\), \(M_{\mathrm{iy}}\), \(M_{\mathrm{iz}}\), the row \(i\) of the matrix \(\mat M\).
  3. Steps 12, 14 and 9 give
    \[\sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} X_{\mathrm{ij}}^2 \le \frac{1}{4\,(1 - 3m)^2}\sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} D_{\mathrm{ij}}^2 \le \frac{9 \cdot 36\,m^4}{4\,(1 - 3m)^2} = \frac{81\,m^4}{(1 - 3m)^2} .\]
  4. Each square \(X_{\mathrm{ij}}^2\) is at most this sum, so by lemma 1.28 \(|X_{\mathrm{ij}}| \le 9m^2/(1 - 3m)\), and since \(1 - 3m \ge \tfrac12\), this is at most \(18m^2\). Since \(X_{\mathrm{ij}} = U_{\mathrm{ij}} - I_{\mathrm{ij}} - \varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\), the first estimate is proved.

6. Rotation.

  1. Let \(\mat Y = \mat R - \mat I - \ten\omega\). By lemma 1.103 on the split into symmetric and antisymmetric parts, \(\mat H = \ten\varepsilon + \ten\omega\), and by step 16, \(\mat U = \mat I + \ten\varepsilon + \mat X\). Since \(\mat R\,\mat U = \mat F = \mat I + \mat H\), the properties of the matrix product (lemma 1.99) give
    \[\begin{aligned} \mat Y\mat U &= \mat R\,\mat U - \mat U - \ten\omega\,\mat U \\ &= \mat I + \ten\varepsilon + \ten\omega - \mat I - \ten\varepsilon - \mat X - \ten\omega - \ten\omega\,(\ten\varepsilon + \mat X) = -\mat N , \end{aligned}\]
    where \(\mat N = \mat X + \ten\omega\,(\ten\varepsilon + \mat X)\).
  2. The elements of the matrix \(\mat N\). By step 16, \(|X_{\mathrm{ij}}| \le 18m^2\). The element \((i, j)\) of the matrix \(\ten\omega\,(\ten\varepsilon + \mat X)\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} \omega_{\mathrm{ik}}\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{kj}} + X_{\mathrm{kj}})\). Since \(\omega_{\mathrm{ii}} = \tfrac12(H_{\mathrm{ii}} - H_{\mathrm{ii}}) = 0\), the sum has only two nonzero terms. In each, \(|\omega_{\mathrm{ik}}| \le m\) and \(|\varepsilon_{\mathrm{kj}} + X_{\mathrm{kj}}| \le m + 18m^2 \le 4m\), since \(18m^2 \le 3m\) for \(m \le \tfrac16\). So \(|N_{\mathrm{ij}}| \le 18m^2 + 2 \cdot 4m^2 = 26m^2\).
  3. Choose a row \(i\). Let \(\vek q\) be the vector with the components \(Y_{\mathrm{ix}}\), \(Y_{\mathrm{iy}}\), \(Y_{\mathrm{iz}}\) and \(\vek p\) the vector with the components \(N_{\mathrm{ix}}\), \(N_{\mathrm{iy}}\), \(N_{\mathrm{iz}}\). The element \((i, j)\) of the matrix \(\mat Y\mat U\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} Y_{\mathrm{ik}}\,U_{\mathrm{kj}} = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} U_{\mathrm{jk}}\,Y_{\mathrm{ik}}\), since \(\mat U\) is symmetric, and this is the component \(j\) of the vector \(\mat U\vek q\). By step 17, therefore, \(\mat U\vek q = -\vek p\).
  4. By (2.60), \((\mat U\vek q)\cdot\vek n_{\mathrm{k}} = \vek q\cdot(\mat U\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}) = \lambda_{\mathrm{k}}\,(\vek q\cdot\vek n_{\mathrm{k}})\). By step 13 and step 6, therefore,
    \[\begin{aligned} |\vek p|^2 = |\mat U\vek q|^2 &= \sum_{\mathrm{k}} \lambda_{\mathrm{k}}^2\,(\vek q\cdot\vek n_{\mathrm{k}})^2 \\ &\ge (1 - 3m)^2\sum_{\mathrm{k}} (\vek q\cdot\vek n_{\mathrm{k}})^2 = (1 - 3m)^2\,|\vek q|^2 , \end{aligned}\]
    and by lemma 1.28 \(|\vek q| \le |\vek p|/(1 - 3m) \le 2\,|\vek p|\).
  5. By step 18, \(|\vek p|^2 \le 3\,(26m^2)^2\), so \(|\vek p| \le 26\sqrt3\,m^2\). No component of a vector is larger in absolute value than its length, so \(|Y_{\mathrm{ij}}| \le |\vek q| \le 52\sqrt3\,m^2\). Since \(1{,}75^2 = 3{,}0625 > 3\), we have \(\sqrt3 < 1{,}75\) (lemma 1.28), and \(52 \cdot 1{,}75 = 91\). This is the second estimate.

Source: CM §3.5.3 (PDF p. 143) splits the displacement gradient into its symmetric and antisymmetric parts and calls them the small strain tensor and the small rotation tensor, and BOWER §2.1.10-§2.1.11 (PDF pp. 52-53) describes a small deformation as a small stretch followed by a small rigid rotation. The estimates and the proof are not from the sources.

The proof showed why the lemma holds. The squares of the matrices \(\mat U\) and \(\mat I + \ten\varepsilon\) differ only by the matrix \(\mat D = \mat H^\T\mat H - \ten\varepsilon^2\), which is made of products of partial derivatives. In the eigendirections of both matrices the difference of the matrices equals the difference of the squares divided by the sum of the eigenvalues (step 11). This sum is close to \(2\), so \(\mat U - \mat I - \ten\varepsilon\) is about half of the matrix \(\mat D\). The rotation then came from \(\mat R\,\mat U = \mat F\). The constants \(18\) and \(91\) are the bounds that the proof gives. Since we estimated crudely in the steps, we expect them to be much too large. With NumPy we computed the polar decomposition of \(200\,000\) random matrices \(\mat F = \mat I + \mat H\) with \(|H_{\mathrm{ij}}| \le m \le \tfrac16\), and the largest differences were \(1{,}6\,m^2\) for \(\mat U\) and \(1{,}7\,m^2\) for \(\mat R\). Let us test them on examples that we computed with a computer to fifty digits.

  • The sheared cube, \(m = 0{,}001\). The bound for \(\mat U\) is \(9m^2/(1 - 3m) = 9{,}03 \cdot 10^{-6}\). The matrix \(\mat U - \mat I - \ten\varepsilon\) has \(-1{,}25 \cdot 10^{-7}\), \(0\) and \(3{,}75 \cdot 10^{-7}\) on the diagonal, and \(-6{,}25 \cdot 10^{-11}\) in row \(x\), column \(z\). The matrix \(\mat D\) has \(-2{,}5 \cdot 10^{-7}\), \(0\) and \(7{,}5 \cdot 10^{-7}\) on the diagonal and zeros elsewhere, and the actual difference is indeed almost exactly half of it. The matrix \(\mat R - \mat I - \ten\omega\) has \(-1{,}25 \cdot 10^{-7}\), \(0\) and \(-1{,}25 \cdot 10^{-7}\) on the diagonal and at most \(6{,}25 \cdot 10^{-11}\) in absolute value off it. The bound \(91m^2\) is \(9{,}1 \cdot 10^{-5}\).
  • The hanging bar at the point \(P\) with \(x_{\mathrm{P}} = 5\) m, \(y_{\mathrm{P}} = 4\) mm and \(z_{\mathrm{P}} = 0\), \(m = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6}\). The bound for \(\mat U\) is \(3{,}4 \cdot 10^{-11}\), while the actual difference is at most \(5{,}9 \cdot 10^{-16}\), in row \(x\), column \(y\). Again it is half of the element \(D_{\mathrm{xy}} = -1{,}19 \cdot 10^{-15}\), which, like the element \(E_{\mathrm{xy}}\) of the Green-Lagrange tensor in section 1.17.3, is a product of partial derivatives of sizes \(10^{-10}\) and \(10^{-6}\).

For small partial derivatives the polar decomposition thus agrees with the split of the matrix \(\mat H\) into its symmetric and antisymmetric parts in section 1.17: \(\mat U - \mat I\) is the small strain tensor, \(\mat R - \mat I\) the small rotation tensor, up to terms that by lemma 2.86 are at most \(18m^2\) and \(91m^2\). For large strains or large rotations, however, as for the band and for the rigid rotation above, we must use the exact \(\mat U\) and \(\mat R\).

2.13.5 Biot stress

Let us now look at how the stress measures behave under a rotation. Take the rubber band of section 2.13.1, 10 cm long with the cross section \(A_0 = 2\ \text{mm}^2\) before loading, which we pull with the axial force \(N = 10\) N so that it stretches to 15 cm. This time, however, we pull it in the direction of the unit vector \(\vek m = 0{,}6\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} + 0{,}8\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\), not in the direction of the axis \(x\) along which it lay before loading (figure 2.93). The band stretches as before and at the same time turns so that it lies along \(\vek m\). Its deformation gradient is \(\mat F = \mat R\,\mat U\), where \(\mat U\) is the diagonal matrix with the diagonal \(1{,}5\), \(0{,}8165\), \(0{,}8165\), the deformation gradient of the band that does not turn, and \(\mat R\) is the rigid rotation with \(c = 0{,}6\) and \(s = 0{,}8\) of section 2.13.4, which maps \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) to \(\vek m\). By the uniqueness in theorem 2.85 these are exactly the matrices of the polar decomposition. The determinant is \(J = \det\mat R\,\det\mat U = 1\).

Let us compute the three stress measures we already know.

  1. The Cauchy stress. On the cross section perpendicular to the band, with the normal \(\vek m\), the traction vector is \(7{,}5\,\vek m\) MPa, because the area of the cross section after loading is again \(1{,}333\ \text{mm}^2\). On the faces along the band there are no forces. The band is a bar along \(\vek m\), and by the example of the bar in section 2.6.6, written in axes one of which lies along \(\vek m\), \(\vek t(\vek n) = 7{,}5\,(\vek m\cdot\vek n)\,\vek m\) MPa for every normal \(\vek n\). Row \(a\) of the matrix \(\ten\sigma\) is \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{a}}) = 7{,}5\,m_{\mathrm{a}}\,\vek m\) MPa, so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ab}} = 7{,}5\,m_{\mathrm{a}} m_{\mathrm{b}}\) MPa:
    \[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 7{,}5 \cdot 0{,}36 = 2{,}7 , \quad \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}} = 7{,}5 \cdot 0{,}48 = 3{,}6 , \quad \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = 7{,}5 \cdot 0{,}64 = 4{,}8 ,\]
    in megapascals, all the other components are zero.
  2. The inverse matrix. Since \(\mat R\,\mat U\,\mat U^{-1}\mat R^\T = \mat R\mat R^\T = \mat I\) and \(\mat U^{-1}\mat R^\T\mat R\,\mat U = \mat U^{-1}\mat U = \mat I\), we have \(\mat F^{-1} = \mat U^{-1}\mat R^\T\). By item 3 of lemma 1.101 on transposing a product and the symmetry of the matrix \(\mat U^{-1}\), \(\mat F^{-\T} = \mat R\,\mat U^{-1}\).
  3. The first Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor is \(\mat P = J\,\mat F^{-1}\ten\sigma = \mat U^{-1}\mat R^\T\ten\sigma\). The matrix \(\mat R^\T\) has the rows \((0{,}6\ \ 0{,}8\ \ 0)\), \((-0{,}8\ \ 0{,}6\ \ 0)\) and \((0\ \ 0\ \ 1)\). The product \(\mat R^\T\ten\sigma\) therefore has in row \(x\) the elements \(0{,}6 \cdot 2{,}7 + 0{,}8 \cdot 3{,}6 = 4{,}5\) and \(0{,}6 \cdot 3{,}6 + 0{,}8 \cdot 4{,}8 = 6\), and in row \(y\) the elements \(-0{,}8 \cdot 2{,}7 + 0{,}6 \cdot 3{,}6 = 0\) and \(-0{,}8 \cdot 3{,}6 + 0{,}6 \cdot 4{,}8 = 0\). The matrix \(\mat U^{-1}\) divides row \(x\) by \(1{,}5\), so the only nonzero elements are
    \[P_{\mathrm{xx}} = 3\ \text{MPa} , \qquad P_{\mathrm{xy}} = 4\ \text{MPa} .\]
    Row \(x\) is \(5\,\vek m\) MPa: on the cross section that had the normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) before loading acts a force of \(5\) N per square millimetre of the area before loading, in the direction \(\vek m\) along which the band now lies. The matrix \(\mat P\) is not symmetric, since \(P_{\mathrm{xy}} = 4\) MPa and \(P_{\mathrm{yx}} = 0\).
  4. The second Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor is, by item 2 of lemma 2.84 on the relations between the stress measures and by step 2, \(\mat S = \mat P\,\mat F^{-\T} = \mat P\,\mat R\,\mat U^{-1}\). Row \(x\) of the matrix \(\mat P\,\mat R\) has the elements \(3 \cdot 0{,}6 + 4 \cdot 0{,}8 = 5\), \(3 \cdot (-0{,}8) + 4 \cdot 0{,}6 = 0\) and \(0\), and the other rows are zero. The matrix \(\mat U^{-1}\) from the right divides column \(x\) by \(1{,}5\), so the only nonzero component is \(S_{\mathrm{xx}} = 3{,}333\) MPa, exactly as for the band that does not turn.

The rotation has thus changed the Cauchy stress and the first Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor, but not the second Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor (figure 2.93). This holds in general. Suppose that we additionally turn the deformed body as a rigid body with an orthogonal matrix \(\mat Q\) with determinant \(1\), just as we turned the band that did not turn into the turned one. This time \(\mat Q\) does not turn the axes, which stay where they are, but the body. Every fibre \(\mat F\vek a\) goes over into \(\mat Q\mat F\vek a\), so the new deformation gradient is \(\mat Q\mat F\), with the same determinant \(J\). We assume that in a rigid rotation the forces between the parts of the body turn together with the body without changing: on the face with the turned normal \(\mat Q\vek n\) acts the turned traction vector \(\mat Q\,\vek t(\vek n)\). The material does not feel which way it faces. CM writes this assumption as \(\vek t^* = \mat Q\cdot\vek t\), \(\vek n^* = \mat Q\cdot\vek n\) (CM (4.6.1), PDF p. 200).

  1. Let \(\ten\sigma'\) be the Cauchy stress of the turned body. The assumption says \(\ten\sigma'\mat Q\vek n = \mat Q\,\ten\sigma\,\vek n\) for every \(\vek n\). For \(\vek n = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) we get in turn the columns of both sides, so \(\ten\sigma'\mat Q = \mat Q\,\ten\sigma\), and after multiplying from the right by \(\mat Q^\T\), \(\ten\sigma' = \mat Q\,\ten\sigma\,\mat Q^\T\).
  2. The inverse of the matrix \(\mat Q\mat F\) is \(\mat F^{-1}\mat Q^\T\), because \(\mat Q\mat F\mat F^{-1}\mat Q^\T = \mat Q\mat Q^\T = \mat I\) and \(\mat F^{-1}\mat Q^\T\mat Q\mat F = \mat F^{-1}\mat F = \mat I\). Its transpose is \(\mat Q\,\mat F^{-\T}\) by item 3 of lemma 1.101 on transposing a product.
  3. The new second Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor is
    \[\mat S' = J\,\mat F^{-1}\mat Q^\T\,\mat Q\,\ten\sigma\,\mat Q^\T\,\mat Q\,\mat F^{-\T} = J\,\mat F^{-1}\,\ten\sigma\,\mat F^{-\T} = \mat S ,\]
    because \(\mat Q^\T\mat Q = \mat I\).
  4. The new first Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor, however, is \(\mat P' = J\,\mat F^{-1}\mat Q^\T\mat Q\,\ten\sigma\,\mat Q^\T = \mat P\,\mat Q^\T\), which differs from \(\mat P\).

For the band \(\mat Q = \mat R\). The band that does not turn has the only nonzero component \(P_{\mathrm{xx}} = 5\) MPa, so row \(x\) of the matrix \(\mat P\,\mat R^\T\) is \(5\) times row \(x\) of the matrix \(\mat R^\T\), that is \(5\,(0{,}6\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} + 0{,}8\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}) = 5\,\vek m\) MPa, as in step 3 above. CM computes the same in its notation (CM (4.6.3), (4.6.4), PDF p. 201) and adds that the second Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor is the stress measure most often chosen in solid mechanics (CM, PDF p. 201).

The second Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor does not see the rotation, but it has another weakness. For the band \(S_{\mathrm{xx}} = N/(\lambda A_0) = 3{,}333\) MPa, which is not a force per unit area we would measure on any area, since the force \(\mat F^{-1}\Delta\vek F\) is fictitious and shorter than the real one by the factor \(\lambda\). Is there a measure that does not see the rotation but does not shorten the force? There is. We turn the force back only by the rotation of the polar decomposition and do not undo the stretch.

  1. The force \(\Delta\vek F\) on the patch, turned back by \(\mat R^\T\), is \(\mat R^\T\Delta\vek F\). Since \(\mat R^\T\mat R = \mat I\), \(\mat R^\T\) undoes the rotation \(\mat R\). It does not change lengths, because by theorem 2.85 on the polar decomposition \(\mat R\) is an orthogonal matrix, so by definition 2.39 of an orthogonal matrix also \(\mat R\mat R^\T = \mat I\), and therefore by (2.78) \(|\mat R^\T\vek v|^2 = \vek v\cdot(\mat R\mat R^\T\vek v) = |\vek v|^2\), just as we showed for \(\mat R\) in section 2.13.4.
  2. Per unit area before loading this is, by (2.81) and \(\vek t_0(\vek n_0) = \mat P^\T\vek n_0\), equal to \(\mat R^\T\mat P^\T\vek n_0 = (\mat P\,\mat R)^\T\vek n_0\), by item 3 of lemma 1.101 on transposing a product.
  3. Just as for the first Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor, row \(a\) of the matrix \(\mat P\,\mat R\) is therefore the turned-back force per unit area before loading on the face that had the normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{a}}\) before loading. The matrix \(\mat P\,\mat R\) is in general not symmetric. We take its symmetric part, and we explain why right after the definition.
Definition 2.87 Biot stress (Biotov tenzor napetosti)

Let \(\mat F = \mat R\,\mat U\) be the polar decomposition of the deformation gradient at a point of a body (theorem 2.85) and \(\mat P\) the first Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor at this point. The Biot stress tensor (Biotov tenzor napetosti) is the matrix

\[\mat T = \tfrac12\,\big(\mat P\,\mat R + \mat R^\T\mat P^\T\big) . \tag{2.86}\]

Source: Our sources do not define the Biot stress tensor. It is built from the polar decomposition (CM §3.9.2, PDF pp. 161-163), the first Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor (CM §4.4.2, PDF pp. 193-194) and the Biot strain \(\mat U - \mat I\), which BOWER calls the Lagrangian nominal strain (Lagrangeova nominalna deformacija) (BOWER §2.1.15, PDF p. 57).

The matrix \(\mat T\) is symmetric, since by items 1 and 3 of lemma 1.101 on transposing a product \((\mat P\mat R)^\T = \mat R^\T\mat P^\T\) and \((\mat R^\T\mat P^\T)^\T = \mat P\mat R\), so transposing only swaps the two terms. Why the symmetric part? Books write every measure of stress in a pair with a measure of strain, so that together they give the work that the stresses do on a unit of volume before loading. BOWER shows this for the pair of the second Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor and the Green-Lagrange strain (BOWER §2.4.2, PDF p. 83). The partner of the Biot stress tensor is the Biot strain \(\mat U - \mat I\). When it changes a little, by \(\Delta\mat U\), the work that the stresses do on a unit of volume before loading is approximately the sum of the products of the corresponding elements of the matrices \(\mat P\,\mat R\) and \(\Delta\mat U\). This follows from the expression with which BOWER writes the work of the first Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor (BOWER §2.4.2, PDF p. 83) and from the split \(\mat F = \mat R\,\mat U\), but we will not do this computation here, because our sources do not treat the Biot pair. The matrix \(\mat U\) is symmetric, so its change \(\Delta\mat U\) is symmetric too, and in the sum the elements \((\mat P\mat R)_{\mathrm{xy}}\) and \((\mat P\mat R)_{\mathrm{yx}}\) appear with the same factor \(\Delta U_{\mathrm{xy}}\). Only their sum counts, and likewise for the other two pairs of off-diagonal elements, so only the symmetric part of the matrix \(\mat P\,\mat R\) counts. The Biot stress tensor is easiest to compute from the second Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor.

Lemma 2.88 Biot stress and the second Piola-Kirchhoff stress (Biotov in drugi Piola-Kirchhoffov tenzor)

Let \(\mat F = \mat R\,\mat U\) be the polar decomposition of the deformation gradient, \(\mat S\) the second Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor and \(\mat T\) the Biot stress tensor at the same point of a body. Then

\[\mat T = \tfrac12\,\big(\mat S\,\mat U + \mat U\,\mat S\big) .\]

When \(\mat S\,\mat U = \mat U\,\mat S\), \(\mat T = \mat S\,\mat U\).

Proof

We express the first Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor through the second and substitute it into equation (2.86) of definition 2.87.

  1. By item 2 of lemma 2.84 on the relations between the stress measures, \(\mat P = \mat S\,\mat F^\T\).
  2. By item 3 of lemma 1.101 on transposing a product, \(\mat F^\T = (\mat R\,\mat U)^\T = \mat U^\T\mat R^\T = \mat U\,\mat R^\T\), because \(\mat U\) is symmetric.
  3. So \(\mat P\,\mat R = \mat S\,\mat U\,\mat R^\T\mat R = \mat S\,\mat U\), because \(\mat R^\T\mat R = \mat I\).
  4. By item 3 of the lemma on transposing a product, \(\mat R^\T\mat P^\T = (\mat P\,\mat R)^\T = (\mat S\,\mat U)^\T = \mat U^\T\mat S^\T = \mat U\,\mat S\), because \(\mat U\) and \(\mat S\) are symmetric (item 1 of the lemma on the relations between the stress measures).
  5. We substitute steps 3 and 4 into (2.86) and get \(\mat T = \tfrac12(\mat S\,\mat U + \mat U\,\mat S)\). If \(\mat S\,\mat U = \mat U\,\mat S\), the two terms are equal and \(\mat T = \mat S\,\mat U\).

Source: The lemma is not from the sources, it follows from CM (4.4.11) (PDF p. 195) and (3.9.1) (PDF p. 161).

The Biot stress tensor therefore does not see the rotation. A body that we turn with the matrix \(\mat Q\) after the deformation has the deformation gradient \(\mat Q\mat F = (\mat Q\mat R)\,\mat U\). The matrix \(\mat Q\mat R\) is orthogonal with determinant \(1\), since \((\mat Q\mat R)^\T\mat Q\mat R = \mat R^\T\mat Q^\T\mat Q\mat R = \mat I\) and \(\det(\mat Q\mat R) = \det\mat Q\,\det\mat R = 1\). By the uniqueness in the theorem on the polar decomposition, \(\mat U\) stays the same, and by the computation above \(\mat S\) stays the same as well. By the lemma, therefore, \(\mat T\) stays the same too.

For the band \(\mat S\) and \(\mat U\) are diagonal, so \(\mat S\,\mat U = \mat U\,\mat S\), and the only nonzero component of the Biot stress tensor is

\[T_{\mathrm{xx}} = S_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\lambda = 3{,}333\ \text{MPa} \cdot 1{,}5 = 5\ \text{MPa} = \frac{N}{A_0} ,\]

for the band that does not turn and for the turned one. The Biot stress tensor thus has both properties we were looking for. It does not see the rotation, and \(T_{\mathrm{xx}}\) is the real axial force divided by the area before loading, measured along the band, whichever way the band faces. Its partner, the Biot strain \(\mat U - \mat I\), has for the band the diagonal \(0{,}5\), \(-0{,}1835\), \(-0{,}1835\). The number \(0{,}5\) is the strain of the band \(\Delta L/L_0 = 5\ \text{cm}/10\ \text{cm}\) of definition 1.2. The Biot pair is thus the nominal stress and the strain \(\Delta L/L_0\) of the test in which we pull the band, also when the band turns during it.

So

\[\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}}{P_{\mathrm{xx}}} = \lambda , \qquad \frac{S_{\mathrm{xx}}}{P_{\mathrm{xx}}} = \frac{1}{\lambda} , \qquad \frac{T_{\mathrm{xx}}}{P_{\mathrm{xx}}} = 1 .\]

At \(\lambda = 1{,}5\) the Cauchy stress and the second Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor differ by the factor \(1{,}5^2 = 2{,}25\), since \(7{,}5/3{,}333 = 2{,}25\) (figure 2.95).

What about the steel bar of section 2.2, with the diameter 10 mm, which we pull with the axial force 10 kN? Its stress \(N/A_0 = 10\,000\ \text{N}/78{,}54\ \text{mm}^2 = 127{,}32\) MPa is nominal by definition 2.6, that is, \(P_{\mathrm{xx}}\). We get the strain as for the hanging bar in section 2.2, by dividing the stress by the number 196 GPa that BOWER gives for steel (Table 3.1, PDF p. 100). Why this gives the strain we will see in chapter 3. The strain is

\[\varepsilon = \frac{127{,}32\ \text{MPa}}{196\ \text{GPa}} = 6{,}496 \cdot 10^{-4} , \qquad \lambda = 1 + \varepsilon = 1{,}00065 .\]

The diameter of the bar decreases by \(0{,}3\) times the strain (section 1.6), that is, by the factor \(1 - 0{,}3 \cdot 6{,}496 \cdot 10^{-4} = 0{,}999805\), and the area of the cross section by the factor \(0{,}999805^2 = 0{,}999610\). The bar does not turn, so \(\mat R = \mat I\), and \(\mat U = \mat F\) is a diagonal matrix. Then

  1. the Cauchy stress is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 127{,}32\ \text{MPa}/0{,}999610 = 127{,}37\) MPa, \(0{,}039\) % more than the nominal stress,
  2. the component of the second Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor is \(S_{\mathrm{xx}} = P_{\mathrm{xx}}/\lambda = 127{,}32\ \text{MPa}/1{,}00065 = 127{,}24\) MPa, \(0{,}065\) % less,
  3. the component of the Biot stress tensor is \(T_{\mathrm{xx}} = S_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\lambda = P_{\mathrm{xx}} = 127{,}32\) MPa, by lemma 2.88, because \(\mat S\) and \(\mat U\) are diagonal.

The Cauchy stress is \(0{,}039\) % larger than the nominal stress and the second Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor \(0{,}065\) % smaller, so these two measures differ from each other by \(0{,}10\) %, while the component of the Biot stress tensor equals the nominal stress. In figure 2.95 the bar lies on the grey vertical line, right next to \(\lambda = 1\), where all the ratios are almost \(1\). The differences are much smaller than the uncertainty of the data for steel: BOWER gives his values of the number 196 GPa as representative and his values of the yield stress as rough (BOWER, PDF pp. 100 and 156). BOWER writes that for small strains and small rotations, that is, when all partial derivatives of the displacements are small, all stress measures are equal (BOWER §2.4.3, PDF p. 84), and CM that in this case no distinction is made between the second Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor and the Cauchy stress (CM, PDF p. 229). In the rest of the book all partial derivatives of the displacements will be small, that is, the strains and the rotations, except where we say otherwise, and we will compute with the Cauchy stress \(\ten\sigma\) and the small strain tensor \(\ten\varepsilon\).

When, then, does the difference matter? In three kinds of cases.

  1. Large strains, as in rubber, which stretches elastically by very large amounts (BOWER §3.5, PDF pp. 120-121), for example our band by half its length.
  2. Large permanent deformations of metals, for example in metal forming (preoblikovanje kovin) (BOWER, PDF p. 31). The permanent strains there are large, the part of the strain that returns on unloading is small, and the material can turn a lot (BOWER §3.9.3, PDF p. 173).
  3. Small strains with large rotations. In a slender bar bent into a circle the material is stretched or compressed only a little, but its ends turn by a quarter of a circle (BOWER §3.4, PDF p. 118). The small strain tensor is not zero for a large rotation, as we saw for the rigid rotation with \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = -0{,}4\) in section 2.13.4. A law relating \(\ten\sigma\) and \(\ten\varepsilon\) would therefore predict a stress for the rotation alone. In such cases BOWER uses the second Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor and the Green-Lagrange strain tensor, which do not see the rotation (BOWER, PDF pp. 118-119).

For large deformations we do not know the shape of the body after loading in advance, since this shape is part of what we are looking for. So books then write the equilibrium of a small piece on the shape before loading, with the first Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor and with derivatives with respect to the coordinates before loading (CM (5.3.17), (5.3.18), PDF p. 228). For small strains and small rotations, that is, when all partial derivatives of the displacements are small, these equations go over into the equilibrium equations (2.31) (CM (5.3.21), (5.3.22), PDF p. 229).

Let us sum up how we arrived at these measures. From three fibres and the ratio of volumes \(J\) we got Nanson's formula, which says how the area and the normal of a small patch change. With it we converted the force after loading, given by the Cauchy stress, to the area before loading and got the first Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor. When we mapped the force back like a fibre, we got the symmetric second Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor. That it does not change in a rigid rotation we showed on the turned band and with a general computation. With the polar decomposition, which splits a deformation into a stretch and a rotation, we defined the Biot stress tensor, which only turns the force back. For steel the measures differ by about a tenth of a per cent or less, for rubber by factors of the stretch. How stress and strain are related for a given material is the question of chapter 3.
