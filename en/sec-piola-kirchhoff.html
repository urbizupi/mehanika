Chapter 2 Stress and equilibrium

2.13 Stress referred to the original shape

When we defined the stress in a bar in section 2.2, we divided the axial force by the area of the cross section before loading. We also wrote that for steel it does not matter which area we take, but for rubber it does. The traction vector of section 2.5, however, is the force per unit area of a cut in the body as it is under load. The area we divide by there is therefore the area after loading. For a steel bar in the elastic range the two areas differ by less than a tenth of a per cent (section 2.13.6: \(0{,}039\) %), so we have not noticed the difference so far. For the rubber band that we stretch from 10 cm to 15 cm (section 1.1), however, the area after loading is a third smaller than the area before it. Then we must say which area the force is referred to.

The three stresses this section separates:

  1. Cauchy stress \(\ten\sigma\): the true force per area after loading (patch).
  2. First Piola-Kirchhoff tensor \(\mat P\): the true force per area before loading (patch before loading).
  3. Second Piola-Kirchhoff tensor \(\mat S\): the force carried back by \(\mat F^{-1}\) into the shape before loading (a fictitious force), per area before loading (in the figure).

From history

The equations of equilibrium at every interior point of a body were derived by Gabrio Piola in 1833 in his memoir La Meccanica de' corpi naturalmente estesi (TP1, PDF p. 434 and 435). He wanted to direct the attention of mathematicians to the principles of Lagrange's Mécanique analytique and, following Lagrange's example, he treated bodies that return to their original shape after unloading (TP1, PDF p. 435). In 1852 Kirchhoff published in the proceedings of the Vienna Academy of Sciences a memoir on the equilibrium of such a body under displacements that are not infinitely small, and derived the equations in two ways, the first of which, in his words, agrees with the way indicated by Saint-Venant (TP2B, PDF p. 60). As variables he took the coordinates of the point after the displacement, not the displacements themselves, and wrote the equations of equilibrium in the usual form (TP2B, PDF p. 60). The stresses in them, however, did not have the usual meaning, for they meant forces across faces that were parallel to the coordinate planes before loading and no longer are afterwards (TP2B, PDF p. 60). So in his words these nine stresses are no longer equal in pairs (TP2B, PDF p. 60).

Let us return to the stress per area before loading: its nine components are no longer equal in pairs. We proved theorem 2.41 on the symmetry of stress by the equilibrium of a small cube with faces in the coordinate planes. Faces that were parallel to these planes before loading are in general no longer parallel to the coordinate planes afterwards, but turn and tilt, like the right face of the sheared cube, whose normal no longer points along the axis \(x\) after loading. We still measure the force components along the axes, so such a proof does not hold for them (TP2B, PDF p. 60). The turned band of section 2.13.5 shows that this stress is not symmetric even when the faces stay perpendicular. The stress on faces from the initial position we shall meet in definition 2.119.

The goal of this section is to write the stress also per unit area before loading, at every point of a body and on every face, not only in a bar. The route has four steps. First we compute how the area and the normal of a small piece of a face change in a deformation. Then we divide the force that acts across such a piece after loading by its area before loading. We get two new measures of stress, which books name after Piola and Kirchhoff. Next we split the deformation gradient into a stretch and a rotation and use the split to define one more measure, named after Biot. Finally we compare all the measures on the rubber band and on the steel bar and say when the difference between them matters.

We compute in the following model. We look at a point of a body that is in equilibrium after loading. At this point we know the deformation gradient \(\mat F = \mat I + \mat H\) (definition 1.113), that is, the matrix that by lemma 1.114 on a short fibre after loading maps the vector of a short fibre before loading to its vector after loading, up to a remainder that is small compared with the length of the fibre. We also know the stress components \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ab}}\) at this point, computed in the body after loading. They form the stress matrix \(\ten\sigma\) (definition 2.48), whose row \(a\) is the traction vector on the face with the normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{a}}\). By theorem 2.41 on the symmetry of stress, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ab}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{ba}}\), so the matrix \(\ten\sigma\) is symmetric. We assume three more things.

  1. The determinant \(J = \det\mat F\) is positive, as CM also assumes (PDF p. 126). By lemma 2.77 on the change of volume, \(J\) is the ratio of the volumes of the parallelepiped spanned after loading by the fibres (\(\mat F\) times the fibres) and of the one they spanned before. The volume ratio of a real small piece approaches \(J\) as the piece shrinks, which we checked in section 2.10.5 on the hanging bar. The general proof would need the volume of bodies with curved faces, which the book defines later. Figure 2.231 shows all three cases in the plane. With \(J > 0\) the piece stays a piece with the same sense of turning from the first fibre to the second. With \(J = 0\) the piece would be squashed into a surface, a line or a point without volume, which matter cannot do. With \(J < 0\) it would turn into its mirror image, and by corollary 2.80 on a positive Jacobian it would have to be flattened on the way during gradual loading (section 2.10.5). We will need a nonzero determinant for the inverse matrix \(\mat F^{-1}\), and a positive one for the outward normal to stay outward and for the split of the deformation gradient into a stretch and a rotation in section 2.13.4.
  2. The pieces of faces we look at are small. So we may describe a face after loading with the deformation gradient at the point, just like fibres. The traction vector on a patch that is a parallelogram after loading, not a square, we take as constant over the patch: definition 2.20 asks for squares, so we cover the patch with small squares on which the traction vector hardly changes because it is continuous. For a linear displacement field, as for the band and for the sheared cube below, this description is exact.
  3. The coordinate system is the same before and after loading, as everywhere in the book (CM §3.2.1, PDF p. 112). It is right-handed (definition 2.32), because we will use the vector product.

At the top is the same small square before loading three times, with the fibres \(\vek a\) and \(\vek b\) along its edges. The grey arrow shows which way we turn from the first fibre to the second, here anticlockwise. Under every square is what the matrix \(\mat F\) makes of it. In the first case it is a parallelogram, and the turn from \(\mat F\vek a\) to \(\mat F\vek b\) has the same sense as before. In the second \(\mat F\vek a\) and \(\mat F\vek b\) lie on the same line, and only a segment is left of the square. In the third the fibres have changed sides, and the turn from the first to the second goes clockwise: this is the mirror image. On the right is the patch of the second assumption, a parallelogram after loading. We cover it with small squares, and on each the traction vector \(\vek t\) is almost the same.

We do not assume, however, that the strains are small. Everything that follows holds also for a band stretched by half its length.

2.13.1 Two areas

Let us start with an example. The rubber band of section 1.1 is \(L_0 = 10\) cm long before loading. Its cross section is a rectangle 2 mm wide and 1 mm thick, with the area \(A_0 = 2\ \text{mm}^2\). The index 0 means that a quantity refers to the shape before loading, as with the length \(L_0\) in section 1.6.2. We lay the axis \(x\) along the band, with the origin at its centre, the axis \(y\) along the width and the axis \(z\) along the thickness. We pull the band at both ends along the axis \(x\) until it is 15 cm long. The stretch (razmerje dolžin) of section 1.1, the new length divided by the old one, is then

\[\lambda = \frac{15\ \text{cm}}{10\ \text{cm}} = 1{,}5 .\]

Let the force with which we pull be 10 N at this length. We chose this number for the example. The real force depends on the kind of rubber, but all the stresses we will compute are proportional to it. The band has the volume \(100\ \text{mm} \cdot 2\ \text{mm}^2 = 200\ \text{mm}^3\) and rubber has a density of at most \(0{,}91\) Mg/m³ (BOWER, Table 3.1, PDF p. 100), so the band weighs less than \(0{,}002\) N. We therefore neglect its weight. As for the bar in section 2.1, the axial force in every cross section equals the force at the end, \(N = 10\) N.

How does the cross section change? We assume three things. First, rubber strongly resists a change of volume (BOWER §3.5, PDF p. 121). The ratio of the relative decrease of thickness to the relative increase of length is \(0{,}49\) for rubber (BOWER, Table 3.1, PDF p. 100), very close to the value \(0{,}5\) for a material that keeps its volume exactly (section 1.6). In the model we therefore assume that the band keeps its volume exactly. This is an idealization, real rubber changes its volume a little. Second, rubber responds in the same way in all directions across the band, so the width and the thickness decrease by the same factor \(k\). Third, the band is stretched equally everywhere. Close to the ends, where we hold it, this is not true, but far from them it almost is, by Saint-Venant's principle (section 2.2). We get the factor \(k\) from the volume, which is the area of the cross section times the length.

  1. The length increases by the factor \(\lambda\), the width and the thickness decrease by the factor \(k\), so the area of the cross section decreases by the factor \(k^2\) and the volume changes by the factor \(\lambda k^2\).
  2. The volume stays the same, so \(\lambda k^2 = 1\) and
    \[k = \frac{1}{\sqrt\lambda} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1{,}5}} = 0{,}8165 .\]
  3. After loading the band is \(2 \cdot 0{,}8165 = 1{,}633\) mm wide and \(0{,}8165\) mm thick, and the area of its cross section is
    \[A = k^2 A_0 = \frac{A_0}{\lambda} = \frac{2\ \text{mm}^2}{1{,}5} = 1{,}333\ \text{mm}^2 .\]

The thickness of the band has thus decreased by \(1 - 0{,}8165 = 0{,}1835\) of its value, and the length has increased by \(0{,}5\) of its value. The ratio is \(0{,}1835/0{,}5 = 0{,}367\), not \(0{,}5\). The number \(0{,}5\) of section 1.6 holds only for small stretches. At \(\lambda = 1{,}001\) we would have \(k = 1/\sqrt{1{,}001} = 0{,}9995\), the thickness would decrease by about \(0{,}0005\) of its value, and the ratio would be \(0{,}4996\), almost \(0{,}5\). The closer \(\lambda\) is to \(1\), the closer the ratio is to \(0{,}5\). For a larger stretch the ratio is smaller, because \(k = 1/\sqrt\lambda\) decreases more and more slowly as \(\lambda\) grows. The number \(0{,}49\) for rubber also holds only for small stretches, since BOWER gives it for a model in which the strain is proportional to the stress and which works only for small shape changes (BOWER §3.2.2, PDF p. 97). So for the band we started from the volume, not from the ratio \(0{,}49\).

In section 2.2 we wrote the area \(A = A_0/\lambda\) as \(A/1{,}5 = 0{,}667\,A\), where \(A\) was the area before loading. Now we divide the axial force by both areas. A megapascal is a newton per square millimetre (section 2.2), so

\[\frac{N}{A_0} = \frac{10\ \text{N}}{2\ \text{mm}^2} = 5\ \text{MPa} , \qquad \frac{N}{A} = \frac{10\ \text{N}}{1{,}333\ \text{mm}^2} = 7{,}5\ \text{MPa} .\]

The second value is exactly \(1{,}5\) times the first, because the area after loading is \(\lambda = 1{,}5\) times smaller. Both numbers are a force per unit area, only per a different area (figure 2.232). In the figure we see the band before loading with the length \(L_0\) and the band after loading with the length \(1{,}5\,L_0\). The axial force \(N\) pulls it at both ends, and the axes \(x\) and \(z\) point along the band and upwards. On the right are the cross sections, seen along the band: the larger section \(A_0\) before loading and the smaller section \(A\) after it, with the axes \(y\) and \(z\). Next to the sections are their sides: 2 mm and 1 mm before loading, 1.633 mm and 0.8165 mm after it. Following BOWER, in section 2.2 we called the first quotient the nominal stress and the second the true stress.

Which of the two numbers does the traction vector describe? Definition 2.20 divides the force by the area of a patch in the body as it is under load, that is, by the area after loading. The traction vector on a cross section of the band after loading is therefore \(7{,}5\) MPa in the direction of the axis \(x\), and the component \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) is \(7{,}5\) MPa. On the faces along the band there are no forces, as for the bar in section 2.6.6, so all the other stress components are zero. The stress described by the traction vector and the components \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ab}}\) is called the Cauchy stress (Cauchyjeva napetost). CM stresses that it is measured per unit area of the body after loading (CM §4.4.1, PDF p. 193), and BOWER that it is the true stress of introductory strength of materials courses (BOWER, PDF p. 71).

For large deformations, then, every piece of a body has two shapes that we must keep apart: the shape before loading and the shape after it. CM calls the shape of the body to which the description of the deformation is referred the reference configuration (referenčna konfiguracija). Usually this is the shape before loading, but it can be any other known shape (CM §3.2.2, PDF p. 112). The shape after loading CM calls the current or deformed configuration (trenutna konfiguracija) (CM §3.2.1, PDF p. 111). We stay with the words before loading and after loading. A patch that has the area \(\Delta A_0\) and the unit normal \(\vek n_0\) before loading has the area \(\Delta A\) and the unit normal \(\vek n\) after loading. Quantities without an index refer to the shape after loading, like the area \(\Delta A\) and the normal \(\vek n\) in definition 2.20, where the patch was a piece of a cut in the loaded body.

Let us also write the deformation gradient of the band. A point at the distance \(x\) from the centre along the band moves to the distance \(1{,}5\,x\), and a point at the distance \(y\) from the axis of the band in the direction of the width moves to the distance \(0{,}8165\,y\). The same holds in the direction of the thickness. The displacement field of the band is therefore

\[u_{\mathrm{x}} = 0{,}5\,x , \qquad u_{\mathrm{y}} = -0{,}1835\,y , \qquad u_{\mathrm{z}} = -0{,}1835\,z ,\]

where \(-0{,}1835 = 0{,}8165 - 1\). The only nonzero partial derivatives are \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial x = 0{,}5\), \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial y = -0{,}1835\) and \(\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}/\partial z = -0{,}1835\), so

\[\mat F = \mat I + \mat H = \begin{bmatrix} 1{,}5 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0{,}8165 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0{,}8165 \end{bmatrix} .\]

The determinant of a diagonal matrix is the product of its diagonal elements, because in the six-term formula (2.70) only the term with the diagonal remains. So \(J = 1{,}5 \cdot 0{,}8165^2 = \lambda \cdot (1/\sqrt\lambda)^2 = 1\): the band keeps its volume, as we assumed. The field is linear, so the difference of the displacements of two points is exactly \(\mat H\) times the difference of their positions, with no remainder, and \(\mat F\) maps the vector of every fibre exactly to its vector after loading.

2.13.2 How a small face changes

For the band we got the area of the cross section after loading from the width and the thickness, and the normal of the cross section stayed \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), the direction of the axis \(x\) along the band. How do the area and the normal change in general? A first guess would be that the normal moves like a fibre, so that after loading it points in the direction of the vector \(\mat F\vek n_0\). The sheared cube shows that this is not so.

Let us return to the steel cube of section 1.6.2. Its edge is 10 mm, its bottom face is glued to the table, and we pushed its top face by 10 µm in the direction of the axis \(x\) (figure 2.233 marks the edge and the shift, the shift is strongly exaggerated). The axis \(z\) points vertically up. The displacement field is (1.17), \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = 0{,}001\,z\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\). The only nonzero partial derivative is \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial z = 0{,}001\), so \(\mat H\) has a single nonzero element, \(0{,}001\) in row \(x\) and column \(z\), and

\[\mat F = \mat I + \mat H = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0{,}001 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} .\]

Let us look at two faces of the cube (figure 2.233). The cube is glued to the table, before loading we draw it blackwhite, after loading orange, and the axes \(x\) and \(z\) point right and up. In the left picture we see the fibre along \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), its image \(\mat F\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), the normal \(\vek n_0\) and the normal \(\vek n\) of the top face. In the right picture we see the fibre along \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), its image \(\mat F\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), the normal \(\vek n_0\) and the normal \(\vek n\) of the right face.

  1. The top face has the outward normal \(\vek n_0 = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) before loading. All its points move by the same vector, by 10 µm in the direction of the axis \(x\), so it stays horizontal, and its normal after loading is again \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\). The vector \(\mat F\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), however, is the third column of the matrix \(\mat F\), that is \(0{,}001\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} + \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\). It leans in the direction of the push, like the vertical edge \(AD\), which is a fibre in the direction \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) and after loading runs from \(A\) to \(D_1\) (we name the corners as in figure 1.95).
  2. The right face has the outward normal \(\vek n_0 = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) before loading. A fibre in the direction \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) does not change, because the first column of the matrix \(\mat F\) is just \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), so \(\mat F\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). The face, however, tilts, because its point at the height \(z\) moves by \(0{,}001\,z\) in the direction of the axis \(x\). It tilts like the right vertical edge \(BC\), which lies in it and after loading runs from \(B\) to \(C_1\). The normal after loading is therefore no longer \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) but leans downwards, perpendicular to the tilted edge.

On the top face, then, the fibre along the normal tilted and the normal did not, and on the right face it was just the other way round: the fibre stayed and the normal tilted. The normal does not move like a fibre. Why? A fibre along the normal does not lie in the face. The face is made of the fibres that lie in it, like the edge \(BC\) in the right face, and the normal after loading is the unit vector perpendicular to these fibres after loading. So we will describe a face by two fibres that lie in it.

As in section 2.5, we take for the patch a small square or, more generally, a parallelogram spanned in the face by the vectors \(\vek a\) and \(\vek b\) of two short fibres that are not parallel (figure 2.234). Before loading the patch is spanned by the fibre \(\vek a\) and the fibre \(\vek b\), and its normal is \(\vek n_0\). After loading it is spanned by \(\mat F\vek a\) and \(\mat F\vek b\), its area is \(\Delta A\) and its normal \(\vek n\). By item 4 of lemma 2.34 on the properties of the vector product, the vector \(\vek a\times\vek b\) is perpendicular to \(\vek a\) and to \(\vek b\), and its length is the area of the parallelogram (section 2.8.1). So

\[\vek a\times\vek b = \Delta A_0\,\vek n_0 ,\]

where \(\Delta A_0\) is the area of the patch before loading and \(\vek n_0\) its unit normal. We choose the order of the fibres so that \(\vek a\times\vek b\) points to the desired side of the face, for example out of the part of the body we are looking at. After loading the fibres become approximately \(\mat F\vek a\) and \(\mat F\vek b\) by lemma 1.114 on a short fibre after loading, and the patch becomes approximately the parallelogram spanned by these two vectors. Its area \(\Delta A\) and its unit normal \(\vek n\) are therefore determined by

\[(\mat F\vek a)\times(\mat F\vek b) = \Delta A\,\vek n .\]

Here we assumed that the patch after loading is a parallelogram. Let us say why we may, when it holds and what it costs. For a linear displacement field, such as the fields of the band and of the sheared cube, this is exact, because \(\mat F\) then maps the vector of every fibre exactly to its vector after loading. For other fields the true patch differs from the parallelogram: its edges are curved, and its corners lie a little off the tips of the vectors \(\mat F\vek a\) and \(\mat F\vek b\), by the remainders of theorem 1.89 on the change of a field over a short step (the figure exaggerates the difference strongly). By lemma 1.114 the remainders are as small compared with the side of the patch as we wish, if only the patch is small enough. Take an example with a made-up field \(u_{\mathrm{z}} = a\,x^2\), \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = u_{\mathrm{y}} = 0\), where \(a = 0{,}001\) per millimetre. A fibre from the origin along the axis \(x\) with the length \(h\) has after loading the vector \((h, 0, a\,h^2)\). At the origin \(\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}/\partial x = 2a \cdot 0 = 0\), so \(\mat F = \mat I\) there, and \(\mat F\) gives the vector \((h, 0, 0)\). The remainder \(a\,h^2\) is \(a\,h\) times the length of the fibre: a thousandth for \(h = 1\) mm, a ten-thousandth for \(h = 0{,}1\) mm. The assumption holds where the displacement field is continuously differentiable, as the lemma requires. Where it is not, for example at a crack, there is no deformation gradient and the assumption fails. The assumption does not affect Nanson's formula below, since we prove the formula for the two parallelograms, and for them it holds exactly. It only affects how well the parallelogram describes the true patch. This is a simplification. To show that even then the ratio of the areas approaches the ratio of the areas of the parallelograms as the patch shrinks, we would need the area of a curved surface, which we have not defined yet. CM computes in the same way as we do, with small parallelograms (CM (3.3.22)-(3.3.24), PDF p. 127).

We must now express the vector \((\mat F\vek a)\times(\mat F\vek b)\) through the vector \(\vek a\times\vek b\). The idea is this. We add a third short fibre \(\vek c\) that does not lie in the face (figure 2.234). The fibre \(\vek c\) together with \(\vek a\) and \(\vek b\) spans the parallelepiped before loading, and after loading \(\mat F\vek c\) with \(\mat F\vek a\) and \(\mat F\vek b\) spans the parallelepiped after loading. The base of the parallelepiped is our patch. Its volume is the area of the base times the height, and the height is the projection of the fibre \(\vek c\) onto the normal of the patch, \(\vek c\cdot\vek n_0\) (lemma 2.69 on the volume of a parallelepiped). If \(\vek c\) points to the side into which \(\vek n_0\) points, the volume before loading is therefore \(\Delta A_0\,(\vek n_0\cdot\vek c) = (\vek a\times\vek b)\cdot\vek c\), after loading it is \(\big((\mat F\vek a)\times(\mat F\vek b)\big)\cdot(\mat F\vek c)\), and by item 1 of lemma 2.77 on the change of volume the second is \(J\) times the first. The figure is drawn for \(J = 1{,}32\). This holds for every fibre \(\vek c\), and that is enough to determine the vector \((\mat F\vek a)\times(\mat F\vek b)\).

For the computation we need two tools. The first moves a matrix from one vector of a scalar product to the other.

Let \(\mat M\) be a \(3\times 3\) matrix and \(\vek c\) and \(\vek v\) vectors, the indices \(i\) and \(j\) run over \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\).

The component \(i\) of \(\mat M\vek c\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} M_{\mathrm{ij}}\,c_{\mathrm{j}}\) (definition 1.97), and by definition 2.8 of the scalar product

\[(\mat M\vek c)\cdot\vek v = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \Big(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} M_{\mathrm{ij}}\,c_{\mathrm{j}}\Big) v_{\mathrm{i}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{j}} \Big(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} M_{\mathrm{ij}}\,v_{\mathrm{i}}\Big) = \vek c\cdot(\mat M^\T\vek v) . \tag{2.137}\]

In the middle step we only added the same nine products \(M_{\mathrm{ij}}\,c_{\mathrm{j}}\,v_{\mathrm{i}}\) in a different order.

The sum \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} M_{\mathrm{ij}}\,v_{\mathrm{i}}\) is the component \(j\) of \(\mat M^\T\vek v\) (definition 1.101).

The second tool is the inverse of the matrix \(\mat F^\T\). Since \(\det\mat F = J \neq 0\), \(\mat F\) has an inverse \(\mat F^{-1}\) (theorem 2.76).

By lemma 1.123 \(\mat F^\T\) also has an inverse, namely \((\mat F^{-1})^\T\). As in section 1.17.7 we write it \(\mat F^{-\T}\).

Lemma 2.118 Nanson's formula (Nansonova formula)

Let \(\mat F\) be a real \(3\times 3\) matrix with the determinant \(J = \det\mat F \neq 0\). Then for any two vectors \(\vek a\) and \(\vek b\)

\[(\mat F\vek a)\times(\mat F\vek b) = J\,\mat F^{-\T}\,(\vek a\times\vek b) . \tag{2.138}\]

Let \(\mat F\) be the deformation gradient at a point of a body with \(J > 0\), and let short non-parallel fibres with the vectors \(\vek a\) and \(\vek b\) span before loading a parallelogram with the area \(\Delta A_0\) and the unit normal \(\vek n_0\), so that \(\vek a\times\vek b = \Delta A_0\,\vek n_0\). Let the vectors \(\mat F\vek a\) and \(\mat F\vek b\) span a parallelogram with the area \(\Delta A\) and the unit normal \(\vek n\), so that \((\mat F\vek a)\times(\mat F\vek b) = \Delta A\,\vek n\). Then

\[\vek n\,\Delta A = J\,\mat F^{-\T}\,\vek n_0\,\Delta A_0 . \tag{2.139}\]
Proof

We must show the equality (2.138), from which (2.139) follows by substitution. We use an arbitrary third vector \(\vek c\) and determinants, which are volumes of parallelepipeds with a sign (lemma 2.69). We show every step on figure 2.234.

  1. Let \(\vek c\) be any vector. We write \(\det(\vek c, \vek a, \vek b)\) for the determinant of the matrix with the columns \(\vek c\), \(\vek a\) and \(\vek b\). By definition 2.72 of the determinant, \(\det(\vek c, \vek a, \vek b) = \vek c\cdot(\vek a\times\vek b)\). In the figure this is the volume of the parallelepiped before loading, the area of the patch times the height \(\vek c\cdot\vek n_0\).
  2. The same holds for the parallelepiped after loading, spanned by \(\mat F\vek c\), \(\mat F\vek a\) and \(\mat F\vek b\). By the same definition
    \[\det(\mat F\vek c, \mat F\vek a, \mat F\vek b) = (\mat F\vek c)\cdot\big((\mat F\vek a)\times(\mat F\vek b)\big) .\]
  3. By item 1 of lemma 2.77 on the change of volume, \(\det(\mat F\vek c, \mat F\vek a, \mat F\vek b) = \det\mat F\,\det(\vek c, \vek a, \vek b) = J\,\det(\vek c, \vek a, \vek b)\): the volume of the parallelepiped after loading is \(J\) times the volume of the parallelepiped before it. Write \(\vek v = (\mat F\vek a)\times(\mat F\vek b)\). This is the vector that is perpendicular to the patch after loading and points along \(\vek n\). From steps 1 and 2
    \[(\mat F\vek c)\cdot\vek v = J\,\vek c\cdot(\vek a\times\vek b) .\]
  4. We rewrite the left side by (2.137), \((\mat F\vek c)\cdot\vek v = \vek c\cdot(\mat F^\T\vek v)\). By the rules for the scalar product (lemma 2.12) therefore
    \[\vek c\cdot\big(\mat F^\T\vek v - J\,(\vek a\times\vek b)\big) = 0 .\]
  5. This holds for every vector \(\vek c\): we may turn the third fibre in any direction, and the patch stays the same. For \(\vek c = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) it says that the component \(x\) of the vector \(\mat F^\T\vek v - J\,(\vek a\times\vek b)\) is zero, and for \(\vek c = \vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\vek c = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) the same for the components \(y\) and \(z\). So \(\mat F^\T\vek v = J\,(\vek a\times\vek b)\).
  6. We multiply both sides by \(\mat F^{-\T}\), the inverse of the matrix \(\mat F^\T\), as lemma 1.123 on the inverse of the transposed matrix says. On the left \(\mat F^{-\T}(\mat F^\T\vek v) = (\mat F^{-\T}\mat F^\T)\,\vek v = \mat I\vek v = \vek v\) remains, by item 1 of lemma 1.100 on the properties of the matrix product and by lemma 1.109 on multiplication by the identity matrix, on the right \(J\,\mat F^{-\T}(\vek a\times\vek b)\), because the number \(J\) may be moved in front of the matrix (lemma 1.98 on the linearity of the product with a matrix). This is (2.138): we have expressed the vector \(\vek v\), perpendicular to the patch after loading, through the vector \(\vek a\times\vek b\), perpendicular to the patch before it.
  7. Into (2.138) we substitute \(\vek a\times\vek b = \Delta A_0\,\vek n_0\) and \((\mat F\vek a)\times(\mat F\vek b) = \Delta A\,\vek n\) and move the number \(\Delta A_0\) in front of the matrix by the same lemma. The first equality describes the patch before loading, the second the patch after it. We get (2.139).

Source: CM (3.3.25) (PDF p. 127), BOWER §2.2.4 (PDF p. 72). The proof with a third vector is not from the sources, CM leaves it to Problem 3.16.

Equation (2.139) is called Nanson's formula (CM, PDF p. 194). It says two things. The area of the patch is multiplied in the deformation by the length of the vector \(J\,\mat F^{-\T}\vek n_0\), and after loading the normal points in the direction of the vector \(\mat F^{-\T}\vek n_0\), not in the direction \(\mat F\vek n_0\). In figure 2.234 the vector \(\mat F\vek n_0\) is drawn grey and dashed from the same point as \(\vek n\). It has stayed vertical while the patch has tilted, so it is no longer perpendicular to the patch. Why precisely \(\mat F^{-\T}\)? The normal must be perpendicular to every fibre of the face after loading. Let \(\vek a\) be a fibre in the face, so \(\vek n_0\cdot\vek a = 0\). After loading this fibre is \(\mat F\vek a\), and by (2.137) with the matrix \(\mat M = \mat F^{-\T}\), whose transpose is \(\mat F^{-1}\),

\[(\mat F^{-\T}\vek n_0)\cdot(\mat F\vek a) = \vek n_0\cdot(\mat F^{-1}\mat F\vek a) = \vek n_0\cdot\vek a = 0 .\]

The matrix \(\mat F^{-\T}\) thus exactly undoes the effect of the matrix \(\mat F\) on the fibres of the face, and the vector \(\mat F^{-\T}\vek n_0\) stays perpendicular to them. The sign is right too. If a fibre \(\vek c\) points to the side of the face into which \(\vek n_0\) points, then \(\vek n_0\cdot\vek c > 0\). By step 3 of the proof, \(\Delta A\,\vek n\cdot(\mat F\vek c) = J\,\Delta A_0\,\vek n_0\cdot\vek c > 0\), because \(J > 0\), so the fibre \(\mat F\vek c\) points to the side into which \(\vek n\) points. The outward normal stays outward.

Let us test the formula. For the band (figure 2.232), \(\mat F\) has on its diagonal \(1{,}5\) along the band and \(0{,}8165\) in both transverse directions. Its inverse \(\mat F^{-1}\) is the diagonal matrix with the reciprocals of this diagonal, \(1/1{,}5 = 0{,}6667\) and \(1/0{,}8165 = 1{,}2247\), because the product of two diagonal matrices multiplies the corresponding diagonal elements and gives \(\mat I\). Since it is diagonal, \(\mat F^{-\T} = \mat F^{-1}\). For the cross section \(\vek n_0 = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(J = 1\), so

\[\vek n\,\Delta A = 1 \cdot \mat F^{-\T}\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\,\Delta A_0 = 0{,}6667\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\,\Delta A_0 .\]

The normal stays \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and the area decreases to \(\Delta A_0/1{,}5\), as we computed from the width and the thickness.

For the sheared cube \(J = \det\mat F = 1\), because \(\mat F\) has only zeros below the diagonal and in the six-term formula (2.70) only the product of the diagonal remains. The inverse is \(\mat F^{-1} = \mat I - \mat H\), because \((\mat I + \mat H)(\mat I - \mat H) = \mat I - \mat H\mat H = \mat I\) and likewise \((\mat I - \mat H)(\mat I + \mat H) = \mat I - \mat H\mat H = \mat I\). The product \(\mat H\mat H\) is the zero matrix: the only nonzero element of \(\mat H\) is in row \(x\) and column \(z\), and row \(z\) is zero. So \(\mat F^{-\T} = \mat I - \mat H^\T\), with the element \(-0{,}001\) in row \(z\) and column \(x\).

  1. For the top face \(\vek n_0 = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), and \(\mat F^{-\T}\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) is the third column of the matrix \(\mat F^{-\T}\), just \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\). The normal stays \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), the area does not change. This agrees with figure 2.233.
  2. For the right face \(\vek n_0 = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(\mat F^{-\T}\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}} - 0{,}001\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), the first column of the matrix \(\mat F^{-\T}\). The normal leans downwards by a thousandth, strongly exaggerated in the figure, perpendicular to the tilted edge \(BC\), and the area is multiplied by the length of this vector, \(\sqrt{1 + 0{,}001^2} = 1{,}0000005\). The right face is spanned by a fibre in the direction \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\), which does not change, and by the edge \(BC\), which like the edge \(AD\) gets longer by \(5 \cdot 10^{-7}\) of its length (the table in section 1.6.2). So the area does increase by the factor \(1{,}0000005\).

2.13.3 The Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensors

Now we can divide the force that acts across a patch after loading by the area of the patch before loading. Take a patch that has the area \(\Delta A_0\) and the unit normal \(\vek n_0\) before loading and the area \(\Delta A\) and the unit normal \(\vek n\) after loading (figure 2.235). On the left is the piece of the body before loading with the patch \(\Delta A_0\) and its normal \(\vek n_0\). On it we have drawn the force \(\Delta\vek F\), unchanged, and the force \(\mat F^{-1}\Delta\vek F\). On the right is the piece after loading with the patch \(\Delta A\), the normal \(\vek n\) and the force \(\Delta\vek F\) acting across the patch.

  1. The part of the body on the side into which the normal \(\vek n\) points acts on the patch with the force \(\Delta\vek F\). By definition 2.20, for a small patch \(\Delta\vek F \approx \vek t(\vek n)\,\Delta A\), where \(\vek t(\vek n)\) is the traction vector after loading.
  2. By Cauchy's formula in the form (2.27), the component \(b\) of the vector \(\vek t(\vek n)\) is \(t_{\mathrm{b}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xb}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yb}}\,n_{\mathrm{y}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zb}}\,n_{\mathrm{z}}\). Since \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ab}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{ba}}\), this is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{bx}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{by}}\,n_{\mathrm{y}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{bz}}\,n_{\mathrm{z}}\), row \(b\) of the matrix \(\ten\sigma\) times the vector \(\vek n\) (definition 1.97). So \(\vek t(\vek n) = \ten\sigma\,\vek n\), which is (2.54).
  3. We substitute this into step 1. The number \(\Delta A\) may be moved to the vector \(\vek n\) (lemma 1.98 on the linearity of the product with a matrix), so \(\Delta\vek F \approx \ten\sigma\,(\vek n\,\Delta A)\).
  4. By Nanson's formula (2.139), \(\vek n\,\Delta A = J\,\mat F^{-\T}\vek n_0\,\Delta A_0\). The formula expresses the normal and the area of the patch after loading by the normal and the area of the patch before loading. When we substitute it and multiply the matrices in the same order (items 1 and 2 of lemma 1.100 on the properties of the matrix product), we get \(\Delta\vek F \approx J\,\ten\sigma\,\mat F^{-\T}\,\vek n_0\,\Delta A_0\).
  5. We divide by the area before loading:
    \[\frac{\Delta\vek F}{\Delta A_0} \approx J\,\ten\sigma\,\mat F^{-\T}\,\vek n_0 . \tag{2.140}\]

The left side is the force after loading per unit area before loading. The right side does not depend on the size of the patch, only on its normal before loading, and it depends on it through the matrix \(J\,\ten\sigma\,\mat F^{-\T}\), just as the traction vector depends on the normal \(\vek n\) through the matrix \(\ten\sigma\). The sign \(\approx\) has two sources. The first is step 1: the traction vector is a limit, and for a patch of finite size \(\Delta\vek F/\Delta A\) is an average over the patch. The second is step 4, where we used assumption 2 from the start of section 2.13: the patch is small, so we describe the patch after loading with the deformation gradient \(\mat F\) at the point, as the parallelogram of the vectors \(\mat F\vek a\) and \(\mat F\vek b\). For a linear displacement field and a stress that is the same everywhere, as in the band, both steps are exact and (2.140) holds with an equals sign. Otherwise \(\mat F\) and \(\ten\sigma\) differ from point to point of the patch, and the equation holds the better the smaller the patch is: under assumption 2 the left side tends to the right side as the patch shrinks. So as the definition of \(\vek t_0\) we take the right side, which does not depend on the size of the patch. We denote the right side by \(\vek t_0(\vek n_0)\). The index 0 says that the force is referred to the area before loading and that \(\vek n_0\) is the normal before loading. The force \(\Delta\vek F\) itself, however, is the real force after loading and points in the direction in which it points after loading. CM calls the vector \(\vek t_0\) the pseudo stress vector (psevdo vektor napetosti) (CM (4.4.2)-(4.4.4), PDF pp. 193-194).

How do we get nine components from this? For the Cauchy stress, row \(a\) of the matrix \(\ten\sigma\) is the traction vector on the face with the normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{a}}\) (definition 2.27). We do the same here.

  1. For \(\vek n_0 = \vek e_{\mathrm{a}}\) we have \(\vek t_0(\vek e_{\mathrm{a}}) = J\,\ten\sigma\,\mat F^{-\T}\vek e_{\mathrm{a}}\). A matrix times \(\vek e_{\mathrm{a}}\) gives its column \(a\), so this is column \(a\) of the matrix \(J\,\ten\sigma\,\mat F^{-\T}\).
  2. Column \(a\) of a matrix is row \(a\) of its transpose (definition 1.101). By item 3 of lemma 1.102 on transposing a product, used twice,
    \[(J\,\ten\sigma\,\mat F^{-\T})^\T = J\,(\mat F^{-\T})^\T\,\ten\sigma^\T = J\,\mat F^{-1}\,\ten\sigma ,\]
    because \(\ten\sigma^\T = \ten\sigma\) and because transposing twice gives back the original matrix, \((\mat F^{-\T})^\T = \mat F^{-1}\).

Row \(a\) of the matrix \(J\,\mat F^{-1}\ten\sigma\) is therefore the force after loading per unit area before loading on the face that had the normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{a}}\) before loading. We give this matrix a name.

Definition 2.119 First Piola-Kirchhoff stress (prvi Piola-Kirchhoffov tenzor napetosti)

Let \(\ten\sigma\) be the symmetric stress matrix after loading at a point of a body (definition 2.48), \(\mat F\) the deformation gradient and \(J = \det\mat F > 0\). The first Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor (prvi Piola-Kirchhoffov tenzor napetosti) at this point is the matrix

\[\mat P = J\,\mat F^{-1}\,\ten\sigma . \tag{2.141}\]

Its element \(P_{\mathrm{ab}}\) is the component \(b\) of the force after loading per unit area before loading on the face that had the unit normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{a}}\) before loading (section 2.13.3).

Source: CM §4.4.2, (4.4.8) (PDF pp. 193-194), BOWER §2.2.4 (PDF pp. 71-72).

The bold \(\mat P\) is a matrix, not a point. Since \(\mat P^\T = J\,\ten\sigma\,\mat F^{-\T}\), we can write (2.140) as \(\vek t_0(\vek n_0) = \mat P^\T\vek n_0\), with the components

\[t_{0\mathrm{b}}(\vek n_0) = P_{\mathrm{xb}}\,n_{0\mathrm{x}} + P_{\mathrm{yb}}\,n_{0\mathrm{y}} + P_{\mathrm{zb}}\,n_{0\mathrm{z}} .\]

This is the same form as Cauchy's formula (2.27): the first index names the face, the second the component of the force. Here, however, the order of the indices really matters, because \(\mat P\) is in general not symmetric (CM, PDF p. 194). We will see this on the turned band in section 2.13.5. BOWER defines the same matrix by the same product \(J\,\mat F^{-1}\ten\sigma\) and calls it the nominal stress (nominalna napetost), but denotes it by the letter \(S\), which we use for another tensor (BOWER, PDF p. 71). CM, however, writes the transposed matrix, \(\mat P = J\,\ten\sigma\cdot\mat F^{-\T}\) (CM (4.4.8), PDF p. 194). CM also points out that \(\mat P\) is a two-point tensor (dvotočkovni tenzor), which connects a point of the body before loading with the same point after loading (CM, PDF p. 194). In our notation the first index refers to the face before loading and the second to the direction of the force after loading. In CM's notation \(\mathbf P = P_{\mathrm{iI}}\,\hat{\mathbf e}_{\mathrm{i}}\,\hat{\mathbf E}_{\mathrm{I}}\) (CM (4.4.5), PDF p. 194) it is the other way round.

In the lectures the first Piola-Kirchhoff tensor is written as \(J\,\ten\sigma\,\mat F^{-\T}\). This is the transpose of our \(\mat P = J\,\mat F^{-1}\ten\sigma\), since \(\ten\sigma\) is symmetric: there the first index gives the direction of the force, here the face. So there \(\ten\sigma = \mat P\,\mat F^\T/J\) and \(\mat S = \mat F^{-1}\mat P\), while here \(\ten\sigma = \mat F\,\mat P/J\) and \(\mat S = \mat P\,\mat F^{-\T}\). The tensor \(\mat S\) is the same in both.

Is \(\mat P\) really a tensor in the sense of definition 2.56? Let us turn the axes with an orthogonal matrix \(\mat Q\), the same for both shapes of the body. The stress components change to \(\bar{\ten\sigma} = \mat Q\,\ten\sigma\,\mat Q^\T\) (theorem 2.53), and the deformation gradient to \(\bar{\mat F} = \mat I + \bar{\mat H} = \mat Q\,\mat F\,\mat Q^\T\), because by lemma 2.54 on the displacement gradient in turned axes \(\bar{\mat H} = \mat Q\,\mat H\,\mat Q^\T\), and \(\mat Q\,\mat I\,\mat Q^\T = \mat Q\mat Q^\T = \mat I\). The determinant does not change, because by theorem 2.74 on the determinant of a product \(\det\bar{\mat F} = \det\mat Q\,\det\mat F\,\det\mat Q^\T = \det\mat F\), since \(\det\mat Q\,\det\mat Q^\T = \det(\mat Q\mat Q^\T) = 1\). The inverse of the matrix \(\bar{\mat F}\) is \(\mat Q\,\mat F^{-1}\mat Q^\T\), because \(\mat Q\mat F\mat Q^\T\mat Q\mat F^{-1}\mat Q^\T = \mat Q\mat F\mat F^{-1}\mat Q^\T = \mat I\) and \(\mat Q\mat F^{-1}\mat Q^\T\mat Q\mat F\mat Q^\T = \mat Q\mat F^{-1}\mat F\mat Q^\T = \mat I\). Therefore

\[\bar{\mat P} = J\,\mat Q\,\mat F^{-1}\mat Q^\T\,\mat Q\,\ten\sigma\,\mat Q^\T = \mat Q\,(J\,\mat F^{-1}\ten\sigma)\,\mat Q^\T = \mat Q\,\mat P\,\mat Q^\T ,\]

which is the rule of the definition of a tensor.

Let us test the definition on the band. There \(J = 1\), \(\mat F^{-1}\) has the diagonal \(0{,}6667\), \(1{,}2247\), \(1{,}2247\), the reciprocals of the factors \(1{,}5\) for the length of the band and \(0{,}8165\) for the sides of the cross section in figure 2.232, and the only nonzero component of the Cauchy stress is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 7{,}5\) MPa. The product \(\mat F^{-1}\ten\sigma\) multiplies row \(x\) of the matrix \(\ten\sigma\) by \(0{,}6667\), so the only nonzero component is

\[P_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0{,}6667 \cdot 7{,}5\ \text{MPa} = 5\ \text{MPa} = \frac{N}{A_0} .\]

This is the nominal stress. The stress in a bar of definition 2.6, the axial force \(N\) divided by the area of the cross section before loading \(A_0\), is therefore the component \(P_{\mathrm{xx}}\) of the first Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor, not a component of the Cauchy stress. CM also calls the first Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor the nominal stress tensor (CM, PDF p. 193). In section 2.6.6 we wrote \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma = N/A\) for the bar. Exactly speaking, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) is the force per area after loading, but for the steel bar the two areas differ by less than a tenth of a per cent, as we will compute in section 2.13.6, so the equation was accurate enough.

The force \(\Delta\vek F\) and the vector \(\vek t_0(\vek n_0)\) point in the direction after loading. Let us see on the band what this means (figure 2.236). In row (1) we only stretch the band before loading into the band after loading. The force \(\Delta\vek F\) on its end face points along the axis \(x\), where the normal of this face pointed already before loading, so here the direction does not matter. In row (2) we also turn the band, stretched just as much, as a rigid body into a new position. The force \(\Delta\vek F\) acting on it turns with it. With it \(\mat P\) changes, although the material is stretched no more than in row (1) (we will compute this in section 2.13.5). So books define one more measure, in which they carry the force back to the shape before loading in the same way as a fibre. A fibre with the vector \(\vek a\) before loading has the vector \(\mat F\vek a\) after loading. Conversely, a vector \(\vek v\) after loading belongs to the fibre with the vector \(\mat F^{-1}\vek v\) before loading, because \(\mat F\) maps it to \(\mat F\mat F^{-1}\vek v = \vek v\). We apply the same rule to the force. The vector \(\mat F^{-1}\Delta\vek F\) (the blue arrow in figure 2.235) is the force as it would be before loading if the deformation mapped it like a fibre (CM (4.4.9), PDF p. 194). This is a fictitious force, since no force acts on the body before loading (BOWER, PDF p. 73).

On the band the fictitious force is the blue arrow in row (1). It is \(1{,}5\) times shorter than the real force, because \(\mat F^{-1}\) multiplies the component \(x\) by \(0{,}6667\). In row (2) we get the same blue arrow on the band before loading. The deformation gradient of the turned band is \(\mat Q\mat F\), where \(\mat Q\) is the matrix of the turn, and the force is \(\mat Q\,\Delta\vek F\) (section 2.13.5). The inverse of the matrix \(\mat Q\mat F\) is \(\mat F^{-1}\mat Q^\T\), because \(\mat F^{-1}\mat Q^\T\mat Q\mat F = \mat I\). It first turns the turned force back and then shortens it, \(\mat F^{-1}\mat Q^\T\mat Q\,\Delta\vek F = \mat F^{-1}\Delta\vek F\). So the fictitious force does not notice the turn.

  1. By (2.140) the fictitious force per unit area before loading is
    \[\frac{\mat F^{-1}\Delta\vek F}{\Delta A_0} \approx J\,\mat F^{-1}\,\ten\sigma\,\mat F^{-\T}\,\vek n_0 .\]
Definition 2.120 Second Piola-Kirchhoff stress (drugi Piola-Kirchhoffov tenzor napetosti)

Let \(\ten\sigma\) be the symmetric stress matrix after loading at a point of a body (definition 2.48), \(\mat F\) the deformation gradient and \(J = \det\mat F > 0\). The second Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor (drugi Piola-Kirchhoffov tenzor napetosti) at this point is the matrix

\[\mat S = J\,\mat F^{-1}\,\ten\sigma\,\mat F^{-\T} . \tag{2.142}\]

Source: CM §4.4.3, (4.4.9)-(4.4.12) (PDF pp. 194-195), BOWER §2.2.4 (PDF pp. 71-73), where it is called the material stress (materialna napetost).

By step 8, \(\mat S\vek n_0\) is the fictitious force \(\mat F^{-1}\Delta\vek F\) per unit area before loading on the patch with the normal \(\vek n_0\) before loading. The three measures of stress are related.

Lemma 2.121 Relations between the stress measures (zveze med merami napetosti)

Let \(\ten\sigma\) be the symmetric stress matrix after loading at a point of a body, \(\mat F\) the deformation gradient with \(J = \det\mat F > 0\), \(\mat P\) the first and \(\mat S\) the second Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor. Then:

  1. \(\mat S\) is symmetric, \(\mat S^\T = \mat S\),
  2. \(\mat P = \mat S\,\mat F^\T\),
  3. \(\ten\sigma = \dfrac{1}{J}\,\mat F\,\mat P = \dfrac{1}{J}\,\mat F\,\mat S\,\mat F^\T\).
Proof

We show all three claims with the definitions 2.119 and 2.120 and the rules for the matrix product.

1. Symmetry.

  1. By item 2 of lemma 1.102 on transposing a product the number \(J\) stays in front of the transposed matrix, and by item 3, applied to a product of three matrices, \(\mat S^\T = J\,(\mat F^{-\T})^\T\,\ten\sigma^\T\,(\mat F^{-1})^\T\).
  2. Transposing twice gives back the original matrix, so \((\mat F^{-\T})^\T = \mat F^{-1}\). The matrix \((\mat F^{-1})^\T\) is \(\mat F^{-\T}\) by our notation, and \(\ten\sigma^\T = \ten\sigma\). So \(\mat S^\T = J\,\mat F^{-1}\,\ten\sigma\,\mat F^{-\T} = \mat S\).

2. The first tensor from the second.

  1. By the definition, \(\mat S\,\mat F^\T = J\,\mat F^{-1}\,\ten\sigma\,\mat F^{-\T}\,\mat F^\T\).
  2. The matrix \(\mat F^{-\T}\) is the inverse of the matrix \(\mat F^\T\), as lemma 1.123 on the inverse of the transposed matrix says, so \(\mat F^{-\T}\mat F^\T = \mat I\), and \(J\,\mat F^{-1}\ten\sigma = \mat P\) remains.

3. The Cauchy stress.

  1. We multiply \(\mat P = J\,\mat F^{-1}\ten\sigma\) from the left by \(\mat F\). Since \(\mat F\mat F^{-1} = \mat I\), we get \(\mat F\,\mat P = J\,\ten\sigma\), and since \(J > 0\), \(\ten\sigma = \mat F\,\mat P/J\).
  2. By item 2, \(\mat F\,\mat P = \mat F\,\mat S\,\mat F^\T\), so also \(\ten\sigma = \mat F\,\mat S\,\mat F^\T/J\).

Source: CM (4.4.8), (4.4.11), (4.4.12) (PDF pp. 194-195) in CM's notation, where \(\mat P\) is transposed, BOWER (PDF pp. 71-72).

For the band \(\mat F^{-\T} = \mat F^{-1}\), and the product \(\mat F^{-1}\ten\sigma\,\mat F^{-1}\) multiplies \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) twice by \(0{,}6667\). The only nonzero component of the second Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor is therefore

\[S_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0{,}6667 \cdot 7{,}5\ \text{MPa} \cdot 0{,}6667 = 3{,}333\ \text{MPa} = \frac{N}{\lambda A_0} .\]

The fictitious force is \(\lambda\) times shorter than the real one, because \(\mat F^{-1}\) shortens every vector in the direction of the axis \(x\) by the factor \(1{,}5\) (in figure 2.236 the blue force \(\mat F^{-1}\Delta\vek F\) is shorter than the force \(\Delta\vek F\) by this factor), and it is divided by the area before loading. Item 2 of the lemma checks out: \(S_{\mathrm{xx}}\,F_{\mathrm{xx}} = 3{,}333 \cdot 1{,}5 = 5 = P_{\mathrm{xx}}\).

Why such a fictitious force? By item 1 of lemma 2.121 \(\mat S\) is symmetric, while \(\mat P\) in general is not, as we will see on the turned band. In section 2.13.5 we will also show that \(\mat S\) does not change when the deformed body is merely turned as a rigid body, just as the blue force in row (2) of figure 2.236 did not change. The Green-Lagrange strain tensor \(\mat E = \tfrac12(\mat F^\T\mat F - \mat I)\) of definition 1.118 does not change under such a turn either. A turn with an orthogonal matrix \(\mat Q\) changes \(\mat F\) into \(\mat Q\mat F\), and \((\mat Q\mat F)^\T\mat Q\mat F = \mat F^\T\mat Q^\T\mat Q\,\mat F = \mat F^\T\mat F\). A law that ties \(\mat S\) to \(\mat E\) therefore describes only the material and not how the piece is turned in space. This is why for large rotations books write the laws of a material with the pair \(\mat S\) and \(\mat E\) (BOWER §3.4, PDF pp. 118-119). Why precisely these two belong together, BOWER shows with the work that the stresses do on a unit of volume before loading (BOWER §2.4.2, PDF pp. 83-84). In chapter 3 we will compute the work of deformation only for small strains and small rotations, where the differences between the stress measures do not matter (section 2.13.6). To separate the rotation from the stretch, however, we need a new theorem.

2.13.4 Stretch and rotation

The deformation gradient describes two things at once: how much the fibres stretch and how they turn. In section 1.17.6 we turned a square plate as a whole about the axis \(z\) by the angle \(\varphi\), with the numbers \(c = 0{,}6\) and \(s = 0{,}8\) (the legs \(3\) and \(4\) divided by the hypotenuse \(5\)), for which \(c^2 + s^2 = 1\). The displacement field \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = -0{,}4\,x - 0{,}8\,y\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = 0{,}8\,x - 0{,}4\,y\), \(u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\) turns every fibre in the plane \(xy\) without changing its length, but its deformation gradient

\[\mat F = \begin{bmatrix} 0{,}6 & -0{,}8 & 0 \\ 0{,}8 & 0{,}6 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\]

is far from the identity matrix. In every deformation we would like to separate the stretch from the rotation. How?

The length of a fibre after loading is determined by the matrix \(\mat F^\T\mat F\). By (2.137) with \(\mat M = \mat F\), \(\vek c = \vek a\) and \(\vek v = \mat F\vek a\), the square of the length of a fibre after loading is

\[|\mat F\vek a|^2 = (\mat F\vek a)\cdot(\mat F\vek a) = \vek a\cdot(\mat F^\T\mat F\,\vek a) .\]

A rotation does not change lengths (the fibre \(OA_1\) has the length \(5\), like the fibre \(OA\)), and by this equation the matrix \(\mat F^\T\mat F\) tells only the lengths. So it does not see the rotation, it sees only the stretch. The element in row \(k\) and column \(l\) of the matrix \(\mat F^\T\mat F\) is row \(k\) of the matrix \(\mat F^\T\) times column \(l\) of the matrix \(\mat F\), that is, the scalar product of the columns \(k\) and \(l\) of the matrix \(\mat F\). For the rigid rotation above, therefore, \(\mat F^\T\mat F = \mat I\), because the first two columns, the turned edges \(OA_1 = (3, 4, 0)\) and \(OC_1 = (-4, 3, 0)\) of the plate divided by \(5\), have the length \(\sqrt{0{,}36 + 0{,}64} = 1\) and are perpendicular, \(0{,}6 \cdot (-0{,}8) + 0{,}8 \cdot 0{,}6 = 0\), and the third column is \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), a unit vector perpendicular to both. The matrix \(\mat F^\T\mat F\) is symmetric, so by theorem 2.83 on the principal directions of a symmetric matrix it has three mutually perpendicular eigenvectors. From them we will build a matrix \(\mat U\) that only stretches the fibres along these three directions, and we will show that the rest, \(\mat R = \mat F\mat U^{-1}\), does not change lengths.

Figure 2.237 shows the idea, in the plane. At the top left is a small square with edges along the eigenvectors \(\vek n_1\) and \(\vek n_2\) of the matrix \(\mat F^\T\mat F\). The matrix \(\mat U\) maps it to a rectangle with the edges \(\lambda_1\vek n_1\) and \(\lambda_2\vek n_2\) along the same directions: next to the square before the stretch, grey dashed, we see that one edge is longer and the other shorter. The matrix \(\mat R\) turns this rectangle from its position before the rotation to the final position, orange. The edges \(\mat F\vek n_1\) and \(\mat F\vek n_2\) therefore have exactly the lengths \(\lambda_1\) and \(\lambda_2\). The whole map is \(\mat F = \mat R\,\mat U\). The bottom row shows how we build the matrix \(\mat U\). A diagonal matrix can stretch along the axes: the matrix \(\mat\Lambda\) with the diagonal \(\lambda_1\), \(\lambda_2\) maps the square with the edges \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) to the rectangle with the edges \(\lambda_1\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(\lambda_2\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\). So we first turn the square with the matrix \(\mat Q\), so that the stretch directions lie on the axes, stretch it there and turn it back with the matrix \(\mat Q^\T\): \(\mat U = \mat Q^\T\mat\Lambda\,\mat Q\). The proof follows these arrows.

Theorem 2.122 Polar decomposition (polarni razcep)

Let \(\mat F\) be a real \(3\times 3\) matrix with \(\det\mat F > 0\). Then there is exactly one pair of matrices \(\mat R\) and \(\mat U\) such that

\[\mat F = \mat R\,\mat U , \tag{2.143}\]

where \(\mat R\) is an orthogonal matrix with \(\det\mat R = 1\) and \(\mat U\) is a symmetric matrix whose eigenvalues are all positive. Every eigenvector of the matrix \(\mat F^\T\mat F\) with the eigenvalue \(\mu\) is an eigenvector of the matrix \(\mat U\) with the eigenvalue \(\sqrt\mu\), and these are all the eigenvalues of the matrix \(\mat U\). The matrix \(\mat U\) is the only symmetric matrix with positive eigenvalues for which \(\mat U^2 = \mat F^\T\mat F\).

Proof

We must show that such a pair exists, that there is only one, that \(\mat U\) has the stated eigenvectors and eigenvalues, and that \(\mat U\) is the only such matrix whose square is \(\mat F^\T\mat F\). The indices \(k\) and \(l\) in the proof count eigenvectors and run over \(1\), \(2\), \(3\). We denote the matrix \(\mat F^\T\mat F\) by \(\mat C\).

1. Existence.

  1. The matrix \(\mat C\) is symmetric. By items 1 and 3 of lemma 1.102 on transposing a product, \(\mat C^\T = \mat F^\T(\mat F^\T)^\T = \mat F^\T\mat F = \mat C\), because transposing twice gives back the original matrix.
  2. By theorem 2.83 on the principal directions of a symmetric matrix there are mutually perpendicular unit vectors \(\vek n_1\), \(\vek n_2\), \(\vek n_3\) and real numbers \(\mu_1\), \(\mu_2\), \(\mu_3\) such that \(\mat C\vek n_{\mathrm{k}} = \mu_{\mathrm{k}}\,\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\).
  3. Every number \(\mu_{\mathrm{k}}\) is positive. The vector \(\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\) is a unit vector, so \(\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\vek n_{\mathrm{k}} = 1\), and by (2.137)
    \[\mu_{\mathrm{k}} = \vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot(\mu_{\mathrm{k}}\,\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}) = \vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot(\mat F^\T\mat F\,\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}) = (\mat F\vek n_{\mathrm{k}})\cdot(\mat F\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}) = |\mat F\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}|^2 .\]
    So the number \(\mu_{\mathrm{k}}\) is the square of the length of the fibre \(\mat F\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\) after loading. The vector \(\mat F\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\) is not the zero vector, because \(\det\mat F \neq 0\) and \(\vek n_{\mathrm{k}} \neq \vek 0\) (lemma 2.75 on the zero determinant). So \(\mu_{\mathrm{k}} > 0\). Here the proof uses the assumption on the determinant for the first time: for \(\det\mat F = 0\) a fibre could be squeezed to a point, \(\mu_{\mathrm{k}}\) would be \(0\) and there would be no division by \(\lambda_{\mathrm{k}}\) in step 11.
  4. By theorem 1.37 on the existence of square roots, \(\lambda_{\mathrm{k}} = \sqrt{\mu_{\mathrm{k}}}\) is a positive number with \(\lambda_{\mathrm{k}}^2 = \mu_{\mathrm{k}}\), the ratio by which the fibre along \(\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\) stretches.
  5. Let \(\mat Q\) be the matrix whose rows are \(\vek n_1^\T\), \(\vek n_2^\T\) and \(\vek n_3^\T\). The element in row \(k\) and column \(l\) of the matrix \(\mat Q\mat Q^\T\) is the scalar product \(\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\vek n_{\mathrm{l}}\), which is \(1\) for \(k = l\) and \(0\) otherwise, so \(\mat Q\mat Q^\T = \mat I\). By theorem 2.74 on the determinant of a product and lemma 2.73 on the properties of the determinant, \((\det\mat Q)^2 = \det\mat Q\,\det\mat Q^\T = \det\mat I = 1\), so \(\mat Q\) has an inverse (theorem 2.76). Multiplying the equation \(\mat Q\mat Q^\T = \mat I\) from the left by \(\mat Q^{-1}\) we get \(\mat Q^\T = \mat Q^{-1}\), and therefore \(\mat Q^\T\mat Q = \mat I\). The matrix \(\mat Q\) is orthogonal (definition 2.46).
  6. The components of the vector \(\mat Q\vek n_1\) are the scalar products \(\vek n_1\cdot\vek n_1 = 1\), \(\vek n_2\cdot\vek n_1 = 0\) and \(\vek n_3\cdot\vek n_1 = 0\), so \(\mat Q\vek n_1 = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). In the same way \(\mat Q\vek n_2 = \vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\mat Q\vek n_3 = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\). Conversely, \(\mat Q^\T\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = \vek n_1\), \(\mat Q^\T\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} = \vek n_2\) and \(\mat Q^\T\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = \vek n_3\), because the columns of the matrix \(\mat Q^\T\) are the rows of the matrix \(\mat Q\).
  7. Let \(\mat\Lambda\) be the diagonal matrix with the diagonal \(\lambda_1\), \(\lambda_2\), \(\lambda_3\) and \(\mat U = \mat Q^\T\mat\Lambda\,\mat Q\). By items 3 and 1 of the lemma on transposing a product, \(\mat U^\T = \mat Q^\T\mat\Lambda^\T(\mat Q^\T)^\T = \mat Q^\T\mat\Lambda^\T\mat Q = \mat U\), because a diagonal matrix equals its transpose. The matrix \(\mat U\) is symmetric.
  8. By step 6, \(\mat U\vek n_1 = \mat Q^\T\mat\Lambda\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = \mat Q^\T(\lambda_1\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = \lambda_1\vek n_1\), and in the same way \(\mat U\vek n_2 = \lambda_2\vek n_2\) and \(\mat U\vek n_3 = \lambda_3\vek n_3\). The vectors \(\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\) are eigenvectors of the matrix \(\mat U\) with the eigenvalues \(\lambda_{\mathrm{k}}\): the rectangle with the edges \(\lambda_1\vek n_1\) and \(\lambda_2\vek n_2\) lies along the edges of the square.
  9. The matrix \(\mat U\) has no other eigenvalues. Let \(\mat U\vek v = \alpha\vek v\) with \(\vek v \neq \vek 0\), and let \(\vek w = \mat Q\vek v\). The vector \(\vek w\) is not the zero vector, because \(\vek w = \vek 0\) would give \(\vek v = \mat Q^\T\vek w = \vek 0\). From \(\mat Q\mat U = \mat Q\mat Q^\T\mat\Lambda\,\mat Q = \mat\Lambda\,\mat Q\) it follows that \(\mat\Lambda\vek w = \mat Q\mat U\vek v = \alpha\,\vek w\). The components of this equation are \(\lambda_1 w_{\mathrm{x}} = \alpha w_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\lambda_2 w_{\mathrm{y}} = \alpha w_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\lambda_3 w_{\mathrm{z}} = \alpha w_{\mathrm{z}}\). At least one component of the vector \(\vek w\) is not zero, so \(\alpha\) equals one of the numbers \(\lambda_{\mathrm{k}}\). All the eigenvalues of the matrix \(\mat U\) are therefore positive.
  10. \(\mat U^2 = \mat C\). The element in row \(k\) and column \(l\) of the matrix \(\mat Q\mat C\mat Q^\T\) is \(\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot(\mat C\vek n_{\mathrm{l}}) = \mu_{\mathrm{l}}\,\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\vek n_{\mathrm{l}}\), which is \(\mu_{\mathrm{k}}\) for \(k = l\) and \(0\) otherwise. So \(\mat Q\mat C\mat Q^\T = \mat\Lambda^2\), the diagonal matrix with the diagonal \(\mu_1\), \(\mu_2\), \(\mu_3\). Multiplying this equation from the left by \(\mat Q^\T\) and from the right by \(\mat Q\), we get by step 5 \(\mat C = \mat Q^\T\mat\Lambda^2\mat Q\). On the other hand, also by step 5, \(\mat U^2 = \mat Q^\T\mat\Lambda\,\mat Q\mat Q^\T\mat\Lambda\,\mat Q = \mat Q^\T\mat\Lambda^2\mat Q\).
  11. Let the diagonal matrix \(\mat\Lambda^{-1}\) have the diagonal \(1/\lambda_1\), \(1/\lambda_2\), \(1/\lambda_3\). The matrix \(\mat Q^\T\mat\Lambda^{-1}\mat Q\) is the inverse of the matrix \(\mat U\), because \(\mat U\,\mat Q^\T\mat\Lambda^{-1}\mat Q = \mat Q^\T\mat\Lambda\,\mat\Lambda^{-1}\mat Q = \mat Q^\T\mat Q = \mat I\), and the same in the reverse order. \(\mat U^{-1}\) is symmetric too, for the same reason as in step 7.
  12. Let \(\mat R = \mat F\,\mat U^{-1}\). Then \(\mat R\,\mat U = \mat F\,\mat U^{-1}\mat U = \mat F\).
  13. By item 3 of the lemma on transposing a product, the symmetry of the matrix \(\mat U^{-1}\) and step 10,
    \[\mat R^\T\mat R = (\mat U^{-1})^\T\mat F^\T\mat F\,\mat U^{-1} = \mat U^{-1}\,\mat U^2\,\mat U^{-1} = \mat I .\]
    So the matrix \(\mat R\) does not change lengths, which we compute after the theorem.
  14. By the theorem on the determinant of a product and the lemma on the properties of the determinant, \(\det\mat C = \det\mat F^\T\det\mat F = (\det\mat F)^2\). From \(\mat C = \mat Q^\T\mat\Lambda^2\mat Q\) and \(\det\mat Q^\T\det\mat Q = 1\) (step 5), \(\det\mat C = \det\mat\Lambda^2 = \mu_1\mu_2\mu_3\), because the determinant of a diagonal matrix is the product of its diagonal. In the same way \(\det\mat U = \lambda_1\lambda_2\lambda_3\). This number is positive, and its square is \(\mu_1\mu_2\mu_3 = (\det\mat F)^2\). The number \(\det\mat F\) is also positive with this square, so by the uniqueness in the theorem on the existence of square roots \(\det\mat U = \det\mat F\). From \(\det\mat F = \det\mat R\,\det\mat U\) it follows that \(\det\mat R = 1\): the matrix \(\mat R\) is a rotation, not a reflection.
  15. Since \(\det\mat R \neq 0\), the matrix \(\mat R\) has an inverse (theorem 2.76). Multiplying the equation \(\mat R^\T\mat R = \mat I\) from the right by \(\mat R^{-1}\) we get \(\mat R^\T = \mat R^{-1}\), and therefore \(\mat R\mat R^\T = \mat I\). Together with step 13, \(\mat R\) is orthogonal.

2. The eigenvectors of the matrix \(\mat C\).

  1. Let \(\mat C\vek m = \mu\,\vek m\) with \(\vek m \neq \vek 0\). By step 3 of item 1, repeated for \(\vek m/|\vek m|\), \(\mu > 0\). Let \(\alpha = \sqrt\mu\).
  2. Let \(\vek w = \mat Q\vek m\). By step 5 of item 1, \(\vek m = \mat Q^\T\vek w = w_{\mathrm{x}}\vek n_1 + w_{\mathrm{y}}\vek n_2 + w_{\mathrm{z}}\vek n_3\), because a matrix times a vector is the sum of its columns multiplied by the components of the vector.
  3. On the left side of the equation \(\mat C\vek m = \alpha^2\vek m\) we have, by lemma 1.98 on the linearity of the product with a matrix and by step 2 of item 1, \(w_{\mathrm{x}}\mu_1\vek n_1 + w_{\mathrm{y}}\mu_2\vek n_2 + w_{\mathrm{z}}\mu_3\vek n_3\), on the right \(\alpha^2 w_{\mathrm{x}}\vek n_1 + \alpha^2 w_{\mathrm{y}}\vek n_2 + \alpha^2 w_{\mathrm{z}}\vek n_3\). The scalar product of both sides with \(\vek n_1\) gives \(w_{\mathrm{x}}\mu_1 = \alpha^2 w_{\mathrm{x}}\), because \(\vek n_1\) is perpendicular to \(\vek n_2\) and \(\vek n_3\). In the same way the scalar products with \(\vek n_2\) and with \(\vek n_3\) give \(w_{\mathrm{y}}\mu_2 = \alpha^2 w_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(w_{\mathrm{z}}\mu_3 = \alpha^2 w_{\mathrm{z}}\).
  4. If \(w_{\mathrm{x}} \neq 0\), then \(\mu_1 = \alpha^2\), and since \(\lambda_1\) and \(\alpha\) are positive, \(\lambda_1 = \alpha\) by the uniqueness of square roots. So the equality \(w_{\mathrm{x}}\lambda_1 = w_{\mathrm{x}}\alpha\) holds in both cases, for \(w_{\mathrm{x}} \neq 0\) and for \(w_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\). In the same way \(w_{\mathrm{y}}\lambda_2 = w_{\mathrm{y}}\alpha\) and \(w_{\mathrm{z}}\lambda_3 = w_{\mathrm{z}}\alpha\).
  5. By step 8 of item 1 and by step 4,
    \[\mat U\vek m = w_{\mathrm{x}}\lambda_1\vek n_1 + w_{\mathrm{y}}\lambda_2\vek n_2 + w_{\mathrm{z}}\lambda_3\vek n_3 = \alpha\,(w_{\mathrm{x}}\vek n_1 + w_{\mathrm{y}}\vek n_2 + w_{\mathrm{z}}\vek n_3) = \alpha\,\vek m .\]
    The vector \(\vek m\) is an eigenvector of the matrix \(\mat U\) with the eigenvalue \(\alpha = \sqrt\mu\). That \(\mat U\) has no other eigenvalues we showed in step 9 of item 1.

3. Uniqueness.

  1. Let \(\mat F = \mat R'\mat U'\), where \(\mat R'\) is orthogonal with determinant \(1\) and \(\mat U'\) symmetric with positive eigenvalues. By item 3 of the lemma on transposing a product, \(\mat C = \mat U'^\T\mat R'^\T\mat R'\,\mat U' = \mat U'^2\), because \(\mat U'^\T = \mat U'\) and \(\mat R'^\T\mat R' = \mat I\).
  2. By the theorem on principal directions, \(\mat U'\) has mutually perpendicular unit eigenvectors \(\vek m_1\), \(\vek m_2\), \(\vek m_3\) with eigenvalues \(\alpha_1\), \(\alpha_2\), \(\alpha_3\), which are positive by assumption. So \(\mat C\vek m_{\mathrm{k}} = \mat U'(\mat U'\vek m_{\mathrm{k}}) = \alpha_{\mathrm{k}}^2\,\vek m_{\mathrm{k}}\).
  3. By item 2, \(\vek m_{\mathrm{k}}\) is an eigenvector of the matrix \(\mat U\) with the eigenvalue \(\sqrt{\alpha_{\mathrm{k}}^2} = \alpha_{\mathrm{k}}\), so \(\mat U\vek m_{\mathrm{k}} = \alpha_{\mathrm{k}}\vek m_{\mathrm{k}} = \mat U'\vek m_{\mathrm{k}}\) for \(k = 1, 2, 3\).
  4. Let \(\mat Q'\) be the matrix with the rows \(\vek m_1^\T\), \(\vek m_2^\T\), \(\vek m_3^\T\). By the same argument as in step 5 of item 1 it is orthogonal. The columns of the matrix \(\mat U\mat Q'^\T\) are \(\mat U\vek m_{\mathrm{k}}\), and the columns of the matrix \(\mat U'\mat Q'^\T\) are \(\mat U'\vek m_{\mathrm{k}}\), so by step 3 \(\mat U\mat Q'^\T = \mat U'\mat Q'^\T\). Multiplying both sides from the right by \(\mat Q'\) we get \(\mat U = \mat U'\), because \(\mat Q'^\T\mat Q' = \mat I\).
  5. Therefore also \(\mat R' = \mat F\,\mat U'^{-1} = \mat F\,\mat U^{-1} = \mat R\).
  6. Steps 2 to 4 used only that \(\mat U'\) is symmetric with positive eigenvalues and that \(\mat U'^2 = \mat C\). So \(\mat U\) is the only such matrix whose square is \(\mat C = \mat F^\T\mat F\), which is the last claim of the theorem.

Source: CM §3.9.2, (3.9.1), (3.9.9), (3.9.10) (PDF pp. 161-163), BOWER §2.1.13-2.1.14 (PDF pp. 54-56). The proof is not from the sources. CM only states the theorem and computes \(\mat U\) as in item 1.

Books call the matrix \(\mat U\) the right stretch tensor (desni tenzor raztezanja) and the matrix \(\mat R\) the rotation tensor (tenzor zasuka) (CM, PDF p. 161, BOWER §2.1.13, PDF p. 54). The eigenvalues \(\lambda_1\), \(\lambda_2\), \(\lambda_3\) of the matrix \(\mat U\) are the principal stretches (glavna razmerja dolžin, glavni raztegi) (CM, PDF p. 163), and we will call its eigenvectors \(\vek n_1\), \(\vek n_2\), \(\vek n_3\) the stretch directions (smeri raztezanja). What does the theorem say? The split \(\mat F\vek a = \mat R\,(\mat U\vek a)\) divides the deformation of a fibre into two steps (CM, PDF p. 162, BOWER, PDF pp. 55-56).

  1. The matrix \(\mat U\) stretches a fibre in the stretch direction \(\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\) by the factor \(\lambda_{\mathrm{k}}\) and does not turn it, because \(\mat U\vek n_{\mathrm{k}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{k}}\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\). A small cube with edges along the stretch directions becomes a box with edges along the same directions.
  2. The matrix \(\mat R\) then turns everything without changing any length. By (2.137) and \(\mat R^\T\mat R = \mat I\), indeed, \(|\mat R\vek v|^2 = \vek v\cdot(\mat R^\T\mat R\,\vek v) = \vek v\cdot\vek v = |\vek v|^2\) for every vector \(\vek v\).

Every deformation of a small piece of a body is thus a stretch in three mutually perpendicular directions followed by a rotation. CM also gives the split in the reverse order, first a rotation and then a stretch (CM (3.9.1), PDF p. 161). We will meet it only in the name of the left Cauchy-Green tensor in definition 2.123.

Let us test the theorem on the deformation that first stretches a square by the factor \(1{,}5\) in the direction of the axis \(x\) and then turns it like the rigid rotation above (figure 2.238). On the left is the square before loading with a fibre along the axis \(x\). The matrix \(\mat U\) maps it to a rectangle in which the fibre is stretched by the factor \(1{,}5\): the length \(1\) becomes \(1{,}5\), and the elongation is \(0{,}5\), measured from the dashed line where the square ended. The matrix \(\mat R\) turns the rectangle from its position before the rotation, grey dashed, by the angle \(\varphi\) of section 1.17.6 into the turned shape with the turned fibre. The whole map \(\mat F = \mat R\,\mat U\) leads straight from the square to the turned shape. Its deformation gradient is

\[\mat F = \begin{bmatrix} 0{,}9 & -0{,}8 & 0 \\ 1{,}2 & 0{,}6 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} ,\]

the first column of the rigid rotation multiplied by \(1{,}5\), the other two columns unchanged. The columns are the images of the edges of the square: the edge \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) is mapped to \((0{,}9;\ 1{,}2)\), the edge \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) to \((-0{,}8;\ 0{,}6)\). We cannot read this off the matrix itself. Let us compute the split by the steps of the proof.

  1. The element in row \(k\) and column \(l\) of the matrix \(\mat F^\T\mat F\) is, as we saw above, the scalar product of the columns \(k\) and \(l\) of the matrix \(\mat F\). The first column has the squared length \(0{,}81 + 1{,}44 = 2{,}25\), the second \(0{,}64 + 0{,}36 = 1\), the third \(1\). The scalar product of the first two is \(0{,}9 \cdot (-0{,}8) + 1{,}2 \cdot 0{,}6 = 0\), and the third column is perpendicular to both. So \(\mat F^\T\mat F\) is the diagonal matrix with the diagonal \(2{,}25\), \(1\), \(1\).
  2. The eigenvectors are \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), with the eigenvalues \(2{,}25\), \(1\), \(1\). The principal stretches are their roots, \(1{,}5\), \(1\) and \(1\).
  3. \(\mat U\) is the diagonal matrix with the diagonal \(1{,}5\), \(1\), \(1\), and \(\mat U^{-1}\) the diagonal matrix with the diagonal \(1/1{,}5\), \(1\), \(1\).
  4. \(\mat R = \mat F\,\mat U^{-1}\) divides the first column of the matrix \(\mat F\) by \(1{,}5\) and leaves the other two, and we get exactly the matrix of the rigid rotation, with the columns \(0{,}6\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} + 0{,}8\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(-0{,}8\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} + 0{,}6\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\). Its determinant is \(0{,}6 \cdot 0{,}6 - (-0{,}8) \cdot 0{,}8 = 1\).

The split found the stretch and the rotation from which we built the deformation. For the rigid rotation alone, however, \(\mat F^\T\mat F = \mat I\), so \(\mat U = \mat I\) and \(\mat R = \mat F\): there is no stretch. The small strain tensor of definition 1.121, on the other hand, has \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = c - 1 = -0{,}4\) for the same rotation, as we saw in section 1.17.6. The split separates the rotation from the stretch exactly, even for a large rotation.

The theorem requires \(\det\mat F > 0\). Let us see what happens without this assumption. The matrix \(\mat F = \mathrm{diag}(-1, 1, 1)\), a diagonal matrix with elements \(-1\), \(1\) and \(1\), reflects space in the plane of the axes \(y\) and \(z\) (figure 2.165, top, the first and the last shape), and its determinant is the product of the diagonal, \(\det\mat F = -1 < 0\). We claim that there is no decomposition \(\mat F = \mat R\,\mat U\) with an orthogonal \(\mat R\), \(\det\mat R = 1\), and a symmetric \(\mat U\) with positive eigenvalues.

  1. Suppose such a decomposition exists. Since \(\mat U\) is symmetric, by theorem 2.83 on principal directions it has three mutually perpendicular eigenvectors, and as in step 14 of item 1 of the proof, \(\det\mat U\) is the product of its eigenvalues.
  2. The eigenvalues are positive, so \(\det\mat U > 0\).
  3. By theorem 2.74 on the determinant of a product, \(\det\mat F = \det\mat R\,\det\mat U = 1 \cdot \det\mat U > 0\).
  4. This contradicts \(\det\mat F = -1\). So there is no decomposition with a rotation \(\mat R\).

A decomposition with an orthogonal matrix does exist, but it is not the one from the theorem. We take \(\mat U = \mat I\) and \(\mat R = \mat F\). The matrix \(\mat F\) is orthogonal, since it has the columns \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), which are unit vectors and mutually perpendicular, and \(\mat F = \mat F\,\mat I\). But it is not a rotation, since its determinant is \(-1\): before the reflection we get from the fibre along the axis \(x\) to the fibre along the axis \(y\) counterclockwise, after it clockwise, and a rotation does not swap this sense. The sign decides, not the size. For \(\mat F = \mathrm{diag}(-0{,}1;\ 0{,}1;\ 0{,}1)\) we have \(\det\mat F = -0{,}001 < 0\) and the proof of the contradiction is the same. Then \(\mat F^\T\mat F = \mathrm{diag}(0{,}01;\ 0{,}01;\ 0{,}01)\), \(\mat U = 0{,}1\,\mat I\) and \(\mat R = \mat F\,\mat U^{-1} = \mathrm{diag}(-1, 1, 1)\), again a reflection with \(\det\mat R = -1\). In the proof of the theorem the hypothesis \(\det\mat F > 0\) is used in step 14 of item 1: there \(\det\mat F > 0\) gives \(\det\mat U = \det\mat F\) and therefore \(\det\mat R = 1\).

The matrix \(\mat F^\T\mat F\), which we called \(\mat C\) in the proof, has a name of its own in the books, and beside it stands a second one with the order swapped, \(\mat F\mat F^\T\). We will need both for rubber (chapter 3), where the strain energy is written with numbers computed from them. What each of them measures we build in figure 2.239 with the deformation gradient of the example above, in the plane \(xy\).

On the left we draw all fibres of length \(1\) from one point before loading: their ends lie on a circle. Among them are the stretch direction \(\vek n_1 = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and the stretch direction \(\vek n_2 = \vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\), which the decomposition found, and an arbitrary fibre \(\vek a\). The matrix \(\mat F\) maps every fibre into the fibre after loading. For the stretch directions \(\mat F\vek n_{\mathrm{k}} = \mat R\,(\mat U\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}) = \lambda_{\mathrm{k}}\,\mat R\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\). We denote the turned stretch direction by \(\vek m_{\mathrm{k}} = \mat R\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\), for the turn of the example \(\vek m_1 = (0{,}6;\ 0{,}8)\) and \(\vek m_2 = (-0{,}8;\ 0{,}6)\). The fibre \(\vek n_1\) thus goes from its position before loading, gets longer by \(0{,}5\) and turns by the angle \(\varphi\) into \(1{,}5\,\vek m_1\), while the fibre \(\vek n_2\) only turns into \(1\,\vek m_2\). Where does an arbitrary fibre go? We write it with its angle \(\theta\) to \(\vek n_1\) as \(\vek a = \cos\theta\,\vek n_1 + \sin\theta\,\vek n_2\). By lemma 1.98 on the linearity of the product with a matrix,

\[\mat F\vek a = 1{,}5\cos\theta\,\vek m_1 + 1 \cdot \sin\theta\,\vek m_2 .\]

The vectors \(\vek m_1\) and \(\vek m_2\) are unit and perpendicular, so \(p = 1{,}5\cos\theta\) and \(q = \sin\theta\) are the components of the fibre \(\mat F\vek a\) along them, and they satisfy \((p/1{,}5)^2 + q^2 = \cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1\). This is the equation of an ellipse with the half-axes \(1{,}5\) and \(1\) along \(\vek m_1\) and \(\vek m_2\). Against the circle before loading, grey dashed, one sees which fibres got longer and which did not.

The matrix \(\mat F^\T\mat F\) lives in the left picture. It takes a fibre before loading and gives the square of its length after it, \(\vek a\cdot(\mat F^\T\mat F\,\vek a) = |\mat F\vek a|^2\), and its eigenvectors are the directions \(\vek n_1\) and \(\vek n_2\). The matrix \(\mat F\mat F^\T\) lives in the right picture: we will show that its eigenvectors are the half-axes of the ellipse \(\vek m_1\) and \(\vek m_2\). The eigenvalues of both are the same, the squares of the half-axes \(1{,}5^2 = 2{,}25\) and \(1^2 = 1\). Why the same? The two matrices differ only by the rotation \(\mat R\), which turns the ellipse, and a rotation changes no length, so it does not change the half-axes either.

Definition 2.123 Right and left Cauchy-Green tensors, principal stretches (desni in levi Cauchy-Greenov tenzor, glavni raztegi)

Let \(\mat F\) be the deformation gradient at a point of the body with \(\det\mat F > 0\). The right Cauchy-Green deformation tensor (desni Cauchy-Greenov deformacijski tenzor) \(\mat C\) and the left Cauchy-Green deformation tensor (levi Cauchy-Greenov deformacijski tenzor) or Finger tensor (Fingerjev tenzor) \(\mat B\) at this point are the matrices

\[\mat C = \mat F^\T\mat F , \qquad \mat B = \mat F\,\mat F^\T . \tag{2.144}\]

The principal stretches (glavna razmerja dolžin, glavni raztegi) \(\lambda_1\), \(\lambda_2\), \(\lambda_3\) are the eigenvalues of the right stretch tensor \(\mat U\) of the polar decomposition \(\mat F = \mat R\,\mat U\) (theorem 2.122).

Source: CM (3.4.3), (3.4.4) (PDF p. 128), principal stretches CM §3.9.2 (PDF p. 163), BOWER §2.1.12 (PDF p. 54) and §2.1.14 (PDF p. 56). CM and BOWER write the left tensor with the capital letter \(\mat B\).

Where do the names right and left come from? In the decomposition \(\mat F = \mat R\,\mat U\) the stretch \(\mat U\) stands to the right of the rotation, and \(\mat C = \mat U^2\) (theorem 2.122). Since \(\mat R^\T\mat R = \mat I\), the same matrix can also be written as \(\mat F = (\mat R\,\mat U\mat R^\T)\,\mat R\), first the turn and then the stretch along the turned directions. Here the stretch \(\mat R\,\mat U\mat R^\T\) stands to the left of the rotation, and its square is \(\mat R\,\mat U\mat R^\T\mat R\,\mat U\mat R^\T = \mat R\,\mat U^2\mat R^\T = \mat F\mat F^\T = \mat B\) (BOWER §2.1.12, PDF p. 54). In the lectures the left tensor is denoted by the small letter \(\mat b\), and the trace of a matrix by sl instead of \(\tr\).

Lemma 2.124 Eigenvalues and invariants of the Cauchy-Green tensors (lastne vrednosti in invariante Cauchy-Greenovih tenzorjev)

Let \(\mat F\) be a real \(3\times 3\) matrix with \(\det\mat F > 0\), \(\mat F = \mat R\,\mat U\) its polar decomposition of theorem 2.122, \(\lambda_1\), \(\lambda_2\), \(\lambda_3\) the principal stretches and \(\vek n_1\), \(\vek n_2\), \(\vek n_3\) pairwise perpendicular unit eigenvectors of the matrix \(\mat U\), \(\mat U\vek n_{\mathrm{k}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{k}}\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\). Let \(\mat C = \mat F^\T\mat F\) and \(\mat B = \mat F\mat F^\T\), and \(\vek m_{\mathrm{k}} = \mat R\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\). Then:

  1. \(\mat C = \mat U^2\) and \(\mat B = \mat R\,\mat C\,\mat R^\T\), and both matrices are symmetric.
  2. \(\mat F\vek n_{\mathrm{k}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{k}}\vek m_{\mathrm{k}}\), \(\mat C\vek n_{\mathrm{k}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{k}}^2\,\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(\mat B\vek m_{\mathrm{k}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{k}}^2\,\vek m_{\mathrm{k}}\), and the vectors \(\vek m_1\), \(\vek m_2\), \(\vek m_3\) are unit and pairwise perpendicular.
  3. For every number \(x\), \(\det(\mat C - x\mat I) = \det(\mat B - x\mat I) = (\lambda_1^2 - x)(\lambda_2^2 - x)(\lambda_3^2 - x)\). The matrices \(\mat C\) and \(\mat B\) therefore have the same eigenvalues, exactly the numbers \(\lambda_1^2\), \(\lambda_2^2\), \(\lambda_3^2\), and the same coefficients \(I_1\), \(I_2\), \(I_3\) of the characteristic polynomial of item 2 of lemma 2.86, the invariants:
    \[\begin{aligned} I_1 &= \lambda_1^2 + \lambda_2^2 + \lambda_3^2 , \\ I_2 &= \lambda_1^2\lambda_2^2 + \lambda_2^2\lambda_3^2 + \lambda_3^2\lambda_1^2 , \\ I_3 &= \lambda_1^2\lambda_2^2\lambda_3^2 . \end{aligned} \tag{2.145}\]
  4. \(J = \det\mat F = \lambda_1\lambda_2\lambda_3\) and \(I_3 = J^2\).
Proof

We have to show four items. The indices \(k\) and \(l\) run over \(1\), \(2\), \(3\). The steps are shown in figure 2.239, where the first two directions are drawn.

  1. By theorem 2.122, \(\mat U^2 = \mat F^\T\mat F = \mat C\). By item 3 of lemma 1.102 on transposing a product, and since \(\mat U^\T = \mat U\), \(\mat B = \mat R\,\mat U\,(\mat R\,\mat U)^\T = \mat R\,\mat U\mat U\,\mat R^\T = \mat R\,\mat C\,\mat R^\T\).
  2. By items 1 and 3 of the same lemma, \(\mat B^\T = (\mat F^\T)^\T\mat F^\T = \mat F\mat F^\T = \mat B\), and likewise \(\mat C^\T = \mat C\). Item 1 is proved.
  3. A fibre along a stretch direction is stretched and turned: \(\mat F\vek n_{\mathrm{k}} = \mat R\,(\mat U\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}) = \lambda_{\mathrm{k}}\,\mat R\vek n_{\mathrm{k}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{k}}\vek m_{\mathrm{k}}\), a half-axis of the ellipse. By lemma 1.98 on the linearity of the product with a matrix, \(\mat C\vek n_{\mathrm{k}} = \mat U(\mat U\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}) = \mat U(\lambda_{\mathrm{k}}\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}) = \lambda_{\mathrm{k}}^2\,\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\).
  4. A rotation changes neither lengths nor right angles. By (2.137) and \(\mat R^\T\mat R = \mat I\), \(\vek m_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\vek m_{\mathrm{l}} = (\mat R\vek n_{\mathrm{k}})\cdot(\mat R\vek n_{\mathrm{l}}) = \vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot(\mat R^\T\mat R\,\vek n_{\mathrm{l}}) = \vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\vek n_{\mathrm{l}}\), which is \(1\) for \(k = l\) and \(0\) otherwise. The vectors \(\vek m_{\mathrm{k}}\) are unit and pairwise perpendicular, like the half-axes in the figure.
  5. By steps 1 and 3, \(\mat B\vek m_{\mathrm{k}} = \mat R\,\mat C\,\mat R^\T\mat R\,\vek n_{\mathrm{k}} = \mat R\,\mat C\vek n_{\mathrm{k}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{k}}^2\,\mat R\vek n_{\mathrm{k}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{k}}^2\,\vek m_{\mathrm{k}}\). Item 2 is proved.
  6. By step 10 of part 1 of the proof of theorem 2.122, \(\mat C = \mat Q^\T\mat\Lambda^2\mat Q\), where \(\mat Q\) is the orthogonal matrix with the rows \(\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\) and \(\mat\Lambda^2\) the diagonal matrix with the diagonal \(\lambda_1^2\), \(\lambda_2^2\), \(\lambda_3^2\). Since \(\mat Q^\T(x\mat I)\mat Q = x\,\mat Q^\T\mat Q = x\mat I\), we have \(\mat C - x\mat I = \mat Q^\T(\mat\Lambda^2 - x\mat I)\,\mat Q\). By theorem 2.74 on the determinant of a product, and since \(\det\mat Q^\T\det\mat Q = \det\mat I = 1\) (step 5 of the same part),
    \[\det(\mat C - x\mat I) = \det(\mat\Lambda^2 - x\mat I) = (\lambda_1^2 - x)(\lambda_2^2 - x)(\lambda_3^2 - x) ,\]
    because the determinant of a diagonal matrix is the product of its diagonal.
  7. In the same way, by step 1 and \(\mat R\,\mat R^\T = \mat I\), the matrix \(\mat B - x\mat I = \mat R\,(\mat C - x\mat I)\,\mat R^\T\), and since \(\det\mat R = \det\mat R^\T = 1\) (lemma 2.73 on the properties of the determinant), \(\det(\mat B - x\mat I) = \det(\mat C - x\mat I)\). The characteristic polynomials are the same because the matrices differ only by the turn.
  8. By item 1 of lemma 2.86, the eigenvalues of both matrices are the numbers \(x\) at which this product is zero, so exactly \(\lambda_1^2\), \(\lambda_2^2\) and \(\lambda_3^2\).
  9. Multiplying out the brackets gives \(-x^3 + (\lambda_1^2 + \lambda_2^2 + \lambda_3^2)\,x^2 - (\lambda_1^2\lambda_2^2 + \lambda_2^2\lambda_3^2 + \lambda_3^2\lambda_1^2)\,x + \lambda_1^2\lambda_2^2\lambda_3^2\). By item 2 of lemma 2.86 the same polynomial equals \(-x^3 + I_1x^2 - I_2x + I_3\) for every \(x\). Polynomials that agree at all \(x\) have the same coefficients, as we showed in steps 5 and 6 of the proof of lemma 2.88. This is (2.145) and item 3.
  10. By step 14 of part 1 of the proof of theorem 2.122, \(\det\mat U = \lambda_1\lambda_2\lambda_3 = \det\mat F\). Hence \(J = \lambda_1\lambda_2\lambda_3\) and \(I_3 = \lambda_1^2\lambda_2^2\lambda_3^2 = J^2\). Item 4 is proved.

Source: CM §3.9.2 (PDF pp. 162-163): \(\mat C\) and \(\mat B\) in terms of \(\mat U\) and \(\mat R\), the eigenvalues of the matrix \(\mat C\) are the squares of the principal stretches, the determinant of the stretch is \(J\). BOWER §2.1.14 (PDF p. 56) and §3.5.2 (PDF p. 123): the invariants of the matrix \(\mat B\) and \(I_3 = J^2\). The proof is not from the sources.

The numbers \(I_1\), \(I_2\), \(I_3\) are invariants in the same sense as the stress invariants of definition 2.87: we compute them by the same formulas (2.87) from the elements of the matrix \(\mat C\) or \(\mat B\) in place of \(\ten\sigma\), and as we showed for that definition, \(I_1 = \tr\mat C\) and \(I_2 = \tfrac12\big((\tr\mat C)^2 - \tr(\mat C\mat C)\big)\). Let us test the lemma on the example of figure 2.239, with the principal stretches \(1{,}5\) and \(1\) in the plane and the third one \(1\) along the axis \(z\):

\[\mat C = \begin{bmatrix} 2{,}25 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} , \qquad \mat B = \begin{bmatrix} 1{,}45 & 0{,}6 & 0 \\ 0{,}6 & 1{,}8 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} .\]

The matrices differ because \(\mat C\) looks at the directions before loading, which are the axes here, and \(\mat B\) at the turned ones. The matrix \(\mat B\) times \(\vek m_1 = (0{,}6;\ 0{,}8;\ 0)\) gives \((1{,}45 \cdot 0{,}6 + 0{,}6 \cdot 0{,}8;\ 0{,}6 \cdot 0{,}6 + 1{,}8 \cdot 0{,}8;\ 0) = (1{,}35;\ 1{,}8;\ 0) = 2{,}25\,\vek m_1\): the half-axis of the ellipse is indeed its eigenvector with the eigenvalue \(1{,}5^2\). We compute the invariants three times:

computed \(I_1\) \(I_2\) \(I_3\)
from the principal stretches, (2.145) \(2{,}25 + 1 + 1 = 4{,}25\) \(2{,}25 + 1 + 2{,}25 = 5{,}5\) \(2{,}25 \cdot 1 \cdot 1 = 2{,}25\)
from the elements of \(\mat C\) \(2{,}25 + 1 + 1 = 4{,}25\) \(2{,}25 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 2{,}25 = 5{,}5\) \(2{,}25\)
from the elements of \(\mat B\) \(1{,}45 + 1{,}8 + 1 = 4{,}25\) \(1{,}45 \cdot 1{,}8 + 1{,}8 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 1{,}45 - 0{,}6^2 = 5{,}5\) \((1{,}45 \cdot 1{,}8 - 0{,}6^2) \cdot 1 = 2{,}25\)

All three rows agree, and \(J = 1{,}5 \cdot 1 \cdot 1 = 1{,}5\) with \(J^2 = 2{,}25 = I_3\). The number \(J\) is the ratio of volumes (lemma 2.77): a cube with edges \(1\) along the stretch directions becomes a cuboid with the edges \(\lambda_1\), \(\lambda_2\), \(\lambda_3\), in the plane the square becomes a rectangle with the sides \(\lambda_1\) and \(\lambda_2\), and the rotation does not change the volume. That is why \(J\) is the product of the principal stretches.

What have the invariants given us? They depend only on the lengths of the half-axes, not on where the half-axes point: if we turn the body differently after stretching it, or choose the axes differently, they stay the same. This is exactly what we will require of the energy of rubber, which is the same in all directions. The assumption \(\det\mat F > 0\) enters the lemma with the polar decomposition in step 1 and always holds for a real deformation (the first assumption of section 2.13). Without it item 4 fails: for the reflection \(\mat F = \mathrm{diag}(-1, 1, 1)\) above, \(\mat C = \mat B = \mat I\), the eigenvalues are \(1\), \(1\), \(1\) with the product of the roots \(1\), while the determinant is \(\det\mat F = -1\).

In this example the stretch directions were just the axes. In general they are not. Take again the sheared cube of section 1.6.2: a steel cube with the edge 10 mm, its bottom face glued to the table, whose top face we pushed by 10 µm in the direction of the axis \(x\). Its deformation gradient has a single off-diagonal element, \(0{,}001\) in row \(x\) and column \(z\). The matrix \(\mat F^\T\mat F\) therefore has the elements \(1\), \(1\) and \(1{,}000001\) on the diagonal and \(0{,}001\) in row \(x\), column \(z\) and in row \(z\), column \(x\). We computed the split with NumPy. The principal stretches are \(\lambda_1 = 1{,}0005\), \(\lambda_2 = 1\) and \(\lambda_3 = 0{,}9995\). The stretch direction \(\vek n_1\) with the stretch \(1{,}0005\) is \(0{,}70693\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} + 0{,}70728\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), almost the diagonal \(AC\) of the front face, whose unit vector is \(0{,}70711\,(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} + \vek e_{\mathrm{z}})\). The stretch direction \(\vek n_3\) with the stretch \(0{,}9995\) is perpendicular to it, almost the diagonal \(BD\), and the stretch direction with the stretch \(1\) is the axis \(y\). More precisely, the stretches are \(1{,}0005001\) and \(0{,}9995001\), so the strains in the stretch directions, \(\lambda_{\mathrm{k}} - 1\), are \(+5{,}001 \cdot 10^{-4}\) and \(-4{,}999 \cdot 10^{-4}\), to four places the same as the strains of the diagonals \(AC\) and \(BD\) in the table of section 1.6.2. The rotation tensor is

\[\mat R = \begin{bmatrix} c & 0 & s \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ -s & 0 & c \end{bmatrix} , \qquad s = 0{,}0005 , \quad c = 0{,}999999875 ,\]

rounded to the places shown, with \(c^2 + s^2 = 1\). The matrix \(\mat R\) maps the vertical unit vector \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) to its third column, \(s\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} + c\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), which leans in the direction of the push, by the angle \(0{,}029^\circ\). So the sheared cube stretches in the direction of the diagonal \(AC\), shrinks in the direction of the diagonal \(BD\), and then turns slightly as a whole about the axis \(y\) in the direction of the push (figure 2.240). On the left is the square of the face of the cube with the corners \(A\), \(B\), \(C\), \(D\), the axes \(x\) and \(z\) and the directions in which the matrix \(\mat U\) stretches and shrinks. The matrix \(\mat U\) maps it to the stretched shape with the directions after stretching: next to the dashed square before the stretch we see that the line along \(\vek n_1\) is longer, \(\lambda_1 > 1\), and the line along \(\vek n_3\) shorter, \(\lambda_3 < 1\). The matrix \(\mat R\) turns that shape from its position before the rotation by the angle that the arc shows into the sheared face of the cube with the turned directions. The book calls the deformation of the sheared cube of section 1.6.2 simple shear (CM, PDF pp. 121 and 123). Here we have taken it apart into a stretch and a rotation.

The numbers of the cube show one more thing. Let us write down what the stretch and the rotation of the cube add to the identity matrix, rounded to three places, with rows and columns in the order \(x\), \(y\), \(z\):

\[\mat U - \mat I = \begin{bmatrix} -1{,}25 \cdot 10^{-7} & 0 & 5 \cdot 10^{-4} \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 5 \cdot 10^{-4} & 0 & 3{,}75 \cdot 10^{-7} \end{bmatrix} , \qquad \mat R - \mat I = \begin{bmatrix} -1{,}25 \cdot 10^{-7} & 0 & 5 \cdot 10^{-4} \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ -5 \cdot 10^{-4} & 0 & -1{,}25 \cdot 10^{-7} \end{bmatrix} .\]

The matrix \(\mat U - \mat I\) has the elements \(5 \cdot 10^{-4}\) in row \(x\), column \(z\) and in row \(z\), column \(x\), and on the diagonal only \(-1{,}25 \cdot 10^{-7}\) and \(3{,}75 \cdot 10^{-7}\). This is almost exactly the small strain tensor of the cube, which has \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zx}} = \tfrac12 \cdot 0{,}001 = 5 \cdot 10^{-4}\) and zeros elsewhere. The matrix \(\mat R - \mat I\) has \(s = 5 \cdot 10^{-4}\) in row \(x\), column \(z\) and \(-5 \cdot 10^{-4}\) in row \(z\), column \(x\), and on the diagonal \(c - 1 = -1{,}25 \cdot 10^{-7}\) in rows \(x\) and \(z\). This is almost exactly the small rotation tensor of definition 1.122, \(\omega_{\mathrm{xz}} = \tfrac12\,(0{,}001 - 0) = 5 \cdot 10^{-4}\) and \(\omega_{\mathrm{zx}} = -5 \cdot 10^{-4}\). This is no coincidence. Let \(\ten\varepsilon = \tfrac12(\mat H + \mat H^\T)\) be the small strain tensor and \(\ten\omega = \tfrac12(\mat H - \mat H^\T)\) the small rotation tensor.

  1. We multiply out the brackets: \(\mat F^\T\mat F = (\mat I + \mat H^\T)(\mat I + \mat H) = \mat I + \mat H + \mat H^\T + \mat H^\T\mat H\).
  2. In the same way \((\mat I + \ten\varepsilon)^2 = \mat I + 2\ten\varepsilon + \ten\varepsilon^2 = \mat I + \mat H + \mat H^\T + \ten\varepsilon^2\).
  3. The two matrices differ only in the last term, \(\mat H^\T\mat H\) against \(\ten\varepsilon^2\). The elements of both are sums of products of two partial derivatives. For partial derivatives of the order \(0{,}001\) these products are of the order \(0{,}000001\), a thousand times smaller than the elements of the matrix \(\mat H\). By the rule of section 1.6.2 we may drop them next to the first-order terms. For the rigid rotation of section 1.17.6, where the partial derivatives are \(-0{,}4\) and \(0{,}8\), the products are not small and this step fails.
  4. By theorem 2.122 on the polar decomposition, \(\mat U\) is the only symmetric matrix with positive eigenvalues whose square is \(\mat F^\T\mat F\). The matrix \(\mat I + \ten\varepsilon\) is symmetric, has eigenvalues close to \(1\), and its square differs from \(\mat F^\T\mat F\) only by such products. So we expect \(\mat U \approx \mat I + \ten\varepsilon\), up to terms of the order of the square of the partial derivatives. The numbers of the cube confirm it, since such a term is exactly \(-1{,}25 \cdot 10^{-7} = -0{,}001^2/8\), and we prove an exact estimate below.
  5. Since \((\mat I + \ten\varepsilon)(\mat I - \ten\varepsilon) = \mat I - \ten\varepsilon^2\), we have \(\mat U^{-1} \approx \mat I - \ten\varepsilon\), and \(\mat R = \mat F\,\mat U^{-1} \approx (\mat I + \mat H)(\mat I - \ten\varepsilon) = \mat I + \mat H - \ten\varepsilon - \mat H\ten\varepsilon \approx \mat I + \ten\omega\), when we drop the product \(\mat H\ten\varepsilon\).

In steps 3 to 5 we dropped terms and only expected them to be small. How small are they? For the cube they were of the order \(0{,}001^2\), the square of the largest partial derivative. Let us show that this is so for every displacement gradient with small elements: if all partial derivatives of the displacement are at most \(m\) in absolute value, then \(\mat U\) differs from \(\mat I + \ten\varepsilon\), and \(\mat R\) from \(\mat I + \ten\omega\), in no element by more than a fixed number times \(m^2\).

The idea is this. Figure 2.241 shows it for two numbers. The square with the side \(g\) lies in the square with the side \(u\). The difference of the areas \(u^2 - g^2\) is the strip of the length \(u\) and the strip of the length \(g\), both of the width \(u - g\), so \(u^2 - g^2 = (u - g)(u + g)\) and

\[u - g = \frac{u^2 - g^2}{u + g} .\]

If \(u\) and \(g\) are close to \(1\), the difference of the numbers is therefore about half the difference of the squares. For matrices it is the same. The matrices \(\mat U\) and \(\mat G = \mat I + \ten\varepsilon\) are symmetric, and by steps 1 and 2 their squares differ only by products of partial derivatives, by the matrix \(\mat H^\T\mat H - \ten\varepsilon^2\). If we write the difference of the squares in the eigendirections of both matrices, the difference of the matrices is the difference of the squares divided by the sum of the eigenvalues, which is close to \(2\). Then we get the rotation from \(\mat R\,\mat U = \mat F\).

Lemma 2.125 Stretch and rotation for small partial derivatives (raztezanje in zasuk pri majhnih parcialnih odvodih)

Let \(\mat F = \mat I + \mat H\) be the deformation gradient at a point of a body with \(\det\mat F > 0\), \(\mat F = \mat R\,\mat U\) its polar decomposition from theorem 2.122, \(\ten\varepsilon = \tfrac12(\mat H + \mat H^\T)\) the small strain tensor and \(\ten\omega = \tfrac12(\mat H - \mat H^\T)\) the small rotation tensor. Let \(m\) be a number with \(0 < m \le \tfrac16\) such that \(|H_{\mathrm{ij}}| \le m\) for all indices \(i\) and \(j\). Then for all \(i\) and \(j\)

\[|U_{\mathrm{ij}} - I_{\mathrm{ij}} - \varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}| \le \frac{9\,m^2}{1 - 3m} \le 18\,m^2 , \qquad |R_{\mathrm{ij}} - I_{\mathrm{ij}} - \omega_{\mathrm{ij}}| \le 91\,m^2 . \tag{2.146}\]
Proof

We must show both estimates. The indices \(i\) and \(j\) run over \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\), while the indices \(k\) and \(l\) count eigenvectors and run over \(1\), \(2\) and \(3\).

1. Product with a unit vector.

  1. Let \(\mat A\) be a \(3 \times 3\) matrix whose elements are at most \(m\) in absolute value, and \(\vek v\) a unit vector. The vector \((|v_{\mathrm{x}}|, |v_{\mathrm{y}}|, |v_{\mathrm{z}}|)\) has the length \(|\vek v| = 1\), and the vector \((1, 1, 1)\) the length \(\sqrt3\). By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality (corollary 2.15) their scalar product is therefore \(|v_{\mathrm{x}}| + |v_{\mathrm{y}}| + |v_{\mathrm{z}}| \le \sqrt3\).
  2. The component \(i\) of the vector \(\mat A\vek v\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} A_{\mathrm{ij}}\,v_{\mathrm{j}}\). By the triangle inequality for numbers and the rule for the absolute value of a product (lemma 1.6) it is at most \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} |A_{\mathrm{ij}}|\,|v_{\mathrm{j}}| \le m\sqrt3\) in absolute value. So \(|\mat A\vek v|^2 \le 3 \cdot 3m^2 = 9m^2\), and by lemma 1.29, that a larger nonnegative number has a larger square, \(|\mat A\vek v| \le 3m\).
  3. The elements of the matrices \(\ten\varepsilon\) and \(\ten\omega\) are at most \(m\) in absolute value, since for instance \(|\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}| = \tfrac12\,|H_{\mathrm{ij}} + H_{\mathrm{ji}}| \le \tfrac12\,(m + m)\). So step 2 holds for \(\mat A = \mat H\) and for \(\mat A = \ten\varepsilon\).

2. Eigenvalues.

  1. The matrix \(\mat G = \mat I + \ten\varepsilon\) is symmetric. By theorem 2.83 on the principal directions of a symmetric matrix it has mutually perpendicular unit eigenvectors \(\vek w_1\), \(\vek w_2\), \(\vek w_3 = \vek w_1\times\vek w_2\) with eigenvalues \(\kappa_1\), \(\kappa_2\), \(\kappa_3\). The vector \(\vek w_{\mathrm{l}}\) is a unit vector, so \(\kappa_{\mathrm{l}} = \vek w_{\mathrm{l}}\cdot(\mat G\vek w_{\mathrm{l}}) = 1 + \vek w_{\mathrm{l}}\cdot(\ten\varepsilon\,\vek w_{\mathrm{l}})\). By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and step 2, \(|\vek w_{\mathrm{l}}\cdot(\ten\varepsilon\,\vek w_{\mathrm{l}})| \le |\ten\varepsilon\,\vek w_{\mathrm{l}}| \le 3m\), so \(\kappa_{\mathrm{l}} \ge 1 - 3m\).
  2. By the proof of theorem 2.122 on the polar decomposition (steps 2, 3, 4 and 8 of item 1) the matrix \(\mat U\) has mutually perpendicular unit eigenvectors \(\vek n_1\), \(\vek n_2\), \(\vek n_3 = \vek n_1\times\vek n_2\) with positive eigenvalues \(\lambda_1\), \(\lambda_2\), \(\lambda_3\), for which \(\lambda_{\mathrm{k}}^2 = |\mat F\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}|^2\).
  3. By item 3 of the triangle inequality for vectors (corollary 2.16), used for the vectors \(\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(-\mat H\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\), and by step 2, \(|\mat F\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}| = |\vek n_{\mathrm{k}} + \mat H\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}| \ge |\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}| - |\mat H\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}| \ge 1 - 3m\). The number \(1 - 3m\) is not smaller than \(\tfrac12\), since \(m \le \tfrac16\). Both numbers \(\lambda_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(1 - 3m\) are positive and \(\lambda_{\mathrm{k}}^2 \ge (1 - 3m)^2\), so by lemma 1.29 \(\lambda_{\mathrm{k}} \ge 1 - 3m\).
  4. Steps 4 and 6 give \(\lambda_{\mathrm{k}} + \kappa_{\mathrm{l}} \ge 2\,(1 - 3m) \ge 1\) for all \(k\) and \(l\).

3. Difference of the squares.

  1. By theorem 2.122, \(\mat U^2 = \mat F^\T\mat F\). As in steps 1 and 2 before the lemma, \(\mat F^\T\mat F = \mat I + \mat H + \mat H^\T + \mat H^\T\mat H\) and \(\mat G^2 = \mat I + \mat H + \mat H^\T + \ten\varepsilon^2\). The matrix \(\mat D = \mat U^2 - \mat G^2\) is therefore \(\mat H^\T\mat H - \ten\varepsilon^2\).
  2. The element \((i, j)\) of the matrix \(\mat H^\T\mat H\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} H_{\mathrm{ki}}\,H_{\mathrm{kj}}\), a sum of three products, each at most \(m^2\) in absolute value. The same holds for \(\ten\varepsilon^2\) by step 3. By the triangle inequality for numbers, therefore, \(|D_{\mathrm{ij}}| \le 3m^2 + 3m^2 = 6m^2\).

4. Difference of the matrices in the eigendirections.

  1. Let \(\mat X = \mat U - \mat G\). The matrix \(\mat U\) is symmetric, so by (2.79) \(\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot(\mat U\vek w_{\mathrm{l}}) = \vek w_{\mathrm{l}}\cdot(\mat U\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}) = \lambda_{\mathrm{k}}\,(\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\vek w_{\mathrm{l}})\). Moreover \(\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot(\mat G\vek w_{\mathrm{l}}) = \kappa_{\mathrm{l}}\,(\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\vek w_{\mathrm{l}})\). So \(\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot(\mat X\vek w_{\mathrm{l}}) = (\lambda_{\mathrm{k}} - \kappa_{\mathrm{l}})\,(\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\vek w_{\mathrm{l}})\).
  2. The matrix \(\mat U^2 = \mat F^\T\mat F\) is symmetric, and \(\mat U^2\vek n_{\mathrm{k}} = \mat U(\lambda_{\mathrm{k}}\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}) = \lambda_{\mathrm{k}}^2\,\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\) by lemma 1.98 on the linearity of the product with a matrix. In the same way \(\mat G^2\vek w_{\mathrm{l}} = \kappa_{\mathrm{l}}^2\,\vek w_{\mathrm{l}}\). As in step 10, therefore,
    \[\begin{aligned} \vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot(\mat D\vek w_{\mathrm{l}}) &= (\lambda_{\mathrm{k}}^2 - \kappa_{\mathrm{l}}^2)\,(\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\vek w_{\mathrm{l}}) = (\lambda_{\mathrm{k}} + \kappa_{\mathrm{l}})(\lambda_{\mathrm{k}} - \kappa_{\mathrm{l}})\,(\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\vek w_{\mathrm{l}}) \\ &= (\lambda_{\mathrm{k}} + \kappa_{\mathrm{l}})\,\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot(\mat X\vek w_{\mathrm{l}}) . \end{aligned}\]
    This is the equation \(u^2 - g^2 = (u + g)(u - g)\) of figure 2.241, with \(\lambda_{\mathrm{k}}\) in place of \(u\) and \(\kappa_{\mathrm{l}}\) in place of \(g\).
  3. The sum \(\lambda_{\mathrm{k}} + \kappa_{\mathrm{l}}\), the total length of the two strips, is by step 7 at least \(2\,(1 - 3m) > 0\), so
    \[\big(\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot(\mat X\vek w_{\mathrm{l}})\big)^2 \le \frac{\big(\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot(\mat D\vek w_{\mathrm{l}})\big)^2}{4\,(1 - 3m)^2} .\]

5. Sum of the squares of the elements.

  1. The vectors \(\vek n_1\), \(\vek n_2\), \(\vek n_3\) are the unit vectors of the axes of a turned coordinate system, as we saw after theorem 2.83 on the principal directions of a symmetric matrix. By lemma 1.21 on a vector as a sum of steps along the axes, used in these axes, and by lemma 2.45 on the components of a vector in turned axes, every vector is \(\vek a = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} (\vek a\cdot\vek n_{\mathrm{k}})\,\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\), and as in step 7 of the proof of corollary 2.85, \(|\vek a|^2 = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} (\vek a\cdot\vek n_{\mathrm{k}})^2\). The same holds for \(\vek w_1\), \(\vek w_2\), \(\vek w_3\).
  2. Let \(\mat M\) be a \(3 \times 3\) matrix. By step 13 for the vector \(\mat M\vek w_{\mathrm{l}}\), by (1.10), by (2.137) and by step 13 for the vector \(\mat M^\T\vek e_{\mathrm{i}}\),
    \[\begin{aligned} \sum_{\mathrm{k}}\sum_{\mathrm{l}} \big(\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot(\mat M\vek w_{\mathrm{l}})\big)^2 &= \sum_{\mathrm{l}} |\mat M\vek w_{\mathrm{l}}|^2 = \sum_{\mathrm{l}}\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \big(\vek e_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot(\mat M\vek w_{\mathrm{l}})\big)^2 \\ &= \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{l}} \big((\mat M^\T\vek e_{\mathrm{i}})\cdot\vek w_{\mathrm{l}}\big)^2 \\ &= \sum_{\mathrm{i}} |\mat M^\T\vek e_{\mathrm{i}}|^2 = \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} M_{\mathrm{ij}}^2 , \end{aligned}\]
    where at the end we used that the vector \(\mat M^\T\vek e_{\mathrm{i}}\) has the components \(M_{\mathrm{ix}}\), \(M_{\mathrm{iy}}\), \(M_{\mathrm{iz}}\), the row \(i\) of the matrix \(\mat M\).
  3. Steps 12, 14 and 9 give
    \[\sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} X_{\mathrm{ij}}^2 \le \frac{1}{4\,(1 - 3m)^2}\sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} D_{\mathrm{ij}}^2 \le \frac{9 \cdot 36\,m^4}{4\,(1 - 3m)^2} = \frac{81\,m^4}{(1 - 3m)^2} .\]
  4. Each square \(X_{\mathrm{ij}}^2\) is at most this sum, so by lemma 1.29 \(|X_{\mathrm{ij}}| \le 9m^2/(1 - 3m)\), and since \(1 - 3m \ge \tfrac12\), this is at most \(18m^2\). Since \(X_{\mathrm{ij}} = U_{\mathrm{ij}} - I_{\mathrm{ij}} - \varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\), the first estimate is proved.

6. Rotation.

  1. Let \(\mat Y = \mat R - \mat I - \ten\omega\). By lemma 1.104 on the split into symmetric and antisymmetric parts, \(\mat H = \ten\varepsilon + \ten\omega\), and by step 16, \(\mat U = \mat I + \ten\varepsilon + \mat X\). Since \(\mat R\,\mat U = \mat F = \mat I + \mat H\), the properties of the matrix product (lemma 1.100) give
    \[\begin{aligned} \mat Y\mat U &= \mat R\,\mat U - \mat U - \ten\omega\,\mat U \\ &= \mat I + \ten\varepsilon + \ten\omega - \mat I - \ten\varepsilon - \mat X - \ten\omega - \ten\omega\,(\ten\varepsilon + \mat X) = -\mat N , \end{aligned}\]
    where \(\mat N = \mat X + \ten\omega\,(\ten\varepsilon + \mat X)\).
  2. The elements of the matrix \(\mat N\). By step 16, \(|X_{\mathrm{ij}}| \le 18m^2\). The element \((i, j)\) of the matrix \(\ten\omega\,(\ten\varepsilon + \mat X)\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} \omega_{\mathrm{ik}}\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{kj}} + X_{\mathrm{kj}})\). Since \(\omega_{\mathrm{ii}} = \tfrac12(H_{\mathrm{ii}} - H_{\mathrm{ii}}) = 0\), the sum has only two nonzero terms. In each, \(|\omega_{\mathrm{ik}}| \le m\) and \(|\varepsilon_{\mathrm{kj}} + X_{\mathrm{kj}}| \le m + 18m^2 \le 4m\), since \(18m^2 \le 3m\) for \(m \le \tfrac16\). So \(|N_{\mathrm{ij}}| \le 18m^2 + 2 \cdot 4m^2 = 26m^2\).
  3. Choose a row \(i\). Let \(\vek q\) be the vector with the components \(Y_{\mathrm{ix}}\), \(Y_{\mathrm{iy}}\), \(Y_{\mathrm{iz}}\) and \(\vek p\) the vector with the components \(N_{\mathrm{ix}}\), \(N_{\mathrm{iy}}\), \(N_{\mathrm{iz}}\). The element \((i, j)\) of the matrix \(\mat Y\mat U\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} Y_{\mathrm{ik}}\,U_{\mathrm{kj}} = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} U_{\mathrm{jk}}\,Y_{\mathrm{ik}}\), since \(\mat U\) is symmetric, and this is the component \(j\) of the vector \(\mat U\vek q\). By step 17, therefore, \(\mat U\vek q = -\vek p\).
  4. By (2.79), \((\mat U\vek q)\cdot\vek n_{\mathrm{k}} = \vek q\cdot(\mat U\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}) = \lambda_{\mathrm{k}}\,(\vek q\cdot\vek n_{\mathrm{k}})\). By step 13 and step 6, therefore,
    \[\begin{aligned} |\vek p|^2 = |\mat U\vek q|^2 &= \sum_{\mathrm{k}} \lambda_{\mathrm{k}}^2\,(\vek q\cdot\vek n_{\mathrm{k}})^2 \\ &\ge (1 - 3m)^2\sum_{\mathrm{k}} (\vek q\cdot\vek n_{\mathrm{k}})^2 = (1 - 3m)^2\,|\vek q|^2 , \end{aligned}\]
    and by lemma 1.29 \(|\vek q| \le |\vek p|/(1 - 3m) \le 2\,|\vek p|\).
  5. By step 18, \(|\vek p|^2 \le 3\,(26m^2)^2\), so \(|\vek p| \le 26\sqrt3\,m^2\). No component of a vector is larger in absolute value than its length, so \(|Y_{\mathrm{ij}}| \le |\vek q| \le 52\sqrt3\,m^2\). Since \(1{,}75^2 = 3{,}0625 > 3\), we have \(\sqrt3 < 1{,}75\) (lemma 1.29), and \(52 \cdot 1{,}75 = 91\). This is the second estimate.

Source: CM §3.5.3 (PDF p. 143) splits the displacement gradient into its symmetric and antisymmetric parts and calls them the small strain tensor and the small rotation tensor, and BOWER §2.1.10-§2.1.11 (PDF pp. 52-53) describes a small deformation as a small stretch followed by a small rigid rotation. The estimates and the proof are not from the sources.

The proof followed the idea of figure 2.241: the difference of the matrices is the difference of the squares \(\mat D = \mat H^\T\mat H - \ten\varepsilon^2\) divided by the sum of the eigenvalues, which is close to \(2\) (step 11), so \(\mat U - \mat I - \ten\varepsilon\) is about half of the matrix \(\mat D\). The proof needs the assumption \(m \le \tfrac16\) in step 6: with it \(1 - 3m \ge \tfrac12\), so \(\lambda_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(\kappa_{\mathrm{l}}\) are at least \(\tfrac12\) and we may divide by their sum. For steel it holds with a wide margin, for the cube \(m = 0{,}001\). For the rigid rotation of section 1.17.6, where \(m = 0{,}8\), it does not hold and the lemma says nothing. It affects the estimate only through the constants: for a smaller \(m\) the bound \(9m^2/(1 - 3m)\) is closer to \(9m^2\). The constants \(18\) and \(91\) are the bounds that the proof gives. Since we estimated crudely in the steps, we expect them to be much too large. With NumPy we computed the polar decomposition of \(200\,000\) random matrices \(\mat F = \mat I + \mat H\) with \(|H_{\mathrm{ij}}| \le m \le \tfrac16\), and the largest differences were \(1{,}6\,m^2\) for \(\mat U\) and \(1{,}7\,m^2\) for \(\mat R\). Let us test them on examples that we computed with a computer to fifty digits.

  • The sheared cube, \(m = 0{,}001\). The bound for \(\mat U\) is \(9m^2/(1 - 3m) = 9{,}03 \cdot 10^{-6}\). The matrix \(\mat U - \mat I - \ten\varepsilon\) has \(-1{,}25 \cdot 10^{-7}\), \(0\) and \(3{,}75 \cdot 10^{-7}\) on the diagonal, and \(-6{,}25 \cdot 10^{-11}\) in row \(x\), column \(z\). The matrix \(\mat D\) has \(-2{,}5 \cdot 10^{-7}\), \(0\) and \(7{,}5 \cdot 10^{-7}\) on the diagonal and zeros elsewhere, and the actual difference is indeed almost exactly half of it. The matrix \(\mat R - \mat I - \ten\omega\) has \(-1{,}25 \cdot 10^{-7}\), \(0\) and \(-1{,}25 \cdot 10^{-7}\) on the diagonal and at most \(6{,}25 \cdot 10^{-11}\) in absolute value off it. The bound \(91m^2\) is \(9{,}1 \cdot 10^{-5}\).
  • The hanging bar at the point \(P\) with \(x_{\mathrm{P}} = 5\) m, \(y_{\mathrm{P}} = 4\) mm and \(z_{\mathrm{P}} = 0\), \(m = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6}\). The bound for \(\mat U\) is \(3{,}4 \cdot 10^{-11}\), while the actual difference is at most \(5{,}9 \cdot 10^{-16}\), in row \(x\), column \(y\). Again it is half of the element \(D_{\mathrm{xy}} = -1{,}19 \cdot 10^{-15}\), which, like the element \(E_{\mathrm{xy}}\) of the Green-Lagrange tensor in section 1.17.3, is a product of partial derivatives of sizes \(10^{-10}\) and \(10^{-6}\).

For small partial derivatives the polar decomposition thus agrees with the split of the matrix \(\mat H\) into its symmetric and antisymmetric parts in section 1.17: \(\mat U - \mat I\) is the small strain tensor, \(\mat R - \mat I\) the small rotation tensor, up to terms that by lemma 2.125 are at most \(18m^2\) and \(91m^2\). For large strains or large rotations, however, as for the band and for the rigid rotation above, we must use the exact \(\mat U\) and \(\mat R\).

2.13.5 Biot stress

Let us now look at how the stress measures behave under a rotation. Take the rubber band of section 2.13.1, 10 cm long with the cross section \(A_0 = 2\ \text{mm}^2\) before loading, which we pull with the axial force \(N = 10\) N so that it stretches to 15 cm. This time, however, we pull it in the direction of the unit vector \(\vek m = 0{,}6\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} + 0{,}8\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\), not in the direction of the axis \(x\) along which it lay before loading (figure 2.242). The band before loading lies along the axis \(x\), and after loading it is stretched and turned. The axes \(x\) and \(y\) lie in the plane of the band. On the end face after loading acts the traction vector \(\vek t(\vek m)\), and on the end face before loading we draw \(\mat P^\T\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(\mat S\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). The grey vector \(\mat T\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and the dashed arc \(\mat R^\T\) next to it will be needed only for the Biot stress tensor below. The band stretches as before and at the same time turns so that it lies along \(\vek m\). Its deformation gradient is \(\mat F = \mat R\,\mat U\), where \(\mat U\) is the diagonal matrix with the diagonal \(1{,}5\), \(0{,}8165\), \(0{,}8165\), the deformation gradient of the band that does not turn, and \(\mat R\) is the rigid rotation with \(c = 0{,}6\) and \(s = 0{,}8\) of section 2.13.4, which maps \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) to \(\vek m\). By the uniqueness in theorem 2.122 these are exactly the matrices of the polar decomposition. The determinant is \(J = \det\mat R\,\det\mat U = 1\).

Let us compute the three stress measures we already know.

  1. The Cauchy stress. On the cross section perpendicular to the band, with the normal \(\vek m\), the traction vector is \(7{,}5\,\vek m\) MPa, because the area of the cross section after loading is again \(1{,}333\ \text{mm}^2\). On the faces along the band there are no forces. The band is a bar along \(\vek m\), and by the example of the bar in section 2.6.6, written in axes one of which lies along \(\vek m\), \(\vek t(\vek n) = 7{,}5\,(\vek m\cdot\vek n)\,\vek m\) MPa for every normal \(\vek n\). Row \(a\) of the matrix \(\ten\sigma\) is \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{a}}) = 7{,}5\,m_{\mathrm{a}}\,\vek m\) MPa, so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ab}} = 7{,}5\,m_{\mathrm{a}} m_{\mathrm{b}}\) MPa:
    \[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 7{,}5 \cdot 0{,}36 = 2{,}7 , \quad \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}} = 7{,}5 \cdot 0{,}48 = 3{,}6 , \quad \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = 7{,}5 \cdot 0{,}64 = 4{,}8 ,\]
    in megapascals, all the other components are zero.
  2. The inverse matrix. Since \(\mat R\,\mat U\,\mat U^{-1}\mat R^\T = \mat R\mat R^\T = \mat I\) and \(\mat U^{-1}\mat R^\T\mat R\,\mat U = \mat U^{-1}\mat U = \mat I\), we have \(\mat F^{-1} = \mat U^{-1}\mat R^\T\). By item 3 of lemma 1.102 on transposing a product and the symmetry of the matrix \(\mat U^{-1}\), \(\mat F^{-\T} = \mat R\,\mat U^{-1}\).
  3. The first Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor is \(\mat P = J\,\mat F^{-1}\ten\sigma = \mat U^{-1}\mat R^\T\ten\sigma\). The matrix \(\mat R^\T\) has the rows \((0{,}6\ \ 0{,}8\ \ 0)\), \((-0{,}8\ \ 0{,}6\ \ 0)\) and \((0\ \ 0\ \ 1)\). The product \(\mat R^\T\ten\sigma\) therefore has in row \(x\) the elements \(0{,}6 \cdot 2{,}7 + 0{,}8 \cdot 3{,}6 = 4{,}5\) and \(0{,}6 \cdot 3{,}6 + 0{,}8 \cdot 4{,}8 = 6\), and in row \(y\) the elements \(-0{,}8 \cdot 2{,}7 + 0{,}6 \cdot 3{,}6 = 0\) and \(-0{,}8 \cdot 3{,}6 + 0{,}6 \cdot 4{,}8 = 0\). The matrix \(\mat U^{-1}\) divides row \(x\) by \(1{,}5\), so the only nonzero elements are
    \[P_{\mathrm{xx}} = 3\ \text{MPa} , \qquad P_{\mathrm{xy}} = 4\ \text{MPa} .\]
    Row \(x\) is \(5\,\vek m\) MPa: on the cross section that had the normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) before loading acts a force of \(5\) N per square millimetre of the area before loading, in the direction \(\vek m\) along which the band now lies. The matrix \(\mat P\) is not symmetric, since \(P_{\mathrm{xy}} = 4\) MPa and \(P_{\mathrm{yx}} = 0\).
  4. The second Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor is, by item 2 of lemma 2.121 on the relations between the stress measures and by step 2, \(\mat S = \mat P\,\mat F^{-\T} = \mat P\,\mat R\,\mat U^{-1}\). Row \(x\) of the matrix \(\mat P\,\mat R\) has the elements \(3 \cdot 0{,}6 + 4 \cdot 0{,}8 = 5\), \(3 \cdot (-0{,}8) + 4 \cdot 0{,}6 = 0\) and \(0\), and the other rows are zero. The matrix \(\mat U^{-1}\) from the right divides column \(x\) by \(1{,}5\), so the only nonzero component is \(S_{\mathrm{xx}} = 3{,}333\) MPa, exactly as for the band that does not turn.

The rotation has thus changed the Cauchy stress and the first Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor, which both point along \(\vek m\), but not the second Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor, which still points along \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) (figure 2.242). This holds in general, and we show it on a small piece of the body.

Take a small piece of the body before loading, which the deformation gradient \(\mat F\) maps into the piece after loading. On its face with the normal \(\vek n\) acts the traction vector \(\vek t(\vek n)\). Suppose that we additionally turn the deformed body as a rigid body with an orthogonal matrix \(\mat Q\) with determinant \(1\), just as we turned the band that did not turn into the turned one. This time \(\mat Q\) does not turn the axes, which stay where they are, but the body. Every fibre \(\mat F\vek a\) goes over into \(\mat Q\mat F\vek a\), so the new deformation gradient is \(\mat Q\mat F\), with the same determinant \(J\). We assume that in a rigid rotation the forces between the parts of the body turn together with the body without changing: on the face with the turned normal \(\mat Q\vek n\) acts the turned traction vector \(\mat Q\,\vek t(\vek n)\), of the same length and at the same angle \(\varphi\) to the normal as before. The material does not feel which way it faces. The assumption is sensible because the rotation \(\mat Q\) keeps all lengths and angles, so the shape of no small piece changes, only its position does. It holds when we turn the body together with everything that loads it, and so slowly that the turning itself needs no forces. It does not hold when the load keeps facing the way it did: a hanging bar is pulled along its axis by its weight, but once we turn it into a horizontal position, the same weight bends it. Step 1 below rests on it, and with it the result \(\mat S' = \mat S\). CM writes this assumption as \(\vek t^* = \mat Q\cdot\vek t\), \(\vek n^* = \mat Q\cdot\vek n\) (CM (4.6.1), PDF p. 200).

  1. Let \(\ten\sigma'\) be the Cauchy stress of the turned body. The assumption says \(\ten\sigma'\mat Q\vek n = \mat Q\,\ten\sigma\,\vek n\) for every \(\vek n\): the left side is the traction vector of the turned body on the face with the normal \(\mat Q\vek n\), the right side the turned vector \(\mat Q\,\vek t(\vek n)\). For \(\vek n = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) we get in turn the columns of both sides, so \(\ten\sigma'\mat Q = \mat Q\,\ten\sigma\), and after multiplying from the right by \(\mat Q^\T\), \(\ten\sigma' = \mat Q\,\ten\sigma\,\mat Q^\T\).
  2. The inverse of the matrix \(\mat Q\mat F\) is \(\mat F^{-1}\mat Q^\T\): we walk the way \(\mat Q\mat F\) back by first undoing the rotation \(\mat Q\) and then the map \(\mat F\). Indeed, \(\mat Q\mat F\mat F^{-1}\mat Q^\T = \mat Q\mat Q^\T = \mat I\) and \(\mat F^{-1}\mat Q^\T\mat Q\mat F = \mat F^{-1}\mat F = \mat I\). Its transpose is \(\mat Q\,\mat F^{-\T}\) by item 3 of lemma 1.102 on transposing a product.
  3. The new second Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor is
    \[\mat S' = J\,\mat F^{-1}\mat Q^\T\,\mat Q\,\ten\sigma\,\mat Q^\T\,\mat Q\,\mat F^{-\T} = J\,\mat F^{-1}\,\ten\sigma\,\mat F^{-\T} = \mat S ,\]
    because \(\mat Q^\T\mat Q = \mat I\). The figure says the same without computing with \(\ten\sigma\). The force on the patch of the turned body is \(\mat Q\,\Delta\vek F\), turned together with the body. The fictitious force of the turned body is therefore \((\mat Q\mat F)^{-1}\mat Q\,\Delta\vek F = \mat F^{-1}\mat Q^\T\mat Q\,\Delta\vek F = \mat F^{-1}\Delta\vek F\), the same as for the body that was not turned. Both ways back end at the same vector \(\mat S\vek n_0\) on the face with the normal \(\vek n_0\).
  4. The new first Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor, however, is \(\mat P' = J\,\mat F^{-1}\mat Q^\T\mat Q\,\ten\sigma\,\mat Q^\T = \mat P\,\mat Q^\T\), which differs from \(\mat P\). By item 3 of lemma 1.102 on transposing a product \(\mat P'^\T = \mat Q\,\mat P^\T\), so the vector \(\mat P'^\T\vek n_0\) is the vector \(\mat P^\T\vek n_0\) turned by \(\mat Q\): the real force per unit area before loading turns with the body.

For the band \(\mat Q = \mat R\). The band that does not turn has the only nonzero component \(P_{\mathrm{xx}} = 5\) MPa, so row \(x\) of the matrix \(\mat P\,\mat R^\T\) is \(5\) times row \(x\) of the matrix \(\mat R^\T\), that is \(5\,(0{,}6\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} + 0{,}8\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}) = 5\,\vek m\) MPa, as in step 3 above. This is the vector \(\mat P^\T\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) in figure 2.242. CM computes the same in its notation (CM (4.6.3), (4.6.4), PDF p. 201) and adds that the second Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor is the stress measure most often chosen in solid mechanics (CM, PDF p. 201).

The second Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor does not see the rotation, but it has another weakness. For the band \(S_{\mathrm{xx}} = N/(\lambda A_0) = 3{,}333\) MPa, which is not a force per unit area we would measure on any area, since the force \(\mat F^{-1}\Delta\vek F\) is fictitious and shorter than the real one by the factor \(\lambda\). Is there a measure that does not see the rotation but does not shorten the force? There is. We turn the force back only by the rotation of the polar decomposition and do not undo the stretch.

  1. The force \(\Delta\vek F\) on the patch, turned back by \(\mat R^\T\), is \(\mat R^\T\Delta\vek F\). Since \(\mat R^\T\mat R = \mat I\), \(\mat R^\T\) undoes the rotation \(\mat R\): for the band it turns the force from the direction \(\vek m\) back into the direction \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). It does not change lengths, because by theorem 2.122 on the polar decomposition \(\mat R\) is an orthogonal matrix, so by definition 2.46 of an orthogonal matrix also \(\mat R\mat R^\T = \mat I\), and therefore by (2.137) \(|\mat R^\T\vek v|^2 = \vek v\cdot(\mat R\mat R^\T\vek v) = |\vek v|^2\), just as we showed for \(\mat R\) in section 2.13.4.
  2. Per unit area before loading this is, by (2.140) and \(\vek t_0(\vek n_0) = \mat P^\T\vek n_0\), equal to \(\mat R^\T\mat P^\T\vek n_0 = (\mat P\,\mat R)^\T\vek n_0\), by item 3 of lemma 1.102 on transposing a product. For the band this is the grey vector, \(5\) MPa in the direction \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), as long as the vector \(\mat P^\T\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) it came from.
  3. Just as for the first Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor, row \(a\) of the matrix \(\mat P\,\mat R\) is therefore the turned-back force per unit area before loading on the face that had the normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{a}}\) before loading. The matrix \(\mat P\,\mat R\) is in general not symmetric. We take its symmetric part, and we explain why right after the definition.
Definition 2.126 Biot stress (Biotov tenzor napetosti)

Let \(\mat F = \mat R\,\mat U\) be the polar decomposition of the deformation gradient at a point of a body (theorem 2.122) and \(\mat P\) the first Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor at this point. The Biot stress tensor (Biotov tenzor napetosti) is the matrix

\[\mat T = \tfrac12\,\big(\mat P\,\mat R + \mat R^\T\mat P^\T\big) . \tag{2.147}\]

Source: Our sources do not define the Biot stress tensor. It is built from the polar decomposition (CM §3.9.2, PDF pp. 161-163), the first Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor (CM §4.4.2, PDF pp. 193-194) and the Biot strain \(\mat U - \mat I\), which BOWER calls the Lagrangian nominal strain (Lagrangeova nominalna deformacija) (BOWER §2.1.15, PDF p. 57).

The matrix \(\mat T\) is symmetric, since by items 1 and 3 of lemma 1.102 on transposing a product \((\mat P\mat R)^\T = \mat R^\T\mat P^\T\) and \((\mat R^\T\mat P^\T)^\T = \mat P\mat R\), so transposing only swaps the two terms. Why the symmetric part? Books write every measure of stress in a pair with a measure of strain, so that together they give the work that the stresses do on a unit of volume before loading. BOWER shows this for the pair of the second Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor and the Green-Lagrange strain (BOWER §2.4.2, PDF p. 83). The partner of the Biot stress tensor is the Biot strain \(\mat U - \mat I\). When it changes a little, by \(\Delta\mat U\), the work that the stresses do on a unit of volume before loading is approximately the sum of the products of the corresponding elements of the matrices \(\mat P\,\mat R\) and \(\Delta\mat U\). This follows from the expression with which BOWER writes the work of the first Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor (BOWER §2.4.2, PDF p. 83) and from the split \(\mat F = \mat R\,\mat U\), but we will not do this computation here, because our sources do not treat the Biot pair. The matrix \(\mat U\) is symmetric, so its change \(\Delta\mat U\) is symmetric too, and in the sum the elements \((\mat P\mat R)_{\mathrm{xy}}\) and \((\mat P\mat R)_{\mathrm{yx}}\) appear with the same factor \(\Delta U_{\mathrm{xy}}\). Only their sum counts, and likewise for the other two pairs of off-diagonal elements, so only the symmetric part of the matrix \(\mat P\,\mat R\) counts. The Biot stress tensor is easiest to compute from the second Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor.

Lemma 2.127 Biot stress and the second Piola-Kirchhoff stress (Biotov in drugi Piola-Kirchhoffov tenzor)

Let \(\mat F = \mat R\,\mat U\) be the polar decomposition of the deformation gradient, \(\mat S\) the second Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor and \(\mat T\) the Biot stress tensor at the same point of a body. Then

\[\mat T = \tfrac12\,\big(\mat S\,\mat U + \mat U\,\mat S\big) .\]

When \(\mat S\,\mat U = \mat U\,\mat S\), \(\mat T = \mat S\,\mat U\).

Proof

We express the first Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor through the second and substitute it into equation (2.147) of definition 2.126.

  1. By item 2 of lemma 2.121 on the relations between the stress measures, \(\mat P = \mat S\,\mat F^\T\).
  2. By item 3 of lemma 1.102 on transposing a product, \(\mat F^\T = (\mat R\,\mat U)^\T = \mat U^\T\mat R^\T = \mat U\,\mat R^\T\), because \(\mat U\) is symmetric.
  3. So \(\mat P\,\mat R = \mat S\,\mat U\,\mat R^\T\mat R = \mat S\,\mat U\), because \(\mat R^\T\mat R = \mat I\).
  4. By item 3 of the lemma on transposing a product, \(\mat R^\T\mat P^\T = (\mat P\,\mat R)^\T = (\mat S\,\mat U)^\T = \mat U^\T\mat S^\T = \mat U\,\mat S\), because \(\mat U\) and \(\mat S\) are symmetric (item 1 of the lemma on the relations between the stress measures).
  5. We substitute steps 3 and 4 into (2.147) and get \(\mat T = \tfrac12(\mat S\,\mat U + \mat U\,\mat S)\). If \(\mat S\,\mat U = \mat U\,\mat S\), the two terms are equal and \(\mat T = \mat S\,\mat U\).

Source: The lemma is not from the sources, it follows from CM (4.4.11) (PDF p. 195) and (3.9.1) (PDF p. 161).

The Biot stress tensor therefore does not see the rotation. A body that we turn with the matrix \(\mat Q\) after the deformation has the deformation gradient \(\mat Q\mat F = (\mat Q\mat R)\,\mat U\). The matrix \(\mat Q\mat R\) is orthogonal with determinant \(1\), since \((\mat Q\mat R)^\T\mat Q\mat R = \mat R^\T\mat Q^\T\mat Q\mat R = \mat I\) and \(\det(\mat Q\mat R) = \det\mat Q\,\det\mat R = 1\). By the uniqueness in the theorem on the polar decomposition, \(\mat U\) stays the same, and by the computation above \(\mat S\) stays the same as well. By the lemma, therefore, \(\mat T\) stays the same too.

For the band \(\mat S\) and \(\mat U\) are diagonal, so \(\mat S\,\mat U = \mat U\,\mat S\), and the only nonzero component of the Biot stress tensor is

\[T_{\mathrm{xx}} = S_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\lambda = 3{,}333\ \text{MPa} \cdot 1{,}5 = 5\ \text{MPa} = \frac{N}{A_0} ,\]

for the band that does not turn and for the turned one. In figure 2.242 the grey vector is the blue vector lengthened by the factor \(1{,}5\). The Biot stress tensor thus has both properties we were looking for. It does not see the rotation, and \(T_{\mathrm{xx}}\) is the real axial force divided by the area before loading, measured along the band, whichever way the band faces. Its partner, the Biot strain \(\mat U - \mat I\), has for the band the diagonal \(0{,}5\), \(-0{,}1835\), \(-0{,}1835\). The number \(0{,}5\) is the strain of the band \(\Delta L/L_0 = 5\ \text{cm}/10\ \text{cm}\) of definition 1.2. The Biot pair is thus the nominal stress and the strain \(\Delta L/L_0\) of the test in which we pull the band, also when the band turns during it.

At \(\lambda = 1{,}5\) the Cauchy stress and the second Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor differ by the factor \(1{,}5^2 = 2{,}25\), since \(7{,}5/3{,}333 = 2{,}25\) (figure 2.245). What about the steel bar of section 2.2, with the diameter 10 mm, which we pull with the axial force 10 kN? Its stress \(N/A_0 = 10\,000\ \text{N}/78{,}54\ \text{mm}^2 = 127{,}32\) MPa is nominal by definition 2.6, that is, \(P_{\mathrm{xx}}\). We get the strain as for the hanging bar in section 2.2, by dividing the stress by the number 196 GPa that BOWER gives for steel (Table 3.1, PDF p. 100). Why this gives the strain we will see in chapter 3. The strain is

\[\varepsilon = \frac{127{,}32\ \text{MPa}}{196\ \text{GPa}} = 6{,}496 \cdot 10^{-4} , \qquad \lambda = 1 + \varepsilon = 1{,}00065 .\]

The diameter of the bar decreases by \(0{,}3\) times the strain (section 1.6), that is, by the factor \(1 - 0{,}3 \cdot 6{,}496 \cdot 10^{-4} = 0{,}999805\), and the area of the cross section by the factor \(0{,}999805^2 = 0{,}999610\). The bar does not turn, so \(\mat R = \mat I\), and \(\mat U = \mat F\) is a diagonal matrix. Then

  1. the Cauchy stress is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 127{,}32\ \text{MPa}/0{,}999610 = 127{,}37\) MPa, the orange point in the magnification, \(0{,}039\) % more than the nominal stress,
  2. the component of the second Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor is \(S_{\mathrm{xx}} = P_{\mathrm{xx}}/\lambda = 127{,}32\ \text{MPa}/1{,}00065 = 127{,}24\) MPa, the blue point, \(0{,}065\) % less,
  3. the component of the Biot stress tensor is \(T_{\mathrm{xx}} = S_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\lambda = P_{\mathrm{xx}} = 127{,}32\) MPa, the point between them, by lemma 2.127, because \(\mat S\) and \(\mat U\) are diagonal.

The Cauchy stress and the second Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor thus differ from each other by \(0{,}10\) %. In figure 2.245 the bar lies on the grey vertical line, right next to \(\lambda = 1\), where all the ratios are almost \(1\). The differences are much smaller than the uncertainty of the data for steel: BOWER gives his values of the number 196 GPa as representative and his values of the yield stress as rough (BOWER, PDF pp. 100 and 156). BOWER writes that for small strains and small rotations, that is, when all partial derivatives of the displacements are small, we neglect the differences between the stress measures (BOWER §2.4.3, PDF p. 84), and CM that in this case no distinction is made between the second Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor and the Cauchy stress (CM, PDF p. 229). In the rest of the book all partial derivatives of the displacements will be small, that is, the strains and the rotations, except where we say otherwise, and we will compute with the Cauchy stress \(\ten\sigma\) and the small strain tensor \(\ten\varepsilon\).

When, then, does the difference matter? In three kinds of cases. Figure 2.246 shows the second and the third.

  1. Large strains, as in rubber, which stretches elastically by very large amounts (BOWER §3.5, PDF pp. 120-121), for example our band by half its length, where at \(\lambda = 1{,}5\) the points of the ratios lie far apart.
  2. Large permanent deformations of metals, for example in metal forming (preoblikovanje kovin) (BOWER, PDF p. 31), where a tool presses a piece of metal into a much lower and wider shape. The permanent strains there are large, the part of the strain that returns on unloading is small, and the material can turn a lot (BOWER §3.9.3, PDF p. 173).
  3. Small strains with large rotations. In a slender bar bent into a semicircle the material is stretched or compressed only a little, but each end turns by a quarter of a circle relative to the middle (BOWER §3.4, PDF p. 118, figure 3.14). The small strain tensor is not zero for a large rotation, as we saw for the rigid rotation with \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = -0{,}4\) in section 2.13.4. A law relating \(\ten\sigma\) and \(\ten\varepsilon\) would therefore predict a stress for the rotation alone. In such cases BOWER uses the second Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor and the Green-Lagrange strain tensor, which do not see the rotation (BOWER, PDF pp. 118-119).

For large deformations we do not know the shape of the body after loading in advance, since this shape is part of what we are looking for. So books then write the equilibrium of a small piece on the shape before loading, with the first Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor and with derivatives with respect to the coordinates before loading (CM (5.3.17), (5.3.18), PDF p. 228). For small strains and small rotations, that is, when all partial derivatives of the displacements are small, these equations go over into the equilibrium equations (2.38) (CM (5.3.21), (5.3.22), PDF p. 229).

Let us sum up how we arrived at these measures. From three fibres and the ratio of volumes \(J\) we got Nanson's formula, which says how the area and the normal of a small patch change. With it we converted the force after loading, given by the Cauchy stress, to the area before loading and got the first Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor. When we mapped the force back like a fibre, we got the symmetric second Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor. That it does not change in a rigid rotation we showed on the turned band and with a general computation. With the polar decomposition, which splits a deformation into a stretch and a rotation, we defined the Biot stress tensor, which only turns the force back. For a steel bar in the elastic range the measures differ by about a tenth of a per cent or less, for rubber by factors of the stretch. How stress and strain are related for a given material is the question of chapter 3.
