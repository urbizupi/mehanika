Chapter 11 The boundary value problem of linear elasticity

11.11 Saint-Venant's principle (Saint-Venantovo načelo)

The cantilever of section 11.10 has an exact solution in the plane problem, given by an Airy function (theorem 11.40). It is \(L = 1\) m long, its cross section is \(20 \times 40\) mm and a force \(F = 500\) N pulls it down at the free end. This solution requires a shear stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) that is parabolic over the height of the cross section at the free end, and at the clamp a normal stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) that is linear over the height, \(93{,}75\) MPa at the top. A real load at the end may be a hook or a bolt, and a real wall does not give exactly this stress distribution. The resultant force and the moment at both ends are right, the stress distribution is not. But how far from the end does the difference in distribution still count, and is that distance large or small compared with the length of the cantilever, 1 m?

The answer has three parts. First we say what the books call Saint-Venant's principle and what they do not say. Then we look for a plane example in which the distance can be computed, a long straight strip, and get a number for the cantilever from it. Finally we ask the same question for the bar of figure 2.14 and for the hanging bars of section 1.7.1.

11.11.1 What the principle says

The notion we need is already hidden in the question: two loads that differ in distribution but have the same total effect on equilibrium. In figure 11.105 the uniform and the linear load have the same resultant but a different distribution.

Definition 11.48 Statically equivalent and self-equilibrated loads (statično enakovredni in samouravnotežene obremenitve)

Let \(S_1\) be a part of the boundary of a body and let \(\vek t^{(1)}\) and \(\vek t^{(2)}\) be two distributions of the stress vector (definition 2.20) over \(S_1\), that is, two vector functions of the point on \(S_1\). The loads are statically equivalent (statično enakovredni) if they have the same resultant and the same moment about every point \(Q\),

\[\begin{aligned} &\int_{\mathrm{S}_1} \vek t^{(1)}\,\mathrm{d}A \\ &\quad = \int_{\mathrm{S}_1} \vek t^{(2)}\,\mathrm{d}A , \\ &\int_{\mathrm{S}_1} \overrightarrow{QP}\times\vek t^{(1)}\,\mathrm{d}A \\ &\quad = \int_{\mathrm{S}_1} \overrightarrow{QP}\times\vek t^{(2)}\,\mathrm{d}A , \end{aligned} \tag{11.66}\]

where \(P\) is the point of integration (definition 2.37). A distribution \(\vek t\) is self-equilibrated (samouravnotežena) if its resultant and its moment about every point are zero. The difference of two statically equivalent loads is a self-equilibrated load, and every self-equilibrated load is the difference of two statically equivalent ones, for example \(\vek t\) and \(\vek 0\).

Source: The definition is ours. The notion "statically equivalent" is used by SADD (§5.6, PDF p. 113) and LOVE (PDF p. 38, "statically equipollent"), "self-equilibrated" by BARBER (§3.1.2, PDF p. 56, "self-equilibrating"). For a zero resultant the moment does not depend on the point by lemma 2.39, so for a difference of loads it does not matter about which point it is measured.

If the body is plane, all integrals in (11.66) are integrals along the boundary curve per unit thickness, and at the end of a strip \(x = \text{const.}\) with outward normal \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) the load is \(\vek t = -(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}})\). It is then self-equilibrated exactly when

\[\int \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}y = 0 , \qquad \int \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\mathrm{d}y = 0 , \qquad \int y\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}y = 0 \tag{11.67}\]

the integrals being over the height of the cross section, namely the resultant force in the directions \(x\) and \(y\) and the moment about the centre of the cross section.

Let us test the equalities (11.66) at the end of a strip without shear stress (figure 11.105). The end of the strip is the blackwhite vertical line in the figure, the strip has height \(2b\), \(-b \le y \le b\), and continues to the right. The arrows are \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) over the height: an arrow from the end to the left is a pull, an arrow into the end is a push. For the uniform load \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_0\) the resultant is \(\int\sigma_0\,\mathrm{d}y = 2b\sigma_0\) and the moment about the centre is \(\int y\,\sigma_0\,\mathrm{d}y = 0\). For the load \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_0\,(1 + y/b)\) the resultant is the same, \(2b\sigma_0\), because \(\int y\,\mathrm{d}y = 0\), but the moment is \(\int y\,\sigma_0\,(1 + y/b)\,\mathrm{d}y = \sigma_0\int (y^2/b)\,\mathrm{d}y = \tfrac23\,b^2\sigma_0 \ne 0\). The first equality holds and the second fails, so the loads are not statically equivalent, although they have the same resultant. Their difference \(\sigma_0\,y/b\) has zero resultant but not zero moment, so it is not self-equilibrated: the third integral in (11.67) is \(\tfrac23\,b^2\sigma_0\). The difference \(\sigma_0\,(1 - 3y^2/b^2)\), on the other hand, passes the test, since \(\int (1 - 3y^2/b^2)\,\mathrm{d}y = 2b - 2b = 0\) and \(\int y\,(1 - 3y^2/b^2)\,\mathrm{d}y = 0\) as the integral of an odd function over an interval symmetric about zero. For the difference \(\sigma_0\,(1 - 2y^2/b^2)\) already the first integral fails, \(2b - \tfrac43\,b = \tfrac23\,b \ne 0\). The coefficient \(3\) is chosen so that the first integral is zero, and symmetry takes care of the zero moment. The odd load \(\sigma_0\,(y/b - 5y^3/(3b^3))\) is self-equilibrated too: its resultant is zero as the integral of an odd function, and its moment is \(\int y\,(y/b - 5y^3/(3b^3))\,\mathrm{d}y = \tfrac23\,b^2 - \tfrac23\,b^2 = 0\). The even one will be the example for the symmetric class of section 11.11.2, the odd one for the antisymmetric class. What does this mean by hand? A self-equilibrated load does not push the strip as a whole anywhere and does not turn it: the pulls and pushes on the same end hold each other, so the strip needs no support because of it.

The books state the principle in a similar way. SADD states it as follows: the stress, strain and displacement fields caused by two different statically equivalent force distributions on parts of the body far from the loading points are approximately the same (SADD §5.6, PDF p. 114). It is named after Saint-Venant, who introduced it (LOVE, PDF p. 38, history below). Chapter 2 already named it (section 2.2) and showed it schematically in figure 2.14. Chapter 5 relies on it in two places: in the solution 5.104, where force and moment at the ends of the beam are right but the stress is not the one that point supports require, and in the price of the slenderness assumption (section 5.15.1, \(L/h = 25\) for the cantilever). Here we state it exactly and check it on numbers.

Three things have to be said along with it.

First, the principle gives no distance. SADD itself writes that the statement contains the descriptive words "far away" and "approximately the same", and that it therefore gives no quantitative estimate of the difference between the two fields. According to SADD, quantitative results were developed by von Mises (1945), Sternberg (1954) and Toupin (1965), and Horgan (1989) gave a review (SADD, PDF p. 114). We do not derive these results here, we only name them. So for the distance we shall give a computation in one example and then a rule of thumb. The books give three rules of thumb. CM says that the distance is usually taken equal to or greater than the length scale of the part of the boundary on which the conditions are replaced by statically equivalent ones, for the cantilever of CM's figure, which has height \(2h\), that is, one height of the beam, \(2h\) (CM §7.5.6, PDF p. 333). BARBER shows for a strip of half-height \(b\) that the error of the end falls to about \(2\,\%\) already at a distance \(b\) from the end (BARBER, PDF p. 98), and BOWER says for a small loaded region of size \(a\) that "far away" usually means a distance \(r\) with \(r/a > 3\) (BOWER §5.1.5, PDF p. 280). All three rules are of the order of one length of the loaded part, no more.

Second, the principle is not a theorem. BARBER says that it seems intuitively reasonable but has not been proved rigorously except in special cases (BARBER §3.1.2, PDF p. 56). BOWER notes that it is "remarkably difficult to prove", partly because it is not easy to state precisely, and names Sternberg's rigorous version among several others (BOWER, PDF p. 280). In this book we therefore never use it as a theorem but as a principle with evidence: in one case we compute it (the strip), in a few cases we check it on numbers (the cantilever, the bar), and later in this chapter we shall see it again at the hole in a plate. Where we use it to justify an approximation, we write so.

Third, the principle holds only for self-equilibrated differences. BARBER explains why (figure 11.106, schematic): if the load paths in a body share the load in proportion to their stiffnesses, the shortest paths are the stiffest, so a self-equilibrated load carried by the shortest paths does not spread far. If the load is not self-equilibrated, part of the force must go by longer paths to distant parts of the boundary, and the stresses in the intermediate regions are not small (BARBER, PDF p. 56). A load path is the chain of parts of the body along which a force gets from the place where it acts to the place where something balances it. In the upper strip the end carries the self-equilibrated load \(\sigma_0\,(1 - 3y^2/b^2)\): the pull in the middle is balanced by the pushes at the edges of the same end. The force need go no further than to its neighbour, so the paths close within about one height \(2b\). Why does the force choose the short path? A shorter piece of the same material deforms less under the same force (a bar half as long stretches half as much), so it is stiffer, and of two parallel paths the stiffer one takes more of the force. In the lower strip a force \(F\) acts on the end, and nothing on that end balances it. Only the distant wall balances it, so all paths must reach the wall. Within the first height \(2b\) they only spread over the cross section, and beyond it the average stress \(F/(2b)\) per unit thickness stays in every cross section up to the wall and does not fall with distance. SADD adds that for materials that respond differently in different directions or at different places, the routine use of the principle is not always justified (SADD, PDF p. 114). We stay with an isotropic and homogeneous material.

The same source introduces two terms we shall use: a boundary condition is satisfied in the weak sense (šibki smisel) when it holds only for the resultant force and the moment on a part of the boundary, and in the strong sense (močni smisel) when it holds pointwise (BARBER, PDF p. 56). The principle allows us, at points far enough from that part of the boundary, to replace the strong condition by the weak one (SADD, PDF p. 114). The cantilever of section 11.10 has both ends, the clamp and the end with the force, satisfied in the weak sense.

11.11.2 Decaying solutions of the strip (padajoče rešitve traku)

How far is far can be computed only where we can solve the plane problem exactly. Take the semi-infinite strip (polneskončni trak) \(x > 0\), \(-b \le y \le b\), that is, the end of a long straight beam of height \(2b\) in a plane problem without body force (figure 11.107, below, the strip is grey). Its longer edges \(y = \pm b\) are free. At the end \(x = 0\) let a self-equilibrated load act, the difference of two statically equivalent loads, in the figure the even load \(\sigma_0\,(1 - 3y^2/b^2)\). We ask how the stress moves away from the end, that is, how much of it is left at the cross sections \(x = b\), \(x = 2b\) and \(x = 3b\).

We look for the solution on the model of chapter 9. The equations in the plane have constant coefficients, and in section 9.4.1 a trial with an exponential function worked for equations with constant coefficients. So we try the Airy function (theorem 11.40) in the form \(\phi = \mathrm{e}^{-\lambda x}\,f(y)\): in the direction \(x\) it keeps its shape and changes only in size. If \(\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda > 0\), this size falls, and how fast is told by \(\lambda\). Since we look for \(\lambda\) among complex numbers (definition 9.24), we must extend cosine and sine to a complex argument. We take the definition from the complex exponential function (definition 9.33),

\[\cos z = \tfrac12\big(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}z} + \mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}z}\big) , \qquad \sin z = \tfrac{1}{2\mathrm{i}}\big(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}z} - \mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}z}\big) . \tag{11.68}\]

For real \(z\) this is Euler's formula (definition 9.33), so the agreement with the real cosine and sine remains. From theorem 9.34 (point 1) we get the same relations as in the real case, for example \(\sin^2 z + \cos^2 z = 1\) and \(\sin 2z = 2\sin z\cos z\), and their derivatives with respect to the real variable \(y\) are \((\cos\lambda y)' = -\lambda\sin\lambda y\) and \((\sin\lambda y)' = \lambda\cos\lambda y\) (point 3 of the same theorem with \(s = \pm\mathrm{i}\lambda\)).

The strip is symmetric about the axis \(y = 0\), so the solutions split into two classes. The function \(f\) is even (symmetric) or odd (antisymmetric):

\[\begin{aligned} &f(y) = A\cos\lambda y + B\,y\sin\lambda y \\ &\quad \text{(symmetric)} , \\ &f(y) = C\sin\lambda y + D\,y\cos\lambda y \\ &\quad \text{(antisymmetric)} . \end{aligned} \tag{11.69}\]

The first class corresponds to a load for which \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) is an even function of \(y\) (a bar that we pull), the second to a load for which \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) is odd (a cantilever bent by a transverse force).

Lemma 11.49 Decaying solutions of a strip (padajoče rešitve traku)

Let \(\lambda \ne 0\) be a complex number with \(\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda > 0\), let \(b > 0\), let \(f\) be one of the functions (11.69) with complex constants, and let \(\phi(x, y) = \mathrm{e}^{-\lambda x} f(y)\) be a complex function on the strip \(x \ge 0\), \(-b \le y \le b\). The stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \partial^2\phi/\partial y^2\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \partial^2\phi/\partial x^2\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = -\partial^2\phi/\partial x\,\partial y\) (taken for the real and the imaginary part separately) are called the stresses of the function \(\phi\). Then:

  1. The function \(\phi\) is biharmonic on the strip, \(\nabla^4\phi = 0\), so its real and imaginary parts are biharmonic, and their stresses are by theorem 11.40 an equilibrium field that satisfies the compatibility condition without body force.
  2. The stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) of the function \(\phi\) vanish on the edges \(y = \pm b\) for all \(x \ge 0\) for some \((A, B) \ne (0, 0)\) exactly when \(\sin 2\lambda b + 2\lambda b = 0\) (symmetric class), and for some \((C, D) \ne (0, 0)\) exactly when \(\sin 2\lambda b - 2\lambda b = 0\) (antisymmetric class).
  3. If the stresses on the edges vanish, then on every cross section \(x = \text{const.}\) the resultant and the moment of the stresses are zero, so the load on the cross section is self-equilibrated.
  4. If the stresses on the edges vanish, there is a constant \(M\), independent of \(x\) and \(y\), such that the absolute value of every stress of the function \(\phi\) and of its real part on the strip is at most \(M\,\mathrm{e}^{-(\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda)\,x}\), that is, below a decaying envelope.
Proof

We must show four points. The idea for points 1 and 2 is a factorization of the operator in \(y\) and, in the trial, the eigenvalue \(\lambda\), and for points 3 and 4 the boundary conditions that make the function \(\phi\) and its first derivatives vanish on the edges.

  1. Let \(L = \mathrm{d}^2/\mathrm{d}y^2 + \lambda^2\), an operator along the height of the strip. For \(u = \cos\lambda y\) and \(v = \sin\lambda y\) we have \(Lu = Lv = 0\) (the derivatives in the introduction of the section). For a product, \((y\,w)'' = 2w' + y\,w''\) (product rule, lemma 9.35), so \(L(y\,w) = 2w' + y\,Lw\). For \(w = v\) or \(w = u\) we have \(Lw = 0\) and \(L(y\,w) = 2w'\), which is again one of the functions \(u\), \(v\) up to a constant, so \(L(2w') = 2(Lw)' = 0\). Hence \(L^2 f = 0\) for both forms (11.69).
  2. Since, differentiating along the strip, \(\partial^2(\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda x})/\partial x^2 = \lambda^2\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda x}\) (point 3 of theorem 9.34), we have \(\nabla^2\phi = \mathrm{e}^{-\lambda x}\,Lf\) and \(\nabla^4\phi = \mathrm{e}^{-\lambda x}\,L^2 f = 0\) by step 1 (definition 11.5). The operator \(\nabla^4\) is linear with real coefficients, so it follows componentwise that \(\mathrm{Re}\,\phi\) and \(\mathrm{Im}\,\phi\) are biharmonic (the same reason as in lemma 9.37). Theorem 11.40 gives point 1.
  3. The stresses on the edge are \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \partial^2\phi/\partial x^2 = \lambda^2\phi = \lambda^2\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda x}f(y)\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = -\partial^2\phi/\partial x\,\partial y = \lambda\,\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda x}f'(y)\). Since \(\lambda \ne 0\) and \(\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda x} \ne 0\) (point 2 of theorem 9.34), both are zero at \(y = \pm b\) for all \(x\) exactly when \(f(\pm b) = 0\) and \(f'(\pm b) = 0\). For even \(f\), \(f'\) is odd and the conditions at \(-b\) follow from those at \(b\), for odd \(f\), \(f'\) is even and it is again so. Two equations remain.
  4. Symmetric class, the two conditions at the edges: \(A\cos\lambda b + B\,b\sin\lambda b = 0\) and \(-A\lambda\sin\lambda b + B(\sin\lambda b + \lambda b\cos\lambda b) = 0\). This is a homogeneous \(2\times 2\) system with a nonzero solution \((A, B)\) exactly when the determinant is zero. The determinant is \(\cos\lambda b\,(\sin\lambda b + \lambda b\cos\lambda b) + \lambda b\sin^2\lambda b = \sin\lambda b\cos\lambda b + \lambda b = \tfrac12(\sin 2\lambda b + 2\lambda b)\), because \(\sin^2 + \cos^2 = 1\) and \(\sin 2z = 2\sin z\cos z\). Antisymmetric class: \(C\sin\lambda b + D\,b\cos\lambda b = 0\) and \(C\lambda\cos\lambda b + D(\cos\lambda b - \lambda b\sin\lambda b) = 0\) with determinant \(\sin\lambda b\,(\cos\lambda b - \lambda b\sin\lambda b) - \lambda b\cos^2\lambda b = \tfrac12(\sin 2\lambda b - 2\lambda b)\). This is point 2.
  1. If the stresses on the edges are zero, then by step 3 \(f(\pm b) = f'(\pm b) = 0\): the curve \(f\) touches the axis at both edges, the value and the slope are zero there. So on the edges at \(y = \pm b\) \(\phi = 0\), \(\partial\phi/\partial y = \mathrm{e}^{-\lambda x}f'(\pm b) = 0\) and \(\partial\phi/\partial x = -\lambda\,\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda x}f(\pm b) = 0\) for all \(x\) on the strip. The resultant force and the moment on a cross section \(x = \text{const.}\), that is, on the arc from the point \(P = (x, -b)\) to the point \(Q = (x, b)\), are by lemma 11.42 differences of expressions in \(\phi\), \(\partial\phi/\partial x\) and \(\partial\phi/\partial y\) between the end points. All three expressions are zero at both points. So the vector \(\vek w = (\partial\phi/\partial y, -\partial\phi/\partial x)\) is zero at \(P\) and at \(Q\), the resultant \(\vek F = \vek w(Q) - \vek w(P)\) is zero, and the moment is the difference \(0 - 0\). The tension and the compression on the cross section therefore balance, as on the cross sections of the strip above. The same holds for the real part, because the resultant and the moment are linear in the stresses. This is point 3.
  1. The stresses of the function \(\phi\) are \(\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda x}f''(y)\), \(\lambda^2\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda x}f(y)\) and \(\lambda\,\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda x}f'(y)\). The functions \(f\), \(f'\), \(f''\) are continuous on the closed interval \([-b, b]\), so they are bounded (theorem 1.74, applied to the real and the imaginary part and to the absolute value): all three curves stay under some horizontal line. Let \(M\) be the largest of \(\max|f''|\), \(|\lambda|^2\max|f|\), \(|\lambda|\max|f'|\), that is the lowest such line. By point 2 of theorem 9.34 \(|\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda x}| = \mathrm{e}^{-(\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda)x}\), so for example \(|\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda x}f''(y)| = \mathrm{e}^{-(\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda)x}\,|f''(y)| \le M\,\mathrm{e}^{-(\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda)x}\). Every stress of the function \(\phi\) at a point \((x, y)\) is therefore a complex number \(w\) with \(|w| \le M\,\mathrm{e}^{-(\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda)x}\), and the arrow \(w\) lies in the circle of this radius. The absolute value of the real part is not larger than the absolute value of the number (lemma 9.27), because \(\mathrm{Re}\,w\) is the orthogonal projection of the arrow onto the real axis and a projection is not longer than the arrow. As \(x\) grows, the length of the arrow falls in proportion to \(\mathrm{e}^{-(\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda)x}\), and the real part changes sign because of \(\mathrm{Im}\,\lambda\) and still stays in the band between \(-M\,\mathrm{e}^{-(\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda)x}\) and \(M\,\mathrm{e}^{-(\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda)x}\). This is point 4. The edge of the band is \(M\) times the orange envelope of figure 11.107, which is on a logarithmic axis there.

Source: BARBER §6.2.2 (6.11)-(6.22) (PDF pp. 95-96): the solution \(\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda x}g(y)\) with four constants, the boundary conditions \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\) on \(y = \pm b\) and the split into a symmetric and an antisymmetric part, which gives equations (6.21) and (6.22). We compared the systems (6.17)-(6.20) with the crops: they agree with our conditions of step 4 (the second equation of the symmetric system differs from (6.18) only in sign), and the determinants we computed ourselves, because BARBER does not write them. The proof is written here in steps, points 3 and 4 are ours. Papkovich and Fadle: BARBER (PDF p. 98).

The lemma showed that functions \(\phi\) exist only for special \(\lambda\), and that they then have three properties: zero on the edges, self-equilibrated on the cross sections, and for \(x \to \infty\) tending to zero like \(\mathrm{e}^{-(\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda)x}\). Do such \(\lambda\) exist? The equations \(\sin 2z \pm 2z = 0\) have among the real numbers only the trivial solution \(z = 0\) (BARBER, figure 6.2, PDF p. 96), which corresponds to an unbounded axial force or moment. The nontrivial solutions are complex numbers, which according to BARBER we find by writing \(\lambda b = c + \mathrm{i}d\) and obtaining from the real and the imaginary part of the equation two equations for the real unknowns \(c\) and \(d\) (BARBER, PDF p. 96). We computed them with the tool mpmath to 25 digits, by Newton's method from a grid of starting points in the region \(0 < \mathrm{Re}\,z \le 8\), \(0 < \mathrm{Im}\,z \le 3\). The grid found two roots with \(\mathrm{Im}\,z > 0\) in each class, the third we found with a start outside the grid. The first three in each class are (figure 11.110):

  • symmetric class, \(\sin 2z + 2z = 0\): \(z = 2{,}10620 + 1{,}12536\,\mathrm{i}\), \(5{,}35627 + 1{,}55157\,\mathrm{i}\), \(8{,}53668 + 1{,}77554\,\mathrm{i}\),
  • antisymmetric class, \(\sin 2z - 2z = 0\): \(z = 3{,}74884 + 1{,}38434\,\mathrm{i}\), \(6{,}94998 + 1{,}67610\,\mathrm{i}\), \(10{,}11926 + 1{,}85838\,\mathrm{i}\).

At each of them the value of the left side of the equation is smaller than \(10^{-23}\) in absolute value, as far as the precision of the computation allows. This shows that these are roots to the written precision, not that they are the only ones, so for the first two in each class we scanned the grid. If \(z\) is a root, so are \(-z\) (both terms of the equation are odd functions of \(z\)) and \(\bar z\) (by (11.68) \(\sin\bar z = \overline{\sin z}\)), hence also \(-\bar z\). For decaying solutions we take a root with \(\mathrm{Re}\,z > 0\). The real part grows from root to root, so the first root gives the slowest decay. The next root in the symmetric class falls at the distance \(b\) to \(\mathrm{e}^{-5{,}356} = 0{,}47\,\%\), in the antisymmetric class to \(\mathrm{e}^{-6{,}950} = 0{,}096\,\%\).

How large is the decay in numbers? At the distance \(x\) from the end the envelope of the stress is \(\mathrm{e}^{-(\mathrm{Re}\,z)\,x/b}\). The height of the strip is \(2b\), so at the distance of one height, \(x = 2b\), the value is \(\mathrm{e}^{-2\,\mathrm{Re}\,z}\). Every step \(b\) multiplies the envelope by the same factor, in the symmetric class by \(\mathrm{e}^{-2{,}1062} = 0{,}122\) and in the antisymmetric class by \(\mathrm{e}^{-3{,}7488} = 0{,}0235\), so on the logarithmic axis the envelopes are straight lines. The values at the three cross sections are:

  • symmetric class: at \(x = b\) it is \(0{,}1217\), at \(x = 2b\) it is \(0{,}01481\), that is \(1{,}48\,\%\), at \(x = 3b\) it is \(0{,}00180\),
  • antisymmetric class: at \(x = b\) it is \(0{,}02355\), at \(x = 2b\) it is \(0{,}000554\), that is \(0{,}055\,\%\), at \(x = 3b\) it is \(1{,}3\cdot 10^{-5}\).

For its own example BARBER says that the error falls to about \(\mathrm{e}^{-4}\), that is about \(2\,\%\) of the end value, within a distance \(b\) (PDF p. 98). This agrees with our antisymmetric class: for the first root of equation (6.22) BARBER gives \(\lambda_{\mathrm{R}} b = 3{,}7\) (PDF p. 98), we \(3{,}7488\), and \(\mathrm{e}^{-3{,}7488} = 0{,}0235\). BARBER's example is antisymmetric, so it is compared with the antisymmetric class, not with the symmetric one. The envelope alone does not tell everything, because the stress in the cross section oscillates: the real part of \(\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda x}f(y)\) is \(\mathrm{e}^{-(\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda)x}\) times a function that also turns with \(x\) (because of \(\mathrm{Im}\,\lambda\)). Moreover, the term is determined only up to a complex factor \(\kappa \ne 0\): we may multiply the constants \(A\), \(B\) of the lemma by \(\kappa\), and the actual stress is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \mathrm{Re}\,\big(\kappa\,\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda x}f''(y)\big)\). So for the first symmetric term and one chosen \(\kappa\) we also computed the actual distribution of the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) over the height of the strip (figure 11.111). The end \(x = 0\) is on the left, the cross section \(x = b\) and the cross section \(x = 2b\) are dashed, each at the distance \(b\) from the previous one. Below the strip there is, for each of the three cross sections, a panel in which the stress is drawn over the height. The largest absolute value of \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) in the cross section, divided by that at the end \(x = 0\), is \(1\) at \(x = 0\), \(0{,}0547\) at \(x = b\), \(0{,}0205\) at \(x = 2b\) and \(0{,}00153\) at \(x = 3b\). For another \(\kappa\) these ratios are different: over all \(\kappa\) the ratio at \(x = b\) lies between \(0{,}029\) and \(0{,}62\), at \(x = 2b\) between \(0{,}0032\) and \(0{,}051\). The envelope, however, does not depend on \(\kappa\). The largest absolute value of the complex expression \(\kappa\,\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda x}f''(y)\) in the cross section is exactly \(\mathrm{e}^{-(\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda)x}\) times that at the end, because \(|\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda x}| = \mathrm{e}^{-(\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda)x}\) (theorem 9.34, point 2), that is \(12{,}2\,\%\) at \(x = b\) and \(1{,}48\,\%\) at \(x = 2b\). The real part is at most that large in absolute value, but at the end it is much smaller for some \(\kappa\), so the ratio of the real parts can exceed the envelope. After one height of the strip there therefore remains for every \(\kappa\) at most \(5{,}1\,\%\) of the largest stress at the end, for the \(\kappa\) of the figure \(2{,}0\,\%\). For the antisymmetric term and one chosen \(\kappa\) the same value is \(0{,}0200\) at \(x = b\), \(0{,}000598\) at \(x = 2b\) and \(6\cdot 10^{-6}\) at \(x = 3b\), and over all \(\kappa\) between \(0{,}0077\) and \(0{,}080\) at \(x = b\) and between \(0{,}00026\) and \(0{,}0011\) at \(x = 2b\).

Now we have single decaying solutions for each class. Can every self-equilibrated end load arise as their sum? This is the question of completeness (popolnost) of the expansion. BARBER says that the analysis originates from the investigation of Papkovich and Fadle in the early 1940s, and that the formal proof of completeness was completed only by Gregory in 1980 (BARBER, PDF p. 98). We do not prove this, we cite it, and everything that follows in this paragraph holds only on the condition that completeness holds. Assume that two statically equivalent loads of the end of the strip each have their own solution (we do not claim existence). The difference of the loads is a self-equilibrated load (definition 11.48), the difference of the fields of the two solutions is by corollary 11.3 the field for this difference, and if completeness holds, this field is a sum of decaying terms of lemma 11.49. A term with larger \(\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda\) decays faster, so at a large enough distance the term with the smallest \(\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda\) among those with a nonzero coefficient in the expansion prevails, as BARBER says (PDF p. 98). The slowest possible term is \(\mathrm{e}^{-2{,}1062\,x/b}\) in the symmetric and \(\mathrm{e}^{-3{,}7488\,x/b}\) in the antisymmetric class. On this condition Saint-Venant's principle is computed for the strip: the distance is about one to two heights of the strip, and more precisely it is given by \(\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda\) of the first term with a nonzero coefficient. Without completeness we have only single decaying solutions, and no claim about an arbitrary difference.

11.11.3 The cantilever, the bar and the hanging bar (konzola, palica in viseča palica)

Let us return to the question at the beginning of the section (figure 11.112). The cross section of the cantilever of section 11.10 is \(2b = 40\) mm high (\(b = c = 20\) mm), the length is \(L = 1\) m, that is \(25\) heights of the cross section, and the wall is on the left. Each end is so far from the other that we treat it in approximation as the end of a semi-infinite strip. This is our assumption, not a proved claim. It is justified here because the envelope of the first antisymmetric term at the distance \(L = 25\) heights, that is \(x = 50\,b\), is still \(\mathrm{e}^{-3{,}7488\cdot 50} \approx 4\cdot 10^{-82}\) of the value at the end, so one end does not feel the disturbance of the other. It fails for a short piece. If the piece is only one height of the cross section long, \(L = 2b\), then \(5{,}5\cdot 10^{-4}\) of the antisymmetric and \(1{,}5\,\%\) of the symmetric envelope still reach the other end, for \(L = b\) already \(2{,}4\,\%\) and \(12\,\%\), and the disturbances of the two ends overlap. Such a piece is no longer a beam either.

What is the difference at the clamp? A real wall prescribes displacements, not stresses, and we do not know what stress distribution arises at the contact. But we know that the cantilever is in equilibrium (lemma 11.2), so the real load of the wall must have the same resultant and the same moment as the load of the solution from the Airy function, that is the force \(F = 500\) N and the moment \(F L = 500\) N m. The loads are therefore statically equivalent (definition 11.48), and their difference is self-equilibrated. Assume that the body and the real wall are symmetric about the axis \(y = 0\), and the force \(F\) is transverse. We mirror the field across the axis and reverse its sign, and we get a solution for the same load (the mirroring reverses the direction of the force, the sign reversal restores it). By uniqueness of the stresses (theorem 11.4, point 1) the fields are equal, so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) is an odd function of \(y\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) an even one, and the same holds for the difference of the real clamp and the solution. The difference belongs to the antisymmetric class. If completeness from the previous section holds, it is led by the root \(3{,}74884 + 1{,}38434\,\mathrm{i}\) with the factor \(\mathrm{e}^{-3{,}7488\,x/b}\), drawn above the axis \(x\). At the distance \(b = 20\) mm from the clamp the envelope is \(2{,}4\,\%\) (the largest value of \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) in the cross section of the first term, divided by that at the clamp, lies between \(0{,}77\) and \(8{,}0\,\%\) depending on the factor \(\kappa\) of section 11.11.2), at the distance of one height of the cross section, \(40\) mm, \(0{,}055\,\%\) (between \(0{,}026\) and \(0{,}11\,\%\)). That is \(4\,\%\) of the length of the cantilever. The symmetry assumption fails if, for example, the cantilever is welded only along its top edge, or the wall is more compliant at the top than at the bottom. The difference then also has a symmetric part, led by the slower root \(2{,}10620\): its envelope \(\mathrm{e}^{-2{,}1062\,x/b}\) is still \(12{,}2\,\%\) at the distance \(b\) and \(1{,}48\,\%\) at the distance \(2b\), instead of \(2{,}4\,\%\) and \(0{,}055\,\%\). The distance stays one to two heights of the cross section, but what remains is larger.

To see what this means in megapascals, take an example that we choose ourselves, not a measurement: let the difference at the clamp have the largest value of \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) equal to the largest bending stress there, \(93{,}75\) MPa, and let it be only the first antisymmetric term with the factor \(\kappa\) for which we computed the ratios \(0{,}0200\), \(0{,}000598\) and \(6\cdot 10^{-6}\) in section 11.11.2. Then, in the enlarged zone at the clamp, there remains at the distance \(b\) from the clamp \(1{,}87\) MPa, at the distance \(2b\) \(0{,}056\) MPa and at the distance \(3b\) \(6\cdot 10^{-4}\) MPa. For the least favourable \(\kappa\) there would remain \(7{,}5\) MPa at the distance \(b\) and \(0{,}10\) MPa at the distance \(2b\). Under these conditions (completeness, the end as a semi-infinite strip, symmetry) the difference of stresses between the real clamp and the idealized solution lies within the first one to two heights of the cross section, that is the first \(4\) to \(8\,\%\) of the length of the cantilever. Completeness is a proved theorem that we only cite (Gregory 1980 according to BARBER, PDF p. 98). The other two conditions fail where we wrote: for a piece about one height of the cross section long, and for a clamp that is not symmetric.

Section 11.10 showed two idealizations of the clamp. In the first the centre of the clamped cross section and the horizontal segment of the axis stay at rest, \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial x = 0\) at the point \((0, 0)\). In the second the vertical segment of the cross section stays at rest, \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial y = 0\) at the same point. The tip deflection is \(7{,}9719\) mm and \(7{,}9844\) mm, the difference is \(0{,}0124\) mm. The stresses of both solutions are equal, because they have the same Airy function, they differ only in a rigid rotation. The rotation is exactly the shear angle at the point \((0, 0)\), the angle \(\gamma\) by which the right angle between the horizontal and the vertical segment changes (the blackwhite segment stays at rest, the orange one turns, the angle is drawn much enlarged): \(\gamma = 2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}/G = F c^2 (1 + \nu)/(E I) = 0{,}9375\ \text{MPa}/75\,385\ \text{MPa} = 1{,}2436\cdot 10^{-5}\) rad, and \(\gamma L = 1{,}2436\cdot 10^{-5}\cdot 1000\ \text{mm} = 0{,}01244\) mm is the difference of the deflection. For the idealized clamp the choice of which segment stays at rest is therefore the whole difference of the deflection, and the lever arm \(L\) enlarges it. What rotation a real wall would give, this computation does not tell: it would depend on the compliance of the wall, and we did not compute that. The rotation is a rigid motion, so the principle about self-equilibrated differences does not treat it.

The second check is the bar of figure 2.14 of chapter 2, which we pull by two pins. Its height in the units of the figure is \(2\), so \(b = 1\), and the cross sections are at the distances \(0{,}5\), \(1{,}5\) and \(4{,}0\) from the end. The load is symmetric about the axis, so the difference from uniform tension belongs to the symmetric class. The figure in chapter 2 is schematic: its profiles were chosen for illustration, not computed. Here we add computed values. If completeness holds, the envelope of the first symmetric term is \(\mathrm{e}^{-2{,}1062\,x/b}\) and at these distances it has the values \(0{,}349\), \(0{,}0425\) and \(0{,}00022\) of the value at the end. From the first cross section to the second the envelope falls by the factor \(\mathrm{e}^{-2{,}1062} = 0{,}122\), from the second to the third by a further \(\mathrm{e}^{-2{,}1062\cdot 2{,}5} = 0{,}0052\). At the third cross section the envelope is \(0{,}022\,\%\) of the value at the end, so the distribution of the force there is uniform, as the figure shows. This is a computation for a strip: the bar in the figure has pins and is not a semi-infinite strip, so the numbers hold as an order of magnitude.

The third example is the hanging bar of chapter 1 (figure 1.103, right): a steel bar of length \(L = 10\) m and diameter \(10\) mm is held by a ceiling to which it is welded over the whole top face. The field (1.19) holds for ideal holding, in which the top face is loaded by a uniform force per unit area \(\rho g L\) upward. The welded bar has a different distribution on the top face, but the same resultant \(\rho g L A\) and the same moment about the centre of the face: the bar is in equilibrium (lemma 11.2), and its weight acts in the axis of the bar and has no moment about the centre of the top face. The difference is therefore self-equilibrated on the top face. The bar is not a strip but a round cylinder, so we do not have lemma 11.49 for it and there is no number for the distance. What remains is the rule of thumb: a distance of one length of the loaded part, here the diameter \(10\) mm (CM, PDF p. 333), which is \(0{,}1\,\%\) of the length of the bar. The BOWER rule \(r/a > 3\) gives \(30\) mm, which is \(0{,}3\,\%\). In both cases, by the rule of thumb, the field (1.19) is approximately valid in \(99{,}7\,\%\) of the bar or more, and this is just what chapter 1 needed.

From history

LOVE records what Saint-Venant achieved for bending and torsion. By his theories of torsion and bending, the forces and couples that twist and bend a bar are the resultants of stresses on the end cross sections, and these are distributed by perfectly definite laws. The forces and couples that act on real structures are seldom distributed in these ways. The application of the theories in practice therefore rests on a principle introduced by Saint-Venant, which was called the "principle of the elastic equivalence of statically equipollent systems of load" (LOVE, PDF p. 38). By it, the effects of deviations from the assigned laws of loading are unimportant except near the ends of the bent beam or twisted bar, and near the ends they produce only "local perturbations". The condition for the validity of the results in practice is that the length of the beam is a considerable multiple of the greatest diameter of its cross section (LOVE, PDF p. 38).

A measurement also showed the difference between the prediction of the usual beam formula and the stress in a beam. TIMO writes that Maxwell had already shown how stresses are measured photoelastically, but that the engineers of his time did not appreciate his work, and that the next attempt to measure stresses by photoelasticity was made after an interval of forty years by Carus Wilson (TIMO, PDF pp. 290-291). He investigated a rectangular beam loaded at the middle and found a considerable discrepancy between the measured stresses and the predictions of the usual beam formula (TIMO, PDF p. 291, the paper in Phil. Mag. in 1891 according to the note on the same page). The explanation was given by Stokes, who advocated adding the stresses from two fields. The first is the simple radial stress field in a semi-infinite plate (TIMO, figure 210a), the second the field in a freely supported beam loaded by radial tensile stresses equal and opposite to the compressive stresses in the semi-infinite plate. The resultant of these tensile stresses on each half of the beam is balanced by the reaction \(P/2\) at the support and by a horizontal force at the point \(m\), so at the cross section \(mn\) there is a bending moment and an axial force (TIMO, PDF p. 291). With this sum Wilson obtained the results of his experiments with sufficient accuracy (TIMO, PDF p. 291).

We looked at how far from the place of loading two statically equivalent distributions still differ. Lemma 11.49 gave decaying solutions for the strip, and if completeness holds, the difference decays like \(\mathrm{e}^{-(\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda)x}\) with \(\lambda b = 2{,}106 + 1{,}125\,\mathrm{i}\) or \(3{,}749 + 1{,}384\,\mathrm{i}\), which after the distance of one height of the strip is \(1{,}5\,\%\) or \(0{,}055\,\%\). The rule of one length of the loaded part suffices for the cantilever and both bars, and later in this chapter we shall meet it again at the hole in a plate.

Two warnings for what follows. First: the principle requires a self-equilibrated difference. If the resultants of the loads do not agree, the force goes by long paths to a distant support and the stress in between does not fall. Second: a computation that takes the weak condition for a part of the boundary is good only outside the neighbourhood of that part. Where we use it, we shall write which condition is replaced and at what distance the solution holds.
