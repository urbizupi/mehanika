The lecture of this chapter (figures built as it is explained)

Chapter 3 continues where the previous two ended. In chapter 1 we computed from the displacement field how much the material close to each point is stretched and sheared. In chapter 2 we described with stress the forces with which the parts of a body act on each other, and wrote down when these forces are in equilibrium. Nowhere did we ask what the body is made of, so the relation between displacements and strains holds for a steel bar just as for a rubber band, and Cauchy's formula holds for steel, rubber and still water (CM §6.1.1, PDF p. 250, BOWER, PDF p. 92, CM §4.2.2, PDF p. 181).

But for this very reason it does not let us compute how a body deforms. Recall the hanging bar, a steel bar of length 10 m hanging under its own weight. We computed from equilibrium the stress in it, \(\sigma(x) = \rho g\,(L - x)\), but its displacement \(u(x)\) we took from the books in chapter 1. A rubber cord would stretch about two thousand to twenty thousand times more than the steel bar at the same stress (BOWER, Table 3.1, PDF p. 100). We saw this in figure 1.1 in chapter 1: the same force makes the rubber band longer by a piece we can see, and the steel bar by so little that we had to magnify the elongation in the figure. Equilibrium and geometry are the same for both, so the material must tell us the relation between stress and strain.

Counting the unknowns says the same (figure 3.1). In a body we look for fifteen quantities, each depending on the position of the point. Let us write them in three columns: three components of displacement, six different components of strain and six different components of stress (nine, among which, by section 2.8, there are three pairs of equal ones). Now let us draw between the columns the equations we already have. Six equations give the strains from the displacements (section 1.17), so we draw a blue arrow between the first and the second column. Three equilibrium equations (section 2.7) tie the stresses to each other and to the load, so we draw them as a blue bracket beside the third column. Between the second and the third column there is nothing: no equation ties the strains to the stresses. We draw an orange dashed arrow with a question mark. Let us count: there are \(3 + 6 + 6 = 15\) unknowns and \(6 + 3 = 9\) equations, so \(15 - 9 = 6\) are missing (SADD, PDF p. 89).

Nine equations cannot determine fifteen unknowns, however simple the body. Why not is shown by the simplest case in figure 3.2. Every pair of numbers \(x\), \(y\) is a point in the plane with the axes \(x\) and \(y\). One equation in two unknowns, \(x + y = 2\), determines neither \(x\) nor \(y\): it is satisfied by every point of the blue line, for example by the point \((0, 2)\) and the point \((1, 1)\), so by infinitely many pairs. Only a second equation, for example \(x - y = 0\), which is the orange line, picks a single pair, the intersection of the two lines, \(x = 1\), \(y = 1\). In the same way, in a body only six new equations besides the nine we have give hope that the unknowns are determined.

The missing equations are called constitutive equations (konstitutivne enačbe) (CM §6.1.1, PDF p. 250, SADD §4.1, PDF p. 89). The word constitutive comes from the Latin verb constituere, to set up, to compose. The equations say how the matter is constituted in the sense of its response, that is, what strain a stress causes in it. We do not derive them from equilibrium or geometry. They are a model of the behaviour of a real material, set up from measurements and checked against measurements (CM, PDF p. 250, BOWER, PDF p. 92). We will start with the simplest model, in which the stress is proportional to the strain. It describes metals, wood, concrete and many other structural materials well, as long as the strains are small (SADD, PDF p. 90).

This matters because only with these equations can we predict how much a structure will deform and how much energy it will store. The chapter promises this and keeps it: with two numbers obtained from a tension test we will at last compute the displacement of the hanging bar.

The route of the chapter is this. In the first section we will pull a bar of a given material and measure how the stress and the strain change together. From this tension test we will get two numbers that describe the material, and with them we will compute the displacement of the hanging bar (section 3.1.10). In section 3.2 we will build from the test the relation between all components of stress and strain for a material that behaves the same in all directions. In section 3.3 we will add the strains caused by heating, and in section 3.4 materials that behave differently in different directions, like wood. Then in section 3.5 we will develop the mathematics with which we add up a very large number of very small contributions, and with it in section 3.6 we will compute how much energy a material stores when we deform it.
