In chapter 1 we computed from the displacement field how much the material close to each point is stretched and sheared. In chapter 2 we described with stress the forces with which the parts of a body act on each other, and wrote down when these forces are in equilibrium. Nowhere did we ask what the body is made of. The relation between displacements and strains holds for a steel bar just as for a rubber band, and Cauchy's formula holds for steel, rubber and still water. All of this holds for every material (CM §6.1.1, PDF p. 250, BOWER, PDF p. 92).

But for this very reason it does not let us compute how a body deforms. Recall the hanging bar, a steel bar of length 10 m hanging under its own weight. In chapter 2 we computed from equilibrium the stress in it, \(\sigma(x) = \rho g\,(L - x)\), where \(x\) is the depth below the top end. We did not compute its displacement \(u(x)\), however. In chapter 1 we took it from the books. How we get from the stress to the strain depends on the material. A rubber cord would stretch about two thousand to twenty thousand times more than the steel bar at the same stress (BOWER, Table 3.1, PDF p. 100). Counting the unknowns says the same. In a body we look for three components of displacement, six different components of strain and six different components of stress (nine, among which, by section 2.8, there are three pairs of equal ones), fifteen quantities in all, each depending on the position of the point. But we have only nine equations: six that give the strains from the displacements (section 1.17) and three equilibrium equations (section 2.7). Six are missing, and they must say how the material behaves (SADD, PDF p. 89).

The equations that relate stress to strain for a given material are called constitutive equations (konstitutivne enačbe) (CM §6.1.1, PDF p. 250, SADD §4.1, PDF p. 89). We do not derive them from equilibrium or from geometry, as we derived the equations of the previous chapters. They are a mathematical model of the behaviour of a real material, set up from measurements and checked against measurements (CM, PDF p. 250, BOWER, PDF p. 92). We will start with the simplest model, in which the stress is proportional to the strain. It describes metals, wood, concrete and many other structural materials well, as long as the strains are small (SADD, PDF p. 90).

The route of the chapter is this. In the first section we will pull a bar of a given material and measure how the stress and the strain change together. From this tension test we will get two numbers that describe the material, and with them we will at last compute the displacement of the hanging bar. In section 3.2 we will build from the test the relation between all components of stress and strain for a material that behaves the same in all directions. In section 3.3 we will add the strains caused by heating, and in section 3.4 materials that behave differently in different directions, like wood. Then in section 3.5 we will develop the mathematics with which we add up a very large number of very small contributions, and with it in section 3.6 we will compute how much energy a material stores when we deform it.
