Chapter 14 Heat conduction

14.4 Steady conduction in one dimension

So far we have introduced temperature (section 14.1), derived the heat equation from the heat balance of a part of the body (theorem 14.8), and settled what must be prescribed on the boundary (section 14.3). Chapter 5 took the temperature over the height of a beam to be linear when it treated bending caused by temperature, and sent the reader here for the real distribution. This section pays that debt and solves three more problems in which the temperature changes along one direction only: a wall in which heat is also produced, a fin that loses heat from its sides, and a tube. In all four the state is steady (stacionarno). The word is from the Latin stare, to stand: a steady state is one that stands still, because no temperature in the body changes with time, \(T_{\mathrm{t}} = 0\). Then the energy of no part of the body changes, so by theorem 14.8 the heat that flows into a piece or is produced in it also flows out. This one thought, the balance of a piece \([a, b]\) at a temperature that stands still, is the whole section.

The model is the same everywhere. The body is at rest, made of a material with constant conductivity \(k\) (an idealization from section 14.2: over a large range of temperature \(k\) changes, which we do not take into account here), and the heat flux is \(\vek q = -k\,\nabla T\) by Fourier's law (definition 14.3). In every case the temperature is a function of one coordinate only. For the wall that is an idealization, which is good when the wall is much longer in the other two directions than it is thick and we are far from its edges. Near an edge heat also flows along the face, and the one-dimensional calculation fails there. This section does not compute the size of that error, the plane field without this idealization is in section 14.6. For the fin it is an idealization that is good when the fin is thin (the subsection on the fin gives an estimate with a bound). For a tube with insulated ends, however, it is no idealization: the proof of the lemma on the tube shows that the temperature is indeed a function of the radius only.

Symbols that meet other meanings in the book (section 14.1 listed them): \(k\) is the conductivity here, the shear factor in chapter 5, \(q\) is the heat flux density here, a distributed load in chapter 5, and \(c\) is the specific heat, a wave speed in chapter 13. In this section \(k\) and \(q\) appear only as heat quantities.

14.4.1 The plane wall

Take a slab (plošča), say the wall of a furnace or the flange of the beam of chapter 5. It is \(L\) thick, made of a material with conductivity \(k\), and much larger in the other two directions. One face is held at temperature \(T_0\), the other at \(T_{\mathrm{L}} < T_0\), because they touch a warmer and a colder medium. Heat flows from the warmer face to the colder one, so we draw the flux as arrows in the direction of the axis \(x\) across the thickness. We write \(q(x)\) for the component of the heat flux density in the direction \(x\) (definition 14.2): how much heat flows per unit time through a unit of area perpendicular to the axis \(x\). Above the wall we draw the axes \(x\) and \(T\), to see the temperature as a graph, and enter the profile \(T(x)\) from \(T_0\) at \(x = 0\) to \(T_{\mathrm{L}}\) at \(x = L\). What shape this profile has is our question.

Why the flux must be the same everywhere is told by the balance. Look at the piece of the wall between \(x = a\) and \(x = b\), as a column with cross-section of area \(A\). Nothing flows through the sides of the column: the temperature does not depend on the coordinates along the faces, so by Fourier's law both components of the flux along the faces, \(-k\,T_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(-k\,T_{\mathrm{z}}\), are zero. There is no source in the wall. The state is steady, so heat does not accumulate in the piece (theorem 14.8 at \(T_{\mathrm{t}} = 0\)). What flows through the section at \(a\) must therefore also flow through the section at \(b\).

Lemma 14.19 Temperature in a plane wall (temperatura v ravni steni)

Let the wall \(0 \le x \le L\) be made of a material with constant conductivity \(k > 0\), with no heat source, in a steady state, and let the temperature \(T(x)\) be a differentiable function of one coordinate with \(T(0) = T_0\) and \(T(L) = T_{\mathrm{L}}\). Then

\[T(x) = T_0 + (T_{\mathrm{L}} - T_0)\,\frac{x}{L} , \qquad q = \frac{k\,(T_0 - T_{\mathrm{L}})}{L} , \tag{14.17}\]

the flux \(q\) is the same at every point of the wall and flows from the warmer face to the colder one.

Proof

We must show that the derivative of the temperature is constant, and then compute the temperature from a derivative that is zero.

  1. Let \([a, b] \subset [0, L]\) and \(A\) the area of the cross-section. By the balance of a piece (theorem 14.8 at \(T_{\mathrm{t}} = 0\), \(Q = 0\), no flux through the sides, as above) \(A\,q(a) - A\,q(b) = 0\).
  2. By Fourier's law (definition 14.3) \(q = -k\,T'\), so \(T'(a) = T'(b)\) for all \(a < b\) in \([0, L]\). The derivative \(T'\) is therefore a constant number \(s\).
  3. The function \(x \mapsto T(x) - s\,x\) has derivative \(T' - s = 0\) on \((0, L)\) and is continuous, so it is constant (corollary 1.85): \(T(x) = c + s\,x\).
  4. From \(T(0) = T_0\) we get \(c = T_0\), from \(T(L) = T_{\mathrm{L}}\) we get \(s = (T_{\mathrm{L}} - T_0)/L\). This is the first equation (14.17).
  5. The flux is \(q = -k\,s = k\,(T_0 - T_{\mathrm{L}})/L\) by step 2, the same at every point. It is positive when \(T_0 > T_{\mathrm{L}}\), so it flows in the direction of increasing \(x\), from the warmer face to the colder one (lemma 14.4).

Source: Not from a source. BARBER §14.2 (PDF p. 235) is the heat equation it follows from, CM §8.3 (PDF p. 402) the one-dimensional form.

The proof showed what a constant flux means: the slope of the temperature is constant because \(k\) is constant, and so the profile is a straight line. Let us test it on chapter 5.

Example 14.20 Free curvature of a beam from a temperature difference (prosta ukrivljenost nosilca iz razlike temperatur)

The beam of chapter 5 is \(40\) mm high, the coordinate \(y\) runs along the height, the upper face (\(y = 20\) mm) is \(20\) K warmer than the lower. By lemma 14.19 (with \(y\) instead of \(x\) and \(L = 40\) mm) the temperature is linear, \(\theta = g\,y\) with \(g = 20\ \text{K}/0{,}040\ \text{m} = 500\) K/m (NumPy). The heat flux flows from the upper face toward the lower one and has the magnitude \(500\,k\) W/m\(^2\), with \(k\) in W/(m K). The book does not yet have a sourced value of \(k\) for steel, so we write no number for it here. The gradient \(g = 500\) K/m is exactly the one that theorem 5.37 took as given, and the free curvature \(\alpha g = 15\cdot 10^{-6}\cdot 500 = 7{,}5\cdot 10^{-3}\) 1/m is the one shown in figure 5.38. The linearity that chapter 5 assumed is thus a consequence of the steady balance, not an extra assumption, when the beam is of one material and the flux through the height is the only one.

The steel bar of chapter 3 (diameter 10 mm, between rigid walls) has, by this lemma, if its lateral surface is thermally insulated and one end is at \(+30\) K and the other at \(0\) K, a linear temperature with mean \(15\) K, because the mean of a linear function is its value at the midpoint. At a uniform warming by \(30\) K the stress there was \(-88{,}2\) MPa (chapter 3). What the stress is now, section 14.7 computes. The mean temperature is the number we shall need.

The lemma requires \(k\) to be the same everywhere. Take a wall of two layers, as in the right slab of figure 14.13: the first layer \(20\) mm thick with conductivity \(k\), the second layer \(20\) mm thick with conductivity \(3k\) (the ratio is chosen for the example, not taken from any material). The faces are \(20\) K apart. Step 1 of the proof stays: a piece that contains the layer interface has \(q(a) = q(b)\) by the balance, so the flux \(q\) is the same in both layers. Steps 2 to 4 however hold only inside each layer, where \(k\) is constant: in the first the slope is \(s_1 = -q/k\), in the second \(s_2 = -q/(3k)\), so \(s_2 = s_1/3\). The temperature drop is \(-s_1 \cdot 20\) mm in the first layer, \(-s_2 \cdot 20\) mm in the second, \(20\) K together. From that \(q = 20\ \text{K}/(0{,}020/k + 0{,}020/(3k))\ \text{m} = 750\,k\) W/m\(^2\), the slopes are \(-750\) K/m and \(-250\) K/m and the drops \(15\) K and \(5\) K (NumPy). The profile is no longer one segment: it has a kink at temperature \(T_*\) at the interface. What stays constant is the flux \(q\), not the slope. So the assumption of chapter 5 that the temperature is linear over the height holds only for a beam of one material. In a layered beam the temperature is linear only inside each layer, not over the whole height.

14.4.2 A wall with a heat source

What if heat is also produced in the wall, for instance because an electric current flows through it? Let \(Q\) denote the heat that the material produces per unit time in a unit of volume (theorem 14.8), constant over the wall. We hold both faces at the same temperature \(T_{\mathrm{s}}\). We draw the wall with dots that show the source, and above it the axis \(x\) and the axis \(T\).

In a wall without a source the inflow and outflow through a piece were equal. Now they differ by the contribution of the source: what flows into the piece \([a, b]\) at \(a\), plus what is produced in it, must flow out at \(b\). So the flux at the right end of the piece must be greater than at the left, by \(Q\,(b - a)\). The flux is no longer constant: right of the middle it flows in the direction of increasing \(x\), left of it toward smaller \(x\), because heat leaves through the nearer face. In the middle the flux is zero. At the faces it is largest. By Fourier's law heat flows in the direction of falling temperature. Since it flows from the middle toward both faces, the temperature is highest in the middle and falls toward the faces.

Lemma 14.21 Wall with a uniform source (stena z enakomernim virom)

Let the wall \(0 \le x \le L\) be made of a material with constant conductivity \(k > 0\), in a steady state, with a uniform source \(Q\) and a differentiable temperature \(T(x)\) with \(T(0) = T(L) = T_{\mathrm{s}}\). Then

\[T(x) - T_{\mathrm{s}} = \frac{Q}{2k}\,x\,(L - x) , \qquad q(x) = Q\Big(x - \frac L2\Big) . \tag{14.18}\]

The largest temperature is \(T_{\mathrm{s}} + Q L^2/(8k)\) in the middle. Through each face \(Q L/2\) per unit area flows out, together as much as is produced in a section of the wall, \(Q L\).

Proof

We must show that \(T' + Q\,x/k\) is constant, and then use twice that a function with derivative zero has a constant value.

  1. Let \([a, b] \subset [0, L]\) and \(A\) the area of the cross-section. The balance of the piece (theorem 14.8 at \(T_{\mathrm{t}} = 0\)): \(A\,q(a) - A\,q(b) + Q\,A\,(b - a) = 0\).
  2. By Fourier's law \(q = -k\,T'\). From step 1 we get \(-k\,T'(a) + k\,T'(b) + Q\,(b - a) = 0\), that is \(T'(b) + Q\,b/k = T'(a) + Q\,a/k\) for all \(a < b\).
  3. The function \(T' + Q\,x/k\) is therefore constant, \(= s\). So the function \(x \mapsto T(x) + Q\,x^2/(2k) - s\,x\) has derivative zero and is continuous, so it is constant (corollary 1.85): \(T = c + s\,x - Q\,x^2/(2k)\).
  4. From \(T(0) = T_{\mathrm{s}}\) we get \(c = T_{\mathrm{s}}\). From \(T(L) = T_{\mathrm{s}}\) we get \(s\,L = Q\,L^2/(2k)\), so \(s = Q\,L/(2k)\). Substituting: \(T - T_{\mathrm{s}} = Q\,x\,(L - x)/(2k)\).
  5. Because \(x\,(L - x) = L^2/4 - (x - L/2)^2\), the largest value is \(L^2/4\), at \(x = L/2\), so \(T_{\max} = T_{\mathrm{s}} + Q L^2/(8k)\).
  6. The flux is \(q = -k\,T' = -k\,(s - Q x/k) = Q\,(x - L/2)\). At \(x = 0\) it is \(q = -Q L/2\) (out of the wall, in the direction of decreasing \(x\)), at \(x = L\) it is \(q = +Q L/2\). Together \(Q L\) flows out.

Source: Not from a source. CM Example 8.3.2 (PDF p. 406) solves the same balance for a cylinder with a uniform source.

Numbers for a wall of thickness \(L = 40\) mm. Let the ratio be \(Q/k = 10^5\) K/m\(^2\) (chosen for the example, not taken from a material). Then the rise in the middle, the measure in figure 14.14, is \(Q L^2/(8k) = 10^5 \cdot 0{,}04^2/8 = 20\) K, and at a quarter and three quarters of the thickness it is \(15\) K above \(T_{\mathrm{s}}\) (\(x/L = 0\), \(\tfrac14\), \(\tfrac12\), \(\tfrac34\), \(1\) gives \(0\), \(15\), \(20\), \(15\), \(0\) K, NumPy). This temperature is not linear over the height, so theorem 5.37 of chapter 5 does not hold for it. The stresses of a beam with any temperature \(T(y)\), a parabola like this one included, are computed in section 14.7.

The hypothesis that the lemma needs is hidden in the boundary conditions. Take the same wall with a source \(Q > 0\), but let both faces be thermally insulated, \(q(0) = q(L) = 0\). Step 2 of the proof at \(a = 0\) and \(b = L\) gives \(0 + Q\,L = 0\), which is false. There is no steady solution, because the heat that is produced could go nowhere. The temperature indeed rises: the uniform temperature \(T_0 + Q\,t/(\rho c)\) satisfies the equation of theorem 14.8 (the left side is \(\rho c\,T_{\mathrm{t}} = Q\), the right side \(k\,T_{\mathrm{xx}} + Q = 0 + Q\)) and has slope zero at both faces. The same holds for the body treated by lemma 14.18. Without a source, \(Q = 0\), and with both faces insulated a steady solution exists, but it is not unique: any constant \(T\) will do. The difference from the walls of lemmas 14.19 and 14.21 is that here no face is prescribed by its temperature.

14.4.3 The fin

Now comes a problem in which heat leaves from the sides. The heat that a body gives to a fluid is proportional to the area of contact, because \(h\,(T - T_\infty)\) leaves per unit area (equation (14.12)). If that area is too small, thin strips or rods of a well-conducting material are attached to the body, fins (rebra), and the heat leaves through a larger area (CM §8.3.2, PDF p. 403). The name comes from a thin outgrowth that sticks out of a surface: the English word fin is the fin of a fish, the Slovenian rebro is a rib, as in the rib cage.

The model. A rod of length \(L\), with constant cross-section of area \(A\) and perimeter \(P\), made of a material with conductivity \(k\), is attached at \(x = 0\) to a wall that has temperature \(T_0\) and keeps it. The surroundings have temperature \(T_\infty\). Through the lateral surface heat leaves by Newton's law of cooling (equation (14.12)): per unit area \(h\,(T - T_\infty)\), where \(h\) is the heat transfer coefficient of section 14.3. Nothing leaves through the tip at \(x = L\), the surface is insulated. Inside the rod heat flows along the axis, the axial flux \(q = -k\,T'\).

For the idealizations hidden in the model we want a bound and a number, not only words.

  • The temperature over the cross-section is the same. In reality it is higher at the axis than at the surface, because heat has to flow there. An estimate for a round rod of radius \(R\): the heat that the rod gives off at the sides per unit length is \(2\pi R\,h\,\theta_{\mathrm{s}}\), where \(\theta_{\mathrm{s}}\) is the excess at the surface. If we imagine it is brought by a uniform source \(Q_{\mathrm{e}}\) over the cross-section \(\pi R^2\), then \(Q_{\mathrm{e}} = 2h\,\theta_{\mathrm{s}}/R\). The function \(T = T_{\mathrm{s}} + Q_{\mathrm{e}}\,(R^2 - r^2)/(4k)\) has \(T'' + T'/r = -Q_{\mathrm{e}}/(2k) - Q_{\mathrm{e}}/(2k) = -Q_{\mathrm{e}}/k\), so it satisfies the equation with source \(Q_{\mathrm{e}}\) (corollary 11.18) and equals \(T_{\mathrm{s}}\) at the surface. The difference between axis and surface is \(Q_{\mathrm{e}} R^2/(4k) = (hR/k)\,\theta_{\mathrm{s}}/2\). At \(hR/k \le 0{,}1\) that is at most \(5\,\%\) of the excess \(\theta_{\mathrm{s}}\). This is an estimate, not a proof, since the flux toward the surface is not uniformly distributed. The book does not yet have a sourced value of \(k\) for steel, so we give no estimate for steel.
  • The insulated tip. Its area \(A\) against the area of the lateral surface \(P\,L\) is, for a round rod of diameter \(d\), \((\pi d^2/4)/(\pi d\,L) = d/(4L)\), at \(L = 10\,d\) that is \(2{,}5\,\%\). For a shorter rod the error is larger. CM Example 8.3.1 (PDF p. 404) exposes the tip to the surroundings. Our case is its special case in which no heat leaves through the tip.
  • The coefficient \(h\) is constant over the surface and independent of temperature (an idealization that the sources adopt, COOK PDF p. 469: \(h\) depends on the fluid and is usually measured).

Let us look at the balance of a piece \([a, b]\) of the fin. Heat flows in at \(a\) with the axial flux \(A\,q(a)\), flows out at \(b\) with \(A\,q(b)\), and at the sides \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b h\,P\,(T - T_\infty)\,\mathrm{d}x\) leaves, because in every section the perimeter is \(P\) and per unit area \(h\,(T - T_\infty)\) leaves. The state is steady, so the inflow equals the outflow plus the loss at the sides. Why the profile is bent as figure 14.15 shows: heat escapes from the sides everywhere, so the axial flux shortens from the root toward the tip, and at the tip, which is insulated, it reaches zero. The flux is \(-k\,T'\), so the slope must shorten, which means that \(\theta\) bends upward toward the tip. The lateral arrows are shorter too, because \(\theta\) is smaller there.

We write \(\theta = T - T_\infty\), \(\theta_0 = T_0 - T_\infty\) and

\[m = \sqrt{\frac{h\,P}{k\,A}} , \tag{14.19}\]

in units 1/m. The numerator \(h\,P\) tells how much heat per unit length leaves at the sides per kelvin of excess, the denominator \(k\,A\) how well the rod conducts along its axis. A large \(m^2\) means that the heat escapes before it gets far. The length \(1/m\) is the scale on which the temperature changes appreciably, and the dimensionless number \(m L\) tells how many such lengths the fin has. At \(m L \ll 1\) little leaves at the sides and the fin is nearly uniformly warm. At \(m L \gg 1\) the heat is used up already near the root and there is almost none at the tip. A round rod of diameter \(d\) has \(P/A = 4/d\) and \(m^2 = 4h/(k\,d)\).

Theorem 14.22 Temperature and heat of a fin (temperatura in toplota rebra)

Let a rod of length \(L\) have constant cross-section \(A\), perimeter \(P\), conductivity \(k > 0\) and heat transfer coefficient \(h > 0\) at its sides. Let the temperature \(T(x)\) have continuous \(T'\) and \(T''\) on \([0, L]\), \(T(0) = T_0\), let the tip be insulated, \(T'(L) = 0\), and \(\theta = T - T_\infty\), \(m\) as in equation (14.19). Then:

  1. The balance of every piece \([a, b]\) is equivalent to \(\theta'' = m^2\,\theta\) at every point.
  2. The problem \(\theta'' = m^2\,\theta\), \(\theta(0) = \theta_0\), \(\theta'(L) = 0\) has exactly one solution,
    \[\theta(x) = \theta_0\,\frac{\cosh m\,(L - x)}{\cosh m L} . \tag{14.20}\]
  3. The heat that flows into the fin through the root is
    \[\Phi_0 = -k\,A\,T'(0) = k\,A\,\theta_0\,m\,\frac{\sinh m L}{\cosh m L} , \tag{14.21}\]
    and it equals the heat that leaves at the sides, \(\int_0^L h\,P\,\theta\,\mathrm{d}x\). The ratio of this heat to the heat \(h\,P\,L\,\theta_0\) that would leave if the whole fin were at the temperature of the root is \(\tanh(m L)/(m L)\), where \(\tanh s = \sinh s/\cosh s\). This ratio is the effectiveness of the fin (učinkovitost rebra).
Proof

We must show first that the balance gives a differential equation, then that the given function satisfies it and is the only one, and finally compute the heat.

  1. By the balance of a piece \(A\,q(a) - A\,q(b) - \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b h\,P\,\theta\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\). With \(q = -k\,T'\) this is \(k\,A\,\big(T'(b) - T'(a)\big) = \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b h\,P\,\theta\,\mathrm{d}x\).
  2. By the fundamental theorem of calculus (theorem 3.40, item 1) \(T'(b) - T'(a) = \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b T''\,\mathrm{d}x\), because \(T''\) is continuous. Step 1 is therefore \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b f\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\) for every piece, where \(f = k\,A\,T'' - h\,P\,\theta\) is a continuous function (\(\theta' = T'\)).
  3. A continuous function with zero integral over every piece is zero everywhere, as in step 5 of theorem 13.3. So \(k\,A\,T'' = h\,P\,\theta\), that is \(\theta'' = m^2\,\theta\). Conversely, if this holds everywhere, \(f\) has all integrals zero and the balance holds for every piece. This shows item 1.
  4. By the chain rule and lemma 9.42 (item 1) the function \(x \mapsto \cosh m\,(L - x)\) has derivative \(-m\,\sinh m\,(L - x)\) and second derivative \(m^2\cosh m\,(L - x)\). The function in equation (14.20) therefore satisfies \(\theta'' = m^2\theta\), its value at \(x = 0\) is \(\theta_0\), and its derivative at \(L\) is \(-\theta_0\,m\,\sinh 0/\cosh m L = 0\) (lemma 9.42, item 2).
  5. Uniqueness. Let \(\theta_1\) and \(\theta_2\) be solutions and \(w = \theta_1 - \theta_2\). Then \(w'' = m^2 w\), \(w(0) = 0\), \(w'(L) = 0\). Multiply by \(w\) and integrate over \([0, L]\): \(\int_0^L w\,w''\,\mathrm{d}x = m^2\int_0^L w^2\,\mathrm{d}x\). By integration by parts (corollary 3.41) the left side is \(w(L)\,w'(L) - w(0)\,w'(0) - \int_0^L (w')^2\,\mathrm{d}x = -\int_0^L (w')^2\,\mathrm{d}x\), because \(w'(L) = 0\) and \(w(0) = 0\). So \(-\int_0^L (w')^2 = m^2 \int_0^L w^2\). The left side is \(\le 0\), the right \(\ge 0\) (lemma 3.39, item 4), so both are zero, and since \(m^2 > 0\), \(\int_0^L w^2\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\).
  6. If \(w(x_0) \ne 0\), then by continuity \(w^2 \ge w(x_0)^2/2\) on some interval around \(x_0\), and by lemma 3.39 (items 2, 3 and 4) the integral would be positive. So \(w = 0\) everywhere and \(\theta_1 = \theta_2\).
  7. Heat at the root. By step 4 \(\theta'(x) = -\theta_0\,m\,\sinh m(L - x)/\cosh m L\), so \(\theta'(0) = -\theta_0\,m\,\sinh m L/\cosh m L\) and \(\Phi_0 = -k\,A\,\theta'(0)\) is equation (14.21).
  8. Heat at the sides. The function \(x \mapsto -\sinh m(L - x)/m\) has derivative \(\cosh m(L - x)\) (lemma 9.42, item 1), so by the fundamental theorem \(\int_0^L \cosh m(L - x)\,\mathrm{d}x = \sinh m L/m\) and \(\int_0^L h\,P\,\theta\,\mathrm{d}x = h\,P\,\theta_0\,\sinh m L/(m\cosh m L)\). Because \(h\,P = k\,A\,m^2\), this is \(k\,A\,\theta_0\,m\,\sinh m L/\cosh m L = \Phi_0\).
  9. The ratio: \(h\,P\,L\,\theta_0 = k\,A\,m^2 L\,\theta_0\), so \(\Phi_0/(h\,P\,L\,\theta_0) = (m \sinh m L/\cosh m L)/(m^2 L) = \tanh(mL)/(mL)\).

Source: CM Example 8.3.1 (PDF pp. 404 and 405, equations (8.3.10) and (5)), a fin whose tip is exposed to the surroundings. The solution with an insulated tip is a special case of its solution (5) at zero tip coefficient. CM defines after this example the effectiveness of a fin as the ratio of the heat given off to the heat that the surface would give off at the temperature of the root (PDF p. 405). The proof by steps, with a piece of finite length and uniqueness by energy, is not from the sources.

A test on two numbers (NumPy). The lower part of figure 14.15 shows the axis \(x\) and the excess \(\theta\) and the profiles for \(m L = 1\) and \(m L = 2\). At \(m L = 1\) the tip is at \(1/\cosh 1 = 0{,}648\) of the temperature excess at the root, and at a quarter, half and three quarters of the length at \(0{,}839\), \(0{,}731\) and \(0{,}668\). The heat at the root is \(k A \theta_0/L \cdot m L \tanh m L = 0{,}7616\; k A\theta_0/L\) and the effectiveness \(0{,}762\). At \(m L = 2\) the tip is at \(1/\cosh 2 = 0{,}266\), the heat at the root \(1{,}928\; k A\theta_0/L\) and the effectiveness \(0{,}482\). If the whole fin were at the temperature of the root, the heat would be \((m L)^2\,k A\theta_0/L\), that is \(1\) at \(m L = 1\) and \(4\) at \(m L = 2\). The real heat is \(76\,\%\) and \(48\,\%\) of that, because the excess \(\theta\) along the fin is smaller than at the root. The effectiveness is the mean of \(\theta/\theta_0\) over the length of the fin (step 8 of the proof): at \(m L = 1\) \(\theta\) is everywhere at least \(0{,}648\,\theta_0\), at \(m L = 2\) the far half of the fin is only at \(0{,}27\) to \(0{,}41\) of the value at the root and contributes little. A consistency check (step 8): at \(m L = 1\) \((m L)^2\int_0^1\theta/\theta_0\,\mathrm{d}\xi = 0{,}76159\), where \(\xi = x/L\), the same number as \(m L\tanh m L\).

Let us also test what the proof of uniqueness really needs. Without losses at the sides, \(h = 0\), we have \(m = 0\) and step 5 gives only \(\int (w')^2 = 0\), so \(w\) is constant. Since the root is prescribed, \(w(0) = 0\), we get \(w = 0\) and uniqueness holds. But if both ends are insulated (\(w'(0) = 0 = w'(L)\)) and \(h = 0\), every constant \(T\) is a solution, the solution is not unique, as in the wall with insulated faces in the previous section. For uniqueness it is therefore enough that one end is prescribed by temperature or that \(h > 0\) (then \(m^2\int w^2\) is a positive term that also removes a constant difference).

14.4.4 A tube: temperature along the radius

The last problem has an area that changes with the radius. A tube (cev) with inner radius \(a\) and outer radius \(b\), of length \(\ell\), is made of a material with constant \(k\) and has its flat ends thermally insulated. The inner surface is at \(T_{\mathrm{a}}\), the outer at \(T_{\mathrm{b}}\), there is no source in the wall. We take the tube of chapter 11: \(a = 40\) mm, \(b = 60\) mm, the inner surface \(100\) K warmer than the outer. In this tube chapter 11 computed the stresses from the oil pressure and promised the stresses from temperature as well. Section 14.8 computes those, and it needs the temperature along the radius.

We draw two circles with radii \(a\) and \(b\) and the heat flux that flows outward from the inner surface, where it is warmer. Through the lateral surface of a cylinder of radius \(r\) and length \(\ell\) (a circle in the cross-section) there flows, by the definition of the flux (definition 14.2), \(q_{\mathrm{r}}\cdot 2\pi r\,\ell\), since the flux is radial and constant on that surface. In a steady state without a source the same heat flows through every such surface, because nothing accumulates between two of them. The area of the surface, \(2\pi r\,\ell\), grows in proportion to \(r\), so \(q_{\mathrm{r}}\) must fall like \(1/r\). In the figure this is drawn as a shorter arrow at the outer surface. We also draw the axes \(r\) and \(T\) and in them the profile \(T(r)\). It falls faster where the flux is larger, so at the inner surface. What function it is, the lemma shows.

Lemma 14.23 Radial conduction through a tube (radialno prevajanje skozi cev)

Let the tube \(a < r < b\) of length \(\ell\) be made of a material with constant conductivity \(k > 0\), with flat ends thermally insulated, \(T = T_{\mathrm{a}}\) at \(r = a\) and \(T = T_{\mathrm{b}}\) at \(r = b\), with no source, steady. Then the temperature is a function of \(r\) only,

\[T(r) = T_{\mathrm{a}} + (T_{\mathrm{b}} - T_{\mathrm{a}})\,\frac{\ln(r/a)}{\ln(b/a)} , \tag{14.22}\]

and the heat per unit length that flows through every circle of radius \(r\) is the same,

\[\Phi' = \frac{2\pi k\,(T_{\mathrm{a}} - T_{\mathrm{b}})}{\ln(b/a)} . \tag{14.23}\]
Proof

We must show that the function of equation (14.22) solves the problem and that the solution is unique. We find the form of the function from the Laplace operator in polar coordinates.

  1. Let the temperature \(T(r)\) be a function of the radius only. By theorem 14.8 at \(T_{\mathrm{t}} = 0\), \(Q = 0\) and constant \(k\) the equation is \(k\,\nabla^2 T = 0\), and by corollary 11.18 \(\nabla^2 T = T'' + T'/r\), because \(T\) does not depend on the polar angle and on \(z\).
  2. Multiply by \(r\): \(r\,T'' + T' = (r\,T')' = 0\) by the product rule. The function \(r\,T'\) has derivative zero, so it is constant (corollary 1.85), \(r\,T' = C\) and \(T' = C/r\).
  3. By lemma 9.11 (item 1) \(\ln r\) has derivative \(1/r\), so \(T - C\ln r\) has derivative zero and is constant: \(T = A + C \ln r\).
  4. From the boundary conditions \(T(a) = T_{\mathrm{a}}\) and \(T(b) = T_{\mathrm{b}}\) we get \(C = (T_{\mathrm{b}} - T_{\mathrm{a}})/\ln(b/a)\) and \(A = T_{\mathrm{a}} - C\ln a\), which gives equation (14.22) (lemma 9.11, item 2: \(\ln r - \ln a = \ln(r/a)\)).
  5. The function of step 4 satisfies the equation at every point (step 1 in the reverse direction), both boundary conditions on the surfaces, and at the flat ends \(T_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\), so the flux \(-k\,T_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\), as the insulation requires. By theorem 14.17 (item 2, the steady case, \(S_{\mathrm{T}}\) nonempty) it is the only solution, so the temperature is indeed a function of \(r\) only (without the assumption of step 1).
  6. The flux is \(q_{\mathrm{r}} = -k\,T' = -k\,C/r\). Through the lateral surface of a cylinder of radius \(r\) and length \(\ell\) there flows \(q_{\mathrm{r}}\cdot 2\pi r\,\ell = -2\pi k\,C\,\ell\), which does not depend on \(r\). Per unit length that is \(-2\pi k\,C = 2\pi k\,(T_{\mathrm{a}} - T_{\mathrm{b}})/\ln(b/a)\).

Source: CM §8.3.3 (PDF pp. 405 and 406): an axisymmetric cylinder, equation (8.3.11), Example 8.3.2 with a source. SADD (12.7.10) to (12.7.12) (PDF pp. 390 and 391): the same equation and the solution \(T_{\mathrm{i}}\,\ln(r_{\mathrm{o}}/r)/\ln(r_{\mathrm{o}}/r_{\mathrm{i}})\) for an annular plate (Example 12.2), the equation with the logarithm. The proof by steps and uniqueness is not from the sources.

Numbers for the tube \(a = 40\) mm, \(b = 60\) mm, \(T_{\mathrm{a}} - T_{\mathrm{b}} = 100\) K, \(T_{\mathrm{b}} = 0\) (NumPy): \(\ln(b/a) = 0{,}4055\). The temperature is \(T(r) = 100\ln(60/r)/\ln 1{,}5\) and at \(r = 40\), \(45\), \(50\), \(55\), \(60\) mm it is \(100\), \(70{,}95\), \(44{,}97\), \(21{,}46\) and \(0\) K. The slope is \(-6{,}17\) K/mm at \(r = 40\) mm and \(-4{,}11\) K/mm at \(60\) mm, the ratio is \(1{,}5 = b/a\), as it must be (\(r\,T'\) constant). The heat per unit length is \(\Phi' = 2\pi k \cdot 100/0{,}4055 = 1549{,}6\,k\) W/m (\(k\) in W/(m K)).

COOK (PDF p. 484, equation (12.6-1)) estimates the flux through a pipe wall as if the wall were plane: \(q = k\,\Delta T/\Delta r\). For our tube that is \(k\cdot 100\ \text{K}/20\ \text{mm} = 5\,k\) K/mm. The true flux is \(6{,}17\,k\) K/mm at the inner and \(4{,}11\,k\) K/mm at the outer surface, the estimate lies between them. COOK calls it an upper limit because it takes the temperatures of the fluids at the surfaces, whose difference is larger than that of the surfaces themselves. With prescribed surface temperatures, as here, the estimate is not an upper bound of the flux at the inner surface. If we multiply it by the perimeter of the middle circle (\(r = 50\) mm), we get \(2\pi\cdot 50\cdot 5 = 1570{,}8\,k\) W/m, which is \(1{,}4\,\%\) more than the true value. The difference is in the radius: the true value corresponds to the radius \((b - a)/\ln(b/a) = 49{,}33\) mm instead of the arithmetic mean \(50\) mm. For a thin wall, \(b/a \to 1\), the ratio of the fluxes at the surfaces, \(b/a\), tends to \(1\) and the estimate from a plane wall is good. This is an estimate of the size of the error, not a proof.

Why not use lemma 14.19 here as well? Let \(T\) be linear in \(r\) between \(T_{\mathrm{a}}\) and \(T_{\mathrm{b}}\). The flux \(q_{\mathrm{r}} = -k\,T'\) would then be the same everywhere. Through the circle of radius \(60\) mm there would flow \(1{,}5\) times as much heat as through the circle of radius \(40\) mm, because the perimeter is \(1{,}5\) times larger, and half of the first value would be created from nothing. The linear profile does not satisfy the balance, because in a wall the area along the flow is constant, and in a tube it grows. The linear profile in figure 14.16 shows it: at \(r = 50\) mm it gives \(50\) K, the true value is \(44{,}97\) K.

What we have seen. In all four problems the idea was one and the same: the balance of a piece at a steady temperature, then Fourier's law, a derivative zero means a constant value, and the boundary conditions fix the constants. A wall without a source has a constant flux and a linear temperature (and so the linear \(\theta\) of chapter 5), a wall with a source a parabola, the fin a hyperbolic cosine with the scale \(m L\), the tube a logarithm, and in each the result is unique, by the balance or by energy uniqueness. What we use later: the temperature of the tube is the datum for the stresses in the tube in section 14.8, the parabola of the wall for a beam with any temperature in section 14.7, and the fin is a case that we shall solve with finite elements in section 14.10 and compare with the function \(\cosh\). The next section drops the steadiness: what happens to the temperature of a bar while it changes with time.
