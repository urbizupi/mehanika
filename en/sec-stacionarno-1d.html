Chapter 14 Heat conduction

14.4 Steady conduction in one dimension

So far we have introduced temperature (section 14.1), derived the heat equation from the heat balance of a part of the body (theorem 14.8), and settled what must be prescribed on the boundary (section 14.3). Chapter 5 took the temperature over the height of a beam to be linear when it treated bending caused by temperature, and sent the reader here for the real distribution. This section pays that debt and solves three more problems in which the temperature changes along one direction only: a wall in which heat is also produced, a fin that loses heat from its sides, and a tube. In all four the state is steady (stacionarno). The word is from the Latin stare, to stand: a steady state is one that stands still, because no temperature in the body changes with time, \(T_{\mathrm{t}} = 0\). Then the energy of no part of the body changes, so by theorem 14.8 the heat that flows into a piece or is produced in it also flows out. This one thought, the balance of a piece \([a, b]\) at a temperature that stands still, is the whole section.

The model is the same everywhere. The body is at rest, made of a material with constant conductivity \(k\) (an idealization from section 14.2: over a large range of temperature \(k\) changes, what that changes we show at the wall), and the heat flux is \(\vek q = -k\,\nabla T\) by Fourier's law (definition 14.3). In every case the temperature is a function of one coordinate only. For the wall that is an idealization, which is good when the wall is much longer in the other two directions than it is thick and we are far from its edges. Near an edge heat also flows along the face, and the one-dimensional calculation fails there. How far this error reaches is told by the series (14.32) of section 14.6, which computes the plane field without this idealization. With faces at prescribed temperature the departure from the one-dimensional profile is a sum of terms of this series, in which \(a\) plays the role of the thickness \(L\), and each term decays with the distance \(d\) from the edge at least like \(\mathrm e^{-\pi d/L}\). At \(d = L\), \(4\,\%\) of its value at the edge remains, at \(d = 2L\) only \(0{,}2\,\%\) (NumPy). In a furnace wall \(4\) cm thick, \(8\) cm from the edge only \(0{,}2\,\%\) of each term of the disturbance thus remains. For the fin it is an idealization that is good when the fin is thin (the subsection on the fin gives an estimate with a bound). For a tube with insulated ends, however, it is no idealization: the proof of the lemma on the tube shows that the temperature is indeed a function of the radius only.

14.4.1 The plane wall

Take a slab (plošča), say the wall of a furnace or the flange of the beam of chapter 5. It is \(L\) thick, made of a material with conductivity \(k\), and much larger in the other two directions. One face is held at temperature \(T_0\), the other at \(T_{\mathrm{L}} < T_0\), because they touch a warmer and a colder medium. Heat flows from the warmer face to the colder one, so we draw the flux as arrows in the direction of the axis \(x\) across the thickness. We write \(q(x)\) for the component of the heat flux density in the direction \(x\) (definition 14.2): how much heat flows per unit time through a unit of area perpendicular to the axis \(x\). Above the wall we draw the axes \(x\) and \(T\), to see the temperature as a graph, and enter the profile \(T(x)\) from \(T_0\) at \(x = 0\) to \(T_{\mathrm{L}}\) at \(x = L\). What shape this profile has is our question.

Why the flux must be the same everywhere is told by the balance. Look at the piece of the wall between \(x = a\) and \(x = b\), a column with cross-section of area \(A\), which the section shows as a rectangle. Nothing flows through its side faces above and below, nor through the other two, which the section does not show: the temperature does not depend on the coordinates along the faces, so by Fourier's law both components of the flux along the faces, \(-k\,T_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(-k\,T_{\mathrm{z}}\), are zero. There is no source in the wall. The state is steady, so heat does not accumulate in the piece (theorem 14.8 at \(T_{\mathrm{t}} = 0\)). What flows through the section at \(a\) must therefore also flow through the section at \(b\).

The proof of the lemma will obtain a line from a constant derivative. On the left we draw the axes \(x\) and \(T\) and the graph of the temperature \(T\), which has the slope \(s\) at every point. Beside it we draw the line \(s\,x\) through the origin, with the same slope. On every small piece both lines rise by the same amount, so the vertical gap between them never changes, and the difference \(T - s\,x\) is a horizontal line at the height \(c\). On the right we draw the axes and the line that must pass through both prescribed temperatures. Its initial height at \(x = 0\) is \(T_0\), and its slope is the change of height \(T_{\mathrm{L}} - T_0\) over the length \(L\), divided by \(L\).

Lemma 14.19 Temperature in a plane wall (temperatura v ravni steni)

Let the wall \(0 \le x \le L\) be made of a material with constant conductivity \(k > 0\), with no heat source, in a steady state, and let the temperature \(T(x)\) be a differentiable function of one coordinate with \(T(0) = T_0\) and \(T(L) = T_{\mathrm{L}}\). Then

\[T(x) = T_0 + (T_{\mathrm{L}} - T_0)\,\frac{x}{L} , \qquad q = \frac{k\,(T_0 - T_{\mathrm{L}})}{L} , \tag{14.17}\]

the flux \(q\) is the same at every point of the wall and flows from the warmer face to the colder one.

Proof

We must show that the derivative of the temperature is constant, and then compute the temperature from a derivative that is zero.

  1. Let \([a, b] \subset [0, L]\) and \(A\) the area of the cross-section. By the balance of a piece (theorem 14.8 at \(T_{\mathrm{t}} = 0\), \(Q = 0\), no flux through the sides, as above) \(A\,q(a) - A\,q(b) = 0\): the inflow at \(a\) equals the outflow at \(b\).
  2. By Fourier's law (definition 14.3) \(q = -k\,T'\), so \(T'(a) = T'(b)\) for all \(a < b\) in \([0, L]\). The derivative \(T'\) is therefore a constant number \(s\), the slope of the profile is the same everywhere.
  3. The function \(x \mapsto T(x) - s\,x\) has derivative \(T' - s = 0\) on \((0, L)\) and is continuous, so it is constant (corollary 1.86): \(T(x) = c + s\,x\). The graph of \(T\) is the line \(s\,x\), raised by the same gap \(c\) everywhere.
  4. From \(T(0) = T_0\) we get \(c = T_0\), from \(T(L) = T_{\mathrm{L}}\) we get \(s = (T_{\mathrm{L}} - T_0)/L\), the change of height over the length \(L\), divided by \(L\). This is the first equation (14.17).
  5. The flux is \(q = -k\,s = k\,(T_0 - T_{\mathrm{L}})/L\) by step 2, the same at every point. It is positive when \(T_0 > T_{\mathrm{L}}\), so it flows in the direction of increasing \(x\), from the warmer face to the colder one (lemma 14.4).

Source: Not from a source. BARBER §14.2 (PDF p. 235) is the heat equation it follows from, CM §8.3 (PDF p. 402) the one-dimensional form.

The proof showed what a constant flux means: the slope of the temperature is constant because \(k\) is constant, and so the profile is a straight line. The assumption of constant conductivity entered only in step 2. Step 1 does not need it, so the flux is the same everywhere also with a conductivity that changes with temperature. The slope, however, is then no longer constant: from \(q = -k\,T'\) the steepness \(-T' = q/k\) is larger where \(k\) is smaller, and the profile bends. By how much, an example tells. Let the faces be \(20\) K apart and let \(k\) grow uniformly with temperature, so that at the warm face it is \(10\,\%\) larger than at the cold one (the number is chosen for the example). Since \(k(T)\,T' = -q\) is constant, by the chain rule the function \(\int_{\mathrm{T}_\mathrm{L}}^{T(x)} k\,\mathrm{d}T\) is linear in \(x\) and at the middle of the wall has half the value it has at the warm face. From this the temperature at the middle is \(10{,}24\) K above the cold face instead of \(10\) K, the departure is \(1{,}2\,\%\) of the difference between the faces (SymPy). For a small relative change \(\varepsilon\) of the conductivity between the faces the departure at the middle is about \(\varepsilon/8\) of the difference between the faces. The assumption is therefore good when \(k\) changes little over the range of temperature of the wall, and this estimate tells how far the profile departs from a straight segment. Let us test the lemma on chapter 5 as well.

Example 14.20 Free curvature of a beam from a temperature difference (prosta ukrivljenost nosilca iz razlike temperatur)

The beam of chapter 5 is \(40\) mm high, the coordinate \(y\) runs along the height, the upper face (\(y = 20\) mm) is \(20\) K warmer than the lower. By lemma 14.19 (with \(y\) instead of \(x\) and \(L = 40\) mm) the temperature is linear like the profile in the wall, \(\theta = g\,y\) with \(g = 20\ \text{K}/0{,}040\ \text{m} = 500\) K/m (NumPy). The heat flux flows from the upper face toward the lower one and has the magnitude \(500\,k\) W/m\(^2\), with \(k\) in W/(m K). The book does not yet have a sourced value of \(k\) for steel, so we write no number for it here. The gradient \(g = 500\) K/m is exactly the one that theorem 5.37 took as given, and the free curvature \(\alpha g = 15\cdot 10^{-6}\cdot 500 = 7{,}5\cdot 10^{-3}\) 1/m is the one with which the free cantilever bends in figure 5.80. The linearity that chapter 5 assumed is thus a consequence of the steady balance, not an extra assumption, when the beam is of one material and the flux through the height is the only one.

The steel bar of chapter 3 (diameter 10 mm, between rigid walls) has, by this lemma, if its lateral surface is thermally insulated and one end is at \(+30\) K and the other at \(0\) K, a linear temperature with mean \(15\) K. The mean of a linear function is its value at the midpoint, as the horizontal line through the middle of the profile shows. Left of the middle the profile is above the line, right of it below, and since the profile is a straight segment, the grey triangle above the line equals the grey triangle below it. The area under the profile is therefore the area of a rectangle of height \((T_0 + T_{\mathrm{L}})/2\), and the mean, which is this area divided by the length, is this height, here \((30 + 0)/2 = 15\) K. At a uniform warming by \(30\) K the stress there was \(-88{,}2\) MPa (chapter 3). What the stress is now, section 14.7 computes. The mean temperature is the number we shall need.

The lemma requires \(k\) to be the same everywhere. Take a wall of two layers, as in the right slab of figure 14.32: the first layer \(20\) mm thick with conductivity \(k\), the second layer \(20\) mm thick with conductivity \(3k\) (the ratio is chosen for the example, not taken from any material). The faces are \(20\) K apart. Step 1 of the proof stays: a piece that contains the layer interface has \(q(a) = q(b)\) by the balance, so the flux \(q\) is the same in both layers. Steps 2 to 4 however hold only inside each layer, where \(k\) is constant: in the first the slope is \(s_1 = -q/k\), in the second \(s_2 = -q/(3k)\), so \(s_2 = s_1/3\). The temperature drop is \(-s_1 \cdot 20\) mm in the first layer, \(-s_2 \cdot 20\) mm in the second, \(20\) K together. From that \(q = 20\ \text{K}/(0{,}020/k + 0{,}020/(3k))\ \text{m} = 750\,k\) W/m\(^2\), the slopes are \(-750\) K/m and \(-250\) K/m and the drops \(15\) K and \(5\) K (NumPy). The profile is no longer one segment: it has a kink at temperature \(T_*\) at the interface. What stays constant is the flux \(q\), not the slope. So the assumption of chapter 5 that the temperature is linear over the height holds only for a beam of one material.

14.4.2 A wall with a heat source

What if heat is also produced in the wall, for instance because an electric current flows through it? Let \(Q\) denote the heat that the material produces per unit time in a unit of volume (theorem 14.8), constant over the wall. We hold both faces at the same temperature \(T_{\mathrm{s}}\). We draw the wall with dots that show the source, and above it the axis \(x\) and the axis \(T\).

In a wall without a source the inflow and outflow through a piece were equal. Now they differ by the contribution of the source: what flows into the piece \([a, b]\) at \(a\), plus what is produced in it (the dots in the piece), must flow out at \(b\). So the flux at the right end of the piece must be greater than at the left, by \(Q\,(b - a)\). The flux is no longer constant: right of the middle it flows in the direction of increasing \(x\), left of it toward smaller \(x\), because heat leaves through the nearer face. In the middle the flux is zero. At the faces it is largest. By Fourier's law heat flows in the direction of falling temperature. Since it flows from the middle toward both faces, the temperature is highest in the middle and falls toward the faces.

The proof of the lemma will write the balance with the derivative of the temperature. On the left we draw the axis \(x\) across the thickness of the wall and above it the derivative \(T'\), as it will come out of the proof: it falls along a straight line. The line \(Q\,x/k\) rises with the same steepness, so their sum is horizontal. On the piece \([a, b]\) this means two vertical segments of equal length, the fall of one line and the rise of the other. On the right we draw the axes, the line \(s\,x\) and the parabola \(Q\,x^2/(2k)\). Both start from zero. Since the temperature at \(x = L\) is again \(T_{\mathrm{s}}\), they must meet again at \(x = L\), and the vertical gap between them is the rise of the temperature above \(T_{\mathrm{s}}\).

Lemma 14.21 Wall with a uniform source (stena z enakomernim virom)

Let the wall \(0 \le x \le L\) be made of a material with constant conductivity \(k > 0\), in a steady state, with a uniform source \(Q\) and a differentiable temperature \(T(x)\) with \(T(0) = T(L) = T_{\mathrm{s}}\). Then

\[T(x) - T_{\mathrm{s}} = \frac{Q}{2k}\,x\,(L - x) , \qquad q(x) = Q\Big(x - \frac L2\Big) . \tag{14.18}\]

The largest temperature is \(T_{\mathrm{s}} + Q L^2/(8k)\) in the middle. Through each face \(Q L/2\) per unit area flows out, together as much as is produced in a section of the wall, \(Q L\).

Proof

We must show that \(T' + Q\,x/k\) is constant, and then use twice that a function with derivative zero has a constant value.

  1. Let \([a, b] \subset [0, L]\) and \(A\) the area of the cross-section. The balance of the piece (theorem 14.8 at \(T_{\mathrm{t}} = 0\)): \(A\,q(a) - A\,q(b) + Q\,A\,(b - a) = 0\). The inflow and the heat produced in the piece together equal the outflow.
  2. By Fourier's law \(q = -k\,T'\). From step 1 we get \(-k\,T'(a) + k\,T'(b) + Q\,(b - a) = 0\), that is \(T'(b) + Q\,b/k = T'(a) + Q\,a/k\) for all \(a < b\). On the piece \([a, b]\) \(T'\) falls by \(Q\,(b - a)/k\) and \(Q\,x/k\) rises by the same amount.
  3. The function \(T' + Q\,x/k\) is therefore constant, \(= s\). So the function \(x \mapsto T(x) + Q\,x^2/(2k) - s\,x\) has derivative zero and is continuous, so it is constant (corollary 1.86): \(T = c + s\,x - Q\,x^2/(2k)\), the line \(s\,x\) minus the parabola \(Q\,x^2/(2k)\), raised by \(c\).
  4. From \(T(0) = T_{\mathrm{s}}\) we get \(c = T_{\mathrm{s}}\). From \(T(L) = T_{\mathrm{s}}\) we get \(s\,L = Q\,L^2/(2k)\), the line and the parabola meet at \(x = L\), so \(s = Q\,L/(2k)\). Substituting: \(T - T_{\mathrm{s}} = Q\,x\,(L - x)/(2k)\).
  5. Because \(x\,(L - x) = L^2/4 - (x - L/2)^2\), the largest value is \(L^2/4\), at \(x = L/2\), so \(T_{\max} = T_{\mathrm{s}} + Q L^2/(8k)\).
  6. The flux is \(q = -k\,T' = -k\,(s - Q x/k) = Q\,(x - L/2)\). At \(x = 0\) it is \(q = -Q L/2\) (out of the wall, in the direction of decreasing \(x\)), at \(x = L\) it is \(q = +Q L/2\). Together \(Q L\) flows out.

Source: Not from a source. CM Example 8.3.2 (PDF p. 406) solves the same balance for a cylinder with a uniform source.

Numbers for a wall of thickness \(L = 40\) mm. Let the ratio be \(Q/k = 10^5\) K/m\(^2\) (chosen for the example, not taken from a material). Then the rise in the middle, the measure in figure 14.34, is \(Q L^2/(8k) = 10^5 \cdot 0{,}04^2/8 = 20\) K, and at a quarter and three quarters of the thickness it is \(15\) K above \(T_{\mathrm{s}}\) (NumPy). Why exactly three quarters of the peak: the product \(x\,(L - x)\) is \(\tfrac14 \cdot \tfrac34\,L^2 = \tfrac{3}{16}L^2\) at \(x = L/4\), and \(\tfrac14 L^2\) in the middle. This temperature is not linear over the height, so theorem 5.37 of chapter 5 does not hold for it. The stresses of a beam with any temperature \(T(y)\), a parabola like this one included, are computed in section 14.7.

The hypothesis that the lemma needs is hidden in the boundary conditions. Take the same wall with a source \(Q > 0\), but let both faces be thermally insulated, \(q(0) = q(L) = 0\). Step 2 of the proof at \(a = 0\) and \(b = L\) gives \(0 + Q\,L = 0\), which is false. There is no steady solution, because the heat that is produced could go nowhere. The temperature indeed rises: the uniform temperature \(T_0 + Q\,t/(\rho c)\) satisfies the equation of theorem 14.8 (the left side is \(\rho c\,T_{\mathrm{t}} = Q\), the right side \(k\,T_{\mathrm{xx}} + Q = 0 + Q\)) and has slope zero at both faces. The same holds for the body treated by lemma 14.18. Without a source, \(Q = 0\), and with both faces insulated a steady solution exists, but it is not unique: any constant \(T\) will do. The difference from the walls of lemmas 14.19 and 14.21 is that here no face is prescribed by its temperature.

14.4.3 The fin

Now comes a problem in which heat leaves from the sides. The heat that a body gives to a fluid is proportional to the area of contact, because \(h\,(T - T_\infty)\) leaves per unit area (equation (14.12)). If that area is too small, thin strips or rods of a well-conducting material are attached to the body, fins (rebra), and the heat leaves through a larger area (CM §8.3.2, PDF p. 403). The name comes from a thin outgrowth that sticks out of a surface: the English word fin is the fin of a fish, the Slovenian rebro is a rib, as in the rib cage. Heat travels along the fin, from the root to the end, and escapes at the sides into the surroundings. We want to know what the temperature along the fin is and how much heat the fin gives off.

The model. A rod of length \(L\), with constant cross-section of area \(A\) and perimeter \(P\), made of a material with conductivity \(k\), is attached at \(x = 0\) to a wall that has temperature \(T_0\) and keeps it. The surroundings have temperature \(T_\infty\). Through the lateral surface heat leaves by Newton's law of cooling (equation (14.12)): per unit area \(h\,(T - T_\infty)\), where \(h\) is the heat transfer coefficient of section 14.3. Nothing leaves through the tip at \(x = L\), the surface is insulated. Inside the rod heat flows along the axis, the axial flux \(q = -k\,T'\).

For the idealizations hidden in the model we want a bound and a number, not only words.

  • The temperature over the cross-section is the same. In reality it is higher at the axis than at the surface, because heat has to flow there. An estimate for a round rod of radius \(R\): the heat that the rod gives off at the sides per unit length is \(2\pi R\,h\,\theta_{\mathrm{s}}\), where \(\theta_{\mathrm{s}}\) is the excess at the surface. If we imagine it is brought by a uniform source \(Q_{\mathrm{e}}\) over the cross-section \(\pi R^2\), then \(Q_{\mathrm{e}} = 2h\,\theta_{\mathrm{s}}/R\). The function \(T = T_{\mathrm{s}} + Q_{\mathrm{e}}\,(R^2 - r^2)/(4k)\) has \(T'' + T'/r = -Q_{\mathrm{e}}/(2k) - Q_{\mathrm{e}}/(2k) = -Q_{\mathrm{e}}/k\), so it satisfies the equation with source \(Q_{\mathrm{e}}\) (corollary 11.18) and equals \(T_{\mathrm{s}}\) at the surface. The difference between axis and surface is \(Q_{\mathrm{e}} R^2/(4k) = (hR/k)\,\theta_{\mathrm{s}}/2\). At \(hR/k \le 0{,}1\) that is at most \(5\,\%\) of the excess \(\theta_{\mathrm{s}}\). This is an estimate, not a proof, since the flux toward the surface is not uniformly distributed.
  • The insulated tip. Its area \(A\) against the area of the lateral surface \(P\,L\) is, for a round rod of diameter \(d\), \((\pi d^2/4)/(\pi d\,L) = d/(4L)\), at \(L = 10\,d\) that is \(2{,}5\,\%\). For a shorter rod the error is larger. CM Example 8.3.1 (PDF p. 404) exposes the tip to the surroundings. Our case is its special case in which no heat leaves through the tip.
  • The coefficient \(h\) is constant over the surface and independent of temperature (an idealization that the sources adopt). It does not always hold: \(h\) is usually measured, because it depends on the shape and roughness of the surface and on the velocity, viscosity, density and specific heat of the fluid, some of which change with temperature (COOK PDF p. 469), and the temperature changes along the fin. How sensitive the result is to \(h\): since \(m\) grows like \(\sqrt h\), an \(h\) larger by \(10\,\%\) makes the heat of the fin (equation (14.21) below) larger by \(7{,}6\,\%\) at \(m L = 1\) and by \(5{,}6\,\%\) at \(m L = 2\) (NumPy).

Let us look at the balance of a piece \([a, b]\) of the fin. Heat flows in at \(a\) with the axial flux \(A\,q(a)\), flows out at \(b\) with \(A\,q(b)\), and at the sides \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b h\,P\,(T - T_\infty)\,\mathrm{d}x\) leaves, because in every section the perimeter is \(P\) and per unit area \(h\,(T - T_\infty)\) leaves. The state is steady, so the inflow equals the outflow plus the loss at the sides. Why the profile is bent as figure 14.36 shows: heat escapes from the sides everywhere, so the axial flux shortens from the root toward the tip, and at the tip, which is insulated, it reaches zero. The flux is \(-k\,T'\), so the slope must shorten, which means that \(\theta\) bends upward toward the tip. The lateral arrows are shorter too, because \(\theta\) is smaller there.

We write \(\theta = T - T_\infty\), \(\theta_0 = T_0 - T_\infty\) and

\[m = \sqrt{\frac{h\,P}{k\,A}} , \tag{14.19}\]

in units 1/m. The numerator \(h\,P\) tells how much heat per unit length leaves at the sides per kelvin of excess, the denominator \(k\,A\) how well the rod conducts along its axis. A large \(m^2\) means that the heat escapes before it gets far. The length \(1/m\) is the scale on which the temperature changes appreciably, and the dimensionless number \(m L\) tells how many such lengths the fin has. At \(m L \ll 1\) little leaves at the sides and the fin is nearly uniformly warm. At \(m L \gg 1\) the heat is used up already near the root and there is almost none at the tip. Between these extremes lie the profiles for \(m L = 1\) and \(m L = 2\): the larger \(m L\), the colder the tip. A round rod of diameter \(d\) has \(P/A = 4/d\) and \(m^2 = 4h/(k\,d)\).

The proof of the theorem will turn the balance of a piece into an equation at every point. At the upper left we draw the axes and the derivative \(T'\), which is negative at the root and zero at the insulated tip, and below it the second derivative \(T''\). The rise \(T'(b) - T'(a)\) on the piece \([a, b]\) equals the area under \(T''\) between \(a\) and \(b\). On the right we draw the axes and the continuous function \(f\), which has integral zero over every piece, but is supposed to be positive at the point \(x_0\). By continuity it stays above \(f(x_0)/2\) on the band around \(x_0\), and the rectangle below it already has a positive area, which does not agree with the integral zero.

The proof will then check the given solution and show that it is the only one. On the left we draw the axes and the curve \(\cosh m\,(L - x)\) for \(m L = 1\), which we continue past the tip. Since \(\cosh(-u) = \cosh u\) (definition 9.46), the curve is symmetric about the vertical line \(x = L\). So it has its lowest point there and a horizontal tangent, as the insulated tip requires. At the root the value is \(\cosh m L = 1{,}543\), and when we divide the curve by it, we get \(1\) at the root, so \(\theta = \theta_0\). On the right we draw the number line. The left side of the equation from the uniqueness proof lies on the nonpositive part, the right side on the nonnegative part, and they can be equal only at the point zero.

Theorem 14.22 Temperature and heat of a fin (temperatura in toplota rebra)

Let a rod of length \(L\) have constant cross-section \(A\), perimeter \(P\), conductivity \(k > 0\) and heat transfer coefficient \(h > 0\) at its sides. Let the temperature \(T(x)\) have continuous \(T'\) and \(T''\) on \([0, L]\), \(T(0) = T_0\), let the tip be insulated, \(T'(L) = 0\), and \(\theta = T - T_\infty\), \(m\) as in equation (14.19). Then:

  1. The balance of every piece \([a, b]\) is equivalent to \(\theta'' = m^2\,\theta\) at every point.
  2. The problem \(\theta'' = m^2\,\theta\), \(\theta(0) = \theta_0\), \(\theta'(L) = 0\) has exactly one solution,
    \[\theta(x) = \theta_0\,\frac{\cosh m\,(L - x)}{\cosh m L} . \tag{14.20}\]
  3. The heat that flows into the fin through the root is
    \[\Phi_0 = -k\,A\,T'(0) = k\,A\,\theta_0\,m\,\frac{\sinh m L}{\cosh m L} , \tag{14.21}\]
    and it equals the heat that leaves at the sides, \(\int_0^L h\,P\,\theta\,\mathrm{d}x\). The ratio of this heat to the heat \(h\,P\,L\,\theta_0\) that would leave if the whole fin were at the temperature of the root is \(\tanh(m L)/(m L)\), where \(\tanh s = \sinh s/\cosh s\). This ratio is the effectiveness of the fin (učinkovitost rebra).
Proof

We must show first that the balance gives a differential equation, then that the given function satisfies it and is the only one, and finally compute the heat.

  1. By the balance of a piece \(A\,q(a) - A\,q(b) - \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b h\,P\,\theta\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\): the inflow equals the outflow plus the heat that leaves at the sides. With \(q = -k\,T'\) this is \(k\,A\,\big(T'(b) - T'(a)\big) = \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b h\,P\,\theta\,\mathrm{d}x\).
  2. By the fundamental theorem of calculus (theorem 3.55, item 1) \(T'(b) - T'(a) = \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b T''\,\mathrm{d}x\), because \(T''\) is continuous: the rise of the derivative is the area under the second derivative. Step 1 is therefore \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b f\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\) for every piece, where \(f = k\,A\,T'' - h\,P\,\theta\) is a continuous function (\(\theta' = T'\)).
  3. A continuous function with zero integral over every piece is zero everywhere, as in step 5 of theorem 13.3. If \(f\) were positive at the point \(x_0\), then by continuity it would be above \(f(x_0)/2\) on the band around \(x_0\), and the integral over this band would be at least the area of the rectangle below it, positive. For a negative value the same holds with the sign reversed. So \(k\,A\,T'' = h\,P\,\theta\), that is \(\theta'' = m^2\,\theta\). Conversely, if this holds everywhere, \(f\) has all integrals zero and the balance holds for every piece. This shows item 1.
  4. By the chain rule and lemma 9.47 (item 1) the function \(x \mapsto \cosh m\,(L - x)\) has derivative \(-m\,\sinh m\,(L - x)\) and second derivative \(m^2\cosh m\,(L - x)\). The function in equation (14.20) therefore satisfies \(\theta'' = m^2\theta\), its value at \(x = 0\) is \(\theta_0\), and its derivative at \(L\) is \(-\theta_0\,m\,\sinh 0/\cosh m L = 0\) (lemma 9.47, item 2).
  5. Uniqueness. Let \(\theta_1\) and \(\theta_2\) be solutions and \(w = \theta_1 - \theta_2\) (the difference, zero at the root, with zero derivative at the insulated tip). Then \(w'' = m^2 w\), \(w(0) = 0\), \(w'(L) = 0\). Multiply by \(w\) and integrate over \([0, L]\): \(\int_0^L w\,w''\,\mathrm{d}x = m^2\int_0^L w^2\,\mathrm{d}x\). By integration by parts (corollary 3.56) the left side is \(w(L)\,w'(L) - w(0)\,w'(0) - \int_0^L (w')^2\,\mathrm{d}x = -\int_0^L (w')^2\,\mathrm{d}x\), because \(w'(L) = 0\) and \(w(0) = 0\). So \(-\int_0^L (w')^2 = m^2 \int_0^L w^2\). The left side is \(\le 0\), the right \(\ge 0\) (lemma 3.54, item 4), so both are zero, and since \(m^2 > 0\), \(\int_0^L w^2\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\).
  6. If \(w(x_0) \ne 0\), then by continuity \(w^2 \ge w(x_0)^2/2\) on some interval around \(x_0\), and by lemma 3.54 (items 2, 3 and 4) the integral would be positive. So \(w = 0\) everywhere and \(\theta_1 = \theta_2\).
  7. Heat at the root. By step 4 \(\theta'(x) = -\theta_0\,m\,\sinh m(L - x)/\cosh m L\), so \(\theta'(0) = -\theta_0\,m\,\sinh m L/\cosh m L\) and \(\Phi_0 = -k\,A\,\theta'(0)\) is equation (14.21).
  8. Heat at the sides. The function \(x \mapsto -\sinh m(L - x)/m\) has derivative \(\cosh m(L - x)\) (lemma 9.47, item 1), so by the fundamental theorem \(\int_0^L \cosh m(L - x)\,\mathrm{d}x = \sinh m L/m\) and \(\int_0^L h\,P\,\theta\,\mathrm{d}x = h\,P\,\theta_0\,\sinh m L/(m\cosh m L)\). Because \(h\,P = k\,A\,m^2\), this is \(k\,A\,\theta_0\,m\,\sinh m L/\cosh m L = \Phi_0\).
  9. The ratio: \(h\,P\,L\,\theta_0 = k\,A\,m^2 L\,\theta_0\), so \(\Phi_0/(h\,P\,L\,\theta_0) = (m \sinh m L/\cosh m L)/(m^2 L) = \tanh(mL)/(mL)\).

Source: CM Example 8.3.1 (PDF pp. 404 and 405, equations (8.3.10) and (5)), a fin whose tip is exposed to the surroundings. The solution with an insulated tip is a special case of its solution (5) at zero tip coefficient. CM defines after this example the effectiveness of a fin as the ratio of the heat given off to the heat that the surface would give off at the temperature of the root (PDF p. 405). The proof by steps, with a piece of finite length and uniqueness by energy, is not from the sources.

A test on two numbers (NumPy). The lower part of figure 14.36 shows the axis \(x\) and the excess \(\theta\) and the profiles for \(m L = 1\) and \(m L = 2\). At \(m L = 1\) the tip is at \(1/\cosh 1 = 0{,}648\) of the temperature excess at the root, and at a quarter, half and three quarters of the length at \(0{,}839\), \(0{,}731\) and \(0{,}668\). The heat at the root is \(k A \theta_0/L \cdot m L \tanh m L = 0{,}7616\; k A\theta_0/L\) and the effectiveness \(0{,}762\). At \(m L = 2\) the tip is at \(1/\cosh 2 = 0{,}266\), the heat at the root \(1{,}928\; k A\theta_0/L\) and the effectiveness \(0{,}482\). If the whole fin were at the temperature of the root, the heat would be \((m L)^2\,k A\theta_0/L\), that is \(1\) at \(m L = 1\) and \(4\) at \(m L = 2\). The real heat is \(76\,\%\) and \(48\,\%\) of that, because the excess \(\theta\) along the fin is smaller than at the root. The effectiveness is the mean of \(\theta/\theta_0\) over the length of the fin (step 8 of the proof), the height of the rectangle that has the same area as the figure under the profile: at \(m L = 1\) \(\theta\) is everywhere at least \(0{,}648\,\theta_0\), at \(m L = 2\) the far half of the fin is only at \(0{,}27\) to \(0{,}41\) of the value at the root and contributes little. A consistency check (step 8): at \(m L = 1\) \((m L)^2\int_0^1\theta/\theta_0\,\mathrm{d}\xi = 0{,}76159\), where \(\xi = x/L\), the same number as \(m L\tanh m L\).

What the scale \(1/m\) means, another view shows. At \(m L = 2\) we have \(1/m = L/2\), and at this distance from the root the excess has fallen to \(0{,}410\,\theta_0\). For a long fin this becomes a rule for every step of length \(1/m\). By definition 9.46

\[\frac{\cosh m(L - x)}{\cosh m L} = \frac{\mathrm e^{m(L - x)} + \mathrm e^{-m(L - x)}}{\mathrm e^{m L} + \mathrm e^{-m L}} = \mathrm e^{-m x}\,\frac{1 + \mathrm e^{-2m(L - x)}}{1 + \mathrm e^{-2m L}} ,\]

and the last fraction is close to \(1\) when \(m L\) and \(m(L - x)\) are large. Then \(\theta \approx \theta_0\,\mathrm e^{-m x}\): over every length \(1/m\) the excess falls by the factor \(\mathrm e = 2{,}718\), at \(x = 1/m\) to \(0{,}368\,\theta_0\). At \(m L = 2\) the fraction at \(x = L/2\) is \((1 + \mathrm e^{-2})/(1 + \mathrm e^{-4}) = 1{,}115\), and \(0{,}368 \cdot 1{,}115 = 0{,}410\) (NumPy). The fin is somewhat warmer there than an infinitely long fin, because beyond the point \(x\) only a piece of length \(L - x\) remains through which the heat can leave. The scale \(1/m\) is thus the length over which the excess falls by the factor \(\mathrm e\).

We draw the axis \(x\) and on it the square of the difference \(w^2\), which is nowhere negative. At the root it is zero, because the temperature is prescribed there, and at the insulated tip its tangent is horizontal. If \(w \ne 0\) at the point \(x_0\), continuity keeps \(w^2\) above half of that value on the interval around it, marked in orange, and this band would give a positive integral. On the right we draw the case \(h = 0\), where the difference is constant.

Let us also test what the proof of uniqueness really needs. Without losses at the sides, \(h = 0\), we have \(m = 0\) and step 5 gives only \(\int (w')^2 = 0\), so \(w\) is constant. Since the root is prescribed, \(w(0) = 0\), we get \(w = 0\) and uniqueness holds. But if both ends are insulated (\(w'(0) = 0 = w'(L)\)) and \(h = 0\), every constant \(T\) is a solution, the solution is not unique, as in the wall with insulated faces in subsection 14.4.2. For uniqueness it is therefore enough that one end is prescribed by temperature or that \(h > 0\) (then \(m^2\int w^2\) is a positive term that also removes a constant difference).

14.4.4 A tube: temperature along the radius

The last problem has an area that changes with the radius. A tube (cev) with inner radius \(a\) and outer radius \(b\), of length \(\ell\), is made of a material with constant \(k\) and has its flat ends thermally insulated. The inner surface is at \(T_{\mathrm{a}}\), the outer at \(T_{\mathrm{b}}\), there is no source in the wall. We take the tube of chapter 11: \(a = 40\) mm, \(b = 60\) mm, the inner surface \(100\) K warmer than the outer. In this tube chapter 11 computed the stresses from the oil pressure and promised the stresses from temperature as well. Section 14.8 computes those, and it needs the temperature along the radius.

We draw two circles with radii \(a\) and \(b\) and the heat flux that flows outward from the inner surface, where it is warmer. Through the lateral surface of a cylinder of radius \(r\) and length \(\ell\) (a circle in the cross-section) there flows, by the definition of the flux (definition 14.2), \(q_{\mathrm{r}}\cdot 2\pi r\,\ell\), since the flux is radial and constant on that surface. In a steady state without a source the same heat flows through every such surface, because nothing accumulates between two of them. The area of the surface, \(2\pi r\,\ell\), grows in proportion to \(r\), so \(q_{\mathrm{r}}\) must fall like \(1/r\). In the figure this is drawn as a shorter arrow at the outer surface.

We see the same in another way, with the paths of heat along the radii. No path starts or ends between the inner and the outer surface, because there is no source in the wall and heat accumulates nowhere. Every circle between \(a\) and \(b\) therefore crosses the same number of paths, eight in the figure. The outer circle is \(b/a = 1{,}5\) times longer than the inner one, so the paths on it are \(1{,}5\) times sparser, and the flux per unit area is smaller by that factor. We also draw the axes \(r\) and \(T\) and in them the profile \(T(r)\). It falls faster where the flux is larger, so at the inner surface. What function it is, the lemma shows.

The proof of the lemma will obtain a logarithm from the constant product \(r\,T'\). On the left we draw the axes \(r\) and \(|T'|\) and the curve \(|T'| = |C|/r\) for our tube. The constant product \(r\,|T'|\) is the area of a rectangle whose opposite corner lies on the curve: at \(r = a\) it is \(40 \cdot 6{,}166\), at \(r = b\) it is \(60 \cdot 4{,}110\), both times \(246{,}6\) K (SymPy). The rectangles differ only in two strips, which have the same area \(82{,}21\) K. On the right we draw the axes and the same curve. Since \(|T'|\) is the rate at which the temperature falls along the radius, the area under the curve from \(a\) to \(r = 50\) mm is the drop of the temperature \(T_{\mathrm{a}} - T(r) = 55{,}03\) K, the area from \(50\) mm to \(b\) is \(44{,}97\) K, together \(100\) K.

Lemma 14.23 Radial conduction through a tube (radialno prevajanje skozi cev)

Let the tube \(a < r < b\) of length \(\ell\) be made of a material with constant conductivity \(k > 0\), with flat ends thermally insulated, \(T = T_{\mathrm{a}}\) at \(r = a\) and \(T = T_{\mathrm{b}}\) at \(r = b\), with no source, steady. Then the temperature is a function of \(r\) only,

\[T(r) = T_{\mathrm{a}} + (T_{\mathrm{b}} - T_{\mathrm{a}})\,\frac{\ln(r/a)}{\ln(b/a)} , \tag{14.22}\]

and the heat per unit length that flows through every circle of radius \(r\) is the same,

\[\Phi' = \frac{2\pi k\,(T_{\mathrm{a}} - T_{\mathrm{b}})}{\ln(b/a)} . \tag{14.23}\]
Proof

We must show that the function of equation (14.22) solves the problem and that the solution is unique. We find the form of the function from the Laplace operator in polar coordinates.

  1. Let the temperature \(T(r)\) be a function of the radius only. By theorem 14.8 at \(T_{\mathrm{t}} = 0\), \(Q = 0\) and constant \(k\) the equation is \(k\,\nabla^2 T = 0\), and by corollary 11.18 \(\nabla^2 T = T'' + T'/r\), because \(T\) does not depend on the polar angle and on \(z\).
  2. Multiply by \(r\): \(r\,T'' + T' = (r\,T')' = 0\) by the product rule. The function \(r\,T'\) has derivative zero, so it is constant (corollary 1.86), \(r\,T' = C\), the rectangles with a corner on the curve have the same area, and \(T' = C/r\).
  3. By lemma 9.16 (item 1) \(\ln r\) has derivative \(1/r\), so \(T - C\ln r\) has derivative zero and is constant: \(T = A + C \ln r\). In the figure the drop \(T_{\mathrm{a}} - T(r)\) is the area under the curve \(|T'|\) from \(a\) to \(r\), \(|C|\ln(r/a)\).
  4. From the boundary conditions \(T(a) = T_{\mathrm{a}}\) and \(T(b) = T_{\mathrm{b}}\) we get \(C = (T_{\mathrm{b}} - T_{\mathrm{a}})/\ln(b/a)\), the whole area from \(a\) to \(b\) is the whole drop \(T_{\mathrm{a}} - T_{\mathrm{b}}\), and \(A = T_{\mathrm{a}} - C\ln a\), which gives equation (14.22) (lemma 9.16, item 2: \(\ln r - \ln a = \ln(r/a)\)).
  5. The function of step 4 satisfies the equation at every point (step 1 in the reverse direction), both boundary conditions on the surfaces, and at the flat ends \(T_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\), so the flux \(-k\,T_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\), as the insulation requires. By theorem 14.17 (item 2, the steady case, \(S_{\mathrm{T}}\) nonempty) it is the only solution, so the temperature is indeed a function of \(r\) only (without the assumption of step 1).
  6. The flux is \(q_{\mathrm{r}} = -k\,T' = -k\,C/r\). Through the lateral surface of a cylinder of radius \(r\) and length \(\ell\) there flows \(q_{\mathrm{r}}\cdot 2\pi r\,\ell = -2\pi k\,C\,\ell\), which does not depend on \(r\). Per unit length that is \(-2\pi k\,C = 2\pi k\,(T_{\mathrm{a}} - T_{\mathrm{b}})/\ln(b/a)\).

Source: CM §8.3.3 (PDF pp. 405 and 406): an axisymmetric cylinder, equation (8.3.11), Example 8.3.2 with a source. SADD (12.7.10) to (12.7.12) (PDF pp. 390 and 391): the same equation and the solution \(T_{\mathrm{i}}\,\ln(r_{\mathrm{o}}/r)/\ln(r_{\mathrm{o}}/r_{\mathrm{i}})\) for an annular plate (Example 12.2), the equation with the logarithm. The proof by steps and uniqueness is not from the sources.

Numbers for the tube \(a = 40\) mm, \(b = 60\) mm, \(T_{\mathrm{a}} - T_{\mathrm{b}} = 100\) K, \(T_{\mathrm{b}} = 0\) (NumPy): \(\ln(b/a) = 0{,}4055\). The temperature is \(T(r) = 100\ln(60/r)/\ln 1{,}5\):

\(r\) (mm) \(40\) \(45\) \(50\) \(55\) \(60\)
\(T\) (K) \(100\) \(70{,}95\) \(44{,}97\) \(21{,}46\) \(0\)

The value \(44{,}97\) K is the point of the profile at \(r = 50\) mm. The slope is \(-6{,}17\) K/mm at \(r = 40\) mm and \(-4{,}11\) K/mm at \(60\) mm, the ratio is \(1{,}5 = b/a\), as it must be (\(r\,T'\) constant), and the arrows \(q(a)\) and \(q(b)\) are in this ratio. The heat per unit length is \(\Phi' = 2\pi k \cdot 100/0{,}4055 = 1549{,}6\,k\) W/m (\(k\) in W/(m K)).

COOK (PDF p. 484, equation (12.6-1)) estimates the flux through a pipe wall as if the wall were plane, with a constant slope: \(q = k\,\Delta T/\Delta r\). For our tube that is \(k\cdot 100\ \text{K}/20\ \text{mm} = 5\,k\) K/mm. The true flux is \(6{,}17\,k\) K/mm at the inner and \(4{,}11\,k\) K/mm at the outer surface, the estimate lies between them. COOK calls it an upper limit because it takes the temperatures of the fluids at the surfaces, whose difference is larger than that of the surfaces themselves. With prescribed surface temperatures, as here, the estimate is not an upper bound of the flux at the inner surface. If we multiply it by the perimeter of the middle circle (\(r = 50\) mm), we get \(2\pi\cdot 50\cdot 5 = 1570{,}8\,k\) W/m, which is \(1{,}4\,\%\) more than the true value. The difference is in the radius: the true value corresponds to the radius \((b - a)/\ln(b/a) = 49{,}33\) mm instead of the arithmetic mean \(50\) mm. For a thin wall, \(b/a \to 1\), the ratio of the fluxes at the surfaces, \(b/a\), tends to \(1\) and the estimate from a plane wall is good. This is an estimate of the size of the error, not a proof.

Why not use lemma 14.19 here as well? Let \(T\) be linear in \(r\) between \(T_{\mathrm{a}}\) and \(T_{\mathrm{b}}\). The flux \(q_{\mathrm{r}} = -k\,T'\) would then be the same everywhere. Through the circle of radius \(60\) mm there would flow \(1{,}5\) times as much heat as through the circle of radius \(40\) mm, because the perimeter is \(1{,}5\) times larger, and half of the first value would be created from nothing. The linear profile does not satisfy the balance, because in a wall the area along the flow is constant, and in a tube it grows. The linear profile in figure 14.40 shows it: at \(r = 50\) mm it gives \(50\) K, the true value is \(44{,}97\) K.

What we have seen. In all four problems the idea was one and the same: the balance of a piece (of the wall, of the wall with a source, of the fin and of the tube wall between two circles) at a steady temperature, then Fourier's law, a derivative zero means a constant value, and the boundary conditions fix the constants. A wall without a source has a constant flux and a linear temperature (and so the linear \(\theta\) of chapter 5), a wall with a source a parabola, the fin a hyperbolic cosine with the scale \(m L\), the tube a logarithm, and in each the result is unique, by the balance or by energy uniqueness. What we use later: the temperature of the tube is the datum for the stresses in the tube in section 14.8, the parabola of the wall for a beam with any temperature in section 14.7, and the fin is a case that we shall solve with finite elements in section 14.10 and compare with the function \(\cosh\). The next section drops the steadiness: what happens to the temperature of a bar while it changes with time.
