Chapter 11 The boundary value problem of linear elasticity

11.5 Mathematics: cylindrical and spherical coordinates

In the thick-walled tube, the short steel tube with inner radius 40 mm and outer radius 60 mm from section 1.18.5, loaded by an internal pressure, no direction around the axis is special. The stresses will therefore depend only on the distance of a point from the axis. In Cartesian coordinates this distance is \(\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\), a function of both coordinates, and the edge of the tube is the curve \(x^2 + y^2 = 60^2\), which is parallel to no axis. We met the same in section 4.3.7, when we cut the ring into pieces with a map that contained the square root of \(1 + s^2\). Which coordinates make round bodies simple, and how do we differentiate in them?

We will need three things. First, the derivative of a function of the point with respect to a new coordinate, for example with respect to the distance from the axis. Second, the divergence of a field, because the equilibrium equations (theorem 2.28) and the divergence theorem (theorem 4.67) rest on it. Third, the unit vectors in which we write the direction of a force, a displacement or a stress at a point. How these vectors change from point to point is the new part here, since the Cartesian vectors \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) stay the same everywhere. Stresses and strains in these coordinates follow in the next section, here we build only the mathematics.

From history

Lamé and Clapeyron wrote their memoir on the internal equilibrium of homogeneous solid bodies during their service in St. Petersburg. They presented it to the French Academy of Sciences, Poinsot and Navier reviewed it in 1828, and it was printed in 1833 (TIMO, PDF p. 123). In the last section of the memoir they treat an infinitely long circular cylinder. There they introduced for the first time coordinates that describe a point by its distance from the axis of the cylinder, by its angle around the axis and by its height. In them the mantle of the cylinder is a surface on which the distance from the axis is constant. The example is the torsion of the cylinder by forces on the mantle that are tangential and perpendicular to the axis, with an intensity that depends only on the distance along the axis (TIMO, PDF p. 124). In 1852 Lamé's book on the theory of elasticity appeared. It contains the results of the memoir, and since Lamé had come to the conclusion that an isotropic material needs two elastic constants, the equations in it are somewhat changed. Curvilinear coordinates (krivočrtne koordinate), in which a point moves along a curve when a single coordinate changes, and the deformations of spherical shells get more space than in the memoir (TIMO, PDF p. 125). In 1859 Lamé's book on curvilinear coordinates and their various applications was published. It gives the general theory of curvilinear coordinates and its application to mechanics, to heat and to the theory of elasticity. The equations of elasticity are transformed in it to curvilinear coordinates, and as an example spherical shells are treated (TIMO, PDF pp. 125 and 126).

We were looking for coordinates that follow the round shape of a body. Lamé and Clapeyron used coordinates fitted to the cylinder for the cylinder, and Lamé built in his book of 1859 a general theory for coordinates of any shape. We too will first describe the coordinates for a cylinder and then what they have in common with the other coordinates we need.

11.5.1 Cylindrical coordinates and the local frame

Let \(P = (x, y, z)\) be a point that does not lie on the axis \(z\). Its distance from the axis is \(r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} > 0\), its height is \(z\). The third coordinate is the angle \(\theta\), measured in radians (definition 8.7) from the positive axis \(x\) towards the positive axis \(y\), that is the angle of the complex number \(x + \mathrm{i}\,y \neq 0\), its argument (definition 9.24) or the argument increased by a multiple of \(2\pi\). By lemma 9.23 on the angle of a complex number this angle is determined only up to a multiple of \(2\pi\), so we choose an open interval \((\theta_0, \theta_0 + 2\pi)\) and take exactly one value in it. The points with angle \(\theta_0\) lie on the half-plane bounded by the axis \(z\), and no angle of the interval belongs to them. We describe them with another interval, for example with the bound \(\theta_0 + \pi\) instead of \(\theta_0\). Figure 11.10 shows the three coordinates. We get the point from them by the equation

\[\Phi(r, \theta, z) = (r\cos\theta,\ r\sin\theta,\ z) . \tag{11.11}\]

The function \(\Phi\) takes three numbers as input and gives a point of space as output.

Let two coordinates be constant and the third vary. With constant \(\theta\) and \(z\) the point traces a ray from the axis, with constant \(r\) and \(z\) a circle around the axis, with constant \(r\) and \(\theta\) a line parallel to the axis. These are the coordinate curves (koordinatne krivulje). Their directions at the point \(P\) in which the coordinates increase are the unit vectors

\[\begin{aligned} &\vek e_{\mathrm{r}} = (\cos\theta,\ \sin\theta,\ 0) , \\ &\vek e_\theta = (-\sin\theta,\ \cos\theta,\ 0) , \\ &\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = (0,\ 0,\ 1) . \end{aligned} \tag{11.12}\]

Each has length \(1\), because \(\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1\) (lemma 8.10, item 1), each pair is perpendicular, for example \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\cdot\vek e_\theta = -\cos\theta\sin\theta + \sin\theta\cos\theta = 0\), and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\times\vek e_\theta = (0\cdot 0 - 0\cdot\cos\theta,\ 0\cdot(-\sin\theta) - \cos\theta\cdot 0,\ \cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta) = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) by the definition of the vector product (definition 2.30). At every point the triple is therefore a right-handed rectangular coordinate system, turned about the axis \(z\) by the angle \(\theta\) relative to the Cartesian one. We call it the local frame (lokalna baza).

Here is the difference from the Cartesian system that we will meet all the time. The Cartesian vector \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) is the same at every point. The vectors \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(\vek e_\theta\), however, turn from point to point, as figure 11.10 shows. At the point with \(\theta = 0\) we have \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}} = (1, 0, 0)\), at the point with \(\theta = 90^\circ\) it is \((0, 1, 0)\). When we differentiate a field written with components in the local frame, we therefore differentiate the frame too. How fast does it turn? At \(\theta = 0\) and a step \(\Delta\theta = 0{,}1\)

\[\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}(0{,}1) - \vek e_{\mathrm{r}}(0) = (\cos 0{,}1 - 1,\ \sin 0{,}1,\ 0) = (-0{,}0050,\ 0{,}0998,\ 0) ,\]

while \(\Delta\theta\,\vek e_\theta(0) = (0,\ 0{,}1000,\ 0)\). The difference of the two vectors is \((-0{,}0050,\ -0{,}0002,\ 0)\), of size \(0{,}0050\), about \(\Delta\theta^2/2\), which is \(5\ \%\) of the change \(0{,}1\). For a small step the change of \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) is therefore about \(\Delta\theta\,\vek e_\theta\), as figure 11.11 shows. The tip of a vector that we turn by the angle \(\Delta\theta\) moves along the circle by an arc of length \(\Delta\theta\), and for a small step the arc is nearly equal to the segment between its two ends. We will get this exactly as a derivative with respect to the angle.

The coordinates \(r\), \(\theta\), \(z\) are only one choice. For a ball, which is also round, but around a point and not around an axis, we need others. So as not to repeat the same reasoning each time, we first say what all coordinates of this kind have in common.

11.5.2 Orthogonal coordinates

Every set of coordinates that we will use is a map from a triple of numbers to a point of space, as the map (11.11) is. We regard the triple of numbers \((q_1, q_2, q_3)\) as a point of an auxiliary space in which the box \(K\) lies, as for the curved box (definition 4.65). For each of the maps \(\Phi\) the coordinate curves are the images of lines parallel to the axes of the auxiliary space. When \(q_{\mathrm{i}}\) changes a little, the point moves, by theorem 1.88 on the change of a field over a short step, approximately in the direction of the column \(\partial\Phi/\partial q_{\mathrm{i}}\) of the Jacobian matrix (definition 4.42), so the columns \(\partial\Phi/\partial q_1\), \(\partial\Phi/\partial q_2\) and \(\partial\Phi/\partial q_3\) give the directions of the coordinate curves. For the map (11.11) these columns are

\[\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial r} = \vek e_{\mathrm{r}} , \qquad \frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\theta} = r\,\vek e_\theta , \qquad \frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial z} = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}} ,\]

mutually perpendicular, with lengths \(1\), \(r\) and \(1\). The length of the second column is the distance the point travels for a unit step of the angle, since a step \(\Delta\theta\) moves it along an arc of length \(r\,\Delta\theta\) on the circle of radius \(r\). The unit vector in the direction of a column is a vector of the local frame.

Definition 11.13 Orthogonal coordinates (ortogonalne koordinate)

Let \(D\) be an open set of triples \((q_1, q_2, q_3)\) and \(\Phi\) a map on \(D\) with points of space as images (definition 4.42) that satisfies three conditions:

  1. the components of \(\Phi\) have continuous partial derivatives of the first and second order at every point of \(D\),
  2. \(\Phi\) is injective on \(D\) and its Jacobian \(J = J_\Phi\) is positive at every point of \(D\),
  3. the columns \(\partial\Phi/\partial q_1\), \(\partial\Phi/\partial q_2\) and \(\partial\Phi/\partial q_3\) of the Jacobian matrix are mutually perpendicular at every point of \(D\).

Then \(q_1\), \(q_2\), \(q_3\) are orthogonal coordinates (ortogonalne koordinate) with the map \(\Phi\). The lengths of the columns \(h_{\mathrm{i}} = |\partial\Phi/\partial q_{\mathrm{i}}|\) are the scale factors (skalirni faktorji), functions on \(D\) that take a triple as input and give a number as output. They are positive, because with a zero column the Jacobian would be \(J = 0\) by linearity of the determinant in that column (lemma 2.51, item 2). The unit vectors

\[\vek e_{\mathrm{i}} = \frac{1}{h_{\mathrm{i}}}\,\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial q_{\mathrm{i}}} , \qquad i = 1, 2, 3 , \tag{11.13}\]

are the local frame (lokalna baza) at the point \(\Phi(q_1, q_2, q_3)\), vectors in space that depend on the triple. Curves on which two coordinates are constant are the coordinate curves.

Source: SADD §1.9, (1.9.4) and (1.9.5) (PDF p. 30): the square of the differential length is \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} (h_{\mathrm{i}}\,\mathrm{d}\xi^i)^2\), the local frame \(\widehat{\vek e}_{\mathrm{i}} = (1/h_{\mathrm{i}})\,\partial x^k/\partial\xi^i\,\vek e_{\mathrm{k}}\). SADD only assumes that invertible coordinate transformations exist, conditions 1 and 2 are ours, because the theorems of chapter 4 on the inverse map and on the divergence need them.

Each condition has its task. Condition 1 is the condition of the curved box, the second derivatives of the map will be needed in the Piola identity. Injectivity says that every point of \(\Phi(D)\) has a single triple, so that a field of the point is a field of the triple and conversely, and the positive Jacobian says that a small cube in the auxiliary space is neither flattened nor reflected. Perpendicular columns (condition 3) make the local frame a rectangular system, and the positive Jacobian makes it right-handed, as the next lemma says. In it the components of a vector and of a tensor follow the rules for turned axes of chapter 2. The scale factor \(h_{\mathrm{i}}\) tells how far the point moves for a step \(\Delta q_{\mathrm{i}}\) along the coordinate curve. By theorem 1.88 on the change of a field over a short step, applied to each component of the map, the point moves for a step \(\Delta q_{\mathrm{i}}\) by approximately \(\Delta q_{\mathrm{i}}\,\partial\Phi/\partial q_{\mathrm{i}} = h_{\mathrm{i}}\,\Delta q_{\mathrm{i}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{i}}\), of length \(h_{\mathrm{i}}\,\Delta q_{\mathrm{i}}\). For the angle \(\theta\) this is \(r\,\Delta\theta\), for \(r\) and \(z\) just the change of the coordinate.

Lemma 11.14 The local frame of orthogonal coordinates (lokalna baza ortogonalnih koordinat)

For orthogonal coordinates (definition 11.13) the local frame \(\vek e_1\), \(\vek e_2\), \(\vek e_3\) is at every point of \(D\) a right-handed triple of mutually perpendicular unit vectors, it satisfies

\[\vek e_1\times\vek e_2 = \vek e_3 , \qquad \vek e_2\times\vek e_3 = \vek e_1 , \qquad \vek e_3\times\vek e_1 = \vek e_2 , \tag{11.14}\]

and the Jacobian is \(J = h_1 h_2 h_3\). The face vectors of the curved box ((4.37)) are

\[\begin{aligned} &\vek N_1 = \frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial q_2}\times\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial q_3} \\ &\quad = \frac{J}{h_1}\,\vek e_1 , \\ &\vek N_2 = \frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial q_3}\times\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial q_1} \\ &\quad = \frac{J}{h_2}\,\vek e_2 , \\ &\vek N_3 = \frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial q_1}\times\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial q_2} \\ &\quad = \frac{J}{h_3}\,\vek e_3 . \end{aligned} \tag{11.15}\]
Proof

We have to show that the vector product of two vectors of the frame is the third, and the determinant and the face vectors follow from this. We write the vectors in the Cartesian system and use the properties of the vector product and of the determinant.

  1. The vectors \(\vek e_{\mathrm{i}}\) are unit vectors by definition and mutually perpendicular by condition 3, since \(\vek e_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\) is a positive multiple of the scalar product of the columns.
  2. The vector \(\vek w = \vek e_1\times\vek e_2\) is perpendicular to \(\vek e_1\) and \(\vek e_2\) (lemma 2.31, item 4) and has length \(1\), because \(|\vek w|^2 = 1\cdot 1 - 0^2\) (the same lemma, item 5).
  3. Three mutually perpendicular unit vectors are the axes of a new rectangular Cartesian system, so by lemma 2.38 \(\vek w = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} (\vek w\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{k}})\,\vek e_{\mathrm{k}} = (\vek w\cdot\vek e_3)\,\vek e_3\), since the components along \(\vek e_1\) and \(\vek e_2\) are zero by step 2. Hence \(\vek w = \pm\vek e_3\), because \(\vek w\) has length \(1\).
  4. The columns of the Jacobian matrix are \(h_{\mathrm{i}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{i}}\) with \(h_{\mathrm{i}} > 0\). By linearity of the determinant in each column (lemma 2.51, item 2) \(J = h_1 h_2 h_3\,\det(\vek e_1, \vek e_2, \vek e_3)\). By cyclic permutation of the columns (the same lemma, item 1) \(\det(\vek e_1, \vek e_2, \vek e_3) = \det(\vek e_3, \vek e_1, \vek e_2)\), which by the definition of the determinant 2.50 equals \(\vek e_3\cdot(\vek e_1\times\vek e_2) = \vek e_3\cdot\vek w\). Since \(J > 0\) and \(h_{\mathrm{i}} > 0\), \(\vek e_3\cdot\vek w > 0\), and by step 3 \(\vek e_3\cdot\vek w = \pm 1\), so \(\vek e_3\cdot\vek w = 1\), \(\vek w = \vek e_3\) and \(\det(\vek e_1, \vek e_2, \vek e_3) = 1\). The triple is right-handed (definition 2.48), \(J = h_1 h_2 h_3\), and the first equation (11.14) holds.
  5. Steps 2 and 3, applied to \(\vek e_2\times\vek e_3\) and to \(\vek e_3\times\vek e_1\), give \(\vek e_2\times\vek e_3 = \pm\vek e_1\) and \(\vek e_3\times\vek e_1 = \pm\vek e_2\). By the definition of the determinant and step 4, \(\vek e_1\cdot(\vek e_2\times\vek e_3) = \det(\vek e_1, \vek e_2, \vek e_3) = 1\), and by cyclic permutation also \(\vek e_2\cdot(\vek e_3\times\vek e_1) = \det(\vek e_2, \vek e_3, \vek e_1) = 1\). So in both cases the sign is \(+\).
  6. Face vectors: \(\vek N_1 = h_2\,\vek e_2\times h_3\,\vek e_3 = h_2 h_3\,\vek e_1\) by item 3 of lemma 2.31 and by step 5, which is \((J/h_1)\,\vek e_1\) by step 4. In the same way \(\vek N_2 = h_3 h_1\,\vek e_2\) and \(\vek N_3 = h_1 h_2\,\vek e_3\).

Source: The proof is not from a source. SADD (1.9.4) and (1.9.5) (PDF p. 30) give the scale factors and the local frame, but not that it is right-handed or its vector products.

The proof showed that the positive Jacobian and the perpendicular columns together make the local frame behave like a Cartesian system. The area of a small face spanned by \(h_2\,\Delta q_2\,\vek e_2\) and \(h_3\,\Delta q_3\,\vek e_3\) is \(h_2 h_3\,\Delta q_2\,\Delta q_3\), and the face vector points along \(\vek e_1\), as equation (11.15) says. On the face \(q_1 = b_1\) of a curved box \(\vek N_1\) points out of the box, on the face \(q_1 = a_1\) it is \(-\vek N_1\) that does, as in definition 4.65.

11.5.3 Cylindrical and spherical coordinates as orthogonal coordinates

For a ball we describe a point \(P\) that does not lie on the axis \(z\) by three other numbers. The first is the distance \(R = |\overrightarrow{OP}| > 0\) from the origin, the second the angle \(\varphi\) between the positive axis \(z\) and the ray \(OP\), \(0 < \varphi < \pi\), the third the angle \(\theta\) around the axis \(z\) as for the map (11.11), measured from the positive axis \(x\) towards the positive axis \(y\) (figure 11.12). The point is

\[\Phi(R, \varphi, \theta) = R\,(\sin\varphi\cos\theta,\ \sin\varphi\sin\theta,\ \cos\varphi) . \tag{11.16}\]

The letter \(\varphi\) means an angle here, in later sections of this chapter a stress function. We will say where they meet.

For both kinds of coordinates we will need the numbers: the scale factors, the frame and its derivatives. They are collected in the next lemma.

Lemma 11.15 Cylindrical and spherical coordinates (valjne in krogelne koordinate)
  1. Cylindrical coordinates (valjne koordinate). Let \(D = \{(r, \theta, z)\colon r > 0,\ \theta_0 < \theta < \theta_0 + 2\pi,\ z \in \mathbb{R}\}\) and \(\Phi\) the map (11.11). Then \(r\), \(\theta\), \(z\) are orthogonal coordinates with the map \(\Phi\) (definition 11.13), with scale factors and Jacobian
    \[h_{\mathrm{r}} = 1 , \qquad h_\theta = r , \qquad h_{\mathrm{z}} = 1 , \qquad J = r ,\]
    and the local frame (11.12). Its derivatives are
    \[\frac{\partial\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}}{\partial\theta} = \vek e_\theta , \qquad \frac{\partial\vek e_\theta}{\partial\theta} = -\vek e_{\mathrm{r}} , \tag{11.17}\]
    and all other derivatives of the vectors \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\), \(\vek e_\theta\) and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) with respect to \(r\), \(\theta\) and \(z\) are zero.
  2. Spherical coordinates (krogelne koordinate). Let \(D = \{(R, \varphi, \theta)\colon R > 0,\ 0 < \varphi < \pi,\ \theta_0 < \theta < \theta_0 + 2\pi\}\) and \(\Phi\) the map (11.16). Then \(R\), \(\varphi\), \(\theta\) are orthogonal coordinates with the map \(\Phi\), with scale factors and Jacobian
    \[h_{\mathrm{R}} = 1 , \qquad h_\varphi = R , \qquad h_\theta = R\sin\varphi , \qquad J = R^2\sin\varphi ,\]
    and the local frame
    \[\begin{aligned} \vek e_{\mathrm{R}} &= (\sin\varphi\cos\theta,\ \sin\varphi\sin\theta,\ \cos\varphi) , \\ \vek e_\varphi &= (\cos\varphi\cos\theta,\ \cos\varphi\sin\theta,\ -\sin\varphi) , \\ \vek e_\theta &= (-\sin\theta,\ \cos\theta,\ 0) . \end{aligned} \tag{11.18}\]
    Its derivatives are
    \[\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial\vek e_{\mathrm{R}}}{\partial\varphi} &= \vek e_\varphi , \\ \frac{\partial\vek e_{\mathrm{R}}}{\partial\theta} &= \sin\varphi\,\vek e_\theta , \\ \frac{\partial\vek e_\varphi}{\partial\varphi} &= -\vek e_{\mathrm{R}} , \\ \frac{\partial\vek e_\varphi}{\partial\theta} &= \cos\varphi\,\vek e_\theta , \\ \frac{\partial\vek e_\theta}{\partial\theta} &= -\sin\varphi\,\vek e_{\mathrm{R}} \\ &\quad - \cos\varphi\,\vek e_\varphi , \end{aligned} \tag{11.19}\]
    and all other derivatives of the vectors of the frame are zero.
Proof

We have to show the three conditions of definition 11.13 and compute the tables. We compute the columns of the Jacobian matrix with components, using the derivatives of sine and cosine (theorem 8.12) and the product rule.

1. Cylindrical coordinates.

  1. The set \(D\) is open. For a triple in \(D\) let \(\rho\) be the smallest of the positive numbers \(r\), \(\theta - \theta_0\) and \(\theta_0 + 2\pi - \theta\). A triple at a distance less than \(\rho\) from it has each coordinate closer than \(\rho\), so it lies in \(D\).
  2. By theorem 8.12 and the rules of differentiation (lemma 1.81) the first partial derivatives of the component \(r\cos\theta\) with respect to \(r\), \(\theta\), \(z\) are \(\cos\theta\), \(-r\sin\theta\), \(0\), the second ones \(0\), \(-\sin\theta\) (mixed), \(-r\cos\theta\) and zero for every derivative with respect to \(z\). For \(r\sin\theta\) it is the same with the two functions exchanged, and the derivatives of the component \(z\) are \(0\), \(0\), \(1\) and zero. The functions \(\cos\) and \(\sin\) are continuous, because they are differentiable (lemma 1.79). The function \((r, \theta, z) \mapsto \theta\) is a polynomial and hence continuous (corollary 1.69, item 2), so by lemma 8.25 on the continuity of a composite function \(\cos\theta\) and \(\sin\theta\) are continuous as functions of the triple too. The products with \(r\) are continuous by item 1 of the same corollary. Condition 1 holds.
  3. The columns of the Jacobian matrix are \(\partial\Phi/\partial r = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta, 0) = \vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\), \(\partial\Phi/\partial\theta = (-r\sin\theta, r\cos\theta, 0) = r\,\vek e_\theta\) and \(\partial\Phi/\partial z = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\). The scalar products of the pairs are zero (computed with components, \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\cdot\vek e_\theta = 0\) as above), so condition 3 holds. The lengths are \(1\), \(r\sqrt{\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta} = r\) and \(1\), so \(h_{\mathrm{r}} = 1\), \(h_\theta = r\), \(h_{\mathrm{z}} = 1\).
  4. The determinant is \(J = \vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\cdot(r\,\vek e_\theta\times\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}) = r\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{r}} = r > 0\), since \(\vek e_\theta\times\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = (\cos\theta\cdot 1 - 0,\ 0 - (-\sin\theta)\cdot 1,\ 0) = \vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) by the definition of the vector product.
  5. Injectivity. Let \(\Phi(r, \theta, z) = \Phi(r', \theta', z')\). The third component gives \(z = z'\). The first two give \(r^2 = (r\cos\theta)^2 + (r\sin\theta)^2 = r'^2\), and since \(r\), \(r'\) are positive, \(r = r'\). Then \(\cos\theta = \cos\theta'\) and \(\sin\theta = \sin\theta'\). Both numbers \(\theta\) and \(\theta'\) therefore satisfy the equations (9.20) for the complex number \(\cos\theta + \mathrm{i}\sin\theta\) of absolute value \(1\) (lemma 8.10, item 1), so by lemma 9.23 on the angle of a complex number each of them equals the same number \(\varphi\) increased by a multiple of \(2\pi\), and \(\theta - \theta' = 2\pi k\) for an integer \(k\). Since both angles lie in an interval of length \(2\pi\), \(|\theta - \theta'| < 2\pi\), so \(k = 0\) and \(\theta = \theta'\).
  6. The derivatives of the frame are the derivatives of the components of the vectors (11.12) by theorem 8.12: \(\partial\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}/\partial\theta = (-\sin\theta, \cos\theta, 0) = \vek e_\theta\) and \(\partial\vek e_\theta/\partial\theta = (-\cos\theta, -\sin\theta, 0) = -\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\). The components of \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(\vek e_\theta\) do not depend on \(r\) and \(z\), and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) is constant, so the other derivatives are zero.

2. Spherical coordinates.

  1. The set \(D\) is open as in step 1 of the first part, with the smallest of the numbers \(R\), \(\varphi\), \(\pi - \varphi\), \(\theta - \theta_0\) and \(\theta_0 + 2\pi - \theta\). The components are products of the number \(R\) with sines and cosines of the angles \(\varphi\) and \(\theta\). Their first and second partial derivatives are again such products by theorem 8.12 and the rules of differentiation, and these are continuous as in step 2 of the first part. Condition 1 holds.
  2. The columns are \(\partial\Phi/\partial R = (\sin\varphi\cos\theta, \sin\varphi\sin\theta, \cos\varphi) = \vek e_{\mathrm{R}}\), \(\partial\Phi/\partial\varphi = R\,(\cos\varphi\cos\theta, \cos\varphi\sin\theta, -\sin\varphi) = R\,\vek e_\varphi\) and \(\partial\Phi/\partial\theta = R\sin\varphi\,(-\sin\theta, \cos\theta, 0) = R\sin\varphi\,\vek e_\theta\) with the vectors (11.18). For \(0 < \varphi < \pi\) we have \(\sin\varphi > 0\) (lemma 8.10, item 3). The vectors multiplied by \(R\) and \(R\sin\varphi\) therefore have lengths \(R\) and \(R\sin\varphi\), if the vectors (11.18) have length \(1\). The length of \(\vek e_{\mathrm{R}}\) is \(\sin^2\varphi\,(\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta) + \cos^2\varphi = 1\), that of \(\vek e_\varphi\) is \(\cos^2\varphi + \sin^2\varphi = 1\) and that of \(\vek e_\theta\) is \(1\).
  3. Scalar products: \(\vek e_{\mathrm{R}}\cdot\vek e_\varphi = \sin\varphi\cos\varphi\,(\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta) - \cos\varphi\sin\varphi = 0\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{R}}\cdot\vek e_\theta = \sin\varphi\,(-\cos\theta\sin\theta + \sin\theta\cos\theta) = 0\) and \(\vek e_\varphi\cdot\vek e_\theta = \cos\varphi\,(-\cos\theta\sin\theta + \sin\theta\cos\theta) = 0\). Condition 3 holds, and the scale factors are \(1\), \(R\), \(R\sin\varphi\).
  4. Determinant: \(J = \vek e_{\mathrm{R}}\cdot(R\,\vek e_\varphi\times R\sin\varphi\,\vek e_\theta) = R^2\sin\varphi\,\vek e_{\mathrm{R}}\cdot(\vek e_\varphi\times\vek e_\theta)\), and
    \[\begin{gathered} \vek e_\varphi\times\vek e_\theta = (\cos\varphi\sin\theta\cdot 0 - (-\sin\varphi)\cos\theta, \\ -\sin\varphi\,(-\sin\theta) - \cos\varphi\cos\theta\cdot 0, \\ \cos\varphi\cos\theta\cos\theta + \cos\varphi\sin\theta\sin\theta) = \vek e_{\mathrm{R}} \end{gathered}\]
    So \(J = R^2\sin\varphi\,\vek e_{\mathrm{R}}\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{R}} = R^2\sin\varphi > 0\), which is \(h_{\mathrm{R}} h_\varphi h_\theta\), as lemma 11.14 says.
  5. Injectivity. From \(\Phi(R, \varphi, \theta) = \Phi(R', \varphi', \theta')\) follows \(R = |\Phi| = R'\) (the length of the vector is \(R\), because the length of \(\vek e_{\mathrm{R}}\) is \(1\)), then \(\cos\varphi = z/R = \cos\varphi'\). The cosine is strictly decreasing on \([0, \pi]\) (lemma 8.10, item 3), so two different angles in \((0, \pi)\) have different cosines, and \(\varphi = \varphi'\). Since \(\sin\varphi > 0\), the first two components give \(\cos\theta = \cos\theta'\) and \(\sin\theta = \sin\theta'\), and \(\theta = \theta'\) as in step 5 of the first part.
  6. Derivatives of the frame: differentiating the components of the vectors (11.18) by theorem 8.12 gives \(\partial\vek e_{\mathrm{R}}/\partial\varphi = (\cos\varphi\cos\theta, \cos\varphi\sin\theta, -\sin\varphi) = \vek e_\varphi\), \(\partial\vek e_{\mathrm{R}}/\partial\theta = (-\sin\varphi\sin\theta, \sin\varphi\cos\theta, 0) = \sin\varphi\,\vek e_\theta\), \(\partial\vek e_\varphi/\partial\varphi = (-\sin\varphi\cos\theta, -\sin\varphi\sin\theta, -\cos\varphi) = -\vek e_{\mathrm{R}}\), \(\partial\vek e_\varphi/\partial\theta = (-\cos\varphi\sin\theta, \cos\varphi\cos\theta, 0) = \cos\varphi\,\vek e_\theta\) and \(\partial\vek e_\theta/\partial\theta = (-\cos\theta, -\sin\theta, 0)\). The last vector equals \(-\sin\varphi\,\vek e_{\mathrm{R}} - \cos\varphi\,\vek e_\varphi\), because \(-\sin\varphi\,(\sin\varphi\cos\theta) - \cos\varphi\,(\cos\varphi\cos\theta) = -\cos\theta\), \(-\sin\varphi\,(\sin\varphi\sin\theta) - \cos\varphi\,(\cos\varphi\sin\theta) = -\sin\theta\) and \(-\sin\varphi\cos\varphi + \cos\varphi\sin\varphi = 0\). None of the three vectors depends on \(R\), and \(\vek e_\theta\) does not depend on \(\varphi\) either, so the other derivatives are zero.

Source: SADD (1.9.19)-(1.9.21) (PDF p. 33) for polar coordinates in the plane (\(h_1 = 1\), \(h_2 = r\), \(\widehat{\vek e}_{\mathrm{r}} = \cos\theta\,\vek e_1 + \sin\theta\,\vek e_2\), \(\partial\widehat{\vek e}_{\mathrm{r}}/\partial\theta = \widehat{\vek e}_\theta\), \(\partial\widehat{\vek e}_\theta/\partial\theta = -\widehat{\vek e}_{\mathrm{r}}\)), CM §2.4.7 (PDF p. 80), (2.4.41)-(2.4.44), where the matrices of direction cosines give the same local frames of cylindrical and spherical coordinates, and CM Table 2.4.2 (PDF p. 81), which lists the two maps, the two local frames and all nonzero derivatives of the frames, the same as here. The proof with components is not from a source, SymPy also checked the tables.

We must also test condition 3 of definition 11.13 on a map that does not satisfy it. Take cylindrical coordinates with a stretch, \(\Phi(r,\theta,z) = (r\cos\theta,\ 2r\sin\theta,\ z)\), on the same \(D\) as in lemma 11.15. Condition 3 demands perpendicular columns at every point of \(D\), so the map fails it if there is at least one point at which a pair of columns is not perpendicular.

  1. Conditions 1 and 2 hold. The components are products of numbers with a sine and a cosine, so they have continuous derivatives. The Jacobian determinant is \(J = 2r\cos^2\theta + 2r\sin^2\theta = 2r > 0\). From \(\Phi(r,\theta,z) = \Phi(r',\theta',z')\) follow \(z = z'\) and, dividing the second component by \(2\), the equality of the points \((r\cos\theta,\ r\sin\theta)\) and \((r'\cos\theta',\ r'\sin\theta')\). This is the equality of values of the map (11.11), which is injective on \(D\) (lemma 11.15), so \((r,\theta,z) = (r',\theta',z')\).
  2. The columns in which perpendicularity could fail are \(\partial\Phi/\partial r = (\cos\theta,\ 2\sin\theta,\ 0)\) and \(\partial\Phi/\partial\theta = (-r\sin\theta,\ 2r\cos\theta,\ 0)\). The third column \((0, 0, 1)\) is perpendicular to both, because their third components are zero.
  3. The scalar product of the first two is \(-r\sin\theta\cos\theta + 4r\sin\theta\cos\theta = 3r\sin\theta\cos\theta = \tfrac32\,r\sin 2\theta\).
  4. Choose the point \(\theta = 45^\circ\), \(r = 10\) mm. The columns are \((0{,}707,\ 1{,}414,\ 0)\) and \((-7{,}07,\ 14{,}14,\ 0)\) mm, the product is \(-5 + 20 = 15\) mm and is not zero. The lengths are \(1{,}581\) and \(15{,}81\) mm, so the cosine of the angle between the columns is \(15/(1{,}581\cdot 15{,}81) = 0{,}6\) and the angle is \(53{,}1^\circ\) instead of \(90^\circ\).
  5. Condition 3 fails at this point, so \(r\), \(\theta\), \(z\) with this \(\Phi\) are not orthogonal coordinates, although conditions 1 and 2 hold (figure 11.13).

It fails everywhere except on the axes, where \(\sin 2\theta = 0\), and one angle is enough. For a stretch by the same factor in both directions, \((2r\cos\theta,\ 2r\sin\theta,\ z)\), condition 3 would hold, since the product is \(4r\,(-\sin\theta\cos\theta + \sin\theta\cos\theta) = 0\). Perpendicularity is spoiled only by different factors in the directions \(x\) and \(y\).

We check the table of the spherical frame at two positions. At \(\varphi = 90^\circ\) and \(\theta = 0\) the point \(P\) is on the axis \(x\) and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{R}} = (1, 0, 0)\), \(\vek e_\varphi = (0, 0, -1)\), \(\vek e_\theta = (0, 1, 0)\). A step in \(\varphi\) moves the point along the meridian towards the south pole, and the vector \(\vek e_{\mathrm{R}}\) turns into the direction in which the point moves, the direction of \(\vek e_\varphi\), which is what \(\partial\vek e_{\mathrm{R}}/\partial\varphi = \vek e_\varphi\) says. A step in \(\theta\) moves the point along the equator, and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{R}}\) turns into the direction of \(\vek e_\theta\), which is what \(\partial\vek e_{\mathrm{R}}/\partial\theta = \sin 90^\circ\,\vek e_\theta = \vek e_\theta\) says. At \(\varphi = 45^\circ\) the vector \(\vek e_{\mathrm{R}}\) has a horizontal component of length \(\sin\varphi = 0{,}71\) and a vertical component \(\cos\varphi\). For a step \(\Delta\theta\) the horizontal component turns by \(\Delta\theta\) as in cylindrical coordinates and therefore changes by \(\sin\varphi\,\Delta\theta\) in the direction of \(\vek e_\theta\), while the vertical component does not change. Hence \(\partial\vek e_{\mathrm{R}}/\partial\theta = \sin\varphi\,\vek e_\theta\) and not \(\vek e_\theta\).

Now we can differentiate.

11.5.4 Derivatives in orthogonal coordinates

Let \(f\) be a function of the point, for example the temperature or one of the displacements. We write it as a function of the coordinates, \(F(q_1, q_2, q_3) = f\big(\Phi(q_1, q_2, q_3)\big)\), and know its derivatives \(\partial F/\partial q_{\mathrm{i}}\). What is the gradient of \(f\)? By the chain rule the derivative with respect to the coordinate \(q_{\mathrm{i}}\) is the directional derivative of \(f\) in the direction of the column \(\partial\Phi/\partial q_{\mathrm{i}}\), that is in the direction of \(\vek e_{\mathrm{i}}\), multiplied by the length of the column.

Lemma 11.16 Derivative of a function and of a field in orthogonal coordinates (odvod funkcije in polja v ortogonalnih koordinatah)

Let \(q_1\), \(q_2\), \(q_3\) be orthogonal coordinates on \(D\) with the map \(\Phi\) (definition 11.13) and \(E\) an open set of points of space that contains \(\Phi(D)\). At a point \((q_1, q_2, q_3)\) of \(D\), \(\nabla f\) and \(\mat H\) below are taken at the point \(\Phi(q_1, q_2, q_3)\).

  1. Let \(f\) be a continuously differentiable function on \(E\). Then at every point of \(D\)
    \[\frac{\partial(f\circ\Phi)}{\partial q_{\mathrm{i}}} = h_{\mathrm{i}}\,\nabla f\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{i}} , \qquad \text{so} \qquad \nabla f\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{i}} = \frac{1}{h_{\mathrm{i}}}\,\frac{\partial(f\circ\Phi)}{\partial q_{\mathrm{i}}} . \tag{11.20}\]
  2. Let \(\vek u\) be a continuously differentiable field on \(E\) and \(\mat H\) the Jacobian matrix of the field \(\vek u\) (the matrix with components \(H_{\mathrm{kl}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{k}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{l}}\)). Then at every point of \(D\)
    \[\mat H\,\vek e_{\mathrm{i}} = \frac{1}{h_{\mathrm{i}}}\,\frac{\partial(\vek u\circ\Phi)}{\partial q_{\mathrm{i}}} . \tag{11.21}\]
  3. A continuously differentiable function \(F\) on \(D\) is also a function of the point: \(f = F\circ\Phi^{-1}\) is continuously differentiable on the open set \(\Phi(D)\) and \(f\circ\Phi = F\).
Proof

We have to show that the derivative of the composite function is the scalar product of the gradient with a column of the Jacobian matrix of the map. The chain rule says this, so we apply it to the function and to each component of the field.

  1. By the chain rule (lemma 4.43), applied to \(f\) and the map \(\Phi\) with values in \(E\), \(\partial(f\circ\Phi)/\partial q_{\mathrm{i}} = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} \partial f/\partial x_{\mathrm{k}}\,\partial\Phi_{\mathrm{k}}/\partial q_{\mathrm{i}}\). This is the scalar product of the gradient \(\nabla f\) with the column \(\partial\Phi/\partial q_{\mathrm{i}} = h_{\mathrm{i}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{i}}\), that is \(h_{\mathrm{i}}\,\nabla f\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{i}}\). Since \(h_{\mathrm{i}} > 0\), we may divide.
  2. The same chain rule, applied to the component \(u_{\mathrm{k}}\), gives \(\partial(u_{\mathrm{k}}\circ\Phi)/\partial q_{\mathrm{i}} = \sum_{\mathrm{l}} H_{\mathrm{kl}}\,\partial\Phi_{\mathrm{l}}/\partial q_{\mathrm{i}} = h_{\mathrm{i}}\,(\mat H\,\vek e_{\mathrm{i}})_{\mathrm{k}}\) by the definition of the product of a matrix and a column. We collect the components into a vector and divide by \(h_{\mathrm{i}}\).
  3. The map \(\Phi\) is continuously differentiable, injective and has \(J > 0\), so by theorem 4.51 on the inverse map the set \(\Phi(D)\) is open and the inverse map \(\Psi = \Phi^{-1}\) is continuously differentiable. By the chain rule \(f = F\circ\Psi\) is a continuously differentiable function on \(\Phi(D)\), with values \(\Psi(P)\) in the open set \(D\).

Source: SADD (1.9.11), (1.9.13) (PDF p. 32) give the form of the derivative of a field and of the gradient of a scalar in orthogonal coordinates. The proof with the columns of the Jacobian matrix is not from a source.

We test equation (11.20) on cylindrical coordinates. The function \(f = x\) has gradient \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(F = r\cos\theta\). By equation (11.20) \(\partial F/\partial\theta = -r\sin\theta\) and \(h_\theta\,\nabla f\cdot\vek e_\theta = r\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\cdot\vek e_\theta = -r\sin\theta\), the same. The function \(f = x^2 + y^2\) gives \(F = r^2\), \(\partial F/\partial r = 2r\) and \(h_{\mathrm{r}}\,\nabla f\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{r}} = (2x, 2y, 0)\cdot(\cos\theta, \sin\theta, 0) = 2r\cos^2\theta + 2r\sin^2\theta = 2r\), and \(\partial F/\partial\theta = 0 = r\,(2x, 2y, 0)\cdot\vek e_\theta = r\,(-2r\cos\theta\sin\theta + 2r\sin\theta\cos\theta)\). A function that depends on both coordinates in Cartesian coordinates depends on only one in cylindrical ones, which is the reason for using them.

The second equation says how we compute the Jacobian matrix of a field from the derivatives of its components with respect to the coordinates, if we write the field in the local frame. The local frame is a rectangular coordinate system, so by lemma 2.38 \(\vek u\circ\Phi = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \tilde u_{\mathrm{j}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\) with the components \(\tilde u_{\mathrm{j}} = (\vek u\circ\Phi)\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\), where \(j\) runs over \(1\), \(2\), \(3\) (in cylindrical coordinates these are \(u_{\mathrm{r}}\), \(u_\theta\), \(u_{\mathrm{z}}\)). By the product rule

\[\frac{\partial(\vek u\circ\Phi)}{\partial q_{\mathrm{i}}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \Big( \frac{\partial\tilde u_{\mathrm{j}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{i}}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{j}} + \tilde u_{\mathrm{j}}\,\frac{\partial\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{i}}} \Big) ,\]

where the derivatives of the frame come from lemma 11.15. This is how we will compute the strains in the next section. When the frame does not turn, the second term is zero and we get the Cartesian derivative.

11.5.5 Divergence and the Laplace operator

The equilibrium equations speak about the divergence of the stress. How do we compute it if the stresses are written in cylindrical coordinates? The divergence theorem for a curved box (theorem 4.67) has already done it: the integral of the divergence over a piece that is the image of a box is the flux through six faces, and the six faces have the face vectors \(\vek N_{\mathrm{i}}\). Figure 11.14 shows a piece of the tube between \(r\) and \(r + \Delta r\), \(\theta\) and \(\theta + \Delta\theta\), \(z\) and \(z + \Delta z\). By lemma 11.14 the face vectors are \(\vek N_{\mathrm{r}} = r\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\), \(\vek N_\theta = \vek e_\theta\) and \(\vek N_{\mathrm{z}} = r\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), and the areas of the faces are approximately \(r\,\Delta\theta\,\Delta z\) on the face \(r = \text{const}\), \(\Delta r\,\Delta z\) on the face \(\theta = \text{const}\) and \(r\,\Delta r\,\Delta\theta\) on the face \(z = \text{const}\). The outer face \(r + \Delta r\) is larger than the inner one, because its arc has length \((r + \Delta r)\,\Delta\theta\) and the arc of the inner one \(r\,\Delta\theta\). In the next section this difference of areas will be one of the consequences of curvature.

For any orthogonal coordinates the next lemma says the same. The proof of the divergence theorem contains steps that do not use the box at all: the computation of the divergence of the field that we build with the face vectors. We repeat these steps for our coordinates.

Lemma 11.17 Divergence in orthogonal coordinates (divergenca v ortogonalnih koordinatah)

Let \(q_1\), \(q_2\), \(q_3\) be orthogonal coordinates on \(D\) with the map \(\Phi\) (definition 11.13), \(E\) an open set of points of space that contains \(\Phi(D)\), and \(\vek v\) a continuously differentiable field on \(E\). Then at every point of \(D\)

\[J\,(\mathrm{div}\,\vek v)\circ\Phi = \sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{3}\frac{\partial}{\partial q_{\mathrm{i}}}\Big( \frac{J}{h_{\mathrm{i}}}\,(\vek v\circ\Phi)\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{i}} \Big) . \tag{11.22}\]
Proof

We have to show that the right-hand side is the sum of the derivatives of the components of the field \(\vek G\) that gave the divergence in theorem 4.67 for a curved box. Steps 2.1 to 2.7 of the proof of that theorem use only the product rule, the chain rule, the Piola identity and the inverse matrix, not the box, so they hold at every point of \(D\) for every map with continuous first and second derivatives and positive Jacobian. Since \(E\) contains \(\Phi(D)\), the set \(D'\) of step 1.1 of that proof is \(D\) itself. We write the steps for our coordinates. Let \(\vek N_{\mathrm{i}}\) be the face vector of equation (11.15), and \(\tilde{\vek v} = \vek v\circ\Phi\).

  1. Let \(G_{\mathrm{i}} = \tilde{\vek v}\cdot\vek N_{\mathrm{i}}\) for \(i = 1, 2, 3\). The components of the vectors \(\vek N_{\mathrm{i}}\) are differences of products of first derivatives of the components of \(\Phi\), and these have continuous derivatives (condition 1), so the \(G_{\mathrm{i}}\) are continuously differentiable (step 2.2 of the proof of theorem 4.67).
  2. By the product rule \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \partial G_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial q_{\mathrm{i}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \partial\tilde{\vek v}/\partial q_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot\vek N_{\mathrm{i}} + \tilde{\vek v}\cdot\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \partial\vek N_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial q_{\mathrm{i}}\), and the second term is zero by the Piola identity (lemma 4.66, step 2.4 of the same proof).
  3. By the chain rule the component \(m\) of the vector \(\partial\tilde{\vek v}/\partial q_{\mathrm{i}}\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} \partial v_{\mathrm{m}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{k}}\,\partial\Phi_{\mathrm{k}}/\partial q_{\mathrm{i}}\) (step 2.5). We insert this into the first term of step 2. Let \(\mat M\) be the matrix with rows \(\vek N_1\), \(\vek N_2\), \(\vek N_3\). By theorem 2.54 on the inverse via the determinant \(\mat M/J\) is the inverse of the Jacobian matrix \(\mat F_\Phi\), so \(\mat F_\Phi\mat M = J\,\mat I\), and the element \(km\) of this equation is \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \partial\Phi_{\mathrm{k}}/\partial q_{\mathrm{i}}\,(\vek N_{\mathrm{i}})_{\mathrm{m}} = J\) for \(k = m\) and \(0\) otherwise (step 2.6). The first term of step 2 is therefore \(\sum_{\mathrm{m}}\sum_{\mathrm{k}} \partial v_{\mathrm{m}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{k}}\,J\,\delta_{\mathrm{km}} = J\sum_{\mathrm{m}} \partial v_{\mathrm{m}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{m}}\), that is \(J\,(\mathrm{div}\,\vek v)\circ\Phi\) (step 2.7).
  4. By lemma 11.14 \(\vek N_{\mathrm{i}} = (J/h_{\mathrm{i}})\,\vek e_{\mathrm{i}}\), so \(G_{\mathrm{i}} = (J/h_{\mathrm{i}})\,\tilde{\vek v}\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{i}}\), and step 3 gives (11.22).

Source: SADD (1.9.14) (PDF p. 32): \(\nabla\cdot\vek u = \frac{1}{h_1h_2h_3}\sum_{\mathrm{i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi^i}\big(\frac{h_1h_2h_3}{h_{\mathrm{i}}}u_{\langle \mathrm{i}\rangle}\big)\), which is the same equation, since \(J = h_1h_2h_3\). The proof with the Piola identity is not from a source.

The proof showed that the divergence in orthogonal coordinates is nothing new. It is the same sum of fluxes as the divergence theorem gives, only written with the coordinates of the faces: \(J/h_{\mathrm{i}}\) is the area of the face on which \(q_{\mathrm{i}}\) is constant, per unit \(\Delta q_{\mathrm{j}}\,\Delta q_{\mathrm{k}}\) of the other two coordinates. For \(\vek v\) with components \(v_{\mathrm{r}} = (\vek v\circ\Phi)\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\), \(v_\theta\), \(v_{\mathrm{z}}\) in the local frame of cylindrical coordinates, lemma 11.15 gives

\[\mathrm{div}\,\vek v = \frac{1}{r}\Big( \frac{\partial(r\,v_{\mathrm{r}})}{\partial r} + \frac{\partial v_\theta}{\partial\theta} + \frac{\partial(r\,v_{\mathrm{z}})}{\partial z} \Big) = \frac{1}{r}\,\frac{\partial(r\,v_{\mathrm{r}})}{\partial r} + \frac{1}{r}\,\frac{\partial v_\theta}{\partial\theta} + \frac{\partial v_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial z} ,\]

since \(J/h_{\mathrm{r}} = r\), \(J/h_\theta = 1\) and \(J/h_{\mathrm{z}} = r\), and \(r\) does not depend on \(\theta\) and \(z\).

We test this on the ring of chapter 4. The piece of the ring of height \(2c\) between the angles \(-45^\circ\) and \(45^\circ\) is, in cylindrical coordinates with \(\theta_0 = -\pi\), the image of the box \(K = [40, 60]\ \text{mm}\times[-\pi/4, \pi/4]\times[-c, c]\), which lies in \(D\). By lemma 11.15 \(J = r\), the map is injective and continuously differentiable, so by theorem 4.59 on the change of variables its volume is

\[\int_{\mathrm{K}} J\,\mathrm{d}V = \int_{-\mathrm{c}}^{c}\int_{-\pi/4}^{\pi/4}\int_{40}^{60} r\,\mathrm{d}r\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,\mathrm{d}z = \frac{60^2 - 40^2}{2}\cdot\frac{\pi}{2}\cdot 2c = 1000\,\pi\,c\ \text{mm}^3 ,\]

the same as in section 4.3.7, where we described the same piece with a map with Jacobian \(J_\Phi = 20\,(40 + 20\lambda)/(1 + s^2)\) and integrated with SymPy. Now the computation is a triple integral of a product of functions of one variable. The face \(r = 60\) has the face vector \(\vek N_{\mathrm{r}} = \partial\Phi/\partial\theta\times\partial\Phi/\partial z = r\,\vek e_\theta\times\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = r\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) (lemma 11.14) of length \(r\), so its area is \(\int_{-\mathrm{c}}^{c}\int_{-\pi/4}^{\pi/4}|\vek N_{\mathrm{r}}|\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,\mathrm{d}z = 60\cdot\frac{\pi}{2}\cdot 2c = 60\,\pi\,c\ \text{mm}^2\), and four such faces give \(240\,\pi\,c = 2\pi\cdot 60\cdot 2c\), the mantle of a cylinder of radius \(60\) mm and height \(2c\). On the face \(r = 40\) the area is \(40\,\pi\,c\ \text{mm}^2\).

One more test, of equation (11.22). The field \(\vek v = (x, y, 0) = r\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) has Cartesian divergence \(2\). In cylindrical coordinates \(v_{\mathrm{r}} = r\), \(v_\theta = v_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\), and \(J\,\mathrm{div}\,\vek v = \partial(r\cdot r)/\partial r = 2r\), so \(\mathrm{div}\,\vek v = 2\). By the divergence theorem the flux through the surface of the piece must be \(2\cdot 1000\,\pi\,c = 2000\,\pi\,c\ \text{mm}^3\). Through the faces \(r = 60\) and \(r = 40\) the flux is \(\vek v\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) times the area, \(60\cdot 60\,\pi\,c = 3600\,\pi\,c\) outwards and \(-40\cdot 40\,\pi\,c = -1600\,\pi\,c\), because there the outward normal is \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\). Through the faces \(\theta = \pm\pi/4\) and \(z = \pm c\) the flux is zero, because \(\vek v\cdot\vek e_\theta = 0\) and \(\vek v\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\). The sum is \(3600\,\pi\,c - 1600\,\pi\,c = 2000\,\pi\,c\) and agrees.

Corollary 11.18 The Laplace operator in cylindrical coordinates (Laplaceov operator v valjnih koordinatah)

Let \(f\) be a function with continuous first and second partial derivatives on an open set \(E\) that contains \(\Phi(D)\) for cylindrical coordinates (lemma 11.15, item 1), and \(F = f\circ\Phi\). Then at every point of \(D\)

\[(\nabla^2 f)\circ\Phi = \frac{\partial^2 F}{\partial r^2} + \frac{1}{r}\,\frac{\partial F}{\partial r} + \frac{1}{r^2}\,\frac{\partial^2 F}{\partial\theta^2} + \frac{\partial^2 F}{\partial z^2} . \tag{11.23}\]
Proof

We have to show that the Laplace operator is the divergence of the gradient, and then we use the lemmas on the derivative and on the divergence.

  1. By definition 11.5 \(\nabla^2 f = \sum_{\mathrm{k}}\partial^2 f/\partial x_{\mathrm{k}}^2 = \mathrm{div}(\nabla f)\), since the component \(k\) of the gradient is \(\partial f/\partial x_{\mathrm{k}}\). The field \(\vek v = \nabla f\) is continuously differentiable, because the second partial derivatives of \(f\) are continuous.
  2. By equation (11.20) of lemma 11.16 \((\vek v\circ\Phi)\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{i}} = (1/h_{\mathrm{i}})\,\partial F/\partial q_{\mathrm{i}}\), so \((\vek v\circ\Phi)\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{r}} = \partial F/\partial r\), \((\vek v\circ\Phi)\cdot\vek e_\theta = (1/r)\,\partial F/\partial\theta\) and \((\vek v\circ\Phi)\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = \partial F/\partial z\). The function \(F\) has continuous second partial derivatives. By the chain rule \(\partial F/\partial q_{\mathrm{i}} = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} (\partial f/\partial x_{\mathrm{k}})\circ\Phi\,\partial\Phi_{\mathrm{k}}/\partial q_{\mathrm{i}}\), where \((\partial f/\partial x_{\mathrm{k}})\circ\Phi\) is continuously differentiable by the chain rule and \(\partial\Phi_{\mathrm{k}}/\partial q_{\mathrm{i}}\) by condition 1 of definition 11.13, and a product and a sum of such functions are continuously differentiable by the product rule and corollary 1.69, item 1.
  3. By equation (11.22) of lemma 11.17 with \(J/h_{\mathrm{r}} = r\), \(J/h_\theta = 1\) and \(J/h_{\mathrm{z}} = r\)
    \[r\,(\nabla^2 f)\circ\Phi = \frac{\partial}{\partial r}\Big(r\,\frac{\partial F}{\partial r}\Big) + \frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\Big(\frac{1}{r}\,\frac{\partial F}{\partial\theta}\Big) + \frac{\partial}{\partial z}\Big(r\,\frac{\partial F}{\partial z}\Big) = r\,\frac{\partial^2 F}{\partial r^2} + \frac{\partial F}{\partial r} + \frac{1}{r}\,\frac{\partial^2 F}{\partial\theta^2} + r\,\frac{\partial^2 F}{\partial z^2} ,\]
    where we used the product rule in the first term and, in the second and third, that \(r\) does not depend on \(\theta\) and \(z\). We divide by \(r > 0\).

Source: SADD (7.6.5) (PDF p. 161) for the plane case, (1.9.15) (PDF p. 32) for the general form \(\nabla^2\phi = \frac{1}{h_1h_2h_3}\sum_{\mathrm{i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi^i}\big(\frac{h_1h_2h_3}{h_{\mathrm{i}}^2}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\xi^i}\big)\) and (1.9.21) (PDF p. 33) for polar coordinates.

In spherical coordinates we take the same three steps for a function \(f\) with continuous first and second partial derivatives for which \(F = f\circ\Phi\) depends only on \(R\), \(F(R, \varphi, \theta) = g(R)\). By lemma 11.16 \(((\nabla f)\circ\Phi)\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{R}} = g'(R)\), and the other two components are zero, because \(F\) does not depend on \(\varphi\) and \(\theta\). By lemma 11.17 with \(J/h_{\mathrm{R}} = R^2\sin\varphi\) therefore \(R^2\sin\varphi\,(\nabla^2 f)\circ\Phi = \partial\big(R^2\sin\varphi\,g'(R)\big)/\partial R = \sin\varphi\,\big(R^2 g'(R)\big)'\). We divide by \(R^2\sin\varphi > 0\) and get \((\nabla^2 f)\circ\Phi = \frac{1}{R^2}\,\big(R^2 g'(R)\big)'\).

We test the corollary on the function \(f = r^3\cos\theta = x\,(x^2 + y^2) = x^3 + xy^2\). In Cartesian form \(\nabla^2 f = 6x + 2x = 8x = 8r\cos\theta\). By equation (11.23) \(\partial^2 F/\partial r^2 = 6r\cos\theta\), \((1/r)\,\partial F/\partial r = 3r\cos\theta\) and \((1/r^2)\,\partial^2 F/\partial\theta^2 = -r\cos\theta\), the sum \(8r\cos\theta\). The function \(\ln r\) gives \(-1/r^2 + 1/r^2 = 0\) in equation (11.23), and the function \(r^2\cos 2\theta = x^2 - y^2\) gives \(2\cos 2\theta + 2\cos 2\theta - 4\cos 2\theta = 0\), which for \(x^2 - y^2\) is indeed \(2 - 2 = 0\). The function \(r^2 = x^2 + y^2\) gives \(2 + 2 = 4\) in both forms. Let us take a harder function too, \(F = r^3\cos^2\theta + r\ln r\,\sin\theta + r^4\cos 2\theta\), which has the long Cartesian form \(f = x^2\sqrt{x^2 + y^2} + \tfrac12\,y\ln(x^2 + y^2) + x^4 - y^4\). At the point \(x = 3/2\), \(y = 1/2\) (that is \(r = 1{,}5811\) and \(\theta = 0{,}32175\) rad) equation (11.23) gives \(34{,}67740\). The Cartesian sum of second derivatives is \(\partial^2 f/\partial x^2 + \partial^2 f/\partial y^2 = 35{,}83668 - 1{,}15928 = 34{,}67740\) (SymPy), the difference is below \(10^{-30}\) (SymPy with 30 digits).

With the lemmas of this section we can differentiate every field in cylindrical and spherical coordinates and compute its divergence and Laplace operator. We still need to know which numbers are the components of stress and strain in the local frame, how the strains follow from the displacements and what the equilibrium equation is. This is the subject of the next section. It will turn out that each of these equations contains a term that appears because the frame turns or because the faces have different sizes.
