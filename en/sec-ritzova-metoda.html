Chapter 6 Weak form, the Ritz and Galerkin method (šibka oblika, Ritzova in Galerkinova metoda)

6.4 The Ritz method (Ritzova metoda)

In chapter 4 we gave the hanging bar a line with one free number and a parabola with two, and in chapter 5 we gave the cantilever the parabola \(a\,x^2\) and then \(a\,x^2 + b\,x^3\). Each time we wrote the energy with the free numbers, differentiated it with respect to them and solved a small system of equations. But what if we want ten free numbers, or a hundred? For that we need three things. A procedure that works for any number of trial functions and any load. Conditions under which the system of equations has exactly one solution. And an answer to the question of what the approximation guarantees, because so far we only know that its energy is not smaller than the true one. This section answers all three questions.

The model is the same as in chapters 4 and 5: a bar that carries only axial force, an Euler-Bernoulli beam and, where we say so explicitly, a body of any shape. Let us list the assumptions that hold throughout the section and say for each where we use it.

  1. The material is linearly elastic and the displacements are small. Therefore the energy \(\Pi\) is a quadratic function of the field and equilibrium is the condition for its smallest value. We use this in the derivation of the equations.
  2. The loads do not change with the deformation. Therefore the work of the loads on the field is a linear function of the field. We use this in the writing of \(\ell(w)\).
  3. The supports hold the body, so that without deformation there is no movement. Therefore the energy is positive for every admissible field that is not zero (lemma 6.7). We use this for the uniqueness of the solution.
  4. The bar has \(EA > 0\) and the beam \(EI > 0\), each constant piece by piece. For the beam the trial functions are continuously differentiable, because the energy contains the second derivative.

We use the symbols of section 6.1.5 for all three models. The bilinear form \(B(u, w)\) (definition 6.4) is \(\int_0^L EA\,u'\,w'\,\mathrm{d}x\) for the bar, \(\int_0^L EI\,u''\,w''\,\mathrm{d}x\) for the beam and \(\int_{\mathrm{V}} \ten\sigma(u) : \ten\varepsilon(w)\,\mathrm{d}V\) for the body. The linear form \(\ell(w)\) (definition 6.5) is the work of the given loads on the field \(w\), for the bar \(\int_0^L q\,w\,\mathrm{d}x + F\,w(L)\). The energy of a field \(v\) is \(\Pi(v) = \tfrac12\,B(v, v) - \ell(v)\), which for the bar is equation (4.62). The admissible fields and the test functions are as in definition 6.6, with the class of fields of section 6.2.

The path is this. First we state Ritz's idea and write it as a system of equations that has exactly one solution. Then we test it on the hanging bar and on the beams of chapter 5. Then we prove what the solution guarantees and what it does not. At the end we take a sequence of ever richer families of fields and show when the approximation approaches the true solution.

6.4.1 The idea of the method (zamisel metode)

When and why did people start looking for an approximation among fields with several free numbers at once?

From history

For systems with continuously distributed properties, closed-form solutions of the differential equations exist only in rare cases (GR, PDF p. 353). Rayleigh approximated the fundamental mode of vibration by one function that satisfies the kinematic conditions of the problem, substituted it into a variational expression and obtained an approximation of the eigenvalue from it (GR, PDF p. 353). He thus computed the frequency directly from the energy, without solving the differential equations (TIMO, PDF p. 277-278). The sources place the beginning of the method in the 1870s (COOK, PDF p. 160) and give it the year 1894 (GR, PDF p. 353).

For the plate the problem was the same. For a thin rectangular plate that is clamped at the edges and loaded by a uniform pressure, no complete analytical solution was known (LOVE, PDF p. 512). Ritz observed that this problem is the same as finding the function for which a certain integral is stationary (LOVE, PDF p. 512). He generalized Rayleigh's idea, since he built the approximation from several functions, each of which satisfies the essential (kinematic) conditions and has its own free number (COOK, PDF p. 160). The coefficients of the expansion are therefore the unknowns of the problem, and the variational principle gives a system of \(n\) linear equations, similar to the system for discrete systems with point masses (GR, PDF p. 353). According to GR, the discretization methods came into being this way (GR, PDF p. 354). The method is now used in elasticity, the theory of structures and nonlinear mechanics, not only for vibration (TIMO, PDF p. 277-278). EP gives the year 1908 for Ritz's work (EP, p. 705). GR gives the year 1909 (GR, PDF p. 353).

Let us return to the hanging bar and the cantilever. The families of fields in chapters 4 and 5 had one or two free numbers, while Ritz's idea allows as many as we like. What changes in the computation?

We write the family of the hanging bar \(\tilde u = c_1\,x + c_2\,x^2\) as \(c_1\,\psi_1 + c_2\,\psi_2\) with the trial functions (poskusne funkcije) \(\psi_1 = x\) and \(\psi_2 = x^2\). Each of them satisfies the fixing, \(\psi(0) = 0\), so every combination satisfies it too. Ritz takes \(n\) functions \(\psi_1, \dots, \psi_{\mathrm{n}}\), each with its own free number \(c_{\mathrm{i}}\), and the field \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} c_{\mathrm{i}}\,\psi_{\mathrm{i}}\). The energy is then a function of \(n\) numbers and we look for its lowest point as for the truss (section 4.6.6), with derivatives with respect to all the numbers. Figure 6.10 shows this for \(n = 2\) on the hanging bar.

But what if a support prescribes a displacement that is not zero? Then a combination of functions that are zero at the support stays zero there and cannot satisfy the condition. So we add one fixed function \(\psi_0\) that satisfies the condition, while the other functions have the value zero at the support (the subsection 6.4.2 has an example). The field of the family is then

\[u_{\mathrm{n}} = \psi_0 + \sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{n} c_{\mathrm{i}}\,\psi_{\mathrm{i}} , \tag{6.16}\]

and it satisfies the condition at the support for every \(c_{\mathrm{i}}\). For zero conditions \(\psi_0 = 0\).

How do we get the numbers \(c_{\mathrm{i}}\)? The same as for the truss: we write the energy with the numbers and differentiate it. The derivation has four steps and holds for the bar, the beam and the body, because it uses only the bilinearity and the symmetry of the form \(B\).

  1. Let \(v = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{j}}\,\psi_{\mathrm{j}}\), so \(u_{\mathrm{n}} = \psi_0 + v\). From \(\Pi(u_{\mathrm{n}}) = \tfrac12\,B(\psi_0 + v, \psi_0 + v) - \ell(\psi_0 + v)\) we expand the brackets by bilinearity and linearity: \(\tfrac12\,B(\psi_0, \psi_0) + \tfrac12\,B(\psi_0, v) + \tfrac12\,B(v, \psi_0) + \tfrac12\,B(v, v) - \ell(\psi_0) - \ell(v)\).
  2. The form \(B\) is symmetric (definition 6.4), so \(B(v, \psi_0) = B(\psi_0, v)\) and the two middle terms give \(B(\psi_0, v)\).
  3. We substitute \(v\) and take out the sums: \(B(v, v) = \sum_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{i}}\,c_{\mathrm{j}}\,B(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}, \psi_{\mathrm{i}})\), \(B(\psi_0, v) = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} c_{\mathrm{i}}\,B(\psi_0, \psi_{\mathrm{i}})\) and \(\ell(v) = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} c_{\mathrm{i}}\,\ell(\psi_{\mathrm{i}})\). With the notation \(K_{\mathrm{ij}} = B(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}, \psi_{\mathrm{i}})\) and \(f_{\mathrm{i}} = \ell(\psi_{\mathrm{i}}) - B(\psi_0, \psi_{\mathrm{i}})\) we have
    \[\Pi(u_{\mathrm{n}}) = \Pi(\psi_0) + \tfrac12 \sum_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{i}}\,K_{\mathrm{ij}}\,c_{\mathrm{j}} - \sum_{\mathrm{i}} f_{\mathrm{i}}\,c_{\mathrm{i}} . \tag{6.17}\]
  4. This is a quadratic function of the numbers \(c_1, \dots, c_{\mathrm{n}}\), so at its lowest point we differentiate it with respect to each \(c_{\mathrm{i}}\) and set the derivative equal to zero (section 4.6.6). Since \(K_{\mathrm{ij}} = K_{\mathrm{ji}}\) by step 2, the derivative with respect to \(c_{\mathrm{i}}\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} K_{\mathrm{ij}}\,c_{\mathrm{j}} - f_{\mathrm{i}}\), and we get \(n\) linear equations
    \[\sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^{n} K_{\mathrm{ij}}\,c_{\mathrm{j}} = f_{\mathrm{i}} , \qquad i = 1, \dots, n , \tag{6.18}\]
    in the matrix form \(\mat K\,\vek c = \vek f\).

A test on the hanging bar with \(\psi_1 = x\) and \(\psi_2 = x^2\): \(K_{\mathrm{ij}} = \int_0^L EA\,\psi_{\mathrm{j}}'\,\psi_{\mathrm{i}}'\,\mathrm{d}x\) and \(f_{\mathrm{i}} = \int_0^L q\,\psi_{\mathrm{i}}\,\mathrm{d}x\) give exactly the system (6.9) of section 6.3, which is also the system of the second trial field in chapter 4. Its solution \(c_1 = 2aL\), \(c_2 = -a\) is the true field.

Definition 6.32 Ritz method (Ritzova metoda)

Let a problem of a bar, a beam or a body be given in the weak form with the bilinear form \(B\), the linear form \(\ell\), admissible fields and test functions (definition 6.6). Choose an admissible field \(\psi_0\) and \(n\) linearly independent test functions \(\psi_1, \dots, \psi_{\mathrm{n}}\) (definition 6.22), that is, functions with zero values of those quantities (for the bar the displacement, for the beam the displacement and the rotation) that the support prescribes. We denote the set of all their combinations \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} c_{\mathrm{i}}\,\psi_{\mathrm{i}}\) by \(\mathcal V_{\mathrm{n}}\) and call it the trial space (poskusni prostor). The Ritz method (Ritz method) with \(n\) terms chooses in the family \(\psi_0 + \mathcal V_{\mathrm{n}}\) the field \(u_{\mathrm{n}}\) for which \(\Pi\) is smallest. The Ritz solution (Ritzova rešitev) \(u_{\mathrm{n}}\) has the numbers \(c_{\mathrm{i}}\) from the system \(\mat K\,\vek c = \vek f\) of equation (6.18), where \(K_{\mathrm{ij}} = B(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}, \psi_{\mathrm{i}})\) and \(f_{\mathrm{i}} = \ell(\psi_{\mathrm{i}}) - B(\psi_0, \psi_{\mathrm{i}})\).

Source: EP (6.3.3) (p. 288), (6.3.13) to (6.3.15) (pp. 291 and 292), COOK §4.5, (4.5-1) to (4.5-9) (PDF pp. 160 to 163), CM (7.6.54) (PDF p. 355). In EP the first function is in the test and \(\psi_0\) is denoted \(\varphi_0\).

The definition assumes that the lowest point is unique and that we get it from the system. This has not been shown yet. The system from the derivation certainly finds a stationary point, but it is the lowest one only if \(\mat K\) is of a special kind. When is this true?

Theorem 6.33 Uniqueness of the Ritz solution (enoličnost Ritzove rešitve)

Within the assumptions of the section, let \(B\) be a symmetric bilinear form for which \(B(w, w) > 0\) for every test function \(w \ne 0\), and let \(\psi_0, \psi_1, \dots, \psi_{\mathrm{n}}\) be as in definition 6.32. Then: (1) the matrix \(\mat K\) is symmetric and positive definite, (2) the system \(\mat K\,\vek c = \vek f\) has exactly one solution \(\vek c\), (3) the field \(u_{\mathrm{n}}\) with this solution has the smallest energy among all the fields of the family \(\psi_0 + \mathcal V_{\mathrm{n}}\) and no other field of the family has the same value.

Proof

We must show that \(\mat K\) is the matrix of energy inner products of independent functions, and is therefore positive definite, and then we use what we know about a quadratic function with such a matrix.

  1. The matrix is symmetric, \(K_{\mathrm{ij}} = B(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}, \psi_{\mathrm{i}}) = B(\psi_{\mathrm{i}}, \psi_{\mathrm{j}}) = K_{\mathrm{ji}}\), because \(B\) is symmetric.
  2. Let \(\vek c \ne \vek 0\) and \(v = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} c_{\mathrm{i}}\,\psi_{\mathrm{i}}\). By bilinearity \(\vek c^{\mathsf T} \mat K\,\vek c = \sum_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{i}}\,c_{\mathrm{j}}\,B(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}, \psi_{\mathrm{i}}) = B(v, v)\).
  3. The functions \(\psi_{\mathrm{i}}\) are independent, so \(v \ne 0\) (definition 6.22). The field \(v\) is a combination of test functions and so a test function, so by assumption \(B(v, v) > 0\). The matrix \(\mat K\) is positive definite (definition 6.27), which is a case of lemma 6.28 with the energy inner product \(B\) (definition 6.16).
  4. By equation (6.17), \(\Pi(u_{\mathrm{n}}) = \Pi(\psi_0) + \tfrac12\,\vek c^{\mathsf T}\mat K\,\vek c - \vek f^{\mathsf T}\vek c\). By lemma 6.30 this function of the numbers \(\vek c\) has exactly one smallest value, at \(\mat K\,\vek c = \vek f\).
  5. By theorem 6.29 the system has exactly one solution and elimination reaches it.
  6. Different numbers \(\vek c\) give different fields, because the \(\psi_{\mathrm{i}}\) are independent, so no other field of the family has the same value of the energy as \(u_{\mathrm{n}}\).

Source: EP §6.3.3, pp. 293 to 296, CM (7.6.63) (PDF p. 358), COOK PDF p. 161 ("must be admissible"). The proof in steps is not from the sources.

The proof showed that the uniqueness of the system is not a coincidence. It comes from the fact that the energy of every field that is not zero is positive and that the functions do not depend on one another. Each requirement of the definition has its purpose:

  1. The field \(\psi_0\) and the functions \(\psi_{\mathrm{i}}\) satisfy the essential conditions, so that every field of the family is admissible. Only then is the energy not smaller than the true one, as we shall show in the subsection 6.4.4. We do not require the natural conditions, because they are already in \(\ell\) (subsection 6.4.2).
  2. The functions are independent, so that \(\mat K\) is positive definite and the system has exactly one solution. If they were dependent, for example \(x\,(L - x)\) and \(L\,x\,(L - x)\) for the simply supported beam, two rows of the system would be multiples of each other, the numbers \(c_{\mathrm{i}}\) would not be determined uniquely and the field would be the same.
  3. The family must be rich enough that the true solution can be approximated as closely as we wish. This requirement is called completeness (polnost). It is not needed for uniqueness, but it is needed for convergence (subsection 6.4.5).
  4. The energy of every function of the family must be finite, so that the numbers \(K_{\mathrm{ij}}\) are determined at all. For the bar, continuous piecewise smooth functions suffice, for the beam a continuous first derivative is also needed (section 6.7.4).

We use the same idea for the natural frequencies of beams and bars, where instead of the energy we minimize the Rayleigh quotient (Rayleighov količnik). It needs mass and vibration, which we do not have yet, so we do this in chapter 13.

6.4.2 Bar (palica)

Let us test the method on the hanging bar of section 4.6.8. The steel bar has length \(L = 10\) m, cross-sectional area \(A = 78{,}54\ \text{mm}^2\) and \(EA = 1{,}539 \cdot 10^7\) N. The axis \(x\) points down, the upper cross section is fixed, the load is the own weight \(q = \rho g A = 6{,}010\) N/m, and there is no force at the lower end. The true field is \(a\,(2Lx - x^2)\) with \(a = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1}\) and energy \(-391{,}03\) µJ.

Take one term, \(n = 1\) and \(\psi_0 = 0\), with the function \(\psi = 3L^2 x - x^3\). It satisfies the essential condition, \(\psi(0) = 0\). Its derivative \(\psi' = 3\,(L^2 - x^2)\) is zero at \(x = L\), which matches the natural condition of the true solution, \(N(L) = 0\), but we did not have to require it. The function does not contain the true field, since the true solution has the constant second derivative \(-2a\) and \(\psi\) has \(-6x\). So the Ritz solution differs from the true one and we compute by how much.

  1. The matrix has one element, \(K = \int_0^L EA\,\psi'^2\,\mathrm{d}x = 9\,EA \int_0^L (L^2 - x^2)^2\,\mathrm{d}x = 9\,EA\,\tfrac{8}{15}\,L^5 = \tfrac{24}{5}\,EA\,L^5\), because \((L^2 - x^2)^2 = L^4 - 2L^2 x^2 + x^4\) and the integrals of these terms are \(L^5\), \(\tfrac23 L^5\) and \(\tfrac15 L^5\).
  2. The right-hand side is \(f = \int_0^L q\,\psi\,\mathrm{d}x = q\,\big(\tfrac32\,L^4 - \tfrac14\,L^4\big) = \tfrac54\,q\,L^4\).
  3. The equation \(K c = f\) gives \(c = \dfrac{5}{4}\,q\,L^4 \Big/ \Big(\dfrac{24}{5}\,EA\,L^5\Big) = \dfrac{25\,q}{96\,EA\,L} = 1{,}017 \cdot 10^{-8}\ \text{m}^{-2}\).
  4. The field is \(u_1 = c\,\psi\). At the end \(u_1(L) = 2c\,L^3 = \tfrac{25}{48}\,q\,L^2/EA = 20{,}33\) µm, at the middle \(u_1(L/2) = \tfrac{11}{8}\,c\,L^3 = 13{,}98\) µm. The true displacements are \(\tfrac12\,q\,L^2/EA = 19{,}52\) µm and \(\tfrac38\,q\,L^2/EA = 14{,}64\) µm.
  5. By equation (6.17) with \(\psi_0 = 0\) the energy is \(\Pi(u_1) = \tfrac12\,K c^2 - f c = -\tfrac12\,f c = -381{,}87\) µJ, where we used \(K c = f\).

So the displacement of the end is 4,2 % too large, the displacement of the middle 4,5 % too small, and the energy is \(9{,}165\) µJ above the true one, \(-381{,}87\) against \(-391{,}03\) µJ (computed with SymPy). The two displacements are not wrong on the same side. Why? Let us look at the strain (figure 6.11). In units of \(q\,L/EA = 3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-6}\) the true strain \(2a\,(L - x)\) is equal to \(1 - x/L\), and the strain of the field \(u_1\) is \(3c\,(L^2 - x^2) = \tfrac{25}{32}\,(1 - x^2/L^2)\). Near the top it is \(0{,}78\) against \(1\), at \(x = L/2\) it is \(0{,}59\) against \(0{,}5\), and at \(x = 2{,}8\) m the curves cross. The displacement of the middle is the integral of the strain up to the middle, where the field has too little strain over the first \(2{,}8\) m and then somewhat too much, so some of the true value is missing. The displacement of the end is the integral over the whole bar and there it exceeds the true value. The method does not estimate either of these two displacements separately, only the energy.

So far the essential condition has been zero. What do we do if a support prescribes a displacement that is not zero? Take the same bar, fixed at both ends. The upper cross section is at rest, \(u(0) = 0\), and the lower end is fixed to the ground, which has settled by \(d = 0{,}1\) mm downward, so \(u(L) = d\). The bar also carries its own weight. There are now two essential conditions and the second is not zero.

The field \(\psi_0\) must satisfy both conditions and let it be the simplest possible. This is the line \(\psi_0 = d\,x/L\) (EP, p. 295, requirement 1: "lowest-order function"). The function \(\psi_1 = x\,(L - x)\) is zero at both ends, which is the homogeneous form of both conditions. The family is \(u_1 = d\,x/L + c\,x\,(L - x)\) (figure 6.12).

  1. The matrix is \(K = \int_0^L EA\,(L - 2x)^2\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac13\,EA\,L^3\), because \(\psi_1' = L - 2x\).
  2. The term \(B(\psi_0, \psi_1) = EA\,\tfrac{d}{L} \int_0^L (L - 2x)\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\), because the integral equals \(L^2 - L^2 = 0\). So \(f = \ell(\psi_1) - 0 = \int_0^L q\,x\,(L - x)\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac16\,q\,L^3\).
  3. The equation gives \(c = \tfrac16\,q\,L^3 \cdot 3/(EA\,L^3) = q/(2EA) = a\). The Ritz field is \(u_1 = a\,x\,(L - x) + d\,x/L\).

Since \(B(\psi_0, \psi_1)\) is zero, the movement of the support did not change \(c\). The reason: the line has the constant strain \(d/L\), and the function \(\psi_1\) is zero at both ends, so the integral of its derivative is zero and the constant strain does no work on it. Is this the true field? The axial force is \(N = EA\,u_1' = EA\,\big(a\,(L - 2x) + d/L\big)\), its derivative \(N' = -2\,EA\,a = -q\) (because \(2EA\,a = \rho g A = q\)) satisfies the equilibrium \(N' + q = 0\), and the field satisfies both conditions. Integrating the equation \(EA\,u'' = -q\) twice (fundamental theorem of calculus 3.40) gives \(u = -q\,x^2/(2EA) + C_1 x + C_2\), the conditions determine \(C_2 = 0\) and \(C_1\), so the solution is unique and \(u_1\) is the true field. It also satisfies the weak form: for a test function \(w\) with \(w(0) = w(L) = 0\) integration by parts (corollary 3.41) gives \(\int_0^L N\,w'\,\mathrm{d}x = -\int_0^L N'\,w\,\mathrm{d}x = \int_0^L q\,w\,\mathrm{d}x = \ell(w)\). The axial forces at the ends are \(N(0) = EA\,(aL + d/L) = 30{,}05 + 153{,}94 = 183{,}99\) N and \(N(L) = EA\,(-aL + d/L) = 123{,}89\) N. The difference is \(60{,}10\) N, the weight of the bar \(q L\), as it must be. The larger part of the force, \(153{,}94\) N, comes from the movement of the support, \(d/L = 10^{-5}\) of constant strain.

Something more is worth saying about the natural conditions. The true field of the hanging bar satisfies \(N(L) = 0\), and so does the cubic function of the first example, because we chose it so: \(EA\,u_1'(L) = 0\). The trial field \(c\,x\) of chapter 4 does not satisfy it. Its axial force is \(N(L) = EA\,aL = 30{,}05\) N, half the weight, at an end where there is no force, and yet it gives the true displacement of the end. The method does not impose the natural condition: it is part of the expression for \(\ell\), and the energy satisfies it as far as the family allows. For the cubic field the axial force at the fixing is \(N(0) = EA\,u_1'(0) = \tfrac{25}{32}\,q\,L = 46{,}95\) N against the true \(q\,L = 60{,}10\) N, so 22 % too little, although the displacement of the end is 4 % too high. A condition that we do not require we get only approximately, and the error in the derivative is larger than the error in the displacement.

6.4.3 Beams (nosilci)

For the beam \(B(v, w) = \int_0^L EI\,v''\,w''\,\mathrm{d}x\) and \(\ell(w) = \int_0^L q\,w\,\mathrm{d}x + F_{\mathrm{y}}\,w(L) + M_0\,w'(L)\) at a free end \(x = L\) with force \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\) and couple \(M_0\). The trial functions must have continuous first two derivatives and satisfy the essential conditions \(v = 0\) and \(v' = 0\) at the clamp (section 5.11.4). We use the beams of section 5.1.3, a steel strip \(20 \times 40\) mm with \(EI = 20\,906{,}7\) N m².

Cantilever with a force at the end. The cantilever is clamped at \(x = 0\), of length \(L = 1\) m, and a force \(F_{\mathrm{y}} = -500\) N acts downward at the end. The true deflection is a cubic polynomial with the displacement of the end \(-7{,}972\) mm. The trial function \(\psi_1 = x^2\) satisfies \(\psi(0) = \psi'(0) = 0\). We get \(K = 4\,EI\,L\), \(f = F_{\mathrm{y}}\,L^2\), \(c = F_{\mathrm{y}}\,L/(4\,EI)\) and the displacement of the end \(c L^2 = F_{\mathrm{y}}\,L^3/(4\,EI) = -5{,}979\) mm, so \(\tfrac34\) of the true value, as in section 5.11.6. With a second function \(\psi_2 = x^3\) we have \(\mat K = EI \begin{pmatrix} 4L & 6L^2 \\ 6L^2 & 12L^3 \end{pmatrix}\), \(\vek f = F_{\mathrm{y}}\,(L^2, L^3)\), the solution \(c_1 = F_{\mathrm{y}}\,L/(2\,EI)\), \(c_2 = -F_{\mathrm{y}}/(6\,EI)\) and the displacement of the end \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\,L^3/(3\,EI) = -7{,}972\) mm, exact. Why exact? The true deflection is a cubic polynomial without a constant and a linear term, so it lies in the family \(\{c_1 x^2 + c_2 x^3\}\). By corollary 5.74 it has the smallest energy among all admissible fields, so it has it also among the fields of the family, and Ritz finds the only field of the family with the smallest energy (theorem 6.33).

Cantilever under its own weight. The same cantilever carries its own weight, \(q = -q_{\mathrm{t}}\) with \(q_{\mathrm{t}} = \rho g A = 61{,}21\) N/m (\(A = 800\ \text{mm}^2\)). The true deflection is a polynomial of the fourth degree, \(v = -q_{\mathrm{t}}\,(x^4 - 4L x^3 + 6L^2 x^2)/(24\,EI)\), with the displacement of the end \(-0{,}3660\) mm and the moment at the clamp \(M(0) = EI\,v''(0) = -q_{\mathrm{t}} L^2/2 = -30{,}61\) N m. The trial functions are \(x^2\), \(x^3\), \(x^4\), each next one adds a term. Since \(\ell(x^k) = -q_{\mathrm{t}}\,L^{k+1}/(k+1)\), we get the system as before. With one term \(K = 4\,EI\,L\), \(f = -\tfrac13\,q_{\mathrm{t}}\,L^3\) and \(c = -q_{\mathrm{t}}\,L^2/(12\,EI)\). With two terms \(\mat K\) is the same as above and \(\vek f = -q_{\mathrm{t}}\,(L^3/3,\ L^4/4)\), which gives \(c_1 = -5\,q_{\mathrm{t}}\,L^2/(24\,EI)\), \(c_2 = q_{\mathrm{t}}\,L/(12\,EI)\) (computed with SymPy). The results:

\(x^2\) \(-0{,}2440\) \(2/3\) \(-10{,}20\) \(1/3\) \(-2489{,}4\)

We notice two things. The displacement of the end is exact already with two terms, but the moment at the clamp only with three. And with one term the displacement of the end is \(\tfrac23\) of the true value, but the moment only \(\tfrac13\).

Why is the displacement of the end exact with two terms? The displacement of the end due to a unit force at the end is the field \((3L x^2 - x^3)/(6\,EI)\), a cubic polynomial without a constant and a linear term, so it is in the family \(\{c_1 x^2 + c_2 x^3\}\). We shall prove that the Ritz displacement at this point is then exact (theorem 6.38 in section 6.5). Why does the moment lag behind? The moment is \(EI\,v''\), the second derivative of the trial function. Two functions that are close to each other can have very different derivatives, and the higher the derivative, the larger the difference. COOK describes this with the functions \(4x\,(1 - x)\) and a sine, which are similar on \([0, 1]\) but have ever less similar successive derivatives (COOK, PDF p. 163). The two-term field is cubic, so its moment is linear, while the true \(M\) is the parabola \(-q_{\mathrm{t}}\,(L - x)^2/2\) (figure 6.13). COOK does not set a rule about the size of stresses, because it would be "either so crude or so equivocal as to be of little value" (COOK, PDF p. 165).

Simply supported beam. A beam of length \(L = 2\) m is supported by a fixed pin support at \(x = 0\) and a movable one at \(x = L\). The essential conditions are \(v(0) = v(L) = 0\), the natural ones \(M(0) = M(L) = 0\). The load is \(q = -q_0\) with \(q_0 = 1\) kN/m. The true displacement at the middle is \(-5\,q_0\,L^4/(384\,EI) = -9{,}965\) mm, the moment \(M = \tfrac12\,q_0\,x\,(L - x)\) reaches \(500\) N m at the middle and is zero at the supports.

The trial function \(\psi_1 = x\,(L - x)\) is zero at both ends, \(\psi_1'' = -2\). We get \(K = 4\,EI\,L\), \(f = -\tfrac16\,q_0\,L^3\) and \(c = -q_0\,L^2/(24\,EI)\). The displacement at the middle is \(c\,L^2/4 = -7{,}972\) mm, \(\tfrac45\) of the true value, which agrees with EP (example 6.3.4, pp. 310 to 312), where the error of one term is 20 %. The moment \(M = EI\,v'' = -2\,EI\,c = q_0 L^2/12 = 333{,}3\) N m is constant over the whole beam, also at the supports, where the true moment is zero. A parabola has constant curvature and cannot satisfy the natural condition \(M = 0\). Why exactly \(333{,}3\) N m? This is the average of the true moment over the length, \(\tfrac1L \int_0^L \tfrac12\,q_0\,x\,(L - x)\,\mathrm{d}x = q_0 L^2/12\). As for the cantilever in chapter 5, the constant curvature of the trial function chooses the average of the true one: the Ritz equation demands \(B(u_1, \psi_1) = \ell(\psi_1)\), the weak form for the true field the same, so \(\int EI\,(u_1'' - v'')\,\psi_1''\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\) and with \(\psi_1'' = -2\) the average of the difference is zero.

Let us add the function \(\psi_2 = x^2\,(L - x)\). We would expect a better result, but the displacement at the middle stays \(-7{,}972\) mm and \(c_2 = 0\) (computed with SymPy). We see the reason if we split \(\psi_2\) into two parts:

\[\psi_2 = \tfrac{L}{2}\,\psi_1 + \chi , \qquad \chi = x\,(L - x)\,\big(x - \tfrac{L}{2}\big) . \tag{6.19}\]

The first part is a multiple of \(\psi_1\), which the family already contains. The second, \(\chi\), is antisymmetric with respect to the middle: \(x\,(L - x)\) is symmetric and \(x - L/2\) is antisymmetric (figure 6.14). The family \(\{\psi_1, \psi_2\}\) is the same as \(\{\psi_1, \chi\}\), so we use it.

  1. The energy product of the functions \(\chi\) and \(\psi_1\) is \(B(\chi, \psi_1) = \int_0^L EI\,\chi''\,(-2)\,\mathrm{d}x = -2\,EI\,\big(\chi'(L) - \chi'(0)\big) = 0\), because \(\chi'\) is symmetric (the derivative of an antisymmetric function), so \(\chi'(L) = \chi'(0)\).
  2. The work of the load on \(\chi\) is \(\ell(\chi) = -q_0 \int_0^L \chi\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\), because the integral of an antisymmetric function over a symmetric interval is zero.
  3. The system falls apart: \(K_{11}\,c_1 = f_1\) and \(K_{22}\,c_\chi = 0\) with \(K_{22} = B(\chi, \chi) > 0\). So \(c_\chi = 0\) and \(c_1\) is the same as before.

A symmetric load does no work on the antisymmetric part of the field and the energy has no cross term, so the Ritz method does not use it. We would have to add a symmetric function. Take \(\psi_3 = x^2\,(L - x)^2\): it is symmetric, because \(x^2\,(L - x)^2 = (L^2/4 - s^2)^2\) at \(s = x - L/2\). With it we have \(\{\psi_1, \psi_3\}\) and \(K_{12} = 0\) (computed with SymPy), \(c_1 = -q_0 L^2/(24\,EI)\), \(c_3 = -q_0/(24\,EI)\) and the field is the true one. Indeed, the true deflection \(-q_0\,(x^4 - 2L x^3 + L^3 x)/(24\,EI)\) is exactly \(\psi_3 + L^2\,\psi_1\), as the expansion \(x^2 L^2 - 2L x^3 + x^4 + L^3 x - L^2 x^2\) shows, so it lies in the family. The moment is the true one, \(M(0) = M(L) = 0\) and \(M(L/2) = 500\) N m. With the two terms \(x\,(L - x)\) and \(x^2\,(L - x)\) the result is the same as with one, which is also written by EP (p. 312, "one should not conclude that the Ritz approximation has already converged") and SADD (example 6.2, PDF pp. 144 and 145). Both obtain the true field only with polynomials up to \(x^3\,(L - x)\), while we get it already with two, if we choose the second term symmetric.

6.4.4 What the Ritz solution guarantees (kaj Ritzova rešitev zagotavlja)

In all the cases above the energy of the Ritz field was above the true one: \(-381{,}87\) against \(-391{,}03\) µJ for the hanging bar, \(-1{,}495\) against \(-1{,}993\) J for the cantilever, \(-5{,}315\) against \(-6{,}378\) J for the simply supported beam. Is this always so and what else follows from it? The theorem holds for the bar, the beam and the body at once, because it uses only the properties of the form \(B\).

Theorem 6.34 Energy of the Ritz solution (energija Ritzove rešitve)

Let \(u\) be the solution of a problem of a bar, a beam or a body in the weak form (definition 6.6), \(B(u, w) = \ell(w)\) for every test function \(w\). Let the supports hold the body, so that \(B(w, w) > 0\) for every test function \(w \ne 0\), and let the loads not depend on the deformation. Let \(u_{\mathrm{n}}\) be the Ritz solution in the family \(\psi_0 + \mathcal V_{\mathrm{n}}\). Then:

  1. For any \(\psi_0\) and for every admissible field \(v\) with the same essential conditions as \(u\), in particular for \(u_{\mathrm{n}}\) and for every field of the family \(\psi_0 + \mathcal V_{\mathrm{n}}\), \(\Pi(v) - \Pi(u) = \tfrac12\,B(v - u, v - u) = \tfrac12\,\|v - u\|_{\mathrm{E}}^2 \ge 0\), with equality only at \(v = u\). So \(\Pi(u_{\mathrm{n}}) \ge \Pi(u)\) and \(\Pi(u_{\mathrm{n}}) - \Pi(u) = \tfrac12\,\|u - u_{\mathrm{n}}\|_{\mathrm{E}}^2\).
  2. Only for zero essential conditions (\(\psi_0 = 0\)), \(B(u_{\mathrm{n}}, u_{\mathrm{n}}) = \ell(u_{\mathrm{n}})\) and \(\Pi(u_{\mathrm{n}}) = -\tfrac12\,\ell(u_{\mathrm{n}})\). The work of the given loads on the Ritz field is not larger than on the true one, \(\ell(u_{\mathrm{n}}) \le \ell(u)\), and the strain energy of the Ritz field is not larger than the true one, \(U(u_{\mathrm{n}}) \le U(u)\).
Proof

We must show that the difference of the energies falls apart into the square of the error (the energy norm of definition 6.18), and then that the Ritz equations give the work of the loads as twice the energy.

  1. Let \(v\) be an admissible field with the same prescribed values at the supports as \(u\) and \(e = u - v\). The field \(e\) is the difference of two fields with the same prescribed values, so it has zero essential conditions and is a test function, for every \(\psi_0\). This holds for \(u_{\mathrm{n}}\), because \(\psi_0\) has the prescribed values and the functions \(\psi_{\mathrm{i}}\) are zero there.
  2. By the bilinearity and symmetry of the form \(B\) and the linearity of the form \(\ell\), \(\Pi(v) = \Pi(u - e) = \tfrac12\,B(u - e, u - e) - \ell(u - e) = \Pi(u) + \big[\ell(e) - B(u, e)\big] + \tfrac12\,B(e, e)\).
  3. The weak form with the test function \(e\) gives \(B(u, e) = \ell(e)\), so the square bracket is zero and \(\Pi(v) - \Pi(u) = \tfrac12\,B(e, e) = \tfrac12\,\|e\|_{\mathrm{E}}^2\). Since \(B(-e, -e) = B(e, e)\), this is also \(\tfrac12\,B(v - u, v - u)\).
  4. By assumption \(B(e, e) > 0\) for \(e \ne 0\), so the difference is non-negative and zero only at \(e = 0\), that is, at \(v = u\). This is point 1.
  5. For \(\psi_0 = 0\) the Ritz equations are \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} K_{\mathrm{ij}}\,c_{\mathrm{j}} = \ell(\psi_{\mathrm{i}})\). Multiply the \(i\)-th by \(c_{\mathrm{i}}\) and sum over \(i\). The left side is \(\sum_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{i}}\,c_{\mathrm{j}}\,B(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}, \psi_{\mathrm{i}}) = B(u_{\mathrm{n}}, u_{\mathrm{n}})\) by bilinearity, the right side \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} c_{\mathrm{i}}\,\ell(\psi_{\mathrm{i}}) = \ell(u_{\mathrm{n}})\) by linearity.
  6. So \(\Pi(u_{\mathrm{n}}) = \tfrac12\,B(u_{\mathrm{n}}, u_{\mathrm{n}}) - \ell(u_{\mathrm{n}}) = -\tfrac12\,\ell(u_{\mathrm{n}})\).
  7. For \(\psi_0 = 0\) the field \(u\) also has zero essential conditions, so it is a test function. The weak form with \(w = u\) gives \(B(u, u) = \ell(u)\) and as in step 6, \(\Pi(u) = -\tfrac12\,\ell(u)\).
  8. From point 1 it follows that \(-\tfrac12\,\ell(u_{\mathrm{n}}) \ge -\tfrac12\,\ell(u)\), so \(\ell(u_{\mathrm{n}}) \le \ell(u)\). The strain energy is \(U(u_{\mathrm{n}}) = \tfrac12\,B(u_{\mathrm{n}}, u_{\mathrm{n}}) = \tfrac12\,\ell(u_{\mathrm{n}}) \le \tfrac12\,\ell(u) = U(u)\).

Source: BATHE (4.89) to (4.91) (PDF p. 258, "the strain energy corresponding to the finite element solution is always smaller than or equal to"), COOK PDF p. 164 ("either exact or too stiff", the computed strain energy is a lower bound). For the bar point 1 is the computation of corollary 4.93, for the beam of corollary 5.74 and for the body of theorem 4.91, written here with the weak form. The proof in steps is not from the sources.

Point 1 of the proof does not use \(\psi_0 = 0\), so it holds also for a prescribed displacement of a support. Point 2 does not: for the bar fixed at both ends with the settlement \(d = 0{,}1\) mm (subsection 6.4.2), \(u\) is a field with the prescribed \(u(L) = d\), which is not a test function, and \(B(u, u) - \ell(u) = N(L)\,d = 123{,}89\ \text{N} \cdot 0{,}1\ \text{mm} = 12{,}39\) mJ, not zero (computed with SymPy).

The proof showed two things. The Ritz energy is above the true one by exactly the strain energy of the error that the trial family missed, and the work of the loads on the Ritz field is always too small because of the equality \(B(u_{\mathrm{n}}, u_{\mathrm{n}}) = \ell(u_{\mathrm{n}})\). A test on the hanging bar: the work of the weight on the true field is \(782{,}06\) µJ, on the cubic Ritz field \(763{,}73\) µJ, so 2,3 % less. The strain energy is \(381{,}87\) against \(391{,}03\) µJ. The difference of the energies, \(9{,}165\) µJ, is the strain energy of the error \(u - u_1\) (computed with SymPy), as the proof predicts. For zero essential conditions one more conclusion follows from point 2: if the family contains the true field, Ritz finds it, because \(\Pi(u_{\mathrm{n}}) \le \Pi(u)\) (the smallest value in the family) and by point 1 at the same time \(\ge\), so equality holds and \(u_{\mathrm{n}} = u\). This is how we got the exact result for the cantilever with a force at the end with \(x^2, x^3\) and for the cantilever under its own weight with \(x^2, x^3, x^4\).

What does the theorem not say? It does not say that every displacement is too small. The work of the loads on the field is an average over the loaded part, while the displacement at one point is a single value. For the hanging bar the work of the weight is 2,3 % too small, while the displacement of the end is 4,2 % too large. It shows more strongly on the stepped bar of section 6.1.3, with the upper \(5\) m of diameter \(10\) mm and the lower \(5\) m of diameter \(5\) mm. The Ritz solution with the single trial function \(\psi = x\) is the best line of section 6.2.2, with the displacement of the end \(13{,}66\) µm instead of \(12{,}20\) µm, 12 % too high. By item 2 the work of the weight is \(-2\Pi\), so with the energies \(-89{,}81\) and \(-106{,}92\) µJ of the same section it is \(179{,}6\) µJ against the true \(213{,}8\) µJ, 16 % too small, as the theorem demands. Ritz therefore bounds the work of the loads, but not every displacement.

The rule holds for the displacement under a force if it is the only load. For one force \(F\) at a point \(P\), \(\ell(w) = F\,w(P)\), so \(\ell(u_{\mathrm{n}}) \le \ell(u)\) means \(F\,u_{\mathrm{n}}(P) \le F\,u(P)\): the displacement of the point \(P\) in the direction of the force is never too large. For the cantilever with a force at the end this is \(\tfrac34\) of the true value and with \(x^2, x^3\) exactly one. COOK (PDF p. 164) writes the same: under a single concentrated force the displacement at its point is certain to be underpredicted, or at best exact in a very simple problem, never overpredicted. COOK does not treat the cantilever there, the two numbers are ours. The common shortening "Ritz displacements are always too small" is wrong, as the hanging bar with its 4,2 % too high displacement of the end shows. The correct statement is: the displacement under the only force is too small or exact, the work of all the loads is too small or exact, and individual displacements can be on either side.

The last example shows how a family of fields can turn out to be too stiff. Take the hanging bar as a body in space, as in section 3.1.5, with a cross section of area \(A\) and a free lateral side. The upper face has a support that holds only the displacement in the direction of the axis, \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\), and other supports prevent rigid movements (an idealization: a real support would also hold the lateral displacements at the edge of the face and give a stress concentration). The true field is \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = a\,(2Lx - x^2)\) with the lateral thinning, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = -\nu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) (chapter 3), \(\nu = 0{,}3\). The trial field \(\vek u = c\,(2Lx - x^2)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) prevents the thinning, \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\).

  1. The strains of the field are \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = 2c\,(L - x)\), all the others are zero, because \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(u_{\mathrm{z}}\) are zero and \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) does not depend on \(y\) and \(z\).
  2. By Hooke's law (3.13), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = (\lambda + 2\mu)\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = \lambda\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\). Since of the strains only \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) is different from zero, \(\ten\sigma : \ten\varepsilon = (\lambda + 2\mu)\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}^2\).
  3. For \(\psi = 2Lx - x^2\), \(K = A\,(\lambda + 2\mu) \int_0^L 4\,(L - x)^2\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac43\,A\,(\lambda + 2\mu)\,L^3\) and \(f = \rho g A \int_0^L \psi\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac23\,\rho g A\,L^3\).
  4. So \(c = \rho g/\big(2\,(\lambda + 2\mu)\big)\). The displacement of the end is \(c\,L^2 = \dfrac{E}{\lambda + 2\mu}\,\dfrac{\rho g L^2}{2E}\).

For steel \(\lambda + 2\mu = 263{,}85\) GPa (definition 3.10), the ratio \(E/(\lambda + 2\mu) = (1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)/(1 - \nu) = 0{,}7429\) and the displacement of the end \(14{,}50\) µm against \(19{,}52\) µm. The cause is the thinning (figure 6.15). The true field thins freely in the lateral direction, so for a uniaxial load \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\). The trial field does not allow the thinning and must have lateral stresses \(\lambda\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), which the body does not have in uniaxial tension, so the material resists with \(\lambda + 2\mu = 263{,}85\) GPa instead of \(E = 196\) GPa, which is \(1{,}346\) times stronger. The solution is too stiff, the displacement of the end is \(0{,}743\) of the true one, and the work of the weight is smaller in the same ratio, as the theorem demands. A family of fields that prevents a deformation that the body needs under this load therefore gives a too stiff solution. The phenomenon is called locking (zaklepanje) and it will appear again in the finite elements of beams and of the continuum (chapters 12 and 15).

6.4.5 A sequence of ever richer families (zaporedje vse bogatejših družin)

We know that the Ritz solution for a given \(n\) is the best in its family. What happens when we increase \(n\), and when does the approximation approach the true solution? In chapter 4 we wrote that a richer family that contains a poorer one does not give a worse estimate. Now we prove this and add when the limit is the true one.

Let \(\mathcal V_1 \subset \mathcal V_2 \subset \dots\), where each family contains the previous one, with a common \(\psi_0\). Such are the families of polynomials \(x, x^2, \dots, x^n\), where each family adds one function, and the piecewise linear fields on meshes, where we get each mesh by halving the pieces of the previous one, because a line on a large piece is a line also on both halves (section 6.7).

Theorem 6.35 Convergence of Ritz solutions (konvergenca Ritzovih rešitev)

Within theorem 6.34, let \(\mathcal V_1 \subset \mathcal V_2 \subset \dots\) be a sequence of trial spaces with the same \(\psi_0\) and \(u_{\mathrm{n}}\) the Ritz solution in \(\psi_0 + \mathcal V_{\mathrm{n}}\). Then \(\Pi(u_1) \ge \Pi(u_2) \ge \dots \ge \Pi(u)\) and \(\|u - u_{\mathrm{n}}\|_{\mathrm{E}}\) does not grow. If moreover for every \(\varepsilon > 0\) there exist \(N\) and a field \(v \in \psi_0 + \mathcal V_{\mathrm{N}}\) with \(\|u - v\|_{\mathrm{E}} < \varepsilon\) (completeness), then \(\|u - u_{\mathrm{n}}\|_{\mathrm{E}} \to 0\) and \(\Pi(u_{\mathrm{n}}) \to \Pi(u)\).

Proof

We must show that a larger family cannot give a higher energy, and then that under the condition of completeness the energy of the error falls below every bound.

  1. The field \(u_{\mathrm{n}}\) is in \(\psi_0 + \mathcal V_{\mathrm{n}} \subset \psi_0 + \mathcal V_{\mathrm{n}+1}\). By definition (theorem 6.33, point 3) the field \(u_{\mathrm{n}+1}\) has the smallest energy in the larger family, so \(\Pi(u_{\mathrm{n}+1}) \le \Pi(u_{\mathrm{n}})\).
  2. By theorem 6.34, point 1, \(\Pi(u_{\mathrm{n}}) \ge \Pi(u)\) and \(\|u - u_{\mathrm{n}}\|_{\mathrm{E}}^2 = 2\,\big(\Pi(u_{\mathrm{n}}) - \Pi(u)\big)\). A decreasing energy therefore means a decreasing energy norm of the error, which is the first claim.
  3. For completeness let \(\varepsilon > 0\) and \(N\), \(v\) as in the theorem. For \(n \ge N\), \(v \in \psi_0 + \mathcal V_{\mathrm{N}} \subset \psi_0 + \mathcal V_{\mathrm{n}}\), so \(\Pi(u_{\mathrm{n}}) \le \Pi(v)\).
  4. By point 2 and step 3, \(\|u - u_{\mathrm{n}}\|_{\mathrm{E}}^2 = 2\,\big(\Pi(u_{\mathrm{n}}) - \Pi(u)\big) \le 2\,\big(\Pi(v) - \Pi(u)\big) = \|u - v\|_{\mathrm{E}}^2 < \varepsilon^2\), where we got the last equality from theorem 6.34, point 1, for the field \(v\) (it is admissible, but not necessarily the Ritz one).
  5. For every \(\varepsilon\) there exists \(N\) such that for \(n \ge N\), \(\|u - u_{\mathrm{n}}\|_{\mathrm{E}} < \varepsilon\), so \(\|u - u_{\mathrm{n}}\|_{\mathrm{E}} \to 0\) (definition 1.38). From \(\Pi(u_{\mathrm{n}}) - \Pi(u) = \tfrac12\,\|u - u_{\mathrm{n}}\|_{\mathrm{E}}^2\) it follows that \(\Pi(u_{\mathrm{n}}) \to \Pi(u)\).

Source: COOK §4.9 (PDF p. 176, "monotonic convergence of structural strain energy"), EP p. 299, point 4 (convergence when the requirements are met, without proof), BATHE PDF p. 258 (a larger space, a greater accuracy). The proof in steps is not from the sources. EP has no explicit statement of monotonicity for nested families.

The theorem showed that the energy cannot increase when the family is enriched, and that the error falls below every bound if the family allows it. Step 3 does not require \(u_{\mathrm{n}}\) to be close to the field \(v\), only that it is not worse than it in energy.

Let us test it on the sequences. For the hanging bar take piecewise linear fields on \(n = 1, 2, 4, 8, 16\) equal pieces: for \(n = 1\) this is the line \(c\,x\) of chapter 4. The energies are \(-293{,}27\), \(-366{,}59\), \(-384{,}92\), \(-389{,}50\) and \(-390{,}65\) µJ against \(-391{,}03\) µJ (figure 6.16), the differences are \(97{,}76/n^2\) µJ. For the cantilever under its own weight with the polynomials \(x^2\), \(x^3\), \(x^4\) the energies are \(-2489{,}4\), \(-4356{,}4\) and \(-4480{,}9\) µJ, from the third term on exactly the true one, because the true deflection lies in the family. In both sequences each energy is below the previous one and above the true one, as the theorem demands.

Is completeness satisfied? For piecewise linear fields we prove it in section 6.5 (theorem 6.41). For polynomials it is given by Weierstrass's theorem: every continuous function on a closed interval can be approximated by a polynomial as closely as we wish at all points (LEBL2, theorem 11.7.1, PDF p. 179). For a bar with constant \(EA\) whose true solution has a continuous derivative \(u'\) and \(\psi_0 = 0\), we take a polynomial \(p\) with \(|u' - p| < \delta\) everywhere and \(v(x) = \int_0^x p\,\mathrm{d}t\), which is a combination of the functions \(x, x^2, \dots, x^n\) for \(n\) large enough. Then \(\|u - v\|_{\mathrm{E}}^2 = \int_0^L EA\,(u' - p)^2\,\mathrm{d}x \le EA\,L\,\delta^2\), as small as we wish. For a beam whose true deflection has a continuous second derivative we do the same with the second derivative. No result of the book rests on this theorem, and the completeness of piecewise linear fields we prove ourselves.

The theorem needs nested families. The uniform meshes with two and three pieces are not one inside the other, because the nodes at \(10/3\) and \(20/3\) m do not coincide with the node at \(5\) m. The theorem then says nothing about the order of their solutions. For our bar the energy of the error still falls, \(24{,}44\) µJ at \(n = 2\) and \(10{,}86\) µJ at \(n = 3\). For every uniform \(n\) the energy of the error is \(EA\,a^2 L^3/(6n^2)\), which we checked with SymPy for \(n = 1, 2, 3, 4, 8, 16\).

The Ritz method is thus explained: as many functions as we like, one solution, an energy above the true one and decreasing as the family is enriched. What remains is the question of how fast and in which quantities the error falls, and when the displacement at a point is exact in spite of the error elsewhere. This will be section 6.5.
