Chapter 14 Heat conduction

14.3 Boundary conditions and uniqueness

In section 14.2 we obtained the heat equation (enačba prevajanja, theorem 14.8) from the heat balance of every finite part of a body, \(\rho c\,T_{\mathrm{t}} = \nabla\cdot(k\nabla T) + Q\), where \(T(P, t)\) is the temperature at the point \(P\) at time \(t\), \(T_{\mathrm{t}}\) its derivative with respect to time and \(Q\) the heat source per unit volume. Now we ask whether this equation alone determines the temperature. We shall show that it does not, and that it is determined once we also know what happens on the surface of the body and what the temperature was at the start. We shall arrange these data into a boundary value problem and prove that it has at most one solution, as for the boundary value problem of elasticity in chapter 11. The proof is the same as there: the difference of two solutions is estimated by an energy.

14.3.1 What the equation does not say

Take the steel bar of chapter 3, diameter \(d = 10\) mm, cross section \(A = 78{,}54\) mm², length \(L\). Let its lateral surface be insulated, which means that no heat flows through it (section 14.3.2 below says exactly what this means). Let the temperature be the same at all points of each cross section. Why may we assume this? Heat enters and leaves evenly over the whole ends, and none flows through the lateral surface, so nothing pushes heat across the axis. Here the assumption even costs nothing: the line \(T = T(0) + a x\), which we shall obtain below, is as a function of three coordinates also a solution of the spatial problem, since its gradient points along the axis and on the lateral surface is perpendicular to the normal, so \(\partial T/\partial n = 0\). It does not hold if the heating foil covers only part of an end or if the bar gives heat to the air through its lateral surface: then heat also flows across, and for a bar warmer than the air the middle of a cross section is warmer than its rim. With this assumption, by corollary 14.10 the temperature \(T(x, t)\) obeys the heat equation of the bar. Look at the steady state, in which the temperature does not change with time and there is no source: \(T_{\mathrm{t}} = 0\) and \(Q = 0\), so \(T'' = 0\). The function \(T'\) has derivative zero on an interval, so by the mean value theorem (theorem 1.85) it is constant, \(T' = a\), and the function \(T - a x\) has derivative zero, so it is constant and equal to its value at \(x = 0\): \(T(x) = T(0) + a x\). Every straight line is a solution. In the lower part of the figure the temperature \(T\) is drawn over the axis \(x\), and the three blue lines \(T_1\), \(T_2\) and \(T_3\) are three of these solutions. The equation leaves two numbers, \(T(0)\) and \(a\), to us.

Which line the bar follows is decided by what we do to its ends. Through the left end we supply a heat flux of \(1000\) W/m² with a heating foil, through the right end we remove an equally strong flux by cooling. The heat flux vector \(\vek q\) (toplotni tok, definition 14.2) has by Fourier's law (definition 14.3) the component \(q_{\mathrm{x}} = -k\,T'\). The flux that leaves the bar is \(\vek q\cdot\vek n\) with the outward normal \(\vek n\). At \(x = L\) the normal \(\vek n\) points in the direction \(+x\), so \(\vek q\cdot\vek n = -k\,T'(L)\), at \(x = 0\) it points in the direction \(-x\) and \(\vek q\cdot\vek n = k\,T'(0)\). The prescription at the left end is \(\vek q\cdot\vek n = -1000\) W/m² (the flux enters), at the right end \(+1000\) W/m². Hence \(T'(0) = -1000/k\) and \(T'(L) = -1000/k\). The two requirements give the same slope \(-q/k\), where \(q = 1000\) W/m², so every line of the family satisfies them, also the line \(T_2\), which is the line \(T_3\) raised by \(50\) K. The datum \(T(0)\) remains unknown: the equation together with the two conditions does not determine the temperature. We shall explain this example in detail at the end of the section and set it next to the uniqueness theorem. Now we look at what kinds of data there are at all.

14.3.2 Three kinds of conditions on the surface

For an elastic body we divided the surface into a part \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\), where supports prescribe the displacement, and a part \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\), where a known force acts (definition 11.1). For heat it is similar, except that there are three possibilities. Take a body \(V\) and its surface \(S\), drawn in cross section in figure 14.23. We denote its outward normal by \(\vek n\), and the flux through the surface per unit area is \(\vek q\cdot\vek n\) (definition 14.2), positive when heat flows out of the body.

The first possibility arises if we press the body against a wall whose temperature we hold, for example against a large bath whose temperature is practically not changed by the heat the body gives or takes. On this part \(S_{\mathrm{T}}\) the temperature is known, \(T = \bar T\), and we call it a prescribed temperature (predpisana temperatura). The second possibility arises if we know the heat flux through the surface, on the part \(S_{\mathrm{q}}\). With a heating foil on the lower face, insulated on its outer side so that all its power goes into the body, we measure the flux as the electric power divided by the area of the foil. The flux enters, so \(\bar q_{\mathrm{n}} < 0\). A special case is insulation (foam, the vacuum between the walls of a thermos flask), which heat does not cross: the flux \(\int\vek q\cdot\vek n\,\mathrm{d}A\) through every piece of the surface is zero, so on this part \(\bar q_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\). The condition is

\[\vek q\cdot\vek n = \bar q_{\mathrm{n}} \quad\text{or}\quad -k\,\frac{\partial T}{\partial n} = \bar q_{\mathrm{n}} , \tag{14.11}\]

where \(\partial T/\partial n = \nabla T\cdot\vek n\) is the derivative of the temperature in the direction of the normal (definition 14.3). With insulation, \(\partial T/\partial n = 0\): the temperature does not change in the direction of the normal and the isotherms (surfaces of constant temperature) meet the surface at a right angle, which COOK (PDF p. 482) gives as a check of an insulated edge. Why at a right angle? Along an isotherm the temperature does not change, so the gradient \(\nabla T\) is perpendicular to it. The condition \(\nabla T\cdot\vek n = 0\) says that at an insulated surface the gradient lies along the surface, and so the isotherm, perpendicular to the gradient, reaches the surface at a right angle. That the flux is zero at every point follows from continuity: a continuous function \(\vek q\cdot\vek n\) with integral zero over every piece is zero everywhere (otherwise it would be positive or negative on a piece around some point and the integral over that piece would not be zero).

The third possibility is the most common. The body is surrounded by air or water, whose temperature we do not hold constant, and we do not know the flux. But we can say what the flux depends on. Heat flows out if the surface is warmer than the fluid far from it, and flows in if it is colder, so \(\vek q\cdot\vek n\) will be positive for \(T > T_\infty\) and negative for \(T < T_\infty\), and zero for \(T = T_\infty\). Here \(T_\infty\) is the temperature of the fluid outside the thin boundary layer at the wall (COOK, PDF p. 469), in which the temperature changes. The simplest dependence with this sign is proportionality, and experiments support it approximately (KREYSZIG, PDF p. 41, more on this below in section 14.3.4): the flux is proportional to the temperature difference. The sources write this as a boundary condition with a name.

Definition 14.13 Newton's law of cooling (Newtonov zakon ohlajanja)

Let the part \(S_{\mathrm{h}}\) of the surface of a body be in contact with a fluid whose temperature outside the boundary layer at the surface is \(T_\infty\), and let \(h \ge 0\). Newton's law of cooling is the boundary condition that requires at every point of \(S_{\mathrm{h}}\)

\[\vek q\cdot\vek n = h\,(T - T_\infty) \quad\text{or}\quad -k\,\frac{\partial T}{\partial n} = h\,(T - T_\infty) , \tag{14.12}\]

where \(\vek n\) is the outward normal and \(\vek q = -k\nabla T\) the heat flux (definition 14.3). The number \(h\) is the heat transfer coefficient (koeficient toplotne prestopnosti).

Source: COOK §12.1 (PDF p. 469): equation (12.1-1), "called Newton's law of cooling", \(T_\infty\) is the temperature of the fluid outside the boundary layer. CM §6.7.3 (PDF p. 282): "heat transfer coefficient or film conductance", the law "also defines h".

The sources do not say where the name comes from, and we do not tell a history of the law here. In the sources \(h\) is also called film conductance (CM, PDF p. 282). In chapters 6 and 7 \(h\) is the length of an element, which in this chapter we write \(h_{\mathrm{e}}\). The unit of \(h\) is W/(m² K), because the flux per unit area is in W/m² and we get it by multiplying \(h\) by a temperature difference in K. The word film points to the thin layer of fluid at the wall, the boundary layer, in which the temperature changes from the temperature of the wall to \(T_\infty\) (COOK, PDF p. 469), that the name refers to exactly this layer is not stated in the sources. The coefficient \(h\) is not a property of a material but depends on the motion of the fluid, the roughness of the wall and more, so it is determined by experiment (COOK, PDF p. 469), for everyday bodies roughly between \(5\) and \(10^5\) W/(m² K), with large values for boiling and condensation. For radiation the flux is not proportional to a temperature difference but to the difference of the fourth powers of absolute temperatures (COOK, PDF p. 469), so we leave it out: the assumption of this chapter is that \(h\) and \(T_\infty\) are known.

What equation (14.12) means for the temperature we show on a slab of thickness \(L\). We hold the left face at \(x = 0\) at \(T_0 = 30\) K above the surroundings. We measure temperatures as the difference from the temperature of the surroundings \(T_\infty\), so on this scale the surroundings have the value \(T_\infty = 0\). The right face at \(x = L\) passes heat to the surroundings. The steady state is a line \(T(x) = T_0 + a\,x\) as before, drawn in the figure over the axis \(x\). The condition (14.12) at \(x = L\) is \(-k\,a = h\,(T_0 + a L - T_\infty)\), from which

\[a = -\frac{h\,(T_0 - T_\infty)}{k + h L} , \qquad T(L) = T_0 - (T_0 - T_\infty)\,\frac{\mathrm{Bi}}{1 + \mathrm{Bi}} , \qquad \mathrm{Bi} = \frac{h L}{k} . \tag{14.13}\]

The number \(\mathrm{Bi}\) (the symbol is ours) is the ratio of two temperature drops for the same flux \(q\): in the slab the temperature drops by \(q L/k\), in the fluid film by \(q/h\), the ratio is \(hL/k\). For \(\mathrm{Bi} = 1\) the drop in the slab and in the film are equal, the right face has \(15\) K, half the way to the surroundings. The line, continued beyond the right face, reaches \(T_\infty\) at \(x = L + k/h\), that is a distance \(k/h\) to the right of the face, which for \(\mathrm{Bi} = 1\) is exactly \(2L\): the tangent at \(x = L\) is the condition (14.12) written as a picture, since \(-k\,T' = h\,(T - T_\infty)\) is exactly \(T - T_\infty = -T'\,(k/h)\). Two more extremes:

  • \(\mathrm{Bi} = 10\): \(T(L) = 2{,}73\) K, the tangent reaches \(T_\infty\) already at \(1{,}1\,L\). The film hardly holds back any drop, the right face is close to \(T_\infty\) (for \(\mathrm{Bi} = 100\) only \(0{,}297\) K above it) and the condition (14.12) becomes the prescribed temperature \(T = T_\infty\). More precisely: \(T(L) - T_\infty = (T_0 - T_\infty)/(1 + \mathrm{Bi})\) tends to zero as \(h \to \infty\).
  • \(\mathrm{Bi} = 0{,}1\): \(T(L) = 27{,}27\) K, the difference \(T(0) - T(L)\) is \(9{,}1\) % of \(T_0 - T_\infty\), the tangent reaches \(T_\infty\) only at \(11\,L\), outside the figure. For \(h \to 0\) the value \(T(L)\) tends to \(T_0\) and the flux to zero: the condition becomes insulation.

Heat transfer is thus a bridge between the first two kinds of condition: a large \(h\) is a prescribed temperature, a small \(h\) is insulation.

Heat transfer in this section is a model. We assume that \(h\) and \(T_\infty\) are known, constant along the surface and independent of the temperature, and that the surroundings are not warmed by the body. A real fluid, in which the temperature goes from the wall to the surroundings along a complicated profile, has these properties only approximately. KREYSZIG (PDF p. 41) says in this connection that such experimental laws are derived under idealized assumptions that rarely hold exactly. The assumption fails when part of the heat is carried off by radiation, which grows with the fourth power of the absolute temperature (COOK, PDF p. 469), since the flux is then no longer proportional to the temperature difference. What an error in \(h\) costs is told by equation (14.13) on the same slab. If the true \(h\) is twice the assumed one, then at \(\mathrm{Bi} = 1\) in fact \(\mathrm{Bi} = 2\), the right face has \(30/3 = 10\) K instead of \(15\) K and the flux through the slab is \(h\,T(L) = 20\,k/L\) instead of \(15\,k/L\), which is \(33\) % more. At \(\mathrm{Bi} = 10\) the same error changes the flux only from \(27{,}27\,k/L\) to \(28{,}57\,k/L\), by \(4{,}8\) %, because there the film holds back only a small part of the drop. An uncertain \(h\) is thus most dangerous when \(\mathrm{Bi}\) is small: at \(\mathrm{Bi} = 0{,}1\) a twice larger \(h\) raises the flux by \(83\) %.

One more property must hold for all three kinds. At each point of the surface we may prescribe only one. With \(T = \bar T\) and at the same time \(\vek q\cdot\vek n = \bar q_{\mathrm{n}}\) we would have too many data. COOK (PDF p. 472) says so: the temperature must not be prescribed on a part where the flux is prescribed. We check it on the slab in figure 14.25.

We draw the temperature over the axis \(x\) for a slab of thickness \(L\). We hold the left face at \(T(0) = 0\), the right one at \(T(L) = 30\) K, steady and without source. The only solution of the equation \(T'' = 0\) with these two data is the line \(T = 30\,x/L\) through the two points. Its slope is thereby determined, and with it the flux at the right end, \(\vek q\cdot\vek n = -k\,T'(L) = -30\,k/L\). Let us also prescribe at the right end the flux \(\bar q_{\mathrm{n}} = -50\,k/L\). It demands the slope \(T'(L) = 50/L\), that is a steeper line through the point \((L, 30\ \mathrm{K})\). At \(x = 0\) it has the value \(30 - 50 = -20\) K and misses the prescribed zero by \(20\) K. No line satisfies both demands, so there is no solution. The parts \(S_{\mathrm{T}}\), \(S_{\mathrm{q}}\) and \(S_{\mathrm{h}}\) must therefore not overlap. Where two parts with different conditions meet, for example in a corner of a plate where an edge with \(\bar T = 0\) and an edge with \(\bar T = 30\) K meet, the condition at the point of contact is not determined (COOK, PDF p. 482), since this point would have to have both temperatures at once. We shall see this in the section on the plane field, on the example of a plate.

14.3.3 The initial state and the boundary value problem

In the heat equation time appears with one derivative, \(T_{\mathrm{t}}\). If we know the temperature at some instant everywhere in the body, the equation tells how fast it changes at each point, and thereby what it will be a little later. For a start one field is enough, the initial temperature \(T^0(P)\) at \(t = 0\), which we measure at all points of the body. In the equation of the string (theorem 13.3) time appears with a second derivative and we need two fields, the position and the velocity. Why the difference is exactly this is shown in figure 14.26.

We draw over the axis \(x\) the initial profile \(T^0\) of a bar with a hump and a trough. In a bar without source the heat equation is \(\rho c\,T_{\mathrm{t}} = k\,T_{\mathrm{xx}}\) (corollary 14.10): how fast a point warms is told by how the profile is bent at it. On the hump, where the profile bends downward, \(T_{\mathrm{xx}} < 0\), the point is warmer than its neighbours and the temperature falls. In the trough \(T_{\mathrm{xx}} > 0\) and the temperature rises. At the inflection the profile is straight and does not change there. All this we read off the profile at \(t = 0\) alone, so one field is enough. For a string with tension \(N\) and mass \(\mu\) per unit length the equation \(u_{\mathrm{tt}} = (N/\mu)\,u_{\mathrm{xx}}\) (theorem 13.3, there with \(T\) for the tension and \(w\) for the deflection) determines from the shape \(u^0\), drawn over the axis \(x\), only the acceleration. We can release the same shape from rest, and the hump starts to sink toward the straight position \(u = 0\), or push it upward, and the hump first grows further. The same initial shape has two different continuations, so for the string we also need the initial velocity. COOK (PDF p. 482) writes, for transient computations, that all initial nodal temperatures must be prescribed.

Now we write everything at once, as for elasticity (definition 11.1).

Definition 14.14 Boundary value problem of heat conduction (robni problem prevajanja toplote)

Let \(V\) be a body made of boxes (definition 4.11) with surface \(S\) and outward normal \(\vek n\), whose interior points form a connected open set (definition 1.136) and each of whose points is a limit of a sequence of interior points. Let the surface \(S\) be the union of three parts \(S_{\mathrm{T}}\), \(S_{\mathrm{q}}\) and \(S_{\mathrm{h}}\) that do not overlap and are made of whole outer pieces of the surface (some parts may be empty). Let the material be homogeneous and isotropic, with constant \(k > 0\), \(\rho > 0\) and \(c > 0\). The data, all continuous, are the source \(Q\) on \(V \times (0, t_1]\), the temperature \(\bar T\) and the flux \(\bar q_{\mathrm{n}}\) on the parts \(S_{\mathrm{T}}\) and \(S_{\mathrm{q}}\) (functions of point and time), the coefficient \(h \ge 0\) and the temperature \(T_\infty\) on \(S_{\mathrm{h}}\) and the initial temperature \(T^0\) on \(V\). For \(t_1 > 0\) a solution is a function \(T(P, t)\) of a point \(P\) of \(V\) and a time \(t\) in \([0, t_1]\) with the following requirements.

  1. The function \(T\) is continuous on \(V \times [0, t_1]\) and \(T(P, 0) = T^0(P)\) (initial condition, začetni pogoj).
  2. The function \(T\) is defined on \(D \times (0, t_1]\) for some open set \(D\) that contains \(V\), and has continuous partial derivatives there with respect to time and the space coordinates up to second order.
  3. At every point of the body and at every \(t \in (0, t_1]\) we have \(\rho c\,T_{\mathrm{t}} = \nabla\cdot(k\nabla T) + Q\) (theorem 14.8).
  4. At every point of the parts of the surface and at every \(t \in (0, t_1]\) the boundary conditions (robni pogoji) hold: \(T = \bar T\) on \(S_{\mathrm{T}}\), \(-k\,\partial T/\partial n = \bar q_{\mathrm{n}}\) on \(S_{\mathrm{q}}\) and \(-k\,\partial T/\partial n = h\,(T - T_\infty)\) on \(S_{\mathrm{h}}\) (equations (14.11) and (14.12)).

The steady boundary value problem has the same requirements 2 to 4 for a function \(T(P)\) that does not depend on time, with the equation \(\nabla\cdot(k\nabla T) + Q = 0\) and data independent of time. There is no requirement 1.

Source: COOK §12.1 (PDF pp. 469 to 472): the three kinds of boundary conditions (prescribed temperature, prescribed flux and heat transfer), "boundary temperature may also be prescribed, but not on the same portion of a boundary where \(f_{\mathrm{B}}\) is prescribed" (PDF p. 472), initial conditions (PDF p. 482). The requirements of continuous differentiability and the division of the surface are ours, so that the principles proved in chapter 4 apply, as in definition 11.1.

Requirement 2 is a simplification. It says that the solution is smooth up to the surface, and it excludes cases that we cannot treat in this section, for example an initial field with a jump (as \(t \to 0\) the solution has no derivatives at the jump) and boundary conditions that meet in a corner with different values. A full description of such solutions needs weak solutions in Sobolev spaces, as for the boundary value problem of elasticity (section 6.2.3 for Sobolev spaces). Later in the chapter we shall treat such cases as limits of smooth solutions and say each time in what sense they solve the problem.

14.3.4 Heat in the whole body

Before we prove uniqueness we need an identity that is the heat equation in integral form: what happens to the heat in the whole body. Section 14.2 derived the equation from the balance of an arbitrary finite part, and now we go the other way, from the equation to the balance of the whole. The stock of heat in the body \(V\) is \(H(t) = \int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho c\,T\,\mathrm{d}V\) (definition 14.6), up to an additive constant that depends on the choice of the zero of temperature.

Lemma 14.15 Heat in the whole body (toplota celega telesa)

Let \(T\) be a solution of the boundary value problem of heat conduction or of its steady version (definition 14.14) and \(H(t) = \int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho c\,T\,\mathrm{d}V\). For the transient problem \(H\) is differentiable on \((0, t_1)\) and

\[H'(t) = \int_{\mathrm{V}} Q\,\mathrm{d}V - \int_{\mathrm{S}} \vek q\cdot\vek n\,\mathrm{d}A , \qquad \vek q = -k\nabla T . \tag{14.14}\]

In the steady case, therefore, \(\int_{\mathrm{S}} \vek q\cdot\vek n\,\mathrm{d}A = \int_{\mathrm{V}} Q\,\mathrm{d}V\).

Proof

We must show that the derivative of \(H\) is the integral of the right-hand side of the heat equation and that the integral of a divergence is the flux through the surface.

  1. The function \(\rho c\,T\) and its partial derivative with respect to time are continuous on \(V \times (0, t_1)\) (requirement 2 of the definition). By lemma 8.32 on differentiating an integral with respect to a parameter, applied to the set with volume \(V\) (lemma 4.32, item 6) and the parameter \(t\) in an open interval, \(H'(t) = \int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho c\,T_{\mathrm{t}}\,\mathrm{d}V\). For the slab \(H\) is the area under the profile, its change in the time \(\Delta t\) is the strip between the profiles, made of columns of height \(\rho c\,T_{\mathrm{t}}\,\Delta t\). The area of the strip divided by \(\Delta t\) tends to \(\int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho c\,T_{\mathrm{t}}\,\mathrm{d}V\) as \(\Delta t\) tends to zero, and this is what the lemma ensures.
  2. By the heat equation (requirement 3 of the definition) \(\rho c\,T_{\mathrm{t}} = \nabla\cdot(k\nabla T) + Q\). By linearity of the integral (lemma 4.32, item 1) \(H' = \int_{\mathrm{V}} \nabla\cdot(k\nabla T)\,\mathrm{d}V + \int_{\mathrm{V}} Q\,\mathrm{d}V\), the second term is the power of the sources in the body.
  3. The field \(\vek v = k\nabla T\) is continuously differentiable on \(D\) at every \(t\) (requirement 2). By the divergence theorem (theorem 4.14) \(\int_{\mathrm{V}} \nabla\cdot\vek v\,\mathrm{d}V = \int_{\mathrm{S}} \vek v\cdot\vek n\,\mathrm{d}A = -\int_{\mathrm{S}} \vek q\cdot\vek n\,\mathrm{d}A\), because \(\vek q = -k\nabla T\). The integral over the body \(V\) passes to the surface with the outward normal \(\vek n\). On the slab cut into slices this can be seen: the flux at an inner cut leaves one slice and enters the next, so it cancels in the sum over the slices, and only the fluxes through the two ends remain.
  4. Steps 2 and 3 together give (14.14): the heat in the body grows with the source and falls with the flux through the surface in the direction of \(\vek n\). The slab has no source, \(2.0288\) leaves through the left face, \(0.8970\) through the right one, and \(H'(0) = -2.9257\) is their sum with the opposite sign. For a steady field \(H' = 0\), since \(T\) does not depend on \(t\), and from \(\nabla\cdot(k\nabla T) + Q = 0\) step 3 gives the equality \(\int_{\mathrm{S}} \vek q\cdot\vek n\,\mathrm{d}A = \int_{\mathrm{V}} Q\,\mathrm{d}V\).

Source: The proof is not from the sources. The heat balance of a body is treated by CM §5.4.2 (PDF pp. 236 to 238) and KREYSZIG §12.5 (PDF pp. 583 to 585) for a part of a body, here it is written for the whole body.

The lemma says: the heat in a body grows with the power of the sources, reduced by the flux through the surface. As for the force on the whole body in chapter 11 (lemma 11.2), this is a consequence of the equation, not an additional requirement. We check it on the slab at \(\mathrm{Bi} = 1\) of figure 14.24, where the source is zero, the lateral faces are insulated and \(T\) is a line. For \(\mathrm{Bi} = 1\) we have \(h = k/L\) and by equation (14.13) \(a\,L = -30\,\mathrm{K}/2 = -15\) K. At \(x = 0\) we have \(\vek q\cdot\vek n = k\,T'(0) = -15\,k/L\) (the flux enters), at \(x = L\) we have \(\vek q\cdot\vek n = -k\,T'(L) = 15\,k/L\). By the heat transfer law (14.12) it is there \(h\,T(L) = (k/L)\cdot 15 = 15\,k/L\), as it must be. Since the slab has the same cross section \(A\) everywhere, the fluxes through the left and the right face are opposite, \(-15\,k\,A/L\) and \(15\,k\,A/L\): as much heat as enters through the left face leaves through the right one, zero together, as the lemma requires in the steady state (step 4).

The lemma has one more consequence, which is a view of heat transfer from the other side. Take a body that has almost the same temperature at every point (such, as KREYSZIG states on PDF p. 41, is a copper ball, which conducts heat well). In the model of uniform temperature we describe it by a single temperature \(T(t)\). Then \(H = \rho c\,V_{\mathrm{V}}\,T\) and the flux is \(h\,(T - T_\infty)\) everywhere on the surface, so by equation (14.14) without a source

\[\rho c\,V_{\mathrm{V}}\,T' = -h\,A_{\mathrm{S}}\,(T - T_\infty) , \tag{14.15}\]

where \(V_{\mathrm{V}}\) is the volume and \(A_{\mathrm{S}}\) the area of the surface. The rate of change of temperature is proportional to the difference from the surroundings, which is the form of Newton's law known from experiments on well conducting bodies (KREYSZIG, PDF p. 41). The model is only an approximation. If the temperature were really the same everywhere, its derivative in the direction of the normal would be zero, by Fourier's law so would the flux through the surface, and the body would not cool. The model is good when \(\mathrm{Bi}\) is small: for \(\mathrm{Bi} = 0{,}1\) the temperature in the slab of figure 14.24 changes from one face to the other by only \(9{,}1\) % of the difference between the left face and the surroundings, for \(\mathrm{Bi} = 1\) by \(50\) %, and there a uniform temperature is no longer a good description.

14.3.5 Uniqueness

For the boundary value problem of elasticity uniqueness followed from the fact that the difference of two solutions solves the problem with zero data and that the energy is not zero unless the field is free of strain. For heat we do the same. The difference of two solutions, \(w = \tilde T - T\), has zero source, zero boundary data and zero initial state. The energy we shall follow is not the energy of the body but \(E(t) = \tfrac12\int_{\mathrm{V}}\rho c\,w^2\,\mathrm{d}V\). Why exactly this one? Because on differentiation with respect to time \(w\) is multiplied by \(\rho c\,w_{\mathrm{t}}\), which the heat equation replaces by a divergence, and by the divergence theorem this passes to the surface, where the boundary conditions tell exactly which signs we get. First we need a rule on integrals, which we shall use twice. Its proof and two steps of the theorem below are shown in figure 14.28.

Lemma 14.16 Integral of a nonnegative continuous function (integral nenegativne zvezne funkcije)

Let \(V\) be a body as in definition 14.14 (a body made of boxes, every point is a limit of interior points) and \(g\) a continuous function \(g\) on \(V\) with \(g \ge 0\). If \(\int_{\mathrm{V}} g\,\mathrm{d}V = 0\), then \(g = 0\) at every point of \(V\).

Proof

We must show that a positive value at one point gives a positive integral, as in steps 3 to 7 of item 2 of the proof of theorem 4.91.

  1. Suppose \(g(P) > 0\) at an interior point \(P\) of the body \(V\). By continuity (definition 1.68) with the error \(g(P)/2\) there is \(\delta > 0\) such that \(g\) is above the level \(g(P)/2\) at the points of the body that are less than \(\delta\) from \(P\).
  2. Since \(P\) is an interior point, a cube \(K_{\mathrm{P}}\) with edge \(d > 0\) lies in the body around it, whose vertices are less than \(\delta\) from \(P\). On it \(g > g(P)/2\).
  3. By monotonicity of the integral over a set (lemma 4.32, item 2) \(0 = \int_{\mathrm{V}} g\,\mathrm{d}V \ge \int_{\mathrm{K}_\mathrm{P}} g\,\mathrm{d}V \ge \tfrac12\,g(P)\,d^3 > 0\), because the cube has volume \(d^3\) (item 6 of the same lemma). In the figure this is the rectangle under the curve with height \(g(P)/2\), which has the area \(\tfrac12\,g(P)\,d\), and in space the volume of the cube \(d^3\) takes the place of the length \(d\). This is a contradiction, so \(g = 0\) at every interior point.
  4. Every point of \(V\), also the point \(R\) on the surface, is a limit of a sequence of interior points \(P_{\mathrm{n}}\), at which \(g(P_{\mathrm{n}}) = 0\) by step 3. Since \(g\) is continuous, by lemma 1.69 \(g(R) = \lim g(P_{\mathrm{n}}) = 0\). A positive value at \(R\) would be a jump, which continuity does not allow. Hence \(g = 0\) on the surface as well.

Source: The proof is not from the sources, it is the same inference as in steps 3 to 7 of item 2 of the proof of theorem 4.91.

Now the theorem. The question is whether one initial field and one kind of condition at each point of the surface are really enough.

Theorem 14.17 Uniqueness of the boundary value problem of heat conduction (enoličnost robnega problema prevajanja)

Let the boundary value problem of heat conduction be as in definition 14.14 and let \(T\) and \(\tilde T\) be solutions for the same data \(Q\), \(\bar T\), \(\bar q_{\mathrm{n}}\), \(h\), \(T_\infty\) and possibly different initial fields \(T^0\) and \(\tilde T^0\). Then:

  1. for every \(t \in [0, t_1]\) the integral \(\int_{\mathrm{V}}\rho c\,(\tilde T - T)^2\,\mathrm{d}V\) at time \(t\) is at most as large as at \(t = 0\). If \(\tilde T^0 = T^0\), then \(\tilde T = T\) on \(V \times [0, t_1]\),
  2. if \(T\) and \(\tilde T\) are solutions of the steady problem for the same data, \(\tilde T - T\) is constant on \(V\), and the constant is zero if \(S_{\mathrm{T}}\) is nonempty or if \(\int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{h}} h\,\mathrm{d}A > 0\).
Proof

We must show that \(E(t) = \tfrac12\int_{\mathrm{V}}\rho c\,w^2\,\mathrm{d}V\) for \(w = \tilde T - T\) is nonnegative, does not grow, and is zero if the initial fields are equal. In the steady part the same computation shows that the integral \(\int_{\mathrm{V}} k\,|\nabla w|^2\,\mathrm{d}V\) is zero.

  1. The difference \(w\) is continuous on \(V \times [0, t_1]\), has continuous derivatives up to second order on \(D \times (0, t_1]\) and satisfies the homogeneous problem: \(\rho c\,w_{\mathrm{t}} = \nabla\cdot(k\nabla w)\) (the source \(Q\) cancels), \(w = 0\) on \(S_{\mathrm{T}}\), \(\partial w/\partial n = 0\) on \(S_{\mathrm{q}}\) and \(-k\,\partial w/\partial n = h\,w\) on \(S_{\mathrm{h}}\) (equation (14.12), in which \(T_\infty\) cancels). All equations are linear and derivatives of a difference are differences of derivatives.
  2. The function \(w^2\) is continuous on the closed bounded set \(V \times [0, t_1]\), so it is uniformly continuous (theorem 4.6). For every error \(\eta > 0\) there is \(\delta > 0\) such that \(|w(P, t)^2 - w(P, s)^2| < \eta\) for every \(P \in V\) and \(|t - s| < \delta\): \(w^2\) at time \(s\) lies in the strip of width \(\eta\) above and below \(w^2\) at time \(t\). By the bound for the integral (lemma 4.32, item 3) \(|E(t) - E(s)| \le \tfrac12\rho c\,V_{\mathrm{V}}\,\eta\), since the area between the curves is not larger than the area of half the strip. In the figure \(|E(0) - E(0{,}01)| = 0{,}0236\), less than \(\tfrac12\rho c\,V_{\mathrm{V}}\,\eta = 0{,}05\). The function \(E\) is therefore continuous on \([0, t_1]\), its graph has no jumps.
  3. By lemma 8.32 with \(f = \tfrac12\rho c\,w^2\) and \(\partial f/\partial t = \rho c\,w\,w_{\mathrm{t}}\), both continuous on \(V \times (0, t_1)\), the function \(E\) is differentiable, its graph has a tangent at every point, and \(E'(t) = \int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho c\,w\,w_{\mathrm{t}}\,\mathrm{d}V\). By step 1 this is \(\int_{\mathrm{V}} w\,\nabla\cdot(k\nabla w)\,\mathrm{d}V\).
  4. By the product rule (lemma 1.82, componentwise) \(\nabla\cdot(w\,k\nabla w) = k\,|\nabla w|^2 + w\,\nabla\cdot(k\nabla w)\). So \(w\,\nabla\cdot(k\nabla w) = \nabla\cdot(w\,k\nabla w) - k\,|\nabla w|^2\). The field \(w\,k\nabla w\) is continuously differentiable on \(D\), so by the divergence theorem (theorem 4.14) and linearity, which take the integral over the body to the surface with the normal \(\vek n\),
    \[E'(t) = \int_{\mathrm{S}} w\,k\,\frac{\partial w}{\partial n}\,\mathrm{d}A - \int_{\mathrm{V}} k\,|\nabla w|^2\,\mathrm{d}V .\]
  5. We divide the surface into three parts. On \(S_{\mathrm{T}}\) we have \(w = 0\) and the product \(w\,k\,\partial w/\partial n\) is zero. On \(S_{\mathrm{q}}\) we have \(\partial w/\partial n = 0\) and the product is zero. On \(S_{\mathrm{h}}\) we have \(k\,\partial w/\partial n = -h\,w\) and the product is \(-h\,w^2\). So
    \[E'(t) = -\int_{\mathrm{V}} k\,|\nabla w|^2\,\mathrm{d}V - \int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{h}} h\,w^2\,\mathrm{d}A \le 0 , \tag{14.16}\]
    because \(k > 0\) and \(h \ge 0\) and the integrands are nonnegative.
  6. Let \(t \in (0, t_1]\). The function \(E\) is continuous on \([0, t]\) and differentiable on \((0, t)\), so by the mean value theorem (theorem 1.85) there is \(\tau \in (0, t)\) with \(E(t) - E(0) = E'(\tau)\,t \le 0\): the secant from \(0\) to \(t\) has the slope of the tangent at \(\tau\), and by step 5 that slope is not positive. So \(E(t) \le E(0)\), the curve \(E\) stays below the level \(E(0)\), which is the first claim of item 1.
  7. If \(\tilde T^0 = T^0\), then \(E(0) = 0\) and \(0 \le E(t) \le 0\): the level \(E(0)\) falls onto the axis \(t\), and the curve below it and above the axis is squeezed to zero. The integrand \(\rho c\,w^2\) is continuous and nonnegative, so by lemma 14.16 \(w(\cdot, t) = 0\) on \(V\) for every \(t\). This is the second claim of item 1.
  8. The steady case: the difference \(w\) solves \(\nabla\cdot(k\nabla w) = 0\) with the same conditions. The same computation without the term with time (steps 4 and 5, \(w \nabla\cdot(k\nabla w) = 0\)) gives \(0 = -\int_{\mathrm{V}} k\,|\nabla w|^2\,\mathrm{d}V - \int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{h}} h\,w^2\,\mathrm{d}A\). Both terms are nonnegative, so each is zero. By lemma 14.16 for \(g = k\,|\nabla w|^2\) we have \(\nabla w = \vek 0\) everywhere. By lemma 1.139 on the connected open set of interior points \(w\) is constant, and by continuity on all of \(V\), since every point is a limit of interior points.
  9. Let \(w = w_0\) be the constant, like the gap between the lines \(T_3\) and \(T_2\) of the bar in figure 14.22. If \(S_{\mathrm{T}}\) is nonempty, \(w = 0\) at some point of that surface, so \(w_0 = 0\). If \(\int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{h}} h\,\mathrm{d}A > 0\), step 8 gives \(0 = \int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{h}} h\,w_0^2\,\mathrm{d}A = w_0^2\int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{h}} h\,\mathrm{d}A\), so \(w_0 = 0\).

Source: COOK §12.1 (PDF pp. 469 to 472) states the form of the problem, KREYSZIG §12.5 (PDF pp. 583 to 585) the equation. Uniqueness by energy is an adaptation of the proof of theorem 11.4, the proof is not from the sources.

What did the theorem show? The difference of two solutions has an energy \(E\) that is forced to fall both by the loss of flux in the interior, \(\int k|\nabla w|^2\), and by the loss at heat transfer, \(\int h\,w^2\). It starts at zero if the initial fields are equal, and so it never grows above its initial value. The first item of the theorem is stronger than uniqueness alone: it says that for different initial fields the difference does not grow in the mean square (\(\int\rho c\,w^2\)). An error in the measurement of the initial temperature therefore does not grow with time, measured by this integral, which will be used with series in the section on the transient bar.

We test the derivative of the energy (14.16) on a slab with \(k = \rho c = L = h = 1\) (no units, \(\mathrm{Bi} = 1\)), whose left face at \(x = 0\) is held at \(w = 0\) and whose right face at \(x = L\) passes heat. In the figure the difference \(w\) is drawn over the axis \(x\). Such is the difference of two solutions if \(w(x, 0) = \sin(z x)\), where \(z = 2{,}02876\) is the first positive root of \(\tan z = -z\) (the condition \(-k\,w'(1) = h\,w(1)\), which \(\sin(zx)\) satisfies because \(-z\cos z = \sin z\) at \(z = 2{,}02876\): both sides are \(0{,}896956\)). A solution of the heat equation is \(w = \sin(z x)\,\mathrm{e}^{-z^2 t}\), since \(w_{\mathrm{t}} = -z^2 w\) and \(w_{\mathrm{xx}} = -z^2 w\). Figure 14.30 shows \(w\) at \(t = 0\), \(0{,}05\) and \(0{,}1\). The energy is \(E(0) = 0{,}298868\) and falls at the rate \(-2{,}46018\) (computed by numerical differentiation of the energy \(E(t) = \tfrac12\int_0^1 w^2\,\mathrm{d}x\)). The right-hand side of (14.16) is \(-\int_0^1 w_{\mathrm{x}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x - w(1)^2 = -2{,}46019\). They agree to rounding. The same at \(t = 0{,}05\) (\(-1{,}63012\) for both) and \(0{,}1\) (\(-1{,}08012\)). The energy falls as \(\mathrm{e}^{-2z^2t}\) and \(2z^2 = 8{,}2317\). The tangent at the right end reaches \(w = 0\) at \(L + k/h = 2\), because \(w'(1) = -w(1)\).

The conditions of the theorem are not there for nothing, as the next lemma shows.

14.3.6 A body with fluxes only

The theorem says that the steady solution is unique up to a constant and that the constant is determined if the temperature is prescribed on some part or there is heat transfer there. What happens if we prescribe the flux on the whole surface, \(S = S_{\mathrm{q}}\)? An example is the bar of figure 14.22, where we left \(T(0)\) arbitrary.

Lemma 14.18 A body with prescribed flux on the whole surface (telo s predpisanim tokom po vsej površini)

Let \(S = S_{\mathrm{q}}\) (\(S_{\mathrm{T}}\) and \(S_{\mathrm{h}}\) in definition 14.14 are empty) and \(T\) a solution of the steady problem. Then:

  1. \(\int_{\mathrm{S}} \bar q_{\mathrm{n}}\,\mathrm{d}A = \int_{\mathrm{V}} Q\,\mathrm{d}V\). If this equality does not hold, there is no solution,
  2. for every number \(C\) the function \(T + C\) is also a solution, so the solution is not unique,
  3. for the transient problem with \(S = S_{\mathrm{q}}\) the solution is unique (theorem 14.17, item 1), and its heat \(H\) of lemma 14.15 has the derivative \(H' = \int_{\mathrm{V}} Q\,\mathrm{d}V - \int_{\mathrm{S}} \bar q_{\mathrm{n}}\,\mathrm{d}A\).
Proof

We must show that the first equality is the condition for existence, that a constant does not affect the requirements of the definition and that the initial state determines the constant.

  1. By lemma 14.15 for a steady solution \(\int_{\mathrm{S}} \vek q\cdot\vek n\,\mathrm{d}A = \int_{\mathrm{V}} Q\,\mathrm{d}V\). On \(S = S_{\mathrm{q}}\) we have \(\vek q\cdot\vek n = \bar q_{\mathrm{n}}\) (equation (14.11)), for the bar the flux through the ends, so \(\int_{\mathrm{S}} \bar q_{\mathrm{n}}\,\mathrm{d}A = \int_{\mathrm{V}} Q\,\mathrm{d}V\).
  2. The function \(T + C\) has the same partial derivatives as \(T\), as a line raised by a constant keeps its slope, so it satisfies requirements 2 to 4 of the definition: the equation and the condition (14.11) contain only derivatives.
  3. The transient problem has the initial field in requirement 1, which determines the constant: \(T + C\) has at \(t = 0\) the value \(T^0 + C \ne T^0\). Item 1 of theorem 14.17 holds, because \(S_{\mathrm{h}}\) is empty and the surface term in equation (14.16) drops out, and the remaining term is nonpositive. The derivative \(H'\) is equation (14.14) with \(\vek q\cdot\vek n = \bar q_{\mathrm{n}}\).

Source: The proof is not from the sources. The same inference for force is lemma 11.2.

A test with numbers is the bar of figure 14.22. With \(1000\) W/m² at both ends \(\int_{\mathrm{S}} \bar q_{\mathrm{n}}\,\mathrm{d}A = (-1000 + 1000)\,A = 0 = \int_{\mathrm{V}} Q\,\mathrm{d}V\) (on the insulated lateral surface \(\bar q_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\)) and the solutions are the lines \(T(x) = T(0) - (1000/k)\,x\) for every value of \(T(0)\), which we can shift by an arbitrary constant (item 2). A case with the condition violated: with the same flux at the left end and \(900\) W/m² at the right, \(\int_{\mathrm{S}} \bar q_{\mathrm{n}}\,\mathrm{d}A = (-1000 + 900)\,A = -100\,A = -0{,}007854\) W, there is no source, so \(0 \ne -0{,}007854\) W and there is no steady solution. What happens in the body? By item 3, \(H' = +0{,}007854\) W, the heat in the bar grows steadily, and the mean temperature grows at the rate \(H'/(\rho c\,A\,L) = 100/(\rho c)\) K/s for \(L = 1\) m (without a value of \(\rho c\) for steel, for which the books have no source). The steady state does not exist because the heat has nowhere to go. What happens to the temperature meanwhile is shown in figure 14.31.

Into the bar \(1000\) W/m² enters through the left end, and \(900\) W/m² leaves through the right one. One solution of the heat equation of the bar with these two conditions is

\[T(x, t) = \frac{100\,t}{\rho c\,L} + \frac{50\,x^2}{k\,L} - \frac{1000\,x}{k} ,\]

since \(\rho c\,T_{\mathrm{t}} = 100/L = k\,T''\), at the left end \(\vek q\cdot\vek n = k\,T'(0) = -1000\) W/m² and at the right end \(-k\,T'(L) = -(-1000 + 100) = 900\) W/m² (checked with SymPy). Over the axis \(x\) the profile at time \(t\) is a parabola. At the left end it has the slope \(-1000/k\), at the right end only \(-900/k\), because less heat leaves there than enters. After a time \(\Delta t\) the profile is the same, only higher: every point warms by \(\Delta T = 100\,\Delta t/(\rho c\,L)\). The heat that stays in the bar goes into raising the temperature equally everywhere.

With balanced fluxes \(H\) is constant, and this is the datum that determines \(T(0)\): the bar keeps the heat it had at the start, and among the parallel lines of the bar with equal fluxes it picks the one with the same heat. How the temperature approaches this line is shown by the section on the transient bar.

So we have seen what the theorem demands. Without a prescribed temperature or heat transfer we get no uniqueness in the steady state, and no existence either if the flux is not balanced by the sources. For the transient problem uniqueness remains, because the initial state guarantees it. In the next section we shall compute steady states with all three kinds of conditions.
