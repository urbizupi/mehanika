Chapter 13 Vibration of continuous systems: string, shaft and beam

13.5 Hamilton's principle for a beam

We treated the string and the bar through the balance of a finite piece, adding only the inertial force. For a beam this route is longer, because during motion we also have to know what holds for the shear force and the bending moment. Chapter 8 showed another route for the bar: from the kinetic and the potential energy we obtain the equation of motion and the condition at a free end at once (theorem 8.118). For the beam such an approach would be welcome, since we already know the energy of a beam (definition 5.76). Chapter 9 therefore only assumed the Lagrange equations for a beam and left the proof to this chapter. We pose three questions. Which equation does stationarity of the action give for a beam, and which conditions at the ends follow from it by themselves? Does the equation agree with the balance of a finite piece of the beam? And is the assumption that the energy of the motion of a beam is written with the deflection \(w\) alone justified?

13.5.1 Model and action

Take a straight prismatic beam of length \(L\) of a homogeneous, linearly elastic material with modulus of elasticity \(E\) and density \(\rho\), with cross-section of area \(A\) and second moment of area \(I\) (definition 5.26). The axis \(x\) runs through the centroids of the cross-sections, the axis \(y\) points up, and the loads lie in the plane \(xy\). The deflection of the axis at time \(t\) is written \(w(x, t)\) (in chapter 5 it was called \(v\)), measured from the undeformed axis. The external load per unit length \(q(x, t)\) points in the direction of \(y\). At the ends a force \(f_0\) and a couple \(\mu_0\) may act at \(x = 0\) and a force \(f_{\mathrm{L}}\) and a couple \(\mu_{\mathrm{L}}\) at \(x = L\), each force in the direction of \(y\) and each couple in the counterclockwise sense, as in theorem 5.70. For the running example we take the steel beam of chapter 5 of length \(L = 1\) m with a rectangular cross-section of width \(b = 20\) mm and height \(h = 40\) mm (the cross-section is on the right of the next figure), bending in the plane of the height. The data and what follows from them are

\[E = 196\ \text{GPa}, \quad \rho = 7800\ \text{kg/m}^3, \quad I = \frac{b h^3}{12} = 1{,}0667\cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^4, \quad EI = 20\,906{,}7\ \text{N m}^2, \quad \rho A = \rho\,b h = 6{,}24\ \text{kg/m} .\]

Figure 13.62 shows what the beam does to a cross-section during motion. The dashed axis is the undeformed beam, the solid curve is its axis at time \(t\), and the distance \(w\) is the deflection. A cross-section that stood at the dashed vertical stays plane and perpendicular to the axis, so it turns by the slope \(w_{\mathrm{x}}\). A point of the cross-section at the height \(y\) therefore moves by \(-y\,w_{\mathrm{x}}\) in the direction \(x\) and by \(w\) upwards. Its velocity has the component \(-y\,w_{\mathrm{xt}}\) and the component \(w_{\mathrm{t}}\). Below, the body is a stack of cross-sections, and one thin slice of finite thickness \(\Delta x\) is coloured. On the right is the cross-section of area \(A\), width \(b\) and height \(h\), drawn in the ratio \(1 : 2\) of the running example. A narrow strip of area \(\Delta A\) lies at the height \(y\) from the axis.

We write down the idealizations the calculation contains. Each is a model and not a theorem. For the second and the third we shall look at the beam as a whole, in the next figure.

  1. The Euler-Bernoulli model (definition 5.14) holds at every time. The cross-sections stay plane and perpendicular to the axis, so the displacement field has the form of theorem 5.15, and the moment is \(M = EI\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) at every time (theorem 5.35). This relation was proved for a beam at rest. During motion we assume it, and this holds two assumptions. First: the material does not lag behind the deformation, the stress at an instant is set by the strain at the same instant, as in Hooke's law, in which time does not appear. For steel this is good: a steel tuning fork rings for several seconds after it is struck, at \(440\) Hz more than a thousand cycles, so it loses only a small part of its energy in one cycle. For rubber it is not so, its stress depends also on the rate of deformation. The equation we shall obtain therefore has no damping term, and a free vibration in it does not die out. Damping of a beam is treated in chapter 20. Second: the cross-section stays perpendicular to the axis, shear is neglected. For a slender beam this is good, for a short one it is not: section 13.10 calculates that shear together with the rotation of the cross-sections (idealization 3) lowers the first frequency of the running beam by \(0{,}27\) %, and at the length \(80\) mm by \(24{,}9\) %.
  2. The slope is small, \(w_{\mathrm{x}}^2 \le 10^{-2}\) as in chapter 5. What this means is shown by a beam held by pins at the ends of the undeformed axis that bends in its first mode into half a wave of length \(L\). The slope is largest at the ends, where the cross-section turns from the vertical position into the turned one by the angle \(w_{\mathrm{x}}\). A short piece of the axis at the end, which before bending had the length \(\Delta x\), is after bending nearly the hypotenuse of a triangle with the legs \(\Delta x\) and \(|w_{\mathrm{x}}|\,\Delta x\), so its length is \(\sqrt{1 + w_{\mathrm{x}}^2}\,\Delta x \le (1 + w_{\mathrm{x}}^2/2)\,\Delta x\). The axis stretches by at most \(w_{\mathrm{x}}^2/2 \le 0{,}5\) %, and this stretch is quadratic in \(w\), so it is absent from an equation that is linear in \(w\). The axial force and the axial displacement of the axis thus do not affect bending, and the energy of the beam is \(\tfrac12\int EI\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) from definition 5.76 with \(u = 0\). In the running example with the amplitude \(a = 1\) mm the largest slope is \(\pi a/L = 3{,}1\cdot 10^{-3}\), so \(w_{\mathrm{x}}^2 \le 10^{-5}\) and the axis stretches by at most \(5\cdot 10^{-6}\). When this fails: if the supports cannot move apart, the stretched axis carries an axial force \(N\), which adds the term \(-N\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) like the tension of a string (section 13.1). For the shape \(w = a\,\sin(kx)\) the mean stretch of the axis is \(a^2 k^2/4\), so \(N = EA\,a^2 k^2/4\), and the term \(-N\,w_{\mathrm{xx}} = N k^2\,w\) compares with the term \(EI\,w_{\mathrm{xxxx}} = EI\,k^4\,w\) in the ratio \(3\,a^2/h^2\) (with \(I/A = h^2/12\)), \(1{,}9\cdot 10^{-3}\) in the example. It becomes comparable when the amplitude is close to the height of the cross-section.
  3. The rotary inertia of the cross-sections is neglected. The figure shows what this means. The cross-section at the crest only moves up and down with the velocity \(w_{\mathrm{t}}\), while the cross-section at the pin only turns, its top and bottom points move along the axis with the velocity \(\frac h2\,w_{\mathrm{xt}}\). We neglect the kinetic energy of this rotation. Why this is reasonable is shown by the next lemma.

We defined the kinetic energy of a body by an integral over the body (definition 8.88). The displacement \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = -y\,w_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = w\) of figure 13.62 is the field of theorem 5.15 if the axis has no axial displacement. The velocity is the time derivative of the displacement, so the points that lie higher move faster in the direction \(x\) (component \(-y\,w_{\mathrm{xt}}\)). How much does this part contribute?

Lemma 13.36 Kinetic energy of a beam (kinetična energija nosilca)

Let the beam be as above, with a rectangular cross-section of width \(b\) and height \(h\), with an axis that has no axial displacement, the displacement field \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = -y\,w_{\mathrm{x}}(x, t)\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = w(x, t)\), \(u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\), and \(w\) with continuous derivatives \(w_{\mathrm{t}}\) and \(w_{\mathrm{xt}}\). Then the kinetic energy of the beam at time \(t\) is

\[T = \frac12\int_0^L \rho A\,w_{\mathrm{t}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x + \frac12\int_0^L \rho I\,w_{\mathrm{xt}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x . \tag{13.40}\]

The first term is the kinetic energy of the translation of the cross-sections, the second is the kinetic energy of their rotation.

Proof

We must show that the square of the velocity has no mixed term and that the integral over the cross-section gives \(A\) and \(I\).

  1. The velocity of a point is the time derivative of the displacement: \(\vek v = ([-y\,w_{\mathrm{xt}}](@fig-nzs-05-prerez:vx),\ [w_{\mathrm{t}}](@fig-nzs-05-prerez:vy),\ 0)\), since \(y\) does not depend on time.
  2. Its square is \(|\vek v|^2 = y^2\,w_{\mathrm{xt}}^2 + w_{\mathrm{t}}^2\). The two components are perpendicular, so there is no mixed term.
  3. The body is the box \([0, L] \times [-h/2, h/2] \times [-b/2, b/2]\), composed of cross-sections, so by the theorem on the order of integration (theorem 4.9) \(T = \tfrac12\int_0^L \rho \int_{\mathrm{A}} |\vek v|^2\,\mathrm{d}A\,\mathrm{d}x\), where \(\int_{\mathrm{A}}\) is the iterated integral in \(y\) and \(z\) over the cross-section. The functions \(w_{\mathrm{t}}\) and \(w_{\mathrm{xt}}\) do not depend on \(y\) and \(z\), so we take them out (lemma 3.54).
  4. The remaining integrals are the area \(\int_{\mathrm{A}} \mathrm{d}A = A\) and the second moment of area \(\int_{\mathrm{A}} y^2\,\mathrm{d}A = I\) (definition 5.26). This proves equation (13.40).

Source: EP (5.3.9) (p. 250) for the kinetic energy of a continuum, CK (12.34) (PDF p. 398) for a torsion rod. CK states the neglect of the inertia of turning cross-sections as assumption 6 (PDF p. 393). The proof is not from a source.

We proved the lemma for a rectangular cross-section, because then the body is a box, for which we have the theorem on the order of integration. For a cross-section of another shape the same calculation gives the same equation with its \(A\) and \(I\). The same holds for every cross-section that is a figure made of rectangles (definition 5.17), and \(I\) is defined only for such (definition 5.26). The body is then a body made of boxes \([0, L] \times R_{\mathrm{ij}}\) (definition 4.11), theorem 4.9 holds on each box, and the sum over the cells gives \(\int_{\mathrm{A}}\).

How large is the second term? Take a shape with wave number \(k\) (the deflection repeats over the length \(2\pi/k\)), \(w = a\,\sin(kx)\,\cos\omega t\). Then

\[w_{\mathrm{t}} = -a\omega\,\sin(kx)\,\sin\omega t , \qquad w_{\mathrm{xt}} = -a k \omega\,\cos(kx)\,\sin\omega t .\]

The velocity of translation \(w_{\mathrm{t}}\) is largest at the crest, where \(\sin(kx) = \pm 1\), and the rate of rotation \(w_{\mathrm{xt}}\) at the pins, where \(\cos(kx) = \pm 1\). Over every half wave of length \(\pi/k\) \(\sin^2\) and \(\cos^2\) have the same integral, so \(\int w_{\mathrm{xt}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) is exactly \(k^2\) times \(\int w_{\mathrm{t}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x\). The ratio of the second term in (13.40) to the first is therefore \(k^2 I/A\), and for a rectangular cross-section with \(I/A = (b h^3/12)/(b h) = h^2/12\) it is \(k^2 h^2/12\). In the first mode of a simply supported beam \(k = \pi/L\), the ratio is \((\pi h)^2/(12 L^2) = 1{,}3\cdot 10^{-3}\) for \(L = 1\) m and \(h = 40\) mm, and it is below \(10^{-2}\) when \(L \ge 9{,}1\,h\). In higher modes \(k\) is larger and the ratio grows with the square of \(k\). The neglect is therefore good for a long beam and low modes. How much it changes the frequency we calculate in section 13.10. Here we stay with the kinetic energy

\[T = \tfrac12\int_0^L \rho A\,w_{\mathrm{t}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x .\]

The potential energy is the total potential energy of the beam at time \(t\) from chapter 5: the strain energy \(\tfrac12\int EI\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) and the potential of the loads, which is \(-\int q\,w\,\mathrm{d}x\) and the end terms, \(-f_0\,w(0, t) - \mu_0\,w_{\mathrm{x}}(0, t)\) and \(-f_{\mathrm{L}}\,w(L, t) - \mu_{\mathrm{L}}\,w_{\mathrm{x}}(L, t)\) (theorem 5.70). The Lagrangian is \(T - V\) and the action (definition 8.116) is

\[\begin{aligned} \mathcal S(w) &= \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2}\!\!\int_0^L \Big(\tfrac12\,\rho A\,w_{\mathrm{t}}^2 - \tfrac12\,EI\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}^2 + q\,w\Big)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t \\ &\quad + \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} \Big[ f_0\,w(0, t) + \mu_0\,w_{\mathrm{x}}(0, t) \\ &\qquad\qquad + f_{\mathrm{L}}\,w(L, t) + \mu_{\mathrm{L}}\,w_{\mathrm{x}}(L, t) \Big]\,\mathrm{d}t . \end{aligned} \tag{13.41}\]

The admissible functions are, as for the bar, those the action can accept and that satisfy the supports. Let \(\Omega = [0, L] \times [t_1, t_2]\). A function \(w\) is admissible if \(w\), \(w_{\mathrm{t}}\), \(w_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(w_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(w_{\mathrm{xxx}}\), \(w_{\mathrm{xxxx}}\) and \(w_{\mathrm{tt}}\) are continuous on \(\Omega\), if the deflections \(w(x, t_1)\) and \(w(x, t_2)\) are prescribed, and if it satisfies what the supports prescribe: at each end the displacement \(w\), the slope \(w_{\mathrm{x}}\), both (as at the clamp in figure 13.64) or neither (as at the free end) may be prescribed, and what is prescribed is zero for every \(t\) (an essential condition, as in section 5.11.4). An admissible variation is a function \(\eta\) on \(\Omega\) with continuous \(\eta\), \(\eta_{\mathrm{t}}\), \(\eta_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(\eta_{\mathrm{xx}}\), which is zero at \(t_1\) and \(t_2\) and satisfies the same essential support with zero (where \(w\) is prescribed, \(\eta = 0\), where \(w_{\mathrm{x}}\) is prescribed, \(\eta_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\)). The first variation \(\delta\mathcal S(w, \eta)\) is the derivative of the function \(\alpha \mapsto \mathcal S(w + \alpha\,\eta)\) at \(\alpha = 0\). Figure 13.64 shows the path \(w\) and a neighbouring path \(w + \alpha\,\eta\) at one time.

We draw the figure as we learn it. The hatched clamp at \(x = 0\) holds the beam so that it can neither move nor turn there, so the variation too must have \(\eta(0, t) = 0\) and \(\eta_{\mathrm{x}}(0, t) = 0\) (the essential conditions). The grey dashed axis is the undeformed beam, the deflection is measured from it. The blue curve is the actual deflection \(w(x, t)\) at one time. The orange curve is the neighbouring path \(w + \alpha\,\eta\). The variation is strongly exaggerated so that the difference can be seen. The length \(L\) reaches from the clamp to the free end. At the free end \(x = L\) the values \(\eta\) and \(\eta_{\mathrm{x}}\) are arbitrary: the difference \(\alpha\,\eta(L, t)\) is drawn with straight lines, and the slopes of the two curves differ. The terms with \(\eta(L, t)\) and \(\eta_{\mathrm{x}}(L, t)\) in the first variation give the conditions at the free end.

13.5.2 The theorem

Figure 13.65 shows the rectangle \(\Omega\) over which we integrate the action, with the axes \(x\) and \(t\). On the lower edge \(t_1\) and the upper edge \(t_2\) we have \(\eta = 0\). The left edge \(x = 0\) and the right edge \(x = L\) are the ends of the beam, where the terms with \(\eta\) and \(\eta_{\mathrm{x}}\) appear. The frame inside is the region where the bump \(\eta\) is not zero.

For the bar we used a variation in the form of a bump to isolate the equation, and a variation that is not zero at the end to isolate the condition. For the beam let the bump be so smooth that its second derivatives are continuous too, since \(\eta_{\mathrm{xx}}\) appears in the action. Lemma 4.123 has such a bump. We also write \(M = EI\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and \(V = -EI\,w_{\mathrm{xxx}}\). These are the names of the moment and the shear force from chapter 5 (theorem 5.35, corollary 5.11) and as such they are determined in this section by \(w\) alone.

For the conditions at the ends we will take four auxiliary variations in steps 8 and 9, drawn in figure 13.66. Each is a product of a shape in \(x\) and the time function \(\psi\), which may be any function with a continuous derivative that is zero at \(t_1\) and \(t_2\). The figure shows one example. \(\eta_1\) goes from zero to 1 with zero slopes, \(\eta_2\) is zero at both ends and has slope 1 at \(x = L\), \(\eta_3\) goes from 1 to zero, and \(\eta_4\) has slope 1 at \(x = 0\).

In step 6 of the proof two terms of the first variation remain at each end, one with the factor \(\eta\) and one with the factor \(\eta_{\mathrm{x}}\). Figure 13.67 shows for four kinds of the right end why both are zero when the natural condition holds. At each end the terms \(\big(f_{\mathrm{L}} - V(L, t)\big)\,\eta(L, t)\) and \(\big(\mu_{\mathrm{L}} - M(L, t)\big)\,\eta_{\mathrm{x}}(L, t)\) are written. A clamped end prescribes the displacement and the slope, so by the definition of an admissible variation \(\eta(L, t)\) and \(\eta_{\mathrm{x}}(L, t)\) are zero, and there is no natural condition. A pinned end prescribes only the displacement. \(\eta(L, t)\) is zero, but the slope is free, so the second term vanishes only when its coefficient is zero, which is the natural condition \(M(L, t) = \mu_{\mathrm{L}}\). An end that prescribes only the slope is the opposite case: \(\eta_{\mathrm{x}}(L, t)\) is zero, and the natural condition is \(V(L, t) = f_{\mathrm{L}}\). We will call such an end a sliding end in section 13.6. A free end prescribes nothing, so both coefficients must be zero, which gives two natural conditions.

In step 8 we will obtain the natural condition from the fact that the integral is zero for every function \(\psi\). Figure 13.68 shows why a single function is not enough. The example \(\psi\) from figure 13.66 is symmetric about the middle \(t_{\mathrm{m}} = (t_1 + t_2)/2\). The function \(t - t_{\mathrm{m}}\) is not zero everywhere, it is negative to the left of the middle and positive to the right. At two mirror times \(\psi\) has the same value and \(t - t_{\mathrm{m}}\) opposite values, so the product has opposite values too. The area below zero is therefore as large as the area above zero, each is \((t_2 - t_1)^2/24\), and the integral of the product is zero. If \(f_{\mathrm{L}} - V(L, \cdot)\) were such a straight line, the integral with this example \(\psi\) would vanish although the condition does not hold. A nonnegative function \(\psi\) that is different from zero only in the right half would give a positive integral, so we need all functions \(\psi\).

Theorem 13.37 Hamilton's principle for a beam (Hamiltonovo načelo za nosilec)

Let the beam be as above with constant \(EI\) and \(\rho A\), let \(q\) be continuous on \(\Omega\) and \(f_0\), \(\mu_0\), \(f_{\mathrm{L}}\), \(\mu_{\mathrm{L}}\) continuous functions of time, and let \(w\) be an admissible function. Then \(w\) is a stationary point of the action (13.41) if and only if

\[\rho A\,w_{\mathrm{tt}} + EI\,w_{\mathrm{xxxx}} = q \quad \text{on } \Omega , \tag{13.42}\]

and for every \(t \in [t_1, t_2]\) the natural conditions at the ends hold

\[\begin{aligned} V(L, t) &= f_{\mathrm{L}} , & M(L, t) &= \mu_{\mathrm{L}} , \\ V(0, t) &= -f_0 , & M(0, t) &= -\mu_0 . \end{aligned} \tag{13.43}\]

The condition \(V = \dots\) holds at an end where the displacement \(w\) is not prescribed, and the condition \(M = \dots\) at an end where the slope \(w_{\mathrm{x}}\) is not prescribed. On an end there can thus be zero, one or two natural conditions.

Proof

We must show that the first variation is the sum of an integral over the rectangle and terms at the ends. Then we isolate the equation with bumps and each natural condition with variations that are not zero at the end. The procedure is the same as in the proof of theorem 8.118, with a second derivative in \(x\).

  1. The integrand in (13.41) at \(w + \alpha\,\eta\) is a polynomial of degree two in \(\alpha\) with coefficients that are continuous functions. Therefore \(\mathcal S(w + \alpha\,\eta) = \mathcal S(w) + \alpha\,B_1 + \alpha^2 B_2\) with
    \[\begin{aligned} B_1 &= \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2}\!\!\int_0^L \big(\rho A\,w_{\mathrm{t}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{t}} - EI\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{xx}} + q\,\eta\big)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t \\ &\quad + \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} \big(f_0\,\eta(0, t) + \mu_0\,\eta_{\mathrm{x}}(0, t) + f_{\mathrm{L}}\,\eta(L, t) + \mu_{\mathrm{L}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{x}}(L, t)\big)\,\mathrm{d}t , \end{aligned}\]
    \(B_2 = \int\!\int \big(\tfrac12\,\rho A\,\eta_{\mathrm{t}}^2 - \tfrac12\,EI\,\eta_{\mathrm{xx}}^2\big)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\). The integrals exist because the integrands are continuous (theorem 3.53), and the splitting follows from linearity (lemma 3.54). The derivative in \(\alpha\) at \(0\) is \(\delta\mathcal S(w, \eta) = B_1\).
  2. For every \(x\), \(\rho A\,w_{\mathrm{t}}(x, \cdot)\) and \(\eta(x, \cdot)\) are continuously differentiable functions of time. Integration by parts (corollary 3.56) gives \(\int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} \rho A\,w_{\mathrm{t}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{t}}\,\mathrm{d}t = \big[\rho A\,w_{\mathrm{t}}\,\eta\big]_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} - \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} \rho A\,w_{\mathrm{tt}}\,\eta\,\mathrm{d}t\), and the bracket is zero because \(\eta(x, t_1) = \eta(x, t_2) = 0\). We swap the order of integration (theorem 4.9), so the first term in \(B_1\) equals \(-\int\!\int \rho A\,w_{\mathrm{tt}}\,\eta\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\).
  3. For every \(t\), \(M = EI\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and \(\eta_{\mathrm{x}}\) are continuously differentiable functions of \(x\) (\(w_{\mathrm{xxx}}\) and \(\eta_{\mathrm{xx}}\) are continuous), so they are piecewise continuously differentiable on the partition with the single piece \([0, L]\) (definition 6.11). Lemma 6.13 with \(N = M\) and the function \(\eta_{\mathrm{x}}\) in the role of the function \(w\) of the lemma gives \(\int_0^L M\,\eta_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}x = \big[M\,\eta_{\mathrm{x}}\big]_0^L - \int_0^L M'\,\eta_{\mathrm{x}}\,\mathrm{d}x\), and \(M' = EI\,w_{\mathrm{xxx}} = -V\) (here we use that \(EI\) is constant), so \(\int_0^L M\,\eta_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}x = \big[M\,\eta_{\mathrm{x}}\big]_0^L + \int_0^L V\,\eta_{\mathrm{x}}\,\mathrm{d}x\). The bracket is the difference of values at both ends.
  4. The functions \(V\) and \(\eta\) are continuously differentiable (\(w_{\mathrm{xxxx}}\) and \(\eta_{\mathrm{x}}\) are continuous). Once more lemma 6.13, with \(N = V\) and the function \(\eta\) in the role of the function \(w\): \(\int_0^L V\,\eta_{\mathrm{x}}\,\mathrm{d}x = \big[V\,\eta\big]_0^L - \int_0^L V'\,\eta\,\mathrm{d}x\) and \(V' = -EI\,w_{\mathrm{xxxx}}\). Inserting into step 3 we obtain \(-\int_0^L EI\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}x = -\big[M\,\eta_{\mathrm{x}} + V\,\eta\big]_0^L - \int_0^L EI\,w_{\mathrm{xxxx}}\,\eta\,\mathrm{d}x\). Both terms in the bracket lie at the ends.
  5. We insert steps 2 to 4 into \(B_1\): the integral over the rectangle gets the function \(R\), and the ends get the coefficients of \(\eta\) and \(\eta_{\mathrm{x}}\). For every admissible variation
    \[\begin{aligned} \delta\mathcal S(w, \eta) &= \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2}\!\!\int_0^L R\,\eta\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t \\ &\quad + \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} \Big[ \big(f_{\mathrm{L}} - V(L, t)\big)\,\eta(L, t) \\ &\qquad\quad + \big(\mu_{\mathrm{L}} - M(L, t)\big)\,\eta_{\mathrm{x}}(L, t) \\ &\qquad\quad + \big(f_0 + V(0, t)\big)\,\eta(0, t) \\ &\qquad\quad + \big(\mu_0 + M(0, t)\big)\,\eta_{\mathrm{x}}(0, t) \Big]\,\mathrm{d}t , \end{aligned} \tag{13.44}\]
    where \(R = -\rho A\,w_{\mathrm{tt}} - EI\,w_{\mathrm{xxxx}} + q\) is a continuous function on \(\Omega\).
  6. If (13.42) and (13.43) hold, then \(R = 0\). At each end each of the two terms is zero: either because the coefficient is zero by a natural condition, or because \(\eta\) (or \(\eta_{\mathrm{x}}\)) is zero there by the definition of an admissible variation. Figure 13.67 shows all four kinds of end at \(x = L\). At \(x = 0\) the terms are \(\big(f_0 + V(0, t)\big)\,\eta(0, t)\) and \(\big(\mu_0 + M(0, t)\big)\,\eta_{\mathrm{x}}(0, t)\) and the reasoning is the same. By step 5, \(\delta\mathcal S = 0\) for every admissible variation, so \(w\) is a stationary point.
  7. Conversely, let \(w\) be a stationary point. We write the bump \(q_{\alpha\beta}\) of lemma 4.123 as \(\kappa_{\alpha\beta}\), because \(q\) is the load. For numbers \(0 < \alpha_1 < \beta_1 < L\), \(t_1 < \alpha_2 < \beta_2 < t_2\) and \(0 < \alpha_3 < \beta_3 < 1\) let \(\eta = \kappa_{\alpha_1\beta_1}(x)\,\kappa_{\alpha_2\beta_2}(t)\). By item 1 of the lemma it has continuous derivatives up to the third order and is zero with all its derivatives at \(x = 0\), \(x = L\), \(t = t_1\) and \(t = t_2\), so it is an admissible variation with vanishing end terms. By step 5, \(\int\!\int R\,\eta\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t = 0\). Let \(g(x, t, s) = R(x, t)\) be the continuous function on the open set \(D = (0, L) \times (t_1, t_2) \times (0, 1)\) and \(\varphi = \kappa_{\alpha_1\beta_1}(x)\,\kappa_{\alpha_2\beta_2}(t)\,\kappa_{\alpha_3\beta_3}(s)\). For a box \(K = [\alpha_1, \beta_1] \times [\alpha_2, \beta_2] \times [\alpha_3, \beta_3]\) in \(D\) we have \(\int_{\mathrm{K}} g\,\varphi\,\mathrm{d}V = \int_{\alpha_3}^{\beta_3} \kappa_{\alpha_3\beta_3}\,\mathrm{d}s \cdot \int\!\int R\,\eta\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t = 0\) (the integral over the box as an iterated integral by theorem 4.9, the inner double integral does not depend on \(s\) and is taken out by lemma 3.54). By item 3 of lemma 4.123, \(R = 0\) on \(D\), and by continuity also on the boundary of the rectangle \(\Omega\). This is (13.42).
  8. Let \(\psi\) be any function on \([t_1, t_2]\) with a continuous derivative and \(\psi(t_1) = \psi(t_2) = 0\) (the example in the figure is \(\psi(t) = 16\,(t - t_1)^2\,(t_2 - t)^2/(t_2 - t_1)^4\)). Let \(w\) not be prescribed at \(x = L\). Take \(\eta = \eta_1(x)\,\psi(t)\) with \(\eta_1(x) = (x/L)^2\,(3 - 2x/L)\), \(\eta_1(0) = \eta_1'(0) = 0\), \(\eta_1(L) = 1\), \(\eta_1'(L) = 0\). The functions \(\eta\), \(\eta_{\mathrm{t}} = \eta_1\,\psi'\), \(\eta_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\eta_{\mathrm{xx}} = \eta_1''\,\psi\) are continuous, \(\eta\) is zero at \(t_1\) and \(t_2\) and at \(x = 0\) together with the slope, and at \(x = L\) it has zero slope, so it is admissible whatever the supports are (\(w\) is free at \(x = L\)). By step 7, \(R = 0\), and (13.44) gives \(0 = \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} \big(f_{\mathrm{L}} - V(L, t)\big)\,\psi\,\mathrm{d}t\), and this for every such function \(\psi\). The function \(f_{\mathrm{L}} - V(L, \cdot)\) is continuous, so by the fundamental lemma (lemma 4.77) it is zero, that is \(V(L, t) = f_{\mathrm{L}}\). A single function \(\psi\) would not suffice: for the example \(\psi\), which is symmetric about the middle \(t_{\mathrm{m}} = (t_1 + t_2)/2\), the integral of the function \(t - t_{\mathrm{m}}\) times \(\psi\) is zero, although \(t - t_{\mathrm{m}}\) is not zero everywhere.
  9. Let \(w_{\mathrm{x}}\) not be prescribed at \(x = L\). Take \(\eta = \eta_2(x)\,\psi(t)\) with \(\eta_2 = x^2\,(x - L)/L^2\), \(\eta_2(0) = \eta_2'(0) = \eta_2(L) = 0\), \(\eta_2'(L) = 1\). In the same way, with every function \(\psi\) of step 8, (13.44) gives the condition \(M(L, t) = \mu_{\mathrm{L}}\). At \(x = 0\) we proceed with \(\eta_3 = (1 - x/L)^2\,(1 + 2x/L)\) (\(\eta_3(0) = 1\), zero slope, zero at \(L\)) and \(\eta_4 = x\,(x - L)^2/L^2\) (\(\eta_4(0) = 0\), \(\eta_4'(0) = 1\), zero at \(L\)) and obtain \(V(0, t) = -f_0\) and \(M(0, t) = -\mu_0\).

Source: CK §12.3 (PDF pp. 397-400) derives the same procedure for a torsion rod: integration by parts in \(x\) and in \(t\) (12.40), (12.41), the conditions at a free end natural. EP (5.3.8), (5.3.11) (pp. 250-251) for the general form and EP example 5.3.1 (pp. 251-252) for the bar. Equation (13.42) is CK (12.25) (PDF p. 394, read). CK §12.3 does not contain a Hamilton derivation for the Euler-Bernoulli beam, the Newton derivation is in §12.2. The proof by steps is not from a source.

What does the theorem say? Equation (13.42) is the equilibrium of the beam from chapter 5, \(EI\,w_{\mathrm{xxxx}} = q\) (theorem 5.42), with the inertial force per unit length \(-\rho A\,w_{\mathrm{tt}}\) added to the load. The conditions (13.43) are the same as at rest: a free end has \(V = 0\) and \(M = 0\) (the table in section 5.11.4), a pinned support has \(M = 0\), a clamp has none. The constant \(EI\) was used only in step 3, where \(M' = EI\,w_{\mathrm{xxx}}\). For a beam with a varying cross-section, with a twice continuously differentiable \(EI(x)\) and a continuous \(\rho A(x)\), \(M' = (EI\,w_{\mathrm{xx}})_{\mathrm{x}}\), and the same steps give the equation \(\rho A\,w_{\mathrm{tt}} + (EI\,w_{\mathrm{xx}})_{\mathrm{xx}} = q\), as CK (12.25) (PDF p. 394) writes it. The conditions at an end contain no acceleration, because the end has no mass. An end with a mass \(m\) is not covered by the theorem, but the derivation is the same. The kinetic energy gets the additional term \(\tfrac12\,m\,w_{\mathrm{t}}(L, t)^2\), and the first variation \(B_1\) the term \(\int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} m\,w_{\mathrm{t}}(L, t)\,\eta_{\mathrm{t}}(L, t)\,\mathrm{d}t\). Integration by parts in time as in step 2 turns it into \(-\int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} m\,w_{\mathrm{tt}}(L, t)\,\eta(L, t)\,\mathrm{d}t\), because \(\eta\) is zero at \(t_1\) and \(t_2\). The coefficient of \(\eta(L, t)\) in (13.44) is therefore \(f_{\mathrm{L}} - m\,w_{\mathrm{tt}}(L, t) - V(L, t)\), and step 8 gives \(V(L, t) = f_{\mathrm{L}} - m\,w_{\mathrm{tt}}(L, t)\). For \(f_{\mathrm{L}} = 0\) this is the condition of CK, example 12.3 (PDF p. 395, equation (3)): \(\big(EI\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}\big)_{\mathrm{x}}(L, t) = m\,w_{\mathrm{tt}}(L, t)\). CK obtains it from Newton's second law for the mass at the end, so the same derivation is also a check of this condition.

13.5.3 A check by the balance of a piece

The second route to the same equation is Newton's: the momentum and the angular momentum of a finite piece. Figure 13.69 shows a piece \([a, b]\) with the forces on it. Let \(0 \le a < b \le L\). The part of the beam with larger \(x\) acts on the piece at \(b\) with the force \(V(b)\) and the couple \(M(b)\), and the part with smaller \(x\) acts at \(a\) with the force \(-V(a)\) and the couple \(-M(a)\) (definitions 5.7 and 5.8). The momentum of the piece is \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \rho A\,w_{\mathrm{t}}\,\mathrm{d}x\) and its time derivative is \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \rho A\,w_{\mathrm{tt}}\,\mathrm{d}x\) (lemma 4.19).

The piece is the part of the beam between \(x = a\) and \(x = b\), the deflection is strongly exaggerated. On the right face the part with larger \(x\) acts with the force \(V(b)\) upwards and the couple \(M(b)\) counterclockwise. On the left face the part with smaller \(x\) acts with the force \(V(a)\) downwards and the couple \(M(a)\) clockwise, for positive \(V\) and \(M\) as in chapter 5. The load \(q\) points upwards. The length of the piece \(b - a\) is the distance between the faces. The sum of these forces must give the time derivative of the momentum of the piece, \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \rho A\,w_{\mathrm{tt}}\,\mathrm{d}x\), and the sum of their moments the time derivative of the angular momentum.

The force equation of the piece and the moment equation about the point of the axis at \(x = a\) are (the sum of the external forces is the derivative of the momentum, the sum of the moments of the external forces is the derivative of the angular momentum, and by idealization 3 we neglect the rotary inertia of the cross-sections)

\[\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \rho A\,w_{\mathrm{tt}}\,\mathrm{d}x = V(b) - V(a) + \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b q\,\mathrm{d}x , \tag{13.45}\]
\[M(b) - M(a) + (b - a)\,V(b) + \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b (x - a)\,\big(q - \rho A\,w_{\mathrm{tt}}\big)\,\mathrm{d}x = 0 . \tag{13.46}\]

In the second, \((b - a)\,V(b)\) is the moment of the force \(V(b)\) about the point at \(a\), and \((x - a)\) is the lever arm of the force per unit length. The question is whether these two equations for every piece imply equation (13.42), and conversely.

Figure 13.70 shows what we need in the proof of the lemma. In the first row, on the axis of the beam, is the piece \([a, b]\), and the arm \(x - a\) is the distance of the point \(x\) from the left face. In the second row, over the length of the piece \(b - a\), is the triangle under the line \(C\,(x - a)\), where \(C\) is the number from the proof, and beside it the same triangle over a piece half as long, as if \(b\) were moved towards \(a\). With them we shall bound the integral in the moment equation. In the third row is the end piece \([a, L]\) with the loads \(f_{\mathrm{L}}\) and \(\mu_{\mathrm{L}}\) at the end.

Lemma 13.38 Equation of motion of a beam piece (gibalna enačba kosa nosilca)

Let \(w\) be an admissible function as in theorem 13.37, \(M = EI\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}\), and let \(V\) be a continuous function on \(\Omega\). Then for a piece:

  1. If (13.45) and (13.46) hold for every pair \(0 \le a < b \le L\) and every \(t\), then \(V = -EI\,w_{\mathrm{xxx}}\) and equation (13.42) holds.
  2. If \(V = -EI\,w_{\mathrm{xxx}}\) and (13.42) hold, then (13.45) and (13.46) hold for every piece.
  3. If for every piece \([a, L]\) at the end, \(0 \le a < L\), the two equations hold with the right-hand side in which the force \(V(b)\) and the couple \(M(b)\) become the loads \(f_{\mathrm{L}}\) and \(\mu_{\mathrm{L}}\), then \(V(L, t) = f_{\mathrm{L}}\) and \(M(L, t) = \mu_{\mathrm{L}}\).
Proof

We must show that shortening the piece gives the equations at a point. The time \(t\) is constant throughout. Let \(C\) be the largest value of the function \(|q - \rho A\,w_{\mathrm{tt}}|\) on \(\Omega\), which is continuous on a closed rectangle.

  1. For item 1 let \(a < L\) and \(b > a\). The function under the integral in (13.46) is at most \(C\,(x - a)\) in absolute value, because the arm \(x - a\) is not negative. The integral is therefore at most the area of the triangle under the line \(C\,(x - a)\) over \([a, b]\) in absolute value (monotonicity, lemma 3.54, item 4), which is \(C\,(b - a)^2/2\).
  2. We divide equation (13.46) by \(b - a\) and let \(b \to a\). The quotient \((M(b) - M(a))/(b - a)\) tends to \(M'(a) = EI\,w_{\mathrm{xxx}}(a)\) (differentiability of \(M\)), \(V(b)\) tends to \(V(a)\) (continuity), and the integral term is at most \(C\,(b - a)/2\) by step 1 and tends to \(0\). This shows on the piece half as long: the triangle is half as high and half as long, its area is a quarter, after the division by the length a half remains. The difference of the moments falls with the length of the piece linearly, the integral quadratically. Therefore \(EI\,w_{\mathrm{xxx}}(a, t) + V(a, t) = 0\) for every \(a < L\) and by continuity also at \(L\).
  3. By step 2, \(V\) is continuously differentiable (\(w_{\mathrm{xxxx}}\) is continuous) and \(V' = -EI\,w_{\mathrm{xxxx}}\). The fundamental theorem (theorem 3.55) gives \(V(b) - V(a) = -\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b EI\,w_{\mathrm{xxxx}}\,\mathrm{d}x\). Inserting into (13.45): \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \big(\rho A\,w_{\mathrm{tt}} + EI\,w_{\mathrm{xxxx}} - q\big)\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\) for every piece.
  4. The function \(r = \rho A\,w_{\mathrm{tt}} + EI\,w_{\mathrm{xxxx}} - q\) is continuous. By the fundamental theorem (item 2) the derivative of the function \(x \mapsto \int_{\mathrm{a}}^x r\,\mathrm{d}s\) is \(r(x)\), and by step 3 this function is identically zero, so \(r = 0\). This is (13.42) and proves item 1.
  5. Item 2: from \(V = -EI\,w_{\mathrm{xxx}}\) we have \(V' = -EI\,w_{\mathrm{xxxx}}\), and by (13.42) \(V' = \rho A\,w_{\mathrm{tt}} - q\). The fundamental theorem (theorem 3.55) gives \(V(b) - V(a) = \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b (\rho A\,w_{\mathrm{tt}} - q)\,\mathrm{d}x\). This is (13.45).
  6. From \(V' = \rho A\,w_{\mathrm{tt}} - q\) we have \(q - \rho A\,w_{\mathrm{tt}} = -V'\). Integration by parts (corollary 3.56) with the functions \(x - a\) and \(V\) gives \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b (x - a)\,(q - \rho A\,w_{\mathrm{tt}})\,\mathrm{d}x = -(b - a)\,V(b) + \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b V\,\mathrm{d}x\). From \(M' = EI\,w_{\mathrm{xxx}} = -V\) we have \(M(b) - M(a) = -\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b V\,\mathrm{d}x\). The left-hand side of (13.46) is therefore \(-\int V + (b - a)\,V(b) - (b - a)\,V(b) + \int V = 0\).
  7. Item 3: for the piece \([a, L]\) the force equation is \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^L \rho A\,w_{\mathrm{tt}}\,\mathrm{d}x = f_{\mathrm{L}} - V(a) + \int_{\mathrm{a}}^L q\,\mathrm{d}x\) and the moment equation is \(\mu_{\mathrm{L}} - M(a) + (L - a)\,f_{\mathrm{L}} + \int_{\mathrm{a}}^L (x - a)\,(q - \rho A\,w_{\mathrm{tt}})\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\). The integrals are at most \(C\,(L - a)\) and \(C\,(L - a)^2/2\) in absolute value. When we let \(a \to L\) they tend to zero, and the continuity of \(V\) and \(M\) gives \(0 = f_{\mathrm{L}} - V(L, t)\) and \(0 = \mu_{\mathrm{L}} - M(L, t)\).

Source: CK (12.18) to (12.25) (PDF pp. 393-394): Newton's laws for an element of the beam, division by the length of the element and the limit, equation (12.25) the same as (13.42). CK (12.23) writes \(S = \partial M/\partial x\), so CK counts \(S\) or \(M\) with the opposite sign to ours (\(V = -M'\)). Because of the double derivative (12.25) is nevertheless the same. The neglect of the inertia of turning cross-sections is CK assumption 6 (PDF p. 393). The proof by steps is not from a source, where each limit is written as a shrinking element.

The lemma showed that the two routes are one route. Newton's needs the shear force and the moment, tied to the deflection by \(M = EI\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and by step 2 of item 1, Hamilton's only two energies. Both give the same equation and the same conditions at the end \(x = L\): the condition \(V(L) = f_{\mathrm{L}}\) is the force balance of a very short piece at the end (item 3), the condition \(M(L) = \mu_{\mathrm{L}}\) its moment balance. The equation and the conditions at \(x = L\) therefore also follow from Newton's laws for a piece, under the same idealizations 1 to 3: both routes used the relation \(M = EI\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) at every instant, which is idealization 1.

13.5.4 Three checks on numbers

Figure 13.71 shows three checks. The supports and the dashed axis of the undeformed beam are the same in all rows. In the first row is the first mode. In the second row are the field \(w = c\,(x^3 - L^2 x)\) and the variation \(\eta\), which has a slope at the end, and the couple \(\mu_{\mathrm{L}}\) that the field needs. In the third row is the first mode with the variation \(\eta = \psi\), which is not zero at the supports, and with the shear forces \(V(0)\) and \(V(L)\) at the supports.

We test the theorem on the running beam: \(EI = 20\,906{,}7\) N m², \(\rho A = 6{,}24\) kg/m, \(L = 1\) m, simply supported, so the essential conditions are \(w(0, t) = w(L, t) = 0\) and the natural ones \(M(0, t) = M(L, t) = 0\) (\(q = 0\), \(\mu_0 = \mu_{\mathrm{L}} = 0\)). Take the field \(w = a\,\sin(\pi x/L)\,\cos\omega t\) with \(a = 1\) mm. The largest slope is \(a\pi/L = 3{,}1\cdot 10^{-3}\), so \(w_{\mathrm{x}}^2 \le 10^{-5}\) and idealization 2 holds. The field is admissible and satisfies the natural conditions, because \(M = -EI\,(\pi/L)^2\,a\,\sin(\pi x/L)\,\cos\omega t\) is zero at \(x = 0\) and \(x = L\). In equation (13.42) we insert \(w_{\mathrm{tt}} = -\omega^2 w\) and \(w_{\mathrm{xxxx}} = (\pi/L)^4\,w\):

\[\rho A\,w_{\mathrm{tt}} + EI\,w_{\mathrm{xxxx}} = \Big(EI\,\frac{\pi^4}{L^4} - \rho A\,\omega^2\Big)\,w ,\]

which is zero for every \(x\) and \(t\) exactly at \(\omega_1^2 = EI\,\pi^4/(\rho A\,L^4)\), that is \(\omega_1 = 571{,}28\) rad/s and \(f_1 = 90{,}92\) Hz. We check this also with the action on the window \(t_1 = 0\), \(t_2 = 10\) ms with the variation \(\eta = \sin(\pi x/L)\,\psi(t)\), where \(\psi(t) = 16\,t^2\,(t_2 - t)^2/t_2^4\) is a dimensionless function with the largest value \(1\) and \(\int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2}\psi\,\mathrm{d}t = 8\,t_2/15 = 5{,}333\) ms. Because \(\eta = 0\) at the ends and both natural coefficients in (13.44) are zero, \(\delta\mathcal S = \int\!\int R\,\eta\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\) with \(R = \rho A\,(\omega^2 - \omega_1^2)\,w\), so

\[\delta\mathcal S = \tfrac12\,L\,a\,\rho A\,(\omega^2 - \omega_1^2) \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} \cos(\omega t)\,\psi(t)\,\mathrm{d}t .\]

At \(\omega = \omega_1\) we get \(\delta\mathcal S = 0\) (SymPy: \(10^{-14}\) N s, which is rounding), at \(f = 100\) Hz (\(\omega = 628{,}32\) rad/s) we get \(\delta\mathcal S = -0{,}5260\) N s, and the calculation directly from \(B_1\) in step 1 of the proof gives the same number. The field with the frequency \(100\) Hz is therefore not a motion. With the variation \(\sin(2\pi x/L)\,\psi\) we get \(\delta\mathcal S = 0\) at both frequencies, because \(\int_0^L \sin(\pi x/L)\,\sin(2\pi x/L)\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\): the error of the frequency shows itself only in the shape that the field itself has.

We do the same calculation at every frequency from \(40\) to \(180\) Hz. The horizontal axis carries the frequency \(f\) of the field, the vertical one the first variation \(\delta\mathcal S\) in N s. The blue curve is \(\delta\mathcal S\) with the window from \(0\) to \(10\) ms. It passes through zero at \(f_1 = 90{,}92\) Hz, where \(\omega^2 - \omega_1^2 = 0\), and at \(100\) Hz it has the value \(-0{,}526\) N s that we calculated above. But it passes through zero also at \(50\) and \(150\) Hz, although the fields with these frequencies are not motions. There the integral \(\int\cos(\omega t)\,\psi(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\) is zero. The function \(\psi\) is symmetric about the middle of the window \(t = 5\) ms, and at \(50\) Hz \(\cos(\omega t)\) is zero exactly there (\(\omega \cdot 5\ \text{ms} = \pi/2\)) and on one side of the middle is the mirror image, with the opposite sign, of what it is on the other, so the two contributions cancel. The orange curve is the same first variation with a function \(\psi\) of the same shape on the window from \(0\) to \(7\) ms. Its zeros are at \(71{,}4\) Hz and at \(f_1\), and at \(50\) Hz it has \(\delta\mathcal S = -1{,}10\) N s. The only common zero of the two curves in the figure is \(f_1\). That is why the theorem requires \(\delta\mathcal S = 0\) for every admissible variation, as in step 8 of the proof: a single variation does not always tell a wrong frequency from the right one.

The second check shows that a natural condition is content of the principle. The same simply supported beam, the field \(w = c\,(x^3 - L^2 x)\) with \(c = 10^{-3}\) m⁻², constant in time. It satisfies the essential conditions (\(w(0) = w(L) = 0\)) and equation (13.42), since \(w_{\mathrm{tt}} = 0\) and \(w_{\mathrm{xxxx}} = 0\). But \(M(L) = 0\) fails: \(M = EI\,w_{\mathrm{xx}} = 6\,c\,EI\,x\), so \(M(L) = 125{,}44\) N m and \(M(0) = 0\). The field is the bending of a simply supported beam with a couple of \(125{,}44\) N m at the end, with the deflection \(-0{,}375\) mm at the middle. Take the variation \(\eta = \psi(t)\,x\,(L - x)/L^2\). It is admissible (\(\eta(0) = \eta(L) = 0\), the slope at the ends is free) and has \(\eta_{\mathrm{x}}(L, t) = -\psi/L\). By (13.44) \(R = 0\) and what remains is

\[\delta\mathcal S = \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} \big(\mu_{\mathrm{L}} - M(L)\big)\,\eta_{\mathrm{x}}(L, t)\,\mathrm{d}t = 6\,c\,EI\int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2}\psi\,\mathrm{d}t = 0{,}6690\ \text{N s} ,\]

which agrees with the direct calculation of \(B_1\) in SymPy. The field is not stationary without the couple \(\mu_{\mathrm{L}} = 125{,}44\) N m at the end, although it satisfies the equation and the essential conditions. The condition \(M(L) = 0\) is not an addition, it follows from the action.

The third check shows what the requirement of admissibility does. Take the true motion of the first mode of the same simply supported beam, \(w = a\,\sin(\pi x/L)\,\cos\omega_1 t\) with \(a = 1\) mm, and the window from \(0\) to \(10\) ms as above. The variation \(\eta = \psi(t)\), independent of \(x\), is not admissible: at \(x = 0\) and \(x = L\) we have \(\eta = \psi \ne 0\), while the supports prescribe \(w = 0\) and therefore require \(\eta = 0\). The field \(w\) is a motion, so \(R = 0\) and the integral over the beam drops out. Steps 1 to 5 of the proof do not use the supports, only continuity and \(\eta = 0\) at \(t_1\) and \(t_2\), so equation (13.44) also holds for this \(\eta\). Because \(\eta_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\) and \(f_0 = f_{\mathrm{L}} = 0\), only the two terms with the shear force remain in equation (13.44), with \(V = -EI\,w_{\mathrm{xxx}} = EI\,a\,(\pi/L)^3\cos(\pi x/L)\cos\omega_1 t\). At \(x = 0\) it is \(V(0, t) = EI\,a\,(\pi/L)^3\cos\omega_1 t\), at \(x = L\) it is \(V(L, t) = -V(0, t)\). Hence

\[\delta\mathcal S = \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} \big(V(0, t) - V(L, t)\big)\,\psi\,\mathrm{d}t = 2\,EI\,a\Big(\frac{\pi}{L}\Big)^3 \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} \cos(\omega_1 t)\,\psi(t)\,\mathrm{d}t = 1296{,}5\ \text{N} \cdot (-2{,}734\ \text{ms}) = -3{,}544\ \text{N s} ,\]

which agrees with the direct calculation of \(B_1\) in SymPy. The first variation is therefore not zero although \(w\) is a motion. The terms \(V(0)\,\eta(0)\) and \(-V(L)\,\eta(L)\) are the work of the forces with which the supports hold the beam. An admissible variation is zero at the supports and removes them, an inadmissible one leaves them.

Theorem 13.37 now gives the equation of motion of the beam and all conditions at the ends from a single action, and the balance of a piece confirms it. What the solutions of equation (13.42) give for various supports is shown by section 13.6 with the separation of variables, by section 13.7, and by the Rayleigh quotient from the kinetic and the potential energy of this section (section 13.9).
