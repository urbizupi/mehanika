Chapter 29 Structural optimization

29.4 KKT optimality conditions (pogoji optimalnosti KKT)

In section 29.2 we minimized the wall truss (bars AC and BC, force \(F = 10\) kN at joint C) by an argument: each stress constraint bounds the area of one bar, the volume grows with every area, so the best choice of areas is the one where the stress in every bar is exactly \(\sigma_0\). The argument rests on each constraint concerning a single area. If we add a constraint that involves both areas at once, for example the largest allowed deflection of joint C, there is no such argument any more. How do we recognize the best choice when the constraints tie the variables together? And what does the number we get along the way tell us about a constraint? The answer is the KKT conditions. This section derives them, shows when they suffice and when they do not, and tests them on the truss.

The model is the same as before: the truss of chapter 4 with two steel bars (\(E = 196\) GPa), lengths \(L_{\mathrm{AC}} = 4\) m and \(L_{\mathrm{BC}} = 5\) m, forces \(N_{\mathrm{AC}} = -13\,333\) N and \(N_{\mathrm{BC}} = 16\,667\) N, which are fixed by the equilibrium of joint C and therefore do not depend on the areas (section 4.1.4, the bars are statically determinate). The allowed stress is \(\sigma_0 = 220\) MPa, without a safety factor, which is our idealization: a factor would multiply all areas by one number.

29.4.1 The problem and the active constraints

For the general theorems we need the letter \(\vek x\) for a point with \(n\) coordinates (definition 8.22), as in chapter 8. For the truss the coordinates are the cross-section areas, \(\vek x = (A_{\mathrm{AC}}, A_{\mathrm{BC}})\). The letter \(x\) is therefore not a position coordinate here. The problem (definition 29.1) is

\[\text{minimize } f(\vek x) \quad \text{subject to} \quad g_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek x) \le 0 , \quad i = 1, \dots, m , \quad \vek x \in D , \tag{29.17}\]

where \(D\) is an open set of points with \(n\) coordinates, \(f\) is the objective function (ciljna funkcija) and \(g_{\mathrm{i}}\) are the constraints (omejitve), all continuously differentiable on \(D\). A point \(\vek x\) of \(D\) that satisfies all \(m\) inequalities is feasible (definition 29.5). The equations of equilibrium do not appear in the problem: for the truss we solve them in advance, because the forces of the bars are known.

Let us draw the problem for the wall truss in the plane of the two areas (figure 29.14, left). Every point of the plane is one choice of both areas, so we draw the axes \(A_{\mathrm{AC}}\) and \(A_{\mathrm{BC}}\). The volume is \(f = V = L_{\mathrm{AC}}A_{\mathrm{AC}} + L_{\mathrm{BC}}A_{\mathrm{BC}}\). Points of equal volume lie on a line of slope \(-L_{\mathrm{AC}}/L_{\mathrm{BC}} = -0{,}8\), so we draw two lines of constant volume, like the contour lines on a map (compare figure 4.78). The stress constraints are \(g_1 = |N_{\mathrm{AC}}| - \sigma_0A_{\mathrm{AC}} \le 0\) and \(g_2 = |N_{\mathrm{BC}}| - \sigma_0A_{\mathrm{BC}} \le 0\). The stress is the force of the bar per area, \(|N|/A \le \sigma_0\), and for \(A > 0\) this is the same requirement written without division. Both have a boundary, which is the line \(g_1 = 0\), that is \(A_{\mathrm{AC}} = 60{,}61\) mm², and the line \(g_2 = 0\), that is \(A_{\mathrm{BC}} = 75{,}76\) mm². The feasible set lies to the right and above. The contour lines of volume \(V_1 = 621{,}2\) cm³ and \(V_2 = 1000\) cm³ show that the volume decreases toward the lower left corner. The best point is therefore the corner \(P\), \(P = (60{,}61;\ 75{,}76)\) mm², in which both constraints hold with equality. We already reached this conclusion in section 29.2. Now we want to say the same with derivatives, because only that will work when the lines become curves.

We cannot improve the point \(P\), because every step in a feasible direction is a step toward larger volume. Let us say this with derivatives. For a move from \(P\) by \(h\,\vek d\) the volume changes by the chain rule (lemma 8.29) by \(h\,\nabla f\cdot\vek d\) to first order, where \(\nabla f = (L_{\mathrm{AC}}, L_{\mathrm{BC}}) = (4, 5)\) m. In both feasible directions along the sides, \(\vek d_1\) toward larger \(A_{\mathrm{AC}}\) and \(\vek d_2\) toward larger \(A_{\mathrm{BC}}\), we have \(\nabla f\cdot\vek d_1 = 4\) m and \(\nabla f\cdot\vek d_2 = 5\) m, both positive. The same is said by writing the vector \(-\nabla f\) as the sum of vectors \(\mu_1\nabla g_1\) and \(\mu_2\nabla g_2\), which point out of the feasible set, with nonnegative numbers \(\mu_1\) and \(\mu_2\). The vector \(-\nabla f\) is indeed the sum of these two at the corner. The nonnegativity of the numbers \(\mu_{\mathrm{i}}\) has a physical reason that we know from section 8.3.7, where the multipliers are the forces of the constraints. A constraint that points out of the feasible set is like a flat wall: the wall only pushes the body back into the feasible region, never pulls it. The force of the wall is \(-\mu_{\mathrm{i}}\nabla g_{\mathrm{i}}\). For \(\mu_{\mathrm{i}} > 0\) it points into the feasible set, for \(\mu_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\) it is zero. If \(\mu_{\mathrm{i}} < 0\), the wall would pull, which a one-sided constraint cannot do.

Definition 29.27 Active constraint (aktivni pogoj)

Let \(\vek x^*\) be a feasible point of problem (29.17). The constraint \(g_{\mathrm{i}}\) is active (aktivna) at \(\vek x^*\) if \(g_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek x^*) = 0\), and inactive (neaktivna) if \(g_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek x^*) < 0\). The set \(I(\vek x^*) = \{\,i : g_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek x^*) = 0\,\}\) is the set of active indices, and we denote its size by \(k\).

The name has a reason. An active constraint at a point is working: it holds the point at the boundary and forbids it to go further. An inactive one does nothing, because \(g_{\mathrm{i}}\) has a negative value at \(\vek x^*\) and by continuity also at nearby points, so a move from \(\vek x^*\) cannot cross its boundary. At the corner \(P\) the active ones are \(g_1\) and \(g_2\). The constraints that the area is not negative, \(g_3 = -A_{\mathrm{AC}} \le 0\) and \(g_4 = -A_{\mathrm{BC}} \le 0\), are inactive, because \(-60{,}61 < 0\) and \(-75{,}76 < 0\).

We write the connection with section 8.3.5 in a function that collects the objective function and the constraints.

Definition 29.28 Lagrangian and KKT conditions (Lagrangeeva funkcija in pogoji KKT)

The Lagrangian (Lagrangeeva funkcija) of problem (29.17) is the function of the point \(\vek x\) of \(D\) and of the numbers \(\mu_1, \dots, \mu_{\mathrm{m}}\),

\[\mathcal{L}(\vek x, \vek\mu) = f(\vek x) + \sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{m}\mu_{\mathrm{i}}\,g_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek x) . \tag{29.18}\]

A feasible point \(\vek x^*\) and numbers \(\mu_1, \dots, \mu_{\mathrm{m}}\) satisfy the KKT conditions (pogoji KKT) if

\[\nabla f(\vek x^*) + \sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{m}\mu_{\mathrm{i}}\,\nabla g_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek x^*) = \vek 0 , \qquad \mu_{\mathrm{i}} \ge 0 , \qquad \mu_{\mathrm{i}}\,g_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek x^*) = 0 \quad (i = 1, \dots, m) . \tag{29.19}\]

The first equality is \(\nabla_{\vek{x}}\mathcal{L}(\vek x^*, \vek\mu) = \vek 0\). The numbers \(\mu_{\mathrm{i}}\) are the multipliers (množitelji) of the constraints. The last equality is the complementarity condition (pogoj komplementarnosti).

Source: CHK §3.3 (PDF pp. 50-51), equations (3.3) and (3.7): \(\mathcal{L}(\vek x, \vek\lambda) = g_0(\vek x) + \sum \lambda_{\mathrm{i}} g_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek x)\) and \(\lambda_{\mathrm{i}} g_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek x) = 0\), at the words "The Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) conditions". CHK writes \(g_0\) for the objective function and \(\lambda_{\mathrm{i}}\) for the multipliers. We write \(f\) and \(\mu_{\mathrm{i}}\), because \(\lambda\) in chapter 10 is an eigenvalue and in chapter 8 the multiplier of equations, which equals \(-\mu_{\mathrm{i}}\) (theorem 8.36 has \(\nabla V = \sum\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\nabla f_{\mathrm{r}}\)).

The names. The function is named after the Lagrange multipliers, because its derivative with respect to \(\vek x\), set equal to zero, is exactly equation (8.21) of chapter 8, only that here the multipliers belong to inequalities and have a sign. A multiplier is the number by which we multiply a constraint before adding it to the objective function. The abbreviation KKT stands for Karush, Kuhn and Tucker, as the source says. The complementarity condition is named for the fact that the numbers \(\mu_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(-g_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek x^*)\), both nonnegative, complement each other: for every \(i\) at least one of them is zero. An inactive constraint has \(\mu_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\), because a wall that is not in contact with the body does not push. A constraint with \(\mu_{\mathrm{i}} > 0\) is active, because a wall pushes only when it touches.

29.4.2 The necessary condition

Are the KKT conditions really satisfied at a local minimum? The idea of the proof follows the figure. We take the active constraints as equations and use theorem 8.36, which gives the multipliers without a sign. The sign comes from a curve that leaves the corner in the direction \(\vek d_1\) or \(\vek d_2\) of the figure: along it we leave the corner so that one active constraint becomes inactive and all the others stay at the boundary. Along this curve \(f\) must not decrease, hence \(\mu_{\mathrm{i}} \ge 0\). For the construction of the curve we need theorem 8.34 and therefore the condition on pivots, as in section 8.3.5.

Theorem 29.29 KKT conditions are necessary at a regular local minimum (pogoji KKT so v regularnem lokalnem minimumu potrebni)

Let \(f\) and \(g_1, \dots, g_{\mathrm{m}}\) be continuously differentiable on an open set \(D\) of points with \(n\) coordinates and let problem (29.17) have a local minimum at the feasible point \(\vek x^*\): there is a ball \(B\) centred at \(\vek x^*\) such that \(f(\vek x) \ge f(\vek x^*)\) for every feasible point \(\vek x\) of \(B\) (definition 29.15). Let the matrix \(\mat G\) with \(k\) rows \(\nabla g_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek x^*)^\T\), \(i \in I(\vek x^*)\) (definition 29.27), have \(k\) pivots (definition 8.20). Then there exist numbers \(\mu_1, \dots, \mu_{\mathrm{m}}\) that together with \(\vek x^*\) satisfy the KKT conditions (29.19), and the numbers \(\mu_{\mathrm{i}}\) with \(i \in I(\vek x^*)\) are uniquely determined.

Proof

We must show the equation with the multipliers, the uniqueness and the sign \(\mu_{\mathrm{i}} \ge 0\). The first two come from the theorem on Lagrange multipliers for the active constraints, the last from the curve along which we leave the feasible set across a single constraint.

  1. Let \(\vek x^*\) be a local minimum in the ball \(B\) and \(k = |I(\vek x^*)|\). For \(j \notin I(\vek x^*)\) we have \(g_{\mathrm{j}}(\vek x^*) < 0\), and since \(g_{\mathrm{j}}\) is continuous, there is a ball centred at \(\vek x^*\) in which \(g_{\mathrm{j}} < 0\) (the definition of continuity with \(\varepsilon = |g_{\mathrm{j}}(\vek x^*)|\)). We therefore shrink the radius of \(B\) so that it lies in \(D\) and that all inactive constraints are negative in it. Every point of the ball with \(g_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\) for \(i \in I(\vek x^*)\) is now feasible, so \(f \ge f(\vek x^*)\) there. The point \(\vek x^*\) is thus a local minimum of \(f\) on the set of solutions of the equations \(g_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\), \(i \in I(\vek x^*)\).
  2. For \(k = 0\) all constraints are inactive in the ball, \(\vek x^*\) is a local minimum of \(f\) on the ball. For every \(i\) the function \(h \mapsto f(\vek x^* + h\,\vek e_{\mathrm{i}})\) has its smallest value at \(h = 0\), so by lemma 1.82 its derivative is \(\partial f/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek x^*) = 0\). Hence \(\nabla f(\vek x^*) = \vek 0\), which is (29.19) with all \(\mu_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\). From here on \(k \ge 1\).
  3. For \(1 \le k < n\) we use theorem 8.36 with \(V = f\), equations \(g_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\) (\(i \in I(\vek x^*)\)), \(m = k\) and the matrix \(\mat G\) with \(k\) pivots: there is exactly one \(k\)-tuple of numbers \(\lambda_{\mathrm{i}}\) such that \(\nabla f(\vek x^*) = \sum_{\mathrm{i} \in \mathrm{I}(\vek{x}^*)}\lambda_{\mathrm{i}}\nabla g_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek x^*)\). For \(k = n\) the matrix \(\mat G\) is square with \(n\) pivots, so by lemma 8.21 \(\mat G^\T\) is invertible and the equation \(\mat G^\T\vek\lambda = \nabla f(\vek x^*)\) has exactly one solution, the same equality. The number \(k\) cannot be larger than \(n\), because the matrix \(\mat G\) has \(n\) columns and every pivot has its own column.
  4. The sign. Choose \(r \in I(\vek x^*)\). We number the coordinates so that the pivot columns of \(\mat G\) are the last ones, \(\vek x = (\vek y, \vek z)\) with \(\vek z\) of \(k\) coordinates, \(\vek x^* = (\vek y^*, \vek z^*)\) and \(\mat G = [\mat G_{\mathrm{y}}\ \mat G_{\mathrm{z}}]\). The matrix \(\mat G_{\mathrm{z}}\) has \(k\) pivots (section 8.3.1). We define \(k\) functions of the number \(t\) and the column \(\vek z\): \(h_{\mathrm{i}}(t, \vek z) = g_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek y^*, \vek z)\) for \(i \ne r\) and \(h_{\mathrm{r}}(t, \vek z) = g_{\mathrm{r}}(\vek y^*, \vek z) + t\). They are continuously differentiable, \(h_{\mathrm{i}}(0, \vek z^*) = 0\) for all \(i \in I(\vek x^*)\) and \(\partial\vek h/\partial\vek z(0, \vek z^*) = \mat G_{\mathrm{z}}\). By theorem 8.34 (with \(N = k + 1\) unknowns and \(k < N\) equations) there exist an open interval \(Y\) around \(0\) and a continuously differentiable map \(\vek\zeta\) on \(Y\) with \(\vek\zeta(0) = \vek z^*\) such that \(\vek h(t, \vek\zeta(t)) = \vek 0\). Let \(\vek x(t) = (\vek y^*, \vek\zeta(t))\). Then \(g_{\mathrm{r}}(\vek x(t)) = -t\) and \(g_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek x(t)) = 0\) for the other \(i \in I(\vek x^*)\).
  5. The map \(\vek x(t)\) is continuous and \(\vek x(0) = \vek x^*\), so there is \(\tau > 0\) such that \(\vek x(t)\) lies in the ball of step 1 for \(|t| < \tau\). For \(0 \le t < \tau\) we have \(g_{\mathrm{r}} = -t \le 0\), the other active ones are zero and the inactive ones are negative (step 1), so \(\vek x(t)\) is a feasible point in \(B\). Hence \(\varphi(t) = f(\vek x(t)) \ge f(\vek x^*) = \varphi(0)\) for \(0 \le t < \tau\).
  6. By the chain rule (lemma 8.29) \(\varphi\) is differentiable at \(0\) with \(\varphi'(0) = \nabla f(\vek x^*)\cdot\vek x'(0)\). If \(\varphi'(0) < 0\), then by the definition of the derivative there would be a small \(t > 0\) with \((\varphi(t) - \varphi(0))/t < 0\), which contradicts step 5. Hence \(\nabla f(\vek x^*)\cdot\vek x'(0) \ge 0\).
  7. We differentiate the equalities \(g_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek x(t)) = -\delta_{\mathrm{ir}}t\) for \(i \in I(\vek x^*)\) at \(0\) by the chain rule: \(\nabla g_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek x^*)\cdot\vek x'(0) = -\delta_{\mathrm{ir}}\) (Kronecker delta, \(1\) for \(i = r\), otherwise \(0\)). By step 3, \(\nabla f(\vek x^*)\cdot\vek x'(0) = \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\lambda_{\mathrm{i}}\nabla g_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek x^*)\cdot\vek x'(0) = -\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\). By step 6, \(-\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} \ge 0\).
  8. We set \(\mu_{\mathrm{i}} = -\lambda_{\mathrm{i}} \ge 0\) for \(i \in I(\vek x^*)\) and \(\mu_{\mathrm{j}} = 0\) for \(j \notin I(\vek x^*)\). Step 3 gives the first equality in (29.19), and the number \(\mu_{\mathrm{i}} g_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek x^*)\) is zero for active \(g_{\mathrm{i}}\) and, for inactive ones, because \(\mu_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\). The uniqueness of the numbers \(\mu_{\mathrm{i}}\) (\(i \in I\)) follows from the uniqueness of the numbers \(\lambda_{\mathrm{i}}\) in step 3.

Source: The proof is not from the source. CHK §3.3 (PDF p. 51) claims that the KKT conditions are necessary for "sufficiently regular nonconvex problems" and cites Theorem 3.4 for convex problems with Slater's condition, without proofs (PDF p. 44: "All theorems are presented without proofs"). The pivot condition is our choice of regularity condition, because we know it from chapter 8.

The proof showed that the multipliers exist where the matrix of active gradients is good enough for the implicit function theorem to give a curve. The sign came from one curve per multiplier, and this step will return for every constraint we treat in this chapter.

29.4.3 Example: the two stress constraints

Let us check the theorem at the corner \(P\) of figure 29.14, left. The problem has four constraints: \(g_1\) and \(g_2\) (the stresses) and \(g_3\) and \(g_4\) (the areas are not negative), all linear. At \(P\) the active ones are \(g_1 = |N_{\mathrm{AC}}| - \sigma_0A_{\mathrm{AC}}\) and \(g_2 = |N_{\mathrm{BC}}| - \sigma_0A_{\mathrm{BC}}\), so \(I(P) = \{1, 2\}\) and \(k = 2\). The gradients are \(\nabla g_1 = (-\sigma_0, 0)\) and \(\nabla g_2 = (0, -\sigma_0)\), the matrix \(\mat G = -\sigma_0\mat I\) has two pivots, regularity holds. The first equality of (29.19) is \((L_{\mathrm{AC}}, L_{\mathrm{BC}}) + \mu_1(-\sigma_0, 0) + \mu_2(0, -\sigma_0) = \vek 0\), so

\[\mu_1 = \frac{L_{\mathrm{AC}}}{\sigma_0} = \frac{4\ \text{m}}{220\cdot 10^6\ \text{Pa}} = 1{,}818\cdot 10^{-8}\ \frac{\text{m}^3}{\text{N}} , \qquad \mu_2 = \frac{L_{\mathrm{BC}}}{\sigma_0} = 2{,}273\cdot 10^{-8}\ \frac{\text{m}^3}{\text{N}} . \tag{29.20}\]

Both are positive. The sum \(\mu_1\nabla g_1 + \mu_2\nabla g_2 = (-4, -5)\) m is the vector \(-\nabla f\) of the figure, and complementarity holds because the inactive constraints \(g_3\) and \(g_4\) get \(\mu_3 = \mu_4 = 0\). The point \(P\) is therefore a KKT point.

The multiplier has the meaning of a price. If the axial force \(|N_{\mathrm{AC}}|\) grows by 1 kN, the bar must carry more, the area \(A_{\mathrm{AC}} = |N_{\mathrm{AC}}|/\sigma_0\) grows and the volume increases by \(\mu_1 \cdot 1000\ \text{N} = 1{,}818\cdot 10^{-5}\) m³ \(= 18{,}18\) cm³. For \(|N_{\mathrm{BC}}|\) it is \(22{,}73\) cm³ per kN. We checked this directly: the least volume is \(V^* = (L_{\mathrm{AC}}|N_{\mathrm{AC}}| + L_{\mathrm{BC}}|N_{\mathrm{BC}}|)/\sigma_0 = 621{,}2\) cm³ and its derivative with respect to \(|N_{\mathrm{AC}}|\) is \(L_{\mathrm{AC}}/\sigma_0 = \mu_1\). That the multiplier of every constraint is such a derivative, we do not prove here in general. For this problem we saw it on a closed form, and for the deflection problem we will check it once more.

Is \(P\) really the best point? Theorem 29.29 says only that a regular local minimum is a KKT point. The converse must be said by another theorem. First, though, let us see what happens when regularity is missing.

29.4.4 An edge where the necessary condition fails

Theorem 29.29 demands that the matrix of active gradients have \(k\) pivots. Let us test whether the condition is really needed: we look for a problem where the minimum is clear and the pivot condition does not hold.

Let us minimize \(f = -x_1\) subject to \(g_1 = x_2 - (1 - x_1)^3 \le 0\) and \(g_2 = -x_2 \le 0\). The feasible set is \(0 \le x_2 \le (1 - x_1)^3\), so we need \((1 - x_1)^3 \ge 0\) and \(x_1 \le 1\) (figure 29.15). In the plane we draw the axes \(x_1\) and \(x_2\). Because \(x_1 \le 1\), the smallest value of \(f\) is \(-1\), reached at the point \(S = (1, 0)\), which is a cusp (ost) of the set where the two curves touch. The set is a grey strip between the \(x_1\) axis and the curve \(x_2 = (1 - x_1)^3\), narrowed to a point at \(S\). The figure shows only its part with \(x_1 \ge 0\), to the left the strip goes on and widens. At \(S\) both constraints are active, the gradients \(\nabla g_1 = (0, 1)\) and \(\nabla g_2 = (0, -1)\) point in opposite directions, and \(\nabla f = (-1, 0)\) is perpendicular to them. Equation (29.19) would demand \((-1, 0) + \mu_1(0, 1) + \mu_2(0, -1) = \vek 0\), so \(-1 = 0\) in the first component. There are no multipliers. The vector \(-\nabla f = (1, 0)\) points out of the set, but is not a combination of the two gradients.

The proof of the theorem shows where it fails. The matrix \(\mat G = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 \end{smallmatrix}\right]\) has after elimination (add the first row to the second) only one pivot, in the second column, so \(1 < k = 2\). Step 4 would need a curve on which \(g_2 = 0\) and \(g_1 = -t\). From \(g_2 = 0\) we get \(x_2 = 0\), from \(g_1 = -t\) we get \((1 - x_1)^3 = t\), so \(x_1 = 1 - t^{1/3}\). The curve exists, but its derivative at \(t = 0\) does not: the quotient \((1 - x_1)/t = t^{-2/3}\) is \(100\) at \(t = 10^{-3}\), \(10^4\) at \(t = 10^{-6}\) and \(10^6\) at \(t = 10^{-9}\) and grows beyond every bound, so \(x'(0)\) does not exist. Without it steps 6 and 7 are gone. The pivot condition is therefore not decoration: without it the theorem does not hold.

29.4.5 A sufficient condition for convex problems

The necessary condition finds candidates. Is a candidate really a minimum? For a function of one variable a zero derivative does not mean a minimum, and so it is here. When, however, \(f\) and the set are convex, definitions 29.17 and 29.16, lemma 29.20 on the tangent plane gives us everything we need.

Theorem 29.30 KKT conditions are sufficient for a convex problem (pogoji KKT so pri konveksni nalogi zadostni)

Let \(D\) be an open convex set of points with \(n\) coordinates and let \(f\) and \(g_1, \dots, g_{\mathrm{m}}\) be convex and continuously differentiable functions on \(D\). Let a feasible point \(\vek x^*\) and numbers \(\mu_1, \dots, \mu_{\mathrm{m}}\) satisfy the KKT conditions (29.19). Then \(f(\vek x) \ge f(\vek x^*)\) for every feasible point \(\vek x\), so \(\vek x^*\) is a global minimum of problem (29.17).

Proof

We must show that the sum of the tangent inequalities, multiplied by the multipliers, gives \(f(\vek x) \ge f(\vek x^*)\). We do not need the pivot condition.

  1. Let \(\vek x\) be a feasible point. By lemma 29.20 for a convex and continuously differentiable function, \(f(\vek x) \ge f(\vek x^*) + \nabla f(\vek x^*)\cdot(\vek x - \vek x^*)\) and \(g_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek x) \ge g_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek x^*) + \nabla g_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek x^*)\cdot(\vek x - \vek x^*)\) for every \(i\).
  2. We multiply the inequalities for \(g_{\mathrm{i}}\) by \(\mu_{\mathrm{i}} \ge 0\), which keeps the direction of the inequality, and add them to the inequality for \(f\): \(f(\vek x) + \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\mu_{\mathrm{i}}g_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek x) \ge f(\vek x^*) + \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\mu_{\mathrm{i}}g_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek x^*) + \big[\nabla f(\vek x^*) + \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\mu_{\mathrm{i}}\nabla g_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek x^*)\big]\cdot(\vek x - \vek x^*)\).
  3. By the first equality of (29.19) the square bracket is \(\vek 0\). By the complementarity condition \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}}\mu_{\mathrm{i}}g_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek x^*) = 0\). Hence \(f(\vek x) + \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\mu_{\mathrm{i}}g_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek x) \ge f(\vek x^*)\).
  4. The point \(\vek x\) is feasible, \(g_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek x) \le 0\), and \(\mu_{\mathrm{i}} \ge 0\), so \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}}\mu_{\mathrm{i}}g_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek x) \le 0\). From step 3 it follows that \(f(\vek x) \ge f(\vek x^*) - \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\mu_{\mathrm{i}}g_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek x) \ge f(\vek x^*)\).

Source: CHK §3.3 (PDF p. 51), Theorem 3.5: "Let (P) be a convex problem, and let (x∗,λ∗) be a KKT point of (P). Then x∗ is a local (i.e. also global) minimum", without proof. The proof is not from the source.

The theorem says that \(P\) of the example of stress constraints is a global minimum: \(f\) and all \(g_{\mathrm{i}}\) are linear functions, hence convex (a linear function lies on its tangent plane), \(D = \mathbb{R}^2\) is convex and \(\mu_1, \mu_2 > 0\). The matter is now proved with derivatives and no longer only by an argument of monotonicity. We also got the \(\mu_{\mathrm{i}}\), which the argument did not give.

Convexity is a condition we must not leave out, as the following problem shows. Let us minimize \(f = -x^2\) subject to \(g_1 = x - 1 \le 0\) and \(g_2 = -x - 1 \le 0\), that is on the interval \([-1, 1]\) (figure 29.16). On the axes we put \(x\) horizontally and the value of \(f\) vertically. We draw the parabola \(f = -x^2\) only between the grey dashed lines \(x = -1\) and \(x = 1\), which are the bounds \(g_2 = 0\) and \(g_1 = 0\). At the point \(x = 1\) the constraint \(g_1\) is active, \(f'(1) = -2\), \(g_1' = 1\) and the equation \(-2 + \mu_1 = 0\) gives \(\mu_1 = 2 \ge 0\), so it is a KKT point, and by symmetry so is \(x = -1\) with \(\mu_2 = 2\). At \(x = 0\) there is no active constraint and \(f'(0) = 0\), so the KKT conditions hold with all \(\mu_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\). The values of \(f\) are \(-1\) at \(x = \pm 1\) and \(0\) at \(x = 0\). The points \(x = \pm 1\) are minima, and the point \(x = 0\) is even a maximum. The gradients \(\nabla f\) and \(\mu_{\mathrm{i}}\nabla g_{\mathrm{i}}\) cancel at all three points, but only at two of them is it a minimum. The function \(-x^2\) is not convex (its second derivative is \(-2 < 0\)), so theorem 29.30 does not apply. KKT points are candidates and nothing more, if the problem is not convex.

29.4.6 Example: the deflection constraint

Now a problem that thinking about each area separately does not solve. Let the wall truss have, besides the stress constraints, the requirement that joint C moves down under the force \(F\) by at most \(\Delta_0 = 2\) mm. This is the requirement of the designer, not a law. The deflection \(\Delta = -v_{\mathrm{C}}\) of bars with areas \(A_{\mathrm{AC}}\) and \(A_{\mathrm{BC}}\) is obtained by the unit force method (theorem 4.95). The forces in the bars for a unit vertical force are, because equilibrium is linear, \(\hat N_{\mathrm{k}} = N_{\mathrm{k}}/F\), so

\[\Delta(\vek x) = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} \frac{N_{\mathrm{k}}^2 L_{\mathrm{k}}}{F\,E\,A_{\mathrm{k}}} = \frac{c_{\mathrm{AC}}}{A_{\mathrm{AC}}} + \frac{c_{\mathrm{BC}}}{A_{\mathrm{BC}}} , \qquad c_{\mathrm{k}} = \frac{N_{\mathrm{k}}^2 L_{\mathrm{k}}}{F E} , \tag{29.21}\]

with \(c_{\mathrm{AC}} = 3{,}628\cdot 10^{-7}\) m³ and \(c_{\mathrm{BC}} = 7{,}086\cdot 10^{-7}\) m³. We check with the equilibrium of section 7.5.2: at \(100\) mm² per bar \(\Delta = (c_{\mathrm{AC}} + c_{\mathrm{BC}})/10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2 = 10{,}714\) mm, which is the deflection \(v_{\mathrm{C}} = -10{,}714\) mm found there. The additional constraint is \(g_{\mathrm{v}}(\vek x) = \Delta(\vek x) - \Delta_0 \le 0\) on \(D = \{A_{\mathrm{AC}} > 0,\ A_{\mathrm{BC}} > 0\}\), which is open and convex. The function \(1/A\) has on \(A > 0\) the second derivative \(2/A^3 > 0\), so it is convex (lemma 29.22), and \(g_{\mathrm{v}}\) is a sum of two convex functions with positive factors, so convex and continuously differentiable. The objective function \(V\) is linear. The problem is therefore convex and theorem 29.30 applies.

First we guess which constraint is active. The stress optimum \(P\) has by (29.21) the deflection \(\Delta = 15{,}34\) mm, which exceeds \(2\) mm, so it is not feasible and the deflection constraint must be active. We guess that the stress constraints are inactive, and check this after the computation. The gradient is \(\nabla g_{\mathrm{v}} = (-c_{\mathrm{AC}}/A_{\mathrm{AC}}^2,\ -c_{\mathrm{BC}}/A_{\mathrm{BC}}^2)\), nonzero, so the matrix with one row has one pivot and regularity holds. The first equality of (29.19) with \(\mu_1 = \mu_2 = 0\) for the stress constraints and \(\mu\) for the deflection is

\[L_{\mathrm{k}} - \mu\,\frac{c_{\mathrm{k}}}{A_{\mathrm{k}}^2} = 0 \quad (k = AC, BC) , \qquad\text{so}\qquad A_{\mathrm{k}} = \sqrt{\mu\,c_{\mathrm{k}}/L_{\mathrm{k}}} . \tag{29.22}\]

We insert this into the active constraint \(\Delta = \Delta_0\): \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} c_{\mathrm{k}}/A_{\mathrm{k}} = \mu^{-1/2}\sum_{\mathrm{k}}\sqrt{c_{\mathrm{k}}L_{\mathrm{k}}} = \Delta_0\). With \(S = \sum_{\mathrm{k}}\sqrt{c_{\mathrm{k}}L_{\mathrm{k}}} = 3{,}087\cdot 10^{-3}\) m² we have \(\sqrt\mu = S/\Delta_0\) and

\[A_{\mathrm{k}} = \sqrt{\frac{c_{\mathrm{k}}}{L_{\mathrm{k}}}}\,\frac{S}{\Delta_0} , \qquad \mu = \Big(\frac{S}{\Delta_0}\Big)^2 = 2{,}382\ \frac{\text{m}^3}{\text{m}} . \tag{29.23}\]

The numbers: \(A_{\mathrm{AC}} = 464{,}85\) mm², \(A_{\mathrm{BC}} = 581{,}07\) mm² and \(V^* = 4764{,}7\) cm³. We draw this in the same plane (figure 29.14, right, 1 cm is now 250 mm²). Outside the figure remain the axes and the stress limits, which lie close to the axes, far from the point we seek. The contour line of volume has the same slope \(-0{,}8\) as before, and the deflection boundary is a curve, because \(\Delta\) is a sum of quotients \(c_{\mathrm{k}}/A_{\mathrm{k}}\). The optimum is the point \(Q\), where the contour line touches the curve. The vector \(\mu\nabla g_{\mathrm{v}}\) and the vector \(-\nabla f\) are equal: \(\mu\nabla g_{\mathrm{v}} = (-4{,}000, -5{,}000)\) m and \(-\nabla f = (-4, -5)\) m, because \(\mu\,c_{\mathrm{k}}/A_{\mathrm{k}}^2 = L_{\mathrm{k}}\) by (29.22). We had the same picture at the corner \(P\), only that the boundary curve is bent and a single constraint is active. CHK solves in example 3.5 (PDF pp. 52-54) a truss of two bars with a deflection and a stress constraint and obtains the same: only the multiplier of the deflection is nonzero.

We still check the guess for the inactive constraints. The stresses are \(|N_{\mathrm{k}}|/A_{\mathrm{k}} = 28{,}68\) MPa in both bars, far below \(220\) MPa, so \(g_1 = |N_{\mathrm{AC}}| - \sigma_0A_{\mathrm{AC}} = -88{,}9\) kN and \(g_2 = -111{,}2\) kN, both negative, and \(\mu_1 = \mu_2 = 0\) agrees with complementarity. That the stresses are equal we did not assume, it came out of (29.22): \(\sigma_{\mathrm{k}}^2 = N_{\mathrm{k}}^2/A_{\mathrm{k}}^2 = N_{\mathrm{k}}^2L_{\mathrm{k}}/(\mu c_{\mathrm{k}}) = EF/\mu\), where we inserted \(c_{\mathrm{k}} = N_{\mathrm{k}}^2L_{\mathrm{k}}/(FE)\) from (29.21). Theorem 29.30 therefore says that \(Q\) is a global minimum: no choice of areas with a deflection up to \(2\) mm uses less material. Bars with equal areas \(535{,}71\) mm² (the same deflection) use \(4821{,}4\) cm³, which is \(1{,}2\) % more.

The multiplier has the same meaning of a price here. If we allow a larger deflection \(\Delta_0\), the least volume from (29.23) is \(V^*(\Delta_0) = L_{\mathrm{AC}}A_{\mathrm{AC}} + L_{\mathrm{BC}}A_{\mathrm{BC}} = S^2/\Delta_0\), so \(\mathrm{d}V^*/\mathrm{d}\Delta_0 = -S^2/\Delta_0^2 = -\mu\). The multiplier \(\mu = 2{,}382\) m³ per metre, that is \(2382\) cm³ per millimetre, tells how much volume we save if we allow the deflection to be a millimetre larger, to first order. The estimate is good for small changes: at \(\Delta_0 = 2{,}1\) mm (5 % more) the exact volume is \(4537{,}8\) cm³, the estimate \(4764{,}7 - 238{,}2 = 4526{,}5\) cm³, a difference of \(0{,}25\) %, because \(V^* \propto 1/\Delta_0\): for \(\Delta_0' = (1 + \varepsilon)\Delta_0\) the estimate is \(V^*(1 - \varepsilon)\), the exact value is \(V^*/(1 + \varepsilon)\), and the relative error is exactly \(\varepsilon^2\), so \(0{,}0025\) for \(\varepsilon = 0{,}05\). At \(\Delta_0 = 3\) mm (\(50\) % more) the estimate fails: \(V^* = S^2/\Delta_0 = 3176{,}5\) cm³, while the estimate is \(4764{,}7 - 2382{,}4 = 2382{,}4\) cm³, which is \(25\) % too low.

The guess about the active constraints holds only in a certain range. The stress \(\sigma = \Delta_0\sqrt{EF}/S\) in both bars grows with the allowed deflection: \(71{,}7\) MPa at \(5\) mm, \(143{,}4\) MPa at \(10\) mm and \(220\) MPa at \(\Delta_0 = 15{,}34\) mm, when both stress limits are touched. That is exactly the deflection of the truss with areas \(60{,}61\) and \(75{,}76\) mm² from the corner \(P\). At a larger \(\Delta_0\) the deflection constraint at \(P\) is no longer violated, and by theorem 29.30 the optimum is again \(P\) with the deflection multiplier zero. Active are therefore either the stress constraints (point \(P\), \(\Delta_0 \ge 15{,}34\) mm) or the deflection constraint (point \(Q\), \(\Delta_0 \le 15{,}34\) mm), never both at once except at the limit that separates them. Complementarity said this without an additional computation.

Summary of the section. We derived the KKT conditions from the theorem on Lagrange multipliers and from a curve that leaves across one constraint, under the pivot condition (theorem 29.29), and showed that without the pivot condition they fail (the cusp). For convex problems they are sufficient (theorem 29.30, proved with tangent planes), for nonconvex ones they are not (\(f = -x^2\)). On the truss we saw both cases: two active constraints at a corner with multipliers \(L_{\mathrm{i}}/\sigma_0\), and one active curve with multiplier \(S^2/\Delta_0^2\). The multiplier is the price of a constraint. We will use these conditions in the following sections of this chapter, in least compliance and in sequential approximations. The Lagrange duality, which turns the KKT conditions into a second problem with the same optimum, we only name here: it is in CHK §3.4 (PDF pp. 55-64) and we do not prove it.
