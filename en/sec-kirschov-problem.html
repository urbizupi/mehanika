Chapter 11 The boundary value problem of linear elasticity

11.13 A circular hole in a plate under tension

A steel plate a few millimetres thick is held at opposite edges in the jaws of a machine, which pull it with a tensile stress \(\sigma_0 = 50\) MPa. In the middle of the plate there is a circular hole of radius \(a = 10\) mm, for example for a bolt that was not inserted. Without the hole the stress would be the same everywhere, as in the tension bar of chapter 2. With the hole this is no longer so: the force that would run through the material inside the hole must run past it. Let us draw this as a flow. The lines divide the plate into bands, and each band carries the same share of the force from jaw to jaw, like water in channels. Far from the hole the lines are parallel and equally spaced, because the stress is the same everywhere there. The band that runs straight towards the upper half of the hole is about as wide as the radius far from the hole. It must give way to the hole and is squeezed next to it to about half. The same force over half the width means twice the mean stress. We computed the lines with the field that we find in this section, and we explain them exactly in section 11.13.4. Without this field no rule so far tells where the stress is largest and by how much. There are two questions. How large is the stress at the hole? And how far from the hole does the plate still feel it?

The path is this. First we say what we assume for the plate. Then, from the Airy function of the uniform field and from the functions of lemma 11.51, we build a field with three unknown constants, which are fixed by the free hole and by the requirement that the disturbance decays with distance. We test this field on the numbers of the plate. Then, by adding two such fields, we obtain the hole in a biaxial and in a shear field, and at the end we ask what the field of the infinite plate says about a plate of finite size.

11.13.1 The plate and the hole

The plate is thin and free on both faces, loaded only in its own plane. We treat it with the model of plane stress (ravninsko napetostno stanje, definition 11.36). Its exact meaning is given by theorem 11.39: if we assume \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) throughout the plate, the thickness averages of the stresses satisfy the plane equations exactly. We understand the stresses of this section as these averages. How close the stresses at the individual points of the thickness are to the average, the theorem does not say. Plane stress is a good approximation everywhere except in a band along the edge of the plate that is about as wide as the plate is thick (BARBER §3.2.1, PDF p. 58), and the edge of the hole is such an edge too. For a plate a few millimetres thick this band is narrower than the radius \(a = 10\) mm. For a plate thicker than the diameter of the hole the band would cover the whole region next to the hole where the stress is raised, and the thickness average would say little about the stress on a single face. The hole is a circle of radius \(a\) with a free edge, so there is no force per unit area on it. The material is isotropic, homogeneous and linearly elastic. Linear elasticity holds as long as the largest stress stays below the yield stress, for our plate up to \(\sigma_0 = 73{,}33\) MPa, as we compute in section 11.13.4. There is no body force. The only one that would come into question is the weight of the plate. In a plate of height \(h = 0{,}2\) m it gives stresses of the order \(\rho g h = 7800 \cdot 9{,}81 \cdot 0{,}2\) Pa \(\approx 0{,}015\) MPa (density of steel from BOWER, table 3.1, PDF p. 100), which is \(0{,}03\) % of \(\sigma_0\), so we leave it out.

The plate is much larger than the hole. For the hole \(a = 10\) mm we take a square \(200 \times 200\) mm, so the edge is \(10\) radii from the centre. Therefore we compute with a simplification: the plate is the whole plane outside the circle \(r \ge a\) (the infinite plate, neskončna plošča), and the tension acts at infinity. Let the \(x\) axis point in the direction of the pull and the origin lie at the centre of the hole. The requirement at infinity is this: as \(r \to \infty\) the stresses approach those of the plate without a hole, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_0\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\). The simplification is justified because the disturbance of the hole decays with distance. For the \(200 \times 200\) mm plate it makes an error of at most \(2{,}5\) % of \(\sigma_0\) on the edge of the plate, which we compute in section 11.13.6. For a plate whose edge is only a few radii from the hole the error is no longer small: at \(5a\) it is already \(9{,}8\) % on the \(x\) axis.

We do not prove uniqueness of the solution. Theorem 11.4 holds for bounded bodies. The infinite plate is not bounded, and a proof would need conditions at infinity, which the book does not treat. We therefore construct the field of this section and check every condition, but we do not claim that it is the only field with the given conditions.

11.13.2 The idea: a uniform field and a disturbance

We do not look for the field of the plate with a hole from the beginning. We build it from a field that we already know and a correction, as figure 11.129 shows. On the left is the plate without a hole, which we pull with the stress \(\sigma_0\). In it draw a circle of radius \(a\) where the hole will be. The material inside the circle, call it the plug, is part of the stretched plate, so it pulls the material around it towards itself, in the direction \(x\). How much, Cauchy's formula (2.25) tells: on a small piece of the edge with normal \(\vek n\) acts the force per unit area \(n_{\mathrm{x}}\,\sigma_0\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), because the stress \(\sigma_0\) acts only in the direction \(x\). The normal of the material around the circle points into the circle, \(n_{\mathrm{x}} = -\cos\theta\), so the size of the force is \(\sigma_0\,|\cos\theta|\): largest at the left and right ends of the circle, where the edge is perpendicular to the pull, and zero at the top and at the bottom, where the edge runs along the pull. Now we drill the plug out. Its forces disappear, and this is the same as adding opposite forces to the material around the hole. This is the disturbance: a plate with a hole that nothing pulls far from the hole, while on the edge of the hole the opposite forces push it outwards. The sum of the two fields has on the edge of the hole the sum of the two forces, which is zero, and this is the free edge of the hole. Far from the hole the sum has only the pull \(\sigma_0\). We know the uniform field, and we must find the disturbance.

We know the stresses of the plate without a hole. Their Airy function (theorem 11.40) is \(\varphi_0 = \sigma_0 y^2/2\), since \(\partial^2\varphi_0/\partial y^2 = \sigma_0 = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and the other second derivatives are zero. In polar coordinates \(y = r\sin\theta\) and by the addition theorem (corollary 8.16) \(\sin^2\theta = (1 - \cos 2\theta)/2\), so

\[\varphi_0 = \frac{\sigma_0}{2}\,r^2\sin^2\theta = \frac{\sigma_0}{4}\,r^2\,(1 - \cos 2\theta) . \tag{11.84}\]

The function \(\varphi_0\) contains only \(1\) and \(\cos 2\theta\) as dependence on the angle. The axes measure the ratio \(r/a\) and the values of the curves, all of which equal \(1\) at \(r = a\). The disturbance must decay with distance, because far from the hole only the pull \(\sigma_0\) remains. Among the functions of lemma 11.51 with angular dependence \(1\) and \(\cos 2\theta\) the following come into question.

  • For \(n = 0\) these are \(1\), \(\ln r\), \(r^2\) and \(r^2\ln r\). The function \(1\) gives no stress, \(r^2\) is the uniform field with constant stresses, \(\ln r\) gives a disturbance whose stresses decay like \(1/r^2\). The function \(r^2\ln r\) gives stresses that grow like \(\ln r\), and by theorem 11.54 its displacement in a full ring is multivalued, so we do not take it.
  • For \(n = 2\) these are \(r^2\cos 2\theta\), \(r^4\cos 2\theta\), \(r^{-2}\cos 2\theta\) and \(\cos 2\theta\). The function \(r^2\cos 2\theta\) already appears in (11.84), \(r^4\cos 2\theta\) gives stresses that grow like \(r^2\), and what remains are \(\cos 2\theta\) with stresses that decay like \(1/r^2\) and \(r^{-2}\cos 2\theta\) with stresses that decay like \(1/r^4\).

So we try the function

\[\varphi = \frac{\sigma_0}{4}\,r^2\,(1 - \cos 2\theta) + A\ln r + \Big(\frac{C}{r^2} + D\Big)\cos 2\theta \tag{11.85}\]

with three unknown constants \(A\), \(C\), \(D\). By lemma 11.50 the stresses of such a function depend on the angle only through \(1\), \(\cos 2\theta\) and \(\sin 2\theta\), so the two conditions of the free edge of the hole will give equations for the coefficients of three functions, and three constants will suffice.

11.13.3 The Kirsch solution

On the left, the axes run in the ratio \(r/a\). The blackwhite curve \(a^2/r^2\) falls to \(1/4\) at \(r = 2a\), the orange curve \(a^4/r^4\) to \(1/16\), and the two lengths at \(r = 2a\) show it. Both curves equal \(1\) at \(r = a\), so the picture shows how fast they fall away from the edge of the hole. On the right, the axis is the same \(r/a\), and on it lies the band between \(\pm\tfrac52\,a^2/r^2\) with half-widths \(5/8\) at \(r = 2a\) and \(5/32\) at \(r = 4a\). The deviation on the \(y\) axis and on the \(x\) axis stays inside the band.

Theorem 11.57 Kirsch solution for a circular hole in an infinite plate (Kirschova rešitev za krožno luknjo v neskončni plošči)

Let \(a > 0\), \(\sigma_0 > 0\), and let the plate be the region \(r \ge a\) of the plane \(x\), \(y\) with polar coordinates \((r, \theta)\) about the centre of the circle, in plane stress (definition 11.36) or plane strain (definition 11.32), of an isotropic and homogeneous material (\(E\), \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\)) and without body force. Let

\[\varphi = \frac{\sigma_0}{4}\,r^2 - \frac{a^2\sigma_0}{2}\,\ln r + \Big(-\frac{\sigma_0}{4}\,r^2 - \frac{a^4\sigma_0}{4}\,r^{-2} + \frac{a^2\sigma_0}{2}\Big)\cos 2\theta . \tag{11.86}\]

Then:

  1. The function \(\varphi\) is biharmonic on \(r > 0\), and its stresses are
    \[\begin{aligned} \sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} &= \frac{\sigma_0}{2}\Big(1 - \frac{a^2}{r^2}\Big) + \frac{\sigma_0}{2}\Big(1 - \frac{4a^2}{r^2} + \frac{3a^4}{r^4}\Big)\cos 2\theta , \\ \sigma_{\theta\theta} &= \frac{\sigma_0}{2}\Big(1 + \frac{a^2}{r^2}\Big) - \frac{\sigma_0}{2}\Big(1 + \frac{3a^4}{r^4}\Big)\cos 2\theta , \\ \sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta} &= -\frac{\sigma_0}{2}\Big(1 + \frac{2a^2}{r^2} - \frac{3a^4}{r^4}\Big)\sin 2\theta . \end{aligned} \tag{11.87}\]
    This is an equilibrium field without body force that satisfies the compatibility condition \(S_1 = 0\) (theorem 11.40, item 3).
  2. The edge of the hole is free: \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(a, \theta) = \sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}(a, \theta) = 0\) for every \(\theta\).
  3. The difference of each Cartesian stress component from the uniform field \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_0\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\) is smaller in absolute value than \(c\,\sigma_0\,a^2/r^2\) for all \(\theta\) and \(r \ge a\), with a constant \(c\) that depends neither on \(r\) nor on \(\theta\).
  4. On the edge of the hole
    \[\sigma_{\theta\theta}(a, \theta) = \sigma_0\,(1 - 2\cos 2\theta) , \tag{11.88}\]
    the largest value is \(3\sigma_0\) at \(\theta = \pm\tfrac{\pi}{2}\), the smallest \(-\sigma_0\) at \(\theta = 0\) and \(\theta = \pi\), and zero at \(\theta = \pm\tfrac{\pi}{6}\) and \(\pm\tfrac{5\pi}{6}\).
  5. Let \(E'\), \(\nu'\) be the constants of the plane system (corollary 11.38: \(E\), \(\nu\) for plane stress, \(E^*\), \(\nu^*\) for plane strain). The displacement field
    \[\begin{aligned} u_{\mathrm{r}} &= \frac{\sigma_0}{2E'}\Big[(1 - \nu')\,r + (1 + \nu')\frac{a^2}{r} \\ &\quad + \Big((1 + \nu')\,r + \frac{4a^2}{r} \\ &\quad - (1 + \nu')\frac{a^4}{r^3}\Big)\cos 2\theta\Big] , \\ u_\theta &= -\frac{\sigma_0}{2E'}\Big[(1 + \nu')\,r + \frac{2(1 - \nu')\,a^2}{r} \\ &\quad + (1 + \nu')\frac{a^4}{r^3}\Big]\sin 2\theta \end{aligned} \tag{11.89}\]
    has the strains that belong to the stresses (11.87), and it is single-valued: on a full turn \(\theta \to \theta + 2\pi\) it returns to the same value. The stresses (11.87) contain neither \(E\) nor \(\nu\), only the displacements depend on them.
Proof

We must show five items. The idea is this: the field (11.85) is biharmonic, its stresses follow from lemma 11.50, three conditions on the edge of the hole fix the three constants, and the displacements are obtained by integrating the strains.

  1. Item 1, biharmonicity. The functions \(r^2\), \(\ln r\), \(r^2\cos 2\theta\), \(r^{-2}\cos 2\theta\) and \(\cos 2\theta\) are biharmonic on \(r > 0\) (lemma 11.51, the cases \(n = 0\) and \(n = 2\)). The operator \(\nabla^4\) is linear (definition 11.5), so every linear combination is biharmonic, also (11.85). The function \(\varphi\) is, in polar coordinates, a function of the point \((x, y)\) on \(r > 0\) with continuous derivatives of all orders, since \(r^2 = x^2 + y^2\), \(\ln r = \tfrac12\ln(x^2 + y^2)\) and \(\cos 2\theta = \cos^2\theta - \sin^2\theta = (x^2 - y^2)/r^2\) (corollary 8.16), and all of these are smooth functions on \(r > 0\). By theorem 11.40 (item 3, \(V = 0\)) the stresses are in equilibrium and satisfy \(S_1 = 0\).

  2. Item 1, stresses. We compute the stresses of each term by lemma 11.50, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = \tfrac1r\,\partial_{\mathrm{r}}\varphi + \tfrac{1}{r^2}\,\partial_\theta^2\varphi\), \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = \partial_{\mathrm{r}}^2\varphi\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = -\partial_{\mathrm{r}}\big(\tfrac1r\,\partial_\theta\varphi\big)\). The angular derivatives of \(\cos 2\theta\) are \(-2\sin 2\theta\) and \(-4\cos 2\theta\). For the term \(C\,r^{-2}\cos 2\theta\), for example, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = \tfrac1r\,(-2C\,r^{-3})\cos 2\theta + \tfrac1{r^2}\,(-4C\,r^{-2})\cos 2\theta = -6C\,r^{-4}\cos 2\theta\). All terms give:

    term of \(\varphi\) \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}\)
    \(\tfrac{\sigma_0}{4}\,r^2\) \(\tfrac{\sigma_0}{2}\) \(\tfrac{\sigma_0}{2}\) \(0\)
    \(-\tfrac{\sigma_0}{4}\,r^2\cos 2\theta\) \(\tfrac{\sigma_0}{2}\cos 2\theta\) \(-\tfrac{\sigma_0}{2}\cos 2\theta\) \(-\tfrac{\sigma_0}{2}\sin 2\theta\)
    \(A\ln r\) \(A/r^2\) \(-A/r^2\) \(0\)
    \(C\,r^{-2}\cos 2\theta\) \(-6C\,r^{-4}\cos 2\theta\) \(6C\,r^{-4}\cos 2\theta\) \(-6C\,r^{-4}\sin 2\theta\)
    \(D\cos 2\theta\) \(-4D\,r^{-2}\cos 2\theta\) \(0\) \(-2D\,r^{-2}\sin 2\theta\)

    The first two rows are the uniform field: \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = \sigma_0\cos^2\theta\), \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = \sigma_0\sin^2\theta\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = -\sigma_0\sin\theta\cos\theta\) are the components of the tensor \(\sigma_0\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\otimes\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) in local axes, since \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = \cos\theta\) and \(\vek e_\theta\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = -\sin\theta\).

  3. Item 2, constants. The body lies outside the circle, so the outward normal on the edge \(r = a\) is \(\vek n = -\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\), it points into the hole, and the force per unit area on the edge of the hole is \(-\ten\sigma\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) with components \(-\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) and \(-\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}\). A free edge requires \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(a, \theta) = \sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}(a, \theta) = 0\) for every \(\theta\). The sum of the rows of the table at \(r = a\) gives

    \[\begin{gathered} \sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(a, \theta) = \Big(\frac{\sigma_0}{2} + \frac{A}{a^2}\Big) + \Big(\frac{\sigma_0}{2} - \frac{6C}{a^4} - \frac{4D}{a^2}\Big)\cos 2\theta , \\ \sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}(a, \theta) = \Big(-\frac{\sigma_0}{2} - \frac{6C}{a^4} - \frac{2D}{a^2}\Big)\sin 2\theta . \end{gathered}\]

    The expression \(\alpha + \beta\cos 2\theta\) is zero for every \(\theta\) exactly when \(\alpha = \beta = 0\): at \(\theta = \pi/4\) we have \(\cos 2\theta = 0\) and get \(\alpha = 0\), at \(\theta = 0\) we get \(\alpha + \beta = 0\). The expression \(\gamma\sin 2\theta\) is zero for every \(\theta\) exactly when \(\gamma = 0\) (at \(\theta = \pi/4\)). The conditions give three equations

    \[\frac{\sigma_0}{2} + \frac{A}{a^2} = 0 , \qquad \frac{\sigma_0}{2} - \frac{6C}{a^4} - \frac{4D}{a^2} = 0 , \qquad -\frac{\sigma_0}{2} - \frac{6C}{a^4} - \frac{2D}{a^2} = 0 .\]

    The first gives \(A = -a^2\sigma_0/2\). The difference of the second and the third is \(\sigma_0 - 2D/a^2 = 0\), so \(D = a^2\sigma_0/2\), and the third gives \(-\sigma_0/2 - 6C/a^4 - \sigma_0 = 0\), so \(C = -a^4\sigma_0/4\). These are the constants in (11.86), and the edge of the hole is free.

  4. Item 1, substitution. If we insert the constants into the table and add the rows, we get (11.87). For example \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = \tfrac{\sigma_0}{2} - \tfrac{\sigma_0}{2}\cos 2\theta - \tfrac{A}{r^2} + 6C\,r^{-4}\cos 2\theta = \tfrac{\sigma_0}{2}\big(1 + \tfrac{a^2}{r^2}\big) - \tfrac{\sigma_0}{2}\big(1 + \tfrac{3a^4}{r^4}\big)\cos 2\theta\).

  5. Item 3, infinity. The first two rows of the table are the uniform field, and rows 3 to 5 are the disturbance. The rows of the disturbance are smaller in absolute value than \(\big(|A| + 6|C|/a^2 + 6|D|\big)/r^2\), since \(r \ge a\) gives \(r^{-4} \le a^{-2}\,r^{-2}\) for each term, so they are smaller than \(c_1\,\sigma_0\,a^2/r^2\) with a constant \(c_1\). The Cartesian components are linear combinations of the polar ones with coefficients \(\cos^2\theta\), \(\sin^2\theta\), \(2\sin\theta\cos\theta\) and \(\pm1\) (theorem 2.53), which are at most \(1\) in absolute value. The difference from the uniform field is therefore smaller than \(c\,\sigma_0\,a^2/r^2\) with a constant \(c\).

  6. Item 4. From (11.87) at \(r = a\) we have \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}(a, \theta) = \sigma_0 - 2\sigma_0\cos 2\theta\). The function \(\cos 2\theta\) has its smallest value \(-1\) at \(\theta = \pm\pi/2\), where \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = 3\sigma_0\), its largest value \(1\) at \(\theta = 0\), \(\pi\), where \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = -\sigma_0\), and the value \(\tfrac12\) at \(2\theta = \pm\pi/3\), \(\pm 5\pi/3\), where \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = 0\).

  7. Item 5, strains. Let the constants \(E'\), \(\nu'\) be those of the plane system (11.50). For plane stress (\(E' = E\), \(\nu' = \nu\)) Hooke's law in local axes (corollary 11.27) at \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) gives the same form for the local components, and for plane strain the same holds with \(E^*\), \(\nu^*\) (corollary 11.38), because \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}) = \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} + \sigma_{\theta\theta})\), since the sum of the in-plane normal stresses does not change under a rotation of the axes. So \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} = (\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} - \nu'\sigma_{\theta\theta})/E'\), \(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} = (\sigma_{\theta\theta} - \nu'\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}})/E'\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = (1 + \nu')\,\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}/E'\). With the stresses (11.87) we get

    \[\begin{gathered} \varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} = \frac{\sigma_0}{2E'}\Big[(1 - \nu') - (1 + \nu')\frac{a^2}{r^2} \\ + \Big((1 + \nu') - \frac{4a^2}{r^2} + 3(1 + \nu')\frac{a^4}{r^4}\Big)\cos 2\theta\Big] , \end{gathered}\]
    \[\begin{gathered} \varepsilon_{\theta\theta} = \frac{\sigma_0}{2E'}\Big[(1 - \nu') + (1 + \nu')\frac{a^2}{r^2} \\ - \Big((1 + \nu') - \frac{4\nu' a^2}{r^2} + 3(1 + \nu')\frac{a^4}{r^4}\Big)\cos 2\theta\Big] , \end{gathered}\]
    \[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = -\frac{(1 + \nu')\,\sigma_0}{2E'}\Big(1 + \frac{2a^2}{r^2} - \frac{3a^4}{r^4}\Big)\sin 2\theta .\]

  8. By corollary 11.21 \(\partial u_{\mathrm{r}}/\partial r = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}}\). The integral over \(r\) is the displacement \(u_{\mathrm{r}}\) from (11.89), except that a function \(f(\theta)\) independent of \(r\) is added to it.

  9. From \(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} = (u_{\mathrm{r}} + \partial u_\theta/\partial\theta)/r\) we get \(\partial u_\theta/\partial\theta = r\,\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} - u_{\mathrm{r}}\). The part without \(\theta\) is therefore \(\tfrac{\sigma_0}{2E'}\big[(1 - \nu')\,r + (1 + \nu')\,a^2/r\big] - \tfrac{\sigma_0}{2E'}\big[(1 - \nu')\,r + (1 + \nu')\,a^2/r\big] = 0\). So the displacement \(u_\theta\) has no term that would grow with \(\theta\) and change the displacement after a full turn (for the ring it was given by the term \(B\,r^2\ln r\) in theorem 11.54). The part before \(\cos 2\theta\) is \(\tfrac{\sigma_0}{2E'}\big[-2(1 + \nu')\,r - 4(1 - \nu')\,a^2/r - 2(1 + \nu')\,a^4/r^3\big]\). Integration over \(\theta\), with \(\int\cos 2\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta = \tfrac12\sin 2\theta\), gives \(u_\theta\) from (11.89), to which \(-F(\theta) + g(r)\) with \(F' = f\) is still added.

  10. The remaining relation is \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = \tfrac12\big(\tfrac1r\,\partial u_{\mathrm{r}}/\partial\theta + \partial u_\theta/\partial r - u_\theta/r\big)\). For the displacements of the hole edge let \(P = (1 + \nu')\,r + 4a^2/r - (1 + \nu')\,a^4/r^3\) and \(Q = -(1 + \nu')\,r - 2(1 - \nu')\,a^2/r - (1 + \nu')\,a^4/r^3\), so that \(u_{\mathrm{r}} = \tfrac{\sigma_0}{2E'}\big[\dots + P\cos 2\theta\big]\) and \(u_\theta = \tfrac{\sigma_0}{2E'}\,Q\sin 2\theta + \dots\). Twice the right-hand side in the terms with \(\sin 2\theta\) is \(\tfrac{\sigma_0}{2E'}\big(-2P/r + Q' - Q/r\big)\sin 2\theta\). The terms are \(-2P/r = -2(1 + \nu') - 8a^2/r^2 + 2(1 + \nu')\,a^4/r^4\), \(Q' = -(1 + \nu') + 2(1 - \nu')\,a^2/r^2 + 3(1 + \nu')\,a^4/r^4\) and \(-Q/r = (1 + \nu') + 2(1 - \nu')\,a^2/r^2 + (1 + \nu')\,a^4/r^4\). The sum is \((1 + \nu')\big(-2 - 4a^2/r^2 + 6a^4/r^4\big)\) and half of the expression \(\tfrac{\sigma_0}{2E'}(\dots)\sin 2\theta\) is indeed \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}\theta}\) from step 7. The remaining terms give \(r\,g' - g = -(F'' + F)\). The left side depends only on \(r\), the right only on \(\theta\), so both equal a constant \(\kappa\). Then \(g = c_3\,r - \kappa\) and \(F = -\kappa + c_1\cos\theta + c_2\sin\theta\), which is a rigid displacement (translation \(f = F'\) and small rotation \(c_3\,r\)) that we leave out.

  11. The functions \(\cos 2\theta\) and \(\sin 2\theta\) have period \(\pi\), so the displacement field (11.89) returns to the same value after a turn \(\theta \to \theta + 2\pi\). The components \(u_{\mathrm{r}}\), \(u_\theta\) are components of the field \(\vek u = u_{\mathrm{r}}\vek e_{\mathrm{r}} + u_\theta\vek e_\theta\), in which \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(\vek e_\theta\) are functions of the point, so \(\vek u\) is single-valued too. The constants \(A\), \(C\), \(D\) from step 3 are fixed by the force conditions, so the stresses contain neither \(E\) nor \(\nu\).

Source: SADD, example 8.7, (8.4.11) to (8.4.17), PDF pp. 192 to 194, CM, example 7.5.7, PDF pp. 335 to 337, BARBER §8.4.1, (8.60), PDF p. 133. Displacements: the integration procedure from BARBER §9.2, PDF pp. 141 to 143, where the same computation is done for a hole in a shear field. The formulas (11.89) were computed and checked with SymPy (rbe, part I). Uniqueness of the solution of the infinite plate is not proved.

The proof showed that the three conditions of the free edge determine the three constants exactly, and gave a field that we can check on numbers. Equilibrium and compatibility are consequences of biharmonicity, the free edge of the hole is the first two conditions of step 3, and the uniform field at infinity is the first two rows of the table.

11.13.4 The stress at the hole

For the plate from the beginning \(a = 10\) mm and \(\sigma_0 = 50\) MPa. Figure 11.132 shows a piece of this plate around the hole of radius \(a\). The blue arrows on the left and right edges of the piece are the pull \(\sigma_0\) in the direction of the \(x\) axis. By (11.88) the hoop stress on the edge of the hole is \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}(a, \theta) = 50\,(1 - 2\cos 2\theta)\) MPa. In the figure it is the orange curve: its offset from the edge of the hole is the stress, outwards tensile, inwards compressive. At \(\theta = 90^\circ\), at the ends of the diameter perpendicular to the pull, it is \(150\) MPa. At \(\theta = 0\), at the ends of the diameter in the direction of the pull, it is \(-50\) MPa. This is compression, although nowhere do we press the plate. It is zero at \(\theta = 30^\circ\), where the orange curve crosses the edge of the hole, which SADD also writes (PDF p. 194).

Why compression, if nowhere do we press the plate? Let us look at how the hole changes under the pull (figure 11.133). The displacements of the edge are given by item 5 of theorem 11.57. At \(r = a\) Poisson's ratio cancels and what remains is

\[u_{\mathrm{r}}(a, \theta) = \frac{\sigma_0 a}{E'}\,(1 + 2\cos 2\theta) , \qquad u_\theta(a, \theta) = -\frac{2\sigma_0 a}{E'}\,\sin 2\theta .\]

Draw the hole before the pull grey and dashed. When we pull the plate in the direction \(x\), the hole stretches into an oval, just as a hole in a rubber glove stretches when we pull the glove. The left and right ends move outwards by \(u_{\mathrm{r}}(a, 0) = 3\sigma_0 a/E\), and the top and the bottom move inwards by \(\sigma_0 a/E\) (in plane stress \(E' = E\)). For our plate with \(E = 196\) GPa these are \(0{,}0077\) mm and \(0{,}0026\) mm, so the displacements in the figure are enlarged about \(1000\) times.

Now take a short piece of the edge at the right end of the hole, between \(\theta = -30^\circ\) and \(\theta = 30^\circ\). After the pull the piece lies on the oval and its two ends have moved closer to the \(x\) axis. The piece has shortened, and material that we shorten is compressed. On the free edge \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = 0\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\), so Hooke's law gives simply \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = E\,\varepsilon_{\theta\theta}\). From the displacements \(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta}(a, 0) = (u_{\mathrm{r}} + \partial u_\theta/\partial\theta)/a = (3 - 4)\,\sigma_0/E = -\sigma_0/E\) (corollary 11.21), so \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = -\sigma_0\). At the top of the hole it is the other way round. The piece of the edge there is horizontal, and on the oval its two ends move apart. The piece stretches by \(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta}(a, \tfrac{\pi}{2}) = (-1 + 4)\,\sigma_0/E = 3\sigma_0/E\) and the stress there is \(3\sigma_0\). The hoop stress on the edge of the hole is therefore the stretching or the shortening of the edge that we see in the figure.

Where does exactly the number \(3\) come from? We put it together from the parts of figure 11.129 and the rows of the table of step 2 of the proof at \(r = a\) and \(\theta = \pi/2\).

  • The uniform field (rows 1 and 2) gives \(\sigma_0\), because at the top of the hole the hoop direction is horizontal, which is the direction of the pull.
  • The forces of the disturbance on the edge of the hole have, perpendicular to the edge, the part \(\sigma_0\cos^2\theta = \tfrac{\sigma_0}{2} + \tfrac{\sigma_0}{2}\cos 2\theta\), which pushes the edge outwards. Its first part is the same in all directions, like a pressure \(\sigma_0/2\) in the hole, and by the Lamé solution (theorem 11.28, \(b \to \infty\)) this stretches the circumference with the stress \(\sigma_0/2\). This is the row \(A\ln r\).
  • The rest, which varies with \(\cos 2\theta\) and \(\sin 2\theta\) (the second part and the force along the edge), gives \(3\sigma_0/2\). This is the row \(C\,r^{-2}\cos 2\theta\), and the row \(D\cos 2\theta\) gives no hoop stress.

Together

\[\sigma_{\theta\theta}(a, \tfrac{\pi}{2}) = \sigma_0 + \frac{\sigma_0}{2} + \frac{3\sigma_0}{2} = 3\sigma_0 , \qquad \sigma_{\theta\theta}(a, 0) = 0 + \frac{\sigma_0}{2} - \frac{3\sigma_0}{2} = -\sigma_0 .\]

At the end of the horizontal diameter the uniform field gives no hoop stress, because the hoop direction there is vertical, perpendicular to the pull.

The ratio of the largest stress to the stress in the plate without a hole is called the stress concentration factor (faktor koncentracije napetosti), \(K_{\mathrm{t}} = \sigma_{\max}/\sigma_0\), where \(\sigma_{\max}\) is the largest principal stress in the plate. On the edge of the hole \(K_{\mathrm{t}} = 3\). That there is no larger principal stress anywhere in the plate we did not prove. We checked it on a grid of about \(1{,}4\) million points over \(1 \le r/a \le 40\) and \(0 \le \theta \le 2\pi\): the largest principal stress is \(3\sigma_0\) on the edge of the hole, the smallest \(-\sigma_0\) where \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = -\sigma_0\). On the edge of the hole \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = 0\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\), so the state there is uniaxial and the von Mises stress (definition 2.107) is \(|\sigma_{\theta\theta}| = 150\) MPa. Take a yield stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{Y}} = 220\) MPa. The von Mises stress is largest on the edge of the hole on the same grid, so the steel starts to yield at the hole when

\[3\sigma_0 = \sigma_{\mathrm{Y}} = 220\ \text{MPa} , \qquad \sigma_0 = \frac{220\ \text{MPa}}{3} = 73{,}33\ \text{MPa} ,\]

that is at a third of the stress at which the plate without a hole would start to yield.

We get the Cartesian components on both axes from (11.87). On the \(y\) axis (\(\theta = \pi/2\)) the local axis \(\vek e_\theta\) points in the direction \(-x\), therefore \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\theta\theta}\), so

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(0, y) = \sigma_0\Big(1 + \frac{a^2}{2y^2} + \frac{3a^4}{2y^4}\Big) , \tag{11.90}\]

Where does the force go that the hole cannot carry? Through the section \(x = 0\), in the plate without a hole, a force \(2a\,\sigma_0\) per unit thickness passes over the width \(2a\). The excess of stress at the hole is by (11.90)

\[2\int_{\mathrm{a}}^\infty \big(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(0, y) - \sigma_0\big)\,\mathrm{d}y = 2\sigma_0\int_{\mathrm{a}}^\infty\Big(\frac{a^2}{2y^2} + \frac{3a^4}{2y^4}\Big)\mathrm{d}y = 2\sigma_0\Big(\frac a2 + \frac a2\Big) = 2a\,\sigma_0 ,\]

exactly what the cut-out part would carry. Here \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^\infty y^{-2}\,\mathrm{d}y = 1/a\) and \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^\infty y^{-4}\,\mathrm{d}y = 1/(3a^3)\). The force that would pass through the hole therefore flows past it along the narrow ligaments next to the hole, and the ligaments carry more than they would without the hole.

This is exactly what the lines of flow from the beginning of the section show. They are drawn so that no force in the direction \(x\) crosses any line. Take the function \(\psi = \partial\varphi/\partial y\). By theorem 11.40 \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \partial\psi/\partial y\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = -\partial\psi/\partial x\). Describe a curve by the point \((x(s), y(s))\), where \(s\) is the length travelled along it. The normal to the curve is then \((y', -x')\) and the \(x\) component of the force per unit length of the curve is

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,y' - \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,x' = \frac{\partial\psi}{\partial y}\,y' + \frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}\,x' = \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\,\psi\big(x(s), y(s)\big) .\]

The force in the direction \(x\) that passes across a piece of the curve is therefore the difference of the values of \(\psi\) at its two ends. The lines of flow are the curves on which \(\psi\) is constant, with the step \(0{,}5\,\sigma_0 a\) between neighbours, so between two neighbouring lines flows the force \(0{,}5\,\sigma_0 a\) per unit thickness. On the \(x\) axis and on the edge of the hole \(\psi = 0\), so the edge of the hole is itself one of the lines: no force flows through a free edge. The shaded band between \(\psi = 0\) and \(\psi = \sigma_0 a\) carries the force \(\sigma_0 a\), which is the force that without the hole would flow through the upper half of the hole. Next to the hole it is only \(0{,}49\,a\) wide, so the mean stress there is \(\sigma_0 a/(0{,}49\,a) = 2{,}05\,\sigma_0\).

Why does the disturbance decay like \((a/r)^2\) and not exponentially, as in the strip in section 11.11? The field (11.87) is the sum of the uniform field and a disturbance. The uniform field on the edge of the hole (outward normal \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\)) has the force per unit area \(-\ten\sigma^{(0)}\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\), and the sum must be zero, so the load of the disturbance on the edge of the hole is \(+\ten\sigma^{(0)}\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\). This load is self-equilibrated (definition 11.48). Its resultant over the circle is \(a\,\ten\sigma^{(0)}\int_0^{2\pi}\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\,\mathrm{d}\theta = \vek 0\), since the components \(\cos\theta\) and \(\sin\theta\) cancel over the full circle: every arrow on the right half has an equally large opposite one on the left. The moment about the centre has only the component \(a^2\int_0^{2\pi}\sigma^{(0)}_{\mathrm{r}\theta}\,\mathrm{d}\theta = -\tfrac{a^2\sigma_0}{2}\int_0^{2\pi}\sin 2\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta = 0\): the arrows at \(\theta\) and \(-\theta\) are equal and their lever arms opposite, so their turning effects cancel. The disturbance is therefore the response to a self-equilibrated load on a part of the edge of size \(2a\), as in section 11.11, and we computed that the stress decays. It decays algebraically because among the functions of lemma 11.51 with angular dependence \(1\) and \(\cos 2\theta\) the slowest decaying are \(\ln r\) and \(\cos 2\theta\), both with stresses that decay like \(r^{-2}\). SADD gives the same rate \(O(1/r^2)\) for a hole under pressure (PDF p. 191). In the strip the decaying solutions with a complex exponent \(\lambda\) remain (lemma 11.49), because the conditions of the free edges of the strip give an equation for \(\lambda\) with such solutions.

From history

The stresses around a circular hole in a plate that is pulled uniformly in one direction were analysed by G. Kirsch in 1898 (TIMO, PDF p. 346, footnote). He showed that the largest stress is on the edge of the hole, at the ends of the diameter perpendicular to the direction of the pull, and that it is three times larger than the applied stress (TIMO, PDF p. 346). According to the text of TIMO it was thereby probably shown for the first time how essential it is to study the local irregularities in the stress distribution produced by holes, and since then engineers have studied stress concentrations intensively, theoretically and experimentally (TIMO, PDF p. 346). With our numbers this means that the stress far from the hole is \(50\) MPa and right next to the hole \(150\) MPa, and a computation with the average over the cross section does not detect this. The plate of finite width with a hole on the axis of symmetry was considered by R. C. J. Howland (TIMO, PDF p. 346), and we replaced it by an infinite plate.

After the story we return to the plate with a hole that we pull in the direction \(x\). We now use the same Kirsch field for two other loads.

11.13.5 Biaxial tension and pure shear

A plate in a structure is often pulled in two directions or loaded in shear. Linearity of the equations lets us obtain these two fields from the Kirsch field without a new computation. The equations and the boundary condition of the free edge in polar coordinates do not contain the angle \(\theta\) explicitly (lemma 11.50), so besides \(\varphi(r, \theta)\) also \(\varphi(r, \theta - \pi/2)\) is a solution, namely for the pull \(\tau\) in the direction of the \(y\) axis: the field of the left panel of figure 11.135, turned by a quarter turn, with the pull \(\tau\) in place of \(\sigma_0\). Since \(\cos 2(\theta - \tfrac{\pi}{2}) = -\cos 2\theta\) and \(\sin 2(\theta - \tfrac{\pi}{2}) = -\sin 2\theta\), the components of the rotated field are

\[\begin{aligned} \sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} &= \frac{\tau}{2}\Big(1 - \frac{a^2}{r^2}\Big) - \frac{\tau}{2}\Big(1 - \frac{4a^2}{r^2} + \frac{3a^4}{r^4}\Big)\cos 2\theta , \\ \sigma_{\theta\theta} &= \frac{\tau}{2}\Big(1 + \frac{a^2}{r^2}\Big) + \frac{\tau}{2}\Big(1 + \frac{3a^4}{r^4}\Big)\cos 2\theta , \\ \sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta} &= \frac{\tau}{2}\Big(1 + \frac{2a^2}{r^2} - \frac{3a^4}{r^4}\Big)\sin 2\theta . \end{aligned} \tag{11.92}\]
Corollary 11.58 A hole in a biaxial and a shear field (luknja v dvoosnem in strižnem polju)

Let the plate be as in theorem 11.57. Then:

  1. The field (11.87), added to the field (11.92) for \(\tau = \sigma_0\), has a free edge of the hole and at infinity the stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} \to \sigma_0\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} \to \sigma_0\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} \to 0\) (biaxial tension). Its stresses are \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = \sigma_0\,(1 - a^2/r^2)\), \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = \sigma_0\,(1 + a^2/r^2)\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = 0\). On the edge of the hole \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}(a, \theta) = 2\sigma_0\) for every \(\theta\), so \(K_{\mathrm{t}} = 2\).
  2. The field (11.87), added to the field (11.92) for \(\tau = -\sigma_0\), has a free edge of the hole and at infinity the stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} \to \sigma_0\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} \to -\sigma_0\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} \to 0\) (pure shear \(\sigma_0\) on planes rotated by \(45^\circ\)). Its stresses are \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = \sigma_0\,(1 - 4a^2/r^2 + 3a^4/r^4)\cos 2\theta\), \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = -\sigma_0\,(1 + 3a^4/r^4)\cos 2\theta\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = -\sigma_0\,(1 + 2a^2/r^2 - 3a^4/r^4)\sin 2\theta\). On the edge of the hole \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}(a, \theta) = -4\sigma_0\cos 2\theta\), the largest value is \(4\sigma_0\) at \(\theta = \pm\pi/2\), so \(K_{\mathrm{t}} = 4\).
Proof

We must show that the sum satisfies the conditions of theorem 11.57 with the second condition at infinity. The idea is linearity.

  1. The field (11.92) is a solution: under the replacement \(\theta \to \theta - \pi/2\) the polar equations from lemma 11.50 and the condition \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(a, \cdot) = \sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}(a, \cdot) = 0\) are preserved, because \(\theta\) does not appear in them explicitly. The displacements rotate in the same way, so they stay single-valued. At infinity the uniform field \(\sigma_0\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\otimes\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) is rotated into \(\tau\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\otimes\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) (theorem 2.53).
  2. The equations of equilibrium, Hooke's law and compatibility are linear and the edge of the hole is free for both fields, so the sum of the solutions is a solution of this problem with the sum of the data at infinity (the idea of corollary 11.3, without uniqueness, because the plate is infinite). For \(\tau = \sigma_0\) (biaxial tension) the terms with \(\cos 2\theta\) and \(\sin 2\theta\) cancel and the terms without the angle add: \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = 2\cdot\tfrac{\sigma_0}{2}(1 - a^2/r^2)\), \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = 2\cdot\tfrac{\sigma_0}{2}(1 + a^2/r^2)\). For \(\tau = -\sigma_0\) (pure shear) the terms without the angle cancel and the terms with \(\cos 2\theta\) and \(\sin 2\theta\) add.
  3. At \(r = a\) by (11.88) and (11.92) \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = \sigma_0\,(1 - 2\cos 2\theta) + \tau\,(1 + 2\cos 2\theta)\). For \(\tau = \sigma_0\) this is \(2\sigma_0\), for \(\tau = -\sigma_0\) it is \(-4\sigma_0\cos 2\theta\).
  4. The tensor \(\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_0, -\sigma_0)\) in axes rotated by \(45^\circ\) has by theorem 2.53 normal components \(0\) and a shear component of absolute value \(\sigma_0\). This is pure shear.

Source: SADD, §8.4.2 and §8.4.3, (8.4.9), (8.4.10), (8.4.18), PDF pp. 191 to 192 and 195 to 196.

Why is biaxial tension more favourable than uniaxial, and shear less favourable? Look at the end of the vertical diameter, \(\theta = 90^\circ\). The pull in the direction \(x\) gives \(3\sigma_0\) there, and the pull \(\tau\) in the direction \(y\) gives \(\tau\,(1 + 2\cos 180^\circ) = -\tau\). For \(\tau = \sigma_0\) the sum is \(2\sigma_0\): the pull in the direction \(y\) lowers the stress there, because at the end of the horizontal diameter it has its concentration, and at the end of the vertical diameter compression. For \(\tau = -\sigma_0\) the sum is \(3\sigma_0 + \sigma_0 = 4\sigma_0\), because the compression in the direction \(y\) raises the stress there. SADD remarks that an additional pull in the other direction lowers the concentration (PDF p. 194). Figure 11.135 shows the hoop stress for all three fields. On the left the arrows pull only in the direction \(x\), and the orange curve has \(3\sigma_0\) at the ends of the vertical diameter. In the middle the arrows pull in both directions, so the orange curve is a circle: the hoop stress is \(2\sigma_0\) everywhere. On the right the arrows pull in the direction \(x\) and push in the direction \(y\). The orange curve there has \(4\sigma_0\) at the ends of the vertical diameter, and at the ends of the horizontal one it reaches deep into the hole, down to \(-4\sigma_0\).

The field of biaxial tension from item 1, the sum of the fields (11.87) and (11.92) at \(\tau = \sigma_0\), is the field of the Lamé tube from theorem 11.28 at \(p_{\mathrm{a}} = 0\) and \(p_{\mathrm{b}} = -\sigma_0\) (tensile pressure), when the outer radius \(b \to \infty\): the constants (11.37) are then \(A \to \sigma_0\) and \(B \to \sigma_0\,a^2\) and the stresses (11.39) are \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = \sigma_0\,(1 - a^2/r^2)\), \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = \sigma_0\,(1 + a^2/r^2)\). This agrees with item 1 and is the path by which SADD derives this field (PDF p. 191).

11.13.6 The finite plate

The Kirsch field is a solution for the infinite plate. The plate from the introduction is \(200 \times 200\) mm with a hole of radius \(a = 10\) mm, so the middle of the edge is \(10\) radii from the centre. What error do we make if we take the plate to be infinite?

The real plate has on the edge \(x = \pm 100\) mm a force per unit area \(\sigma_0\) in the direction \(x\) and \(0\) in the direction \(y\), and on the edge \(y = \pm 100\) mm it has \(0\). We compute the Kirsch field at the points of the edge by (11.87) and rotate it into Cartesian components (theorem 2.53, rbe, part I):

edge component real plate Kirsch field
\(x = \pm 100\) mm \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) \(50\) MPa from \(48{,}758\) to \(50{,}248\) MPa
\(x = \pm 100\) mm \(\lvert\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\rvert\) \(0\) up to \(0{,}574\) MPa
\(y = \pm 100\) mm \(\lvert\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\rvert\) \(0\) up to \(0{,}743\) MPa
\(y = \pm 100\) mm \(\lvert\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\rvert\) \(0\) up to \(0{,}291\) MPa

The largest deviation is therefore \(50 - 48{,}758 = 1{,}242\) MPa, which is \(2{,}5\) % of the stress \(\sigma_0\), on the left and right edges. Why exactly this much? The middle of the edge \(x = 100\) mm is on the \(x\) axis at \(r = 10a\) and (11.91) gives \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_0\,\big(1 - \tfrac52\cdot 10^{-2} + \tfrac32\cdot 10^{-4}\big) = 0{,}97515\,\sigma_0 = 48{,}758\) MPa. The largest deviation is therefore the leading term of the disturbance \(-\tfrac52(a/r)^2\) at \(r = 10a\). Figure 11.136 shows the forces on the edge that the Kirsch field requires, with the deviation enlarged \(20\) times. On the square plate with the hole at its centre the arrows are drawn at points of the edge \(40\) mm apart, and the blue arrow is the scale \(\sigma_0 = 50\) MPa. On the left and right edges the real load would make all arrows horizontal and as long as the blue one. In the middle of these edges the arrow is shorter, because the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 48{,}758\) MPa is smallest there. Nearer the corners the arrows are tilted, because the shear stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) acts there. On the top and bottom edges the real load would have no arrows. The Kirsch field requires a small pull in the middle of these edges and a small push near the corners.

What does this say about the real plate? The Kirsch field solves the problem of the \(200 \times 200\) mm plate with an edge load that differs from the real one by \(\Delta\bar{\vek t}\), and this is at most \(2{,}5\) % of \(\sigma_0\) in absolute value. If the problem of the plate with the edge load \(-\Delta\bar{\vek t}\) and a free hole has a solution (existence is not proved in the book, section 11.1), the real solution is the sum of the Kirsch field and this solution by corollary 11.3, and by theorem 11.4 it is the only one (the finite plate is a bounded body). The load \(\Delta\bar{\vek t}\) is self-equilibrated: the Kirsch field is in equilibrium in a plate with a free hole, so the forces on the outer edge have resultant and moment zero, and the same holds for two opposite uniform pulls. By the principle of Saint-Venant (section 11.11) we expect the correction at the hole, which is \(10\) radii away from the edge, to be small. We do not compute the correction in the interior, we computed only the error of the load on the edge.

SADD writes that the solution of the infinite plate is a good approximation for finite plates with edges that are more than about five radii from the centre of the hole (PDF p. 196). Our numbers complement this rule: at \(5a\) the deviation is still \(9{,}8\) % on the \(x\) axis and \(2{,}2\) % on the \(y\) axis, and at \(10a\) it is \(2{,}5\) % on the \(x\) axis. The rule of five radii therefore holds for the order of magnitude of the error and not for accuracy below a few per cent.

11.13.7 What we have shown

The question was how large the stress at the hole is and how far the hole disturbs the plate. With the Airy function in polar coordinates we built a field that has a free hole and a uniform pull at infinity: the function of the uniform field, three biharmonic functions that decay, and three equations from the free edge. The field is constructed and checked, but not proved to be the only one. The hoop stress on the edge of the hole is \(\sigma_0\,(1 - 2\cos 2\theta)\) with the largest value \(3\sigma_0 = 150\) MPa and the smallest \(-50\) MPa, and the disturbance decays like \((a/r)^2\), on the \(y\) axis to \(2{,}2\) % at \(5a\) and on the \(x\) axis to \(9{,}8\) % at \(5a\). We looked at the concentration from three sides: the flow of force is squeezed next to the hole to half its width, the piece of the edge at the top of the oval stretches by \(3\sigma_0/E\), and \(3\sigma_0\) is the sum of \(\sigma_0\) from the uniform field and \(2\sigma_0\) from the disturbance. The stresses do not depend on \(E\) and \(\nu\). A hole in a biaxial field gives \(2\sigma_0\), in a shear field \(4\sigma_0\). The \(200 \times 200\) mm plate deviates from the infinite one by at most \(2{,}5\) % of the stress \(\sigma_0\) on the edge, and the first yield is at \(\sigma_0 = 73{,}33\) MPa.
