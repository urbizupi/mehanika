Chapter 10 Vibration of systems with several degrees of freedom and modal analysis (nihanje sistemov z več prostostnimi stopnjami in modalna analiza)

10.8 The mass matrix of a bar element (masna matrika končnega elementa palice)

Chapter 7 cut the bar into elements and built its stiffness matrix \(\mat K\) from them. But stiffness alone does not determine the frequency of vibration. For the system with one degree of freedom of chapter 9 we had \(\omega = \sqrt{k/m}\), so the mass matters as much as the stiffness. Where is the mass in a model made of elements? And how close are the frequencies of the cut bar to those of the true bar? We can answer the second question exactly, because the uniform bar fixed at one end has the frequencies of the cut bar in closed form.

The model is the same as in chapter 7. The bar has constant cross-section area \(A\), constant density \(\rho\) and constant axial stiffness \(EA\), its cross-sections move only along the axis, and the displacements are small. We divide it into elements with two nodes (definition 7.1) and allow only motions for which the displacement field is piecewise linear at every instant,

\[u_{\mathrm{h}}(x, t) = \sum_{\mathrm{I}} d_{\mathrm{I}}(t)\,\psi_{\mathrm{I}}(x) ,\]

where \(\psi_{\mathrm{I}}\) are the hat functions of section 6.7.1 and \(d_{\mathrm{I}}(t)\) are the nodal displacements, which change with time. This is an idealisation: the true bar has infinitely many degrees of freedom, our model only as many as there are nodes, so it is a system with \(n\) degrees of freedom as treated by theorem 10.3. The assumption of a piecewise linear field is good when the true shape of vibration curves only a little over one element, that is, when there are many elements per period of the shape. It fails for shapes that bend noticeably over an element: with two elements the first frequency of our bar will be wrong by \(2{,}6\) % and the second by \(19{,}5\) % (we compute both in section 10.8.5, where we also show that the error falls with the square of the element length). Its potential energy is \(V = \tfrac12\,\vek d^\T\mat K\,\vek d\), the energy of the field \(u_{\mathrm{h}}\) by point 3 of theorem 7.13. The kinetic energy must be a quadratic form in the nodal velocities, \(T = \tfrac12\,\dot{\vek d}^\T\mat M\,\dot{\vek d}\), and the matrix \(\mat M\) is what we are looking for. By definition 8.88 the kinetic energy of a body is \(\tfrac12\int\rho\,|\vek v|^2\,\mathrm{d}V\). For a bar whose cross-sections all move along the axis, the velocity of the points of a cross-section equals \(\dot u(x, t)\) (the dot denotes the derivative with respect to time), and the energy is \(\tfrac12\int_0^L\rho A\,\dot u^2\,\mathrm{d}x\).

10.8.1 The mass of an element (masa elementa)

Take one element \([x_{\mathrm{a}}, x_{\mathrm{b}}]\) of length \(h\). By equation (7.3) its field is \(u_{\mathrm{h}} = \mat\psi_{\mathrm{e}}(x)\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}(t)\). The shape functions are functions of position: \(\psi_1^e\) falls from \(1\) at the left node to \(0\) at the right one, \(\psi_2^e\) rises from \(0\) to \(1\). The nodal displacements are functions of time. The velocity of a point of the element is therefore \(\dot u_{\mathrm{h}} = \mat\psi_{\mathrm{e}}\,\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}\), a linear combination of the nodal velocities with the same weights as for the displacement. What about the kinetic energy? It contains the square of the velocity, hence products of the shape functions: \((\psi_1^e)^2\), \(\psi_1^e\psi_2^e\) and \((\psi_2^e)^2\). The areas under them will be the entries of the mass matrix.

Lemma 10.47 Consistent mass matrix of a bar element (konsistentna masna matrika elementa palice)

Let \([x_{\mathrm{a}}, x_{\mathrm{b}}]\) be a finite element of a bar of length \(h\) (definition 7.1), with constant area \(A\) and density \(\rho\), and let \(u_{\mathrm{h}}(x, t) = \mat\psi_{\mathrm{e}}(x)\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}(t)\) be the field on it, where the nodal displacements \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}(t)\) are functions of time. The kinetic energy of the element \(T_{\mathrm{e}} = \tfrac12\int_{\mathrm{x}_\mathrm{a}}^{x_{\mathrm{b}}}\rho A\,\dot u_{\mathrm{h}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) is

\[T_{\mathrm{e}} = \tfrac12\,\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\,\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}} , \qquad \mat m_{\mathrm{e}} = \int_{\mathrm{x}_\mathrm{a}}^{x_{\mathrm{b}}}\mat\psi_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,\rho A\,\mat\psi_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{\rho A h}{6}\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} . \tag{10.64}\]

The matrix \(\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\) is symmetric and positive definite and \(\vek 1^\T\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek 1 = \rho A h\), the mass of the element.

Proof

We must show that the kinetic energy is a quadratic form with this matrix, and compute its four entries.

  1. The shape functions are functions of position, so differentiation with respect to time gives \(\dot u_{\mathrm{h}} = \mat\psi_{\mathrm{e}}\,\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}\), with the same weights \(\psi_1^e\) and \(\psi_2^e\) as for the displacement. The number \(\dot u_{\mathrm{h}}\) equals its transpose, so by lemma 1.102 \(\dot u_{\mathrm{h}}^2 = \dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat\psi_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat\psi_{\mathrm{e}}\,\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}\).
  2. The column \(\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}\) does not depend on \(x\), so by the linearity of the integral (lemma 6.10) we take it out of the integral and get \(T_{\mathrm{e}} = \tfrac12\,\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\big(\int\rho A\,\mat\psi_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat\psi_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mathrm{d}x\big)\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}\).
  3. With \(s = x - x_{\mathrm{a}}\) we have \(\psi_1^e = 1 - s/h\) and \(\psi_2^e = s/h\) (equation (7.2)). The first integral, the area under \((\psi_1^e)^2\), is \(\int_0^h (1 - s/h)^2\,\mathrm{d}s = h/3\), because \(-\tfrac{h}{3}(1 - s/h)^3\) has this derivative. The second, the area under \((\psi_2^e)^2\), is \(\int_0^h s^2/h^2\,\mathrm{d}s = h/3\). The mixed one, the area under the product \(\psi_1^e\psi_2^e\), is \(\int_0^h (1 - s/h)(s/h)\,\mathrm{d}s = h/2 - h/3 = h/6\). It is smaller because the product is zero at both nodes and only \(\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 = \tfrac14\) at the middle.
  4. Multiplication by \(\rho A\) gives \(\mat m_{\mathrm{e}} = \rho A h\begin{pmatrix} 1/3 & 1/6 \\ 1/6 & 1/3 \end{pmatrix} = \tfrac{\rho A h}{6}\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\). The symmetry is visible.
  5. For \(\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}} = (a, b)\) we have \(\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\,\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}} = \tfrac{\rho A h}{6}\,(2a^2 + 2ab + 2b^2) = \tfrac{\rho A h}{6}\,\big(a^2 + b^2 + (a + b)^2\big)\), which is positive for \((a, b) \ne (0, 0)\).
  6. The sum of all four entries is \(\tfrac{\rho A h}{6}(2 + 1 + 1 + 2) = \rho A h\). Step 1 gives the same: for \(\dot d_1 = \dot d_2 = v\) we have \(\dot u_{\mathrm{h}} = v\) everywhere by lemma 7.3, so \(T_{\mathrm{e}} = \tfrac12\,\rho A h\,v^2\).

Source: COOK (11.3-4) (PDF p. 392), CK (14.8) (PDF p. 439), GR (PDF p. 383-384): the matrix is \(\tfrac{m}{6}\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\) with mass \(m = \rho A h\), in COOK for lateral displacement, in CK for axial. GR calls the matrix consistent ("consistent"), because it follows from the same approximation of displacements as the stiffness matrix.

The proof showed three things. The matrix is not diagonal, because the field of the element couples the nodes: the off-diagonal entry is the area under the product of the two shape functions, and this is not zero, because a point in the middle of the element moves whenever either node moves. The acceleration of one node therefore also pulls the mass near the other. It is positive definite, because the kinetic energy is positive for every motion that is not rest. And the sum of its entries is the mass, because the motion of the element as a whole gives the true kinetic energy. It is called consistent because it uses the same shape functions \(\mat\psi_{\mathrm{e}}\) as the stiffness matrix \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) of lemma 7.5, which is the same integral but with derivatives.

We test it on a steel bar that will run through the whole section (\(E = 196\) GPa, \(\rho = 7800\) kg/m\(^3\), \(A = 100\) mm\(^2\), length \(L = 1\) m, density per length \(\rho A = 0{,}78\) kg/m). An element with \(h = 0{,}25\) m has mass \(\rho A h = 0{,}195\) kg and \(\mat m_{\mathrm{e}} = 0{,}0325\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\) kg. At nodal velocities \((1, 1)\) m/s the element moves as a whole and \(T_{\mathrm{e}} = \tfrac12\cdot 0{,}0325\cdot 6 = 0{,}0975\) J, as \(\tfrac12\cdot 0{,}195\cdot 1^2\). At velocities \((1, -1)\) m/s, when the two ends move towards each other, the velocity line falls from \(1\) to \(-1\) and crosses zero at the middle of the element. Then \(T_{\mathrm{e}} = \tfrac12\cdot 0{,}0325\,(2 - 2 + 2) = 0{,}0325\) J, a third of the previous value, because the squared velocity equals \(1\) only at the nodes and falls to zero towards the middle, and the area under this parabola is \(h/3\) instead of \(h\). We shall need this at once.

10.8.2 Lumped mass (zbrana masa)

An older and simpler idea is to divide the mass of the element between the nodes, as if we placed a point mass at each end.

Definition 10.48 Lumped mass of an element (zbrana masa elementa)

Let \([x_{\mathrm{a}}, x_{\mathrm{b}}]\) be an element of a bar of length \(h\), area \(A\) and density \(\rho\), with mass \(m = \rho A h\). The lumped mass matrix (zbrana masna matrika) of the element is the diagonal matrix of order 2

\[\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}^{\mathrm{z}} = \frac{\rho A h}{2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} , \tag{10.65}\]

that is, a point mass \(m/2\) at each end. It has the same total mass, \(\vek 1^\T\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}^{\mathrm{z}}\,\vek 1 = \rho A h\), and the same centre of mass (definition 8.81): two equal masses at the ends have their centre of mass at the middle of the element, like the uniform bar.

Source: COOK (11.3-1) (PDF p. 391): "Particle lumping places a particle of mass m/2 at each node" and \([\mathbf m] = \tfrac{m}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\).

The lumped mass is not the kinetic energy of the field \(u_{\mathrm{h}}\) of lemma 10.47, but of two point masses that move with the nodal velocities. At the velocities \((1, -1)\) m/s of the element above, its kinetic energy is \(\tfrac12\cdot 0{,}0975\cdot 2 = 0{,}0975\) J, three times the consistent \(0{,}0325\) J. The consistent one has less because the field on the element crosses zero between the nodes and the middle of the element is at rest, while the lumped one does not know this: both halves of the element move with the full velocity of the end. The lumped mass is therefore exact when the element moves as a whole, and the worse the more the nodal velocities differ. On the frequencies it acts in the opposite direction to the consistent one: with four elements the first frequency of our bar will be \(0{,}64\) % too low instead of too high (section 10.8.5). How large is the difference of the energies in general?

Lemma 10.49 Lumped mass never gives less kinetic energy (zbrana masa ne da manj kinetične energije)

For the bar element of definition 10.48

\[\begin{aligned} &\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}^{\mathrm{z}} - \mat m_{\mathrm{e}} = \frac{\rho A h}{6}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} , \\ &\qquad \dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\big(\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}^{\mathrm{z}} - \mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\big) \\ &\quad \dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}} = \frac{\rho A h}{6}\,(\dot d_1 - \dot d_2)^2 \ge 0 . \end{aligned} \tag{10.66}\]

with equality exactly when \(\dot d_1 = \dot d_2\), that is, when the element is at rest or moves as a whole. The difference matrix is positive semidefinite (definition 7.15).

Proof

We must show the difference of the matrices and its quadratic form.

  1. Diagonal: \(\tfrac{\rho A h}{2} - \tfrac{2\rho A h}{6} = \tfrac{\rho A h}{6}\). Off the diagonal: \(0 - \tfrac{\rho A h}{6}\). This is the first equality.
  2. The quadratic form of the matrix \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\) is \(a^2 - 2ab + b^2 = (a - b)^2\) for \((a, b) = (\dot d_1, \dot d_2)\).
  3. The square of a real number is not negative and is zero exactly when the number is zero.

Source: Not from a source.

The picture shows the source as a difference of two areas. The kinetic energy of the consistent mass is \(\tfrac12\rho A\int\dot u_{\mathrm{h}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x\), the area under the curve \(\dot u_{\mathrm{h}}^2\), which for linear \(\dot u_{\mathrm{h}}\) is a parabola. The kinetic energy of the lumped masses is \(\tfrac12\rho A\) times the area of the trapezoid spanned by the chord between the points \((x_{\mathrm{a}}, \dot d_1^2)\) and \((x_{\mathrm{b}}, \dot d_2^2)\). The chord lies above the convex parabola, and the notch between them, a parabolic segment, is the difference from the lemma (figure 10.71).

10.8.3 The assembled mass matrix (sestavljena masna matrika)

We assemble the element matrices into the matrix of the bar as we did the stiffness matrices in theorem 7.13. The mesh \(0 = x_0 < \dots < x_{\mathrm{n}} = L\) and the connectivity table are the same (definitions 7.10 and 7.11), element \(e\) has mass \(\rho_{\mathrm{e}} A_{\mathrm{e}} h_{\mathrm{e}}\) and \(\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\) from equation (10.64) with \(\rho_{\mathrm{e}} A_{\mathrm{e}} h_{\mathrm{e}}\) in place of \(\rho A h\).

Lemma 10.50 Mass matrix of a bar (masna matrika palice)

Let the bar be divided into \(n\) elements, \(\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}\) the selection matrices (definition 7.11) and

\[\mat M = \sum_{\mathrm{e}=1}^{n}\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}} , \qquad \mat M^{\mathrm{z}} = \sum_{\mathrm{e}=1}^{n}\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}^{\mathrm{z}}\,\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}} . \tag{10.67}\]

Then for every field \(u_{\mathrm{h}} = \sum_{\mathrm{I}} d_{\mathrm{I}}(t)\,\psi_{\mathrm{I}}(x)\) we have \(\int_0^L\rho A\,\dot u_{\mathrm{h}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x = \dot{\vek d}^\T\mat M\,\dot{\vek d}\), hence \(T = \tfrac12\,\dot{\vek d}^\T\mat M\,\dot{\vek d}\). The matrix \(\mat M\) is symmetric and positive definite, and

\[\dot{\vek d}^\T\big(\mat M^{\mathrm{z}} - \mat M\big)\dot{\vek d} = \sum_{\mathrm{e}=1}^{n}\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{e}} A_{\mathrm{e}} h_{\mathrm{e}}}{6}\,\big(\dot d_1^e - \dot d_2^e\big)^2 \ge 0 . \tag{10.68}\]

The matrix \(\mat M^{\mathrm{z}}\) is diagonal, its entry at node \(I\) is half the mass of all elements that have this node. For a uniform bar with elements of length \(h\) we have \(\mat M = \tfrac{\rho A h}{6}\,\mathrm{tridiag}(1, 4, 1)\) (a tridiagonal matrix with \(1\) below and above the diagonal and \(4\) on it) with the number \(2\) at both ends of the diagonal (nodes \(0\) and \(n\)) and \(\mat M^{\mathrm{z}} = \rho A h\,\mathrm{diag}(\tfrac12, 1, \dots, 1, \tfrac12)\).

Proof

We must show that the kinetic energy splits over the elements as the potential energy did, and that the lumped masses assemble in the same way.

  1. By steps 4 and 5 of the proof of theorem 7.13, \(u_{\mathrm{h}}\) on element \(e\) equals \(\mat\psi_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) with \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d\) (lemma 7.12, point 1). The matrix \(\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}\) does not depend on time, so \(\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}\,\dot{\vek d}\).
  2. The integral over \([0, L]\) is the sum of the integrals over the elements (lemma 6.10), each equal to \(\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\,\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}\) by lemma 10.47. By lemma 1.102, \(\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\,\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}} = \dot{\vek d}^\T\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}\,\dot{\vek d}\). The sum over \(e\) gives \(\dot{\vek d}^\T\mat M\,\dot{\vek d}\).
  3. Symmetry: as in step 1 of the proof of corollary 7.14, because \(\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\) is symmetric.
  4. Positive definiteness: let \(\dot{\vek d}^\T\mat M\,\dot{\vek d} = 0\). By step 2, \(\int_0^L\rho A\,\dot u_{\mathrm{h}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\), the integrand is continuous on every element and non-negative, so by lemma 6.10 (point 3) it is zero everywhere. The field \(\dot u_{\mathrm{h}}\) at node \(x_{\mathrm{I}}\) equals \(\dot d_{\mathrm{I}}\), because \(\psi_{\mathrm{J}}(x_{\mathrm{I}}) = 1\) for \(J = I\) and \(0\) otherwise, so \(\dot{\vek d} = \vek 0\).
  5. Equation (10.68): the same procedure as in step 2, applied to the quadratic form of the matrix \(\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}^{\mathrm{z}} - \mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\) of lemma 10.49, with \(\dot d_1^e\) and \(\dot d_2^e\) as the components of the column \(\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}\,\dot{\vek d}\).
  6. By point 2 of lemma 7.12, \(\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}^{\mathrm{z}}\,\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}\) has entry \((I, J)\) equal to \((\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}^{\mathrm{z}})_{\mathrm{ij}}\) for \(I = g_{\mathrm{ei}}\), \(J = g_{\mathrm{ej}}\) and zero otherwise. Because \(\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}^{\mathrm{z}}\) is diagonal, only the entries \(I = J\) remain, and entry \(I\) of the sum is the sum of the halves of the masses of the elements with node \(I\).
  7. Uniform bar: an interior node belongs to two elements, so on the diagonal the areas \(h/3\) of the left and of the right element add up, \(\mat M_{\mathrm{II}} = 2\cdot\tfrac{2\rho A h}{6} = \tfrac{4\rho A h}{6}\), and \(\mat M^{\mathrm{z}}_{\mathrm{II}} = 2\cdot\tfrac{\rho A h}{2}\). The end nodes belong to one element, so \(\tfrac{2\rho A h}{6}\) and \(\tfrac{\rho A h}{2}\). Neighbouring nodes share one element and with it one area \(h/6\), so \(\mat M_{\mathrm{I},\mathrm{I}+1} = \tfrac{\rho A h}{6}\), and elsewhere off the diagonal zero.

Source: COOK §2.5, (11.2-6) (PDF p. 390, text on p. 391: the consistent mass matrix is assembled like the stiffness matrix, "conceptual expansion of element matrices to structure size and summation of overlapping terms"). The proof is not from the source.

We fix the bar at \(x = 0\) as in section 7.3: node \(0\) is at rest for all time, \(d_0 \equiv 0\) and \(\dot d_0 \equiv 0\). In the kinetic energy only the block of free nodes remains, \(T = \tfrac12\,\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{f}}^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{ff}}\,\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{f}}\), and in the potential energy \(V = \tfrac12\,\vek d_{\mathrm{f}}^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{ff}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{f}}\). By theorem 10.3 and definition 10.13 the eigenproblem and the vibration here are those of the pair \((\mat K_{\mathrm{ff}}, \mat M_{\mathrm{ff}})\). From now on we write both blocks without the index \(\mathrm{ff}\) and speak of the matrices \(\mat K\) and \(\mat M\) of order \(n\) for the unknowns \(d_1, \dots, d_{\mathrm{n}}\). The blocks are the matrices of the lemma with the row and column of node \(0\) removed. The block \(\mat M_{\mathrm{ff}}\) is positive definite, because its quadratic form is the quadratic form of the matrix \(\mat M\) at \(\dot d_0 = 0\).

10.8.4 A bar in the plane (palica v ravnini)

The truss of chapter 7 has bars in different directions. The stiffness matrix of a bar in global axes required a rotation (lemma 7.28), because the bar resists only axial extension. What about the mass? A bar that is joined to the nodes by pins has mass also in transverse motion: the motion of its middle in the transverse direction carries kinetic energy, although the stiffness does not oppose it. So we interpolate the displacement field everywhere in the plane, not only along the axis, linearly: the displacement of a point of the element is the vector \(\vek u_{\mathrm{h}} = \psi_1^e\,\vek u_{\mathrm{i}} + \psi_2^e\,\vek u_{\mathrm{j}}\) with the displacements of the ends \(\vek u_{\mathrm{i}}\), \(\vek u_{\mathrm{j}}\) (each with two components).

Lemma 10.51 Mass matrix of a bar in the plane (masna matrika palice v ravnini)

Let a straight bar of length \(L\), area \(A\) and density \(\rho\) lie between nodes \(i\) and \(j\), and let \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = (\vek u_{\mathrm{i}}, \vek u_{\mathrm{j}})\) be the column of four components of the end displacements in two perpendicular axes. The kinetic energy of the bar with linear interpolation of both components is \(T_{\mathrm{e}} = \tfrac12\,\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\,\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}\) with

\[\mat m_{\mathrm{e}} = \frac{\rho A L}{6}\begin{pmatrix} 2\mat I & \mat I \\ \mat I & 2\mat I \end{pmatrix} , \tag{10.69}\]

where \(\mat I\) is the \(2 \times 2\) matrix. The same matrix holds in every pair of perpendicular axes: under a rotation of the axes with the rotation matrix \(\mat R = \begin{pmatrix} \mat Q & \mat 0 \\ \mat 0 & \mat Q \end{pmatrix}\) (lemma 7.29) we have \(\mat R\,\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mat R^\T = \mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\). So no rotation is needed, in contrast to the stiffness matrix.

Proof

We must show that each component follows the lemma on the element and that the matrix does not change under a rotation.

  1. The kinetic energy is \(\tfrac12\int\rho A\,|\dot{\vek u}_{\mathrm{h}}|^2\,\mathrm{d}s\) along the axis of the bar (definition 8.88). The scalar product of the vector \(\dot{\vek u}_{\mathrm{h}} = \psi_1\,\dot{\vek u}_{\mathrm{i}} + \psi_2\,\dot{\vek u}_{\mathrm{j}}\) with itself is \(\psi_1^2\,\dot{\vek u}_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot\dot{\vek u}_{\mathrm{i}} + 2\psi_1\psi_2\,\dot{\vek u}_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot\dot{\vek u}_{\mathrm{j}} + \psi_2^2\,\dot{\vek u}_{\mathrm{j}}\cdot\dot{\vek u}_{\mathrm{j}}\).
  2. By step 3 of the proof of lemma 10.47 the integrals \(\int\rho A\psi_{\mathrm{a}}\psi_{\mathrm{b}}\,\mathrm{d}s\) equal \(\tfrac{\rho A L}{3}\) for \(a = b\) and \(\tfrac{\rho A L}{6}\) for \(a \ne b\). Hence \(T_{\mathrm{e}} = \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{\rho A L}{6}\big(2\,\dot{\vek u}_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot\dot{\vek u}_{\mathrm{i}} + 2\,\dot{\vek u}_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot\dot{\vek u}_{\mathrm{j}} + 2\,\dot{\vek u}_{\mathrm{j}}\cdot\dot{\vek u}_{\mathrm{j}}\big)\).
  3. The scalar product \(\dot{\vek u}_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot\dot{\vek u}_{\mathrm{j}}\) is \(\dot{\vek u}_{\mathrm{i}}^\T\mat I\,\dot{\vek u}_{\mathrm{j}}\), which by the rule for the product of block matrices (definition 1.99) is equation (10.69).
  4. The local column is \(\bar{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat R\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\), so \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat R^\T\bar{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}\) and by lemma 1.102 \(T_{\mathrm{e}} = \tfrac12\,\dot{\bar{\vek d}}_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,(\mat R\,\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mat R^\T)\,\dot{\bar{\vek d}}_{\mathrm{e}}\).
  5. By the rule for the product of block matrices, \(\mat R\,\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mat R^\T = \tfrac{\rho A L}{6}\begin{pmatrix} 2\mat Q\mat Q^\T & \mat Q\mat Q^\T \\ \mat Q\mat Q^\T & 2\mat Q\mat Q^\T \end{pmatrix}\), and \(\mat Q\mat Q^\T = \mat I\) (definition 2.46), which gives \(\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\).

Source: COOK figure 11.3-1 (PDF p. 392) shows the linear field of lateral displacements of a two-node element as the origin of the consistent matrix and says that the lumped matrix holds "whether displacement is axial or lateral" (PDF p. 391). The proof is not from the source.

The matrix of a bar is the same in all directions, because mass does not resist in one direction only: the inertia of a point is the same in every direction of displacement, while the bar resists rigidly only displacement along its axis. The assembled matrix of a truss is obtained as in lemma 10.50: at a node the blocks of the bars that have it are added, and each bar contributes \(\tfrac{\rho A L}{6}\,2\mat I\) at its end.

We test it on the wall truss \(A\), \(B\), \(C\) of section 7.5.2 (\(EA = 19{,}6\) MN, \(A = 100\) mm\(^2\), \(\rho = 7800\) kg/m\(^3\)) with a weight at node \(C\). Our model: at node \(C\) there is a point mass \(m = 1000\) kg (its weight \(9{,}81\) kN is close to the load \(10\) kN of that section), and the bars \(AC\) (\(4\) m) and \(BC\) (\(5\) m) are massless in the main model, so \(\mat M = m\,\mat I\) and \(\mat K = \begin{pmatrix} 7{,}4088 & -1{,}8816 \\ -1{,}8816 & 1{,}4112 \end{pmatrix}\cdot 10^6\) N/m. The eigenfrequencies are \(\omega = 29{,}4919\) and \(89{,}1641\) 1/s. The bars have mass \(\rho A\,(4 + 5) = 7{,}020\) kg, which is \(0{,}7\) % of the mass \(m\). The points \(A\) and \(B\) are at rest, so at \(C\) each bar by lemma 10.50 contributes only the block \(\tfrac{2\rho A L}{6}\,\mat I = \tfrac{\rho A L}{3}\,\mat I\). For \(AC\) this is \(1{,}040\) kg, for \(BC\) \(1{,}300\) kg, together \(2{,}340\) kg. A direct argument gives the same result. A bar one end of which is at rest and the other moves with velocity \(v\) has \(\dot u_{\mathrm{h}} = v\,s/L\) and kinetic energy \(\tfrac12\int_0^L\rho A\,(v s/L)^2\,\mathrm{d}s = \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{\rho A L}{3}\,v^2\). The moving end therefore carries a third of the mass of the bar, not all of it, because most of the bar moves more slowly than the end (figure 10.73).

The matrix \(\mat M = (m + 2{,}340\ \text{kg})\,\mat I = 1002{,}34\,\mat I\) kg is still a multiple of the identity matrix, so the modes do not change (with \(\mat M = \mu\mat I\), \(\mat K\vek\phi = \lambda\mu\vek\phi\) and \(\lambda\) is smaller than before by the factor \(m/\mu\) at every mode). Both frequencies decrease by the factor \(\sqrt{1000/1002{,}34}\), that is, by \(0{,}1168\) %. Approximately this is \(\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{2{,}34}{1000} = 0{,}117\) %, because \(\omega \propto M^{-1/2}\). Lumped masses would give half the mass of each bar, \(3{,}510\) kg, and a decrease of \(0{,}175\) %. Neglecting the mass of the bars is therefore for this bracket an error of about a tenth of a percent in the frequency, and this is why we could neglect it in the main model.

10.8.5 Frequencies of a bar from finite elements (frekvence palice iz končnih elementov)

Now we have \(\mat K\) and \(\mat M\) and can ask how good their eigenfrequencies are. We must compare them with the true ones. A continuous bar (every cross-section moves separately, so infinitely many degrees of freedom), fixed at \(x = 0\) and free at \(x = L\), has by CK (8), PDF p. 408, the frequencies

\[\omega_{\mathrm{r}} = \frac{(2r - 1)\,\pi}{2L}\,c , \qquad c = \sqrt{E/\rho} , \qquad r = 1, 2, \dots , \tag{10.70}\]

and shapes \(\sin(k_{\mathrm{r}} x)\) with \(k_{\mathrm{r}} = (2r - 1)\pi/(2L)\). Since there is no force at the free end, all three have a horizontal slope \(u'(L) = 0\). The derivation is in chapter 13, here we take them as a measure. The speed \(c\) has the unit m/s, and in this chapter it is only an abbreviation. For our steel \(c = 5012{,}80\) m/s and \(f_{\mathrm{r}} = \omega_{\mathrm{r}}/2\pi = 1253{,}20\), \(3759{,}60\), \(6266{,}01\) Hz for \(r = 1, 2, 3\).

First two simple cases, to see what to expect.

We plot the frequencies on the axis \(\omega L/c\). With one element (\(n = 1\), \(h = L\)) we have \(\mat K = (EA/L)\) and consistent \(\mat M = \rho A L/3\), so \(\lambda = 3c^2/L^2\), \(\omega = \sqrt{3}\,c/L = 1{,}7321\,c/L\). The exact value is \(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\,c/L = 1{,}5708\,c/L\), an error of \(+10{,}27\) %. With the lumped mass \(\mat M^{\mathrm{z}} = \rho A L/2\), \(\lambda = 2c^2/L^2\), \(\omega = 1{,}4142\,c/L\), an error of \(-9{,}97\) %. One element thus gives an upper bound with the consistent and a lower bound with the lumped mass, each about a tenth off.

With two elements (\(n = 2\), \(h = L/2\)) the matrices are \(\mat K = \tfrac{EA}{h}\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\) and \(\mat M = \tfrac{\rho A h}{6}\begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\) (lemma 10.50). With the notation \(x = \lambda\rho h^2/(6E)\) the equation \(\det(\mat K - \lambda\mat M) = 0\) by the \(2 \times 2\) determinant (definition 2.72) is \((2 - 4x)(1 - 2x) - (1 + x)^2 = 7x^2 - 10x + 1 = 0\), so \(x = (5 \mp 3\sqrt2)/7 = 0{,}10819\) and \(1{,}32038\). The frequencies are \(f_1 = 1285{,}61\) Hz (\(+2{,}586\) %) and \(f_2 = 4491{,}13\) Hz, the exact second is \(3759{,}60\) Hz, an error of \(+19{,}46\) %. For the second mode the error with two elements is large: its shape, \(\sin(3\pi x/(2L))\), makes three quarters of a period over the length \(L\), which two elements barely capture (\(\theta_2 = 3\pi/4 = 2{,}36\)). The first mode is already good.

The following theorem gives the general answer. It says that here the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\) has eigenpairs that we can write in closed form, and that the eigenshapes are the true shapes, sampled at the nodes.

Why should samples of a sine be an eigenvector? A row of the matrix \(\mat K\) compares a node with its two neighbours: the expression \(-\phi_{\mathrm{j}-1} + 2\phi_{\mathrm{j}} - \phi_{\mathrm{j}+1}\) is twice the difference between the value at the node and the mean of the neighbours. Take three consecutive samples of a sine, \(\phi_{\mathrm{j}-1} = \sin((j-1)\theta)\), \(\phi_{\mathrm{j}} = \sin(j\theta)\) and \(\phi_{\mathrm{j}+1} = \sin((j+1)\theta)\), and join the two neighbours by a chord. The middle of the chord is the mean of the neighbours. The addition theorem says that at every \(j\) it lies at the height \(\cos\theta\,\phi_{\mathrm{j}}\), that is, closer to the axis than the middle sample by the same factor \(\cos\theta\). In the figure \(\theta = 0{,}6\) and \(\cos\theta = 0{,}825\):

Every interior row of the matrix therefore only multiplies the samples of the sine by the same number \(2 - 2\cos\theta\), and this is exactly what an eigenvector demands. The end remains. At the free end there is no node \(n + 1\), and the last row is half of an interior one exactly when the shape continues beyond the end as its own mirror image, with the imagined sample \(\phi_{\mathrm{n}+1} = \phi_{\mathrm{n}-1}\). This happens when the sine has a crest at the end, and this condition picks the angles \(\theta_{\mathrm{r}}\) of the theorem. At a wall the condition is simpler still, the sample at the wall must be zero. Section 10.8.6 treats this case.

Theorem 10.52 Frequencies of a uniform bar of finite elements (frekvence enakomerne palice iz končnih elementov)

Let a uniform bar of length \(L\) with constant \(EA\) and \(\rho A\), fixed at \(x = 0\) and free at \(x = L\), be divided into \(n\) equal elements of length \(h = L/n\), nodes \(x_{\mathrm{j}} = jh\), unknowns \(d_1, \dots, d_{\mathrm{n}}\). The matrix \(\mat K\) is \(\tfrac{EA}{h}\,\mathrm{tridiag}(-1, 2, -1)\) with \(1\) in the last place of the diagonal, \(\mat M\) and \(\mat M^{\mathrm{z}}\) are the blocks of lemma 10.50. For \(r = 1, \dots, n\) let

\[\begin{aligned} &\theta_{\mathrm{r}} = \frac{(2r - 1)\,\pi}{2n} = k_{\mathrm{r}} h , \\ &\qquad \vek\phi_{\mathrm{r}} = \big(\sin(\theta_{\mathrm{r}}),\ \sin(2\theta_{\mathrm{r}}),\ \dots, \\ &\qquad\quad \sin(n\theta_{\mathrm{r}})\big) . \end{aligned} \tag{10.71}\]

Then \(\vek\phi_{\mathrm{r}}\) is an eigenvector of the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\) and of the pair \((\mat K, \mat M^{\mathrm{z}})\), and the eigenvalues are

\[\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{k}} = \frac{6c^2}{h^2}\,\frac{1 - \cos\theta_{\mathrm{r}}}{2 + \cos\theta_{\mathrm{r}}} , \qquad \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{z}} = \frac{2c^2}{h^2}\,\big(1 - \cos\theta_{\mathrm{r}}\big) , \tag{10.72}\]

with \(c^2 = E/\rho\). Both sequences increase, \(0 < \lambda_1 < \dots < \lambda_{\mathrm{n}}\), and these are all the eigenvalues of each pair, each simple.

Proof

We must show that every row of the equation \(\mat K\vek\phi = \lambda\mat M\vek\phi\) is satisfied by the same \(\lambda\), then that the values are distinct and hence there are no more. With \(\theta = \theta_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(\phi_{\mathrm{j}} = \sin(j\theta)\) for every integer \(j\) (also \(j = 0\) and \(j = n + 1\), where there is no node) the components of \(\vek\phi_{\mathrm{r}}\) are the numbers \(\phi_1, \dots, \phi_{\mathrm{n}}\), and \(\phi_0 = 0\).

  1. For \(j \le n - 1\) row \(j\) of the matrix \(\mat K\) on \(\vek\phi_{\mathrm{r}}\) equals \(\tfrac{EA}{h}\,(-\phi_{\mathrm{j}-1} + 2\phi_{\mathrm{j}} - \phi_{\mathrm{j}+1})\). By the addition theorem (corollary 8.16 with \(\alpha = j\theta\) and \(\beta = \pm\theta\)) \(\sin(\alpha + \beta) + \sin(\alpha - \beta) = 2\sin\alpha\cos\beta\), so \(\phi_{\mathrm{j}-1} + \phi_{\mathrm{j}+1} = 2\cos\theta\,\phi_{\mathrm{j}}\): the middle of the chord between the neighbours is at the height \(\cos\theta\,\phi_{\mathrm{j}}\). The row is \(\tfrac{EA}{h}\,(2 - 2\cos\theta)\,\phi_{\mathrm{j}}\). At \(j = 1\) the term \(\phi_0 = 0\) appears, which is the removed node \(0\).
  2. Row \(j \le n - 1\) of the matrix \(\mat M\) is \(\tfrac{\rho A h}{6}\,(\phi_{\mathrm{j}-1} + 4\phi_{\mathrm{j}} + \phi_{\mathrm{j}+1}) = \tfrac{\rho A h}{6}\,(4 + 2\cos\theta)\,\phi_{\mathrm{j}}\) because \(\phi_{\mathrm{j}-1} + \phi_{\mathrm{j}+1} = 2\cos\theta\,\phi_{\mathrm{j}}\), and of the matrix \(\mat M^{\mathrm{z}}\) it is \(\rho A h\,\phi_{\mathrm{j}}\) (lemma 10.50).
  3. By steps 1 and 2 row \(j\) of the equation \(\mat K\vek\phi = \lambda\mat M\vek\phi\) is \(\tfrac{EA}{h}\,(2 - 2\cos\theta)\,\phi_{\mathrm{j}} = \lambda\,\tfrac{\rho A h}{6}\,(4 + 2\cos\theta)\,\phi_{\mathrm{j}}\). At \(\lambda = \lambda^{\mathrm{k}}\) of the theorem the coefficients in front of \(\phi_{\mathrm{j}}\) are equal, so the equation is satisfied for every \(j \le n - 1\), whatever \(\phi_{\mathrm{j}}\) is. For \(\mat M^{\mathrm{z}}\) the row is \(\tfrac{EA}{h}\,(2 - 2\cos\theta)\,\phi_{\mathrm{j}} = \lambda\,\rho A h\,\phi_{\mathrm{j}}\), which \(\lambda^{\mathrm{z}}\) satisfies in the same way.
  4. The last row \(j = n\). At \(\theta = (2r - 1)\pi/(2n)\) we have \(n\theta = (2r - 1)\pi/2\) and \(\cos(n\theta) = 0\), \(\sin(n\theta) = \pm 1\). The addition theorem gives \(\phi_{\mathrm{n}+1} = \sin(n\theta)\cos\theta + \cos(n\theta)\sin\theta = \sin(n\theta)\cos\theta\) and \(\phi_{\mathrm{n}-1} = \sin(n\theta)\cos\theta - \cos(n\theta)\sin\theta = \sin(n\theta)\cos\theta\), so \(\phi_{\mathrm{n}+1} = \phi_{\mathrm{n}-1}\).
  5. The last node \(n\) has a neighbour only on the left, so the last row of \(\mat K\) equals \(\tfrac{EA}{h}\,(-\phi_{\mathrm{n}-1} + \phi_{\mathrm{n}})\). By step 4 this is \(\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{EA}{h}\,(-\phi_{\mathrm{n}-1} + 2\phi_{\mathrm{n}} - \phi_{\mathrm{n}+1})\), half the row of step 1. The last row of \(\mat M\) is \(\tfrac{\rho A h}{6}\,(\phi_{\mathrm{n}-1} + 2\phi_{\mathrm{n}}) = \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{\rho A h}{6}\,(\phi_{\mathrm{n}-1} + 4\phi_{\mathrm{n}} + \phi_{\mathrm{n}+1})\), half the row of step 2, and the lumped \(\tfrac{\rho A h}{2}\,\phi_{\mathrm{n}}\) is half of \(\rho A h\,\phi_{\mathrm{n}}\). Both sides are halves of the same expressions, so the same \(\lambda\) solves the last row.
  6. The vector \(\vek\phi_{\mathrm{r}}\) is not zero, because \(\phi_{\mathrm{n}} = \pm 1\). So \((\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}, \vek\phi_{\mathrm{r}})\) is an eigenpair (definition 10.13) of both pairs.
  7. Increase. The function \(1 - \cos\theta\) has derivative \(\sin\theta > 0\) on \((0, \pi)\) (lemma 8.11, point 3). The quotient \(g(\theta) = (1 - \cos\theta)/(2 + \cos\theta)\) has by the quotient rule (lemma 4.109, point 3) the derivative \(\big(\sin\theta\,(2 + \cos\theta) + (1 - \cos\theta)\sin\theta\big)/(2 + \cos\theta)^2 = 3\sin\theta/(2 + \cos\theta)^2 > 0\). Both functions have a positive derivative on \((0, \pi)\), so they increase strictly: for \(a < b\) in \((0, \pi)\), by theorem 1.85, \(f(b) - f(a) = f'(\xi)\,(b - a) > 0\). The angles \(\theta_1 < \dots < \theta_{\mathrm{n}} = \pi - \pi/(2n)\) lie in \((0, \pi)\), so \(0 < \lambda_1 < \dots < \lambda_{\mathrm{n}}\).
  8. All of them. By theorem 10.21 the pair has \(n\) eigenvalues, counted with multiplicity, and by corollary 10.22 every eigenvalue of the pair equals one of them. We have \(n\) distinct eigenvalues, so these are all, each simple.

Lemma 10.53 Estimate of the cosine (ocena kosinusa)

For every \(\theta \ge 0\), \(1 - \cos\theta\) lies between \(\theta^2/2 - \theta^4/24\) and \(\theta^2/2\):

\[\frac{\theta^2}{2} - \frac{\theta^4}{24} \le 1 - \cos\theta \le \frac{\theta^2}{2} . \tag{10.74}\]
Proof

We must show both sides. We start from \(\cos s \le 1\) and integrate the inequality four times over \(s \in [0, t]\) for \(t \ge 0\). All functions are continuous, so the monotonicity of the integral holds (lemma 3.54, point 4), and we compute the integrals by the fundamental theorem (theorem 3.55, point 1) with the antiderivatives from theorem 8.13.

  1. By lemma 8.11, point 1, \(\cos s \le 1\). The integral \(\int_0^t\cos s\,\mathrm{d}s = \sin t - \sin 0 = \sin t\) is therefore at most \(\int_0^t 1\,\mathrm{d}s = t\). Hence \(\sin s \le s\) for every \(s \ge 0\).
  2. By step 1, \(\int_0^t \sin s\,\mathrm{d}s = 1 - \cos t\) (antiderivative \(-\cos s\), \(\cos 0 = 1\)) is at most \(\int_0^t s\,\mathrm{d}s = t^2/2\). This is the right inequality. Hence \(1 - \cos s \le s^2/2\) for every \(s \ge 0\).
  3. By step 2, \(\int_0^t (1 - \cos s)\,\mathrm{d}s = t - \sin t\) is at most \(\int_0^t s^2/2\,\mathrm{d}s = t^3/6\). Hence \(\sin s \ge s - s^3/6\) for every \(s \ge 0\).
  4. By step 3, \(1 - \cos t = \int_0^t\sin s\,\mathrm{d}s\) is at least \(\int_0^t (s - s^3/6)\,\mathrm{d}s = t^2/2 - t^4/24\). This is the left inequality.

Corollary 10.55 Lumped masses give lower frequencies (zbrane mase dajo nižje frekvence)

We must show the first inequality from the difference of the masses and the second from the estimate of the cosine.

  1. By equation (10.68), \(\dot{\vek d}^\T(\mat M^{\mathrm{z} - \mat M)\dot{\vek d} \ge 0\) for every column (for one element the lumped masses give more energy than the consistent one), so the difference is positive semidefinite (definition 7.15). The same holds for the block of free nodes, because its quadratic form is the quadratic form of the full matrix at \(\dot d_0 = 0\).
  2. The matrix \(\mat K\) is symmetric and positive semidefinite, because \(\vek d^\T\mat K\,\vek d = \tfrac{EA}{h}\sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^n (d_{\mathrm{j}} - d_{\mathrm{j}-1})^2 \ge 0\) with \(d_0 = 0\), and \(\mat M\) is symmetric and positive definite (lemma 10.50). By point 2 of corollary 10.46 of section 10.7, with \(\mat K' = \mat K\), \(\mat M' = \mat M^{\mathrm{z}}\) and the positive semidefinite difference \(\mat M' - \mat M\), increasing the mass raises no eigenvalue: \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{z} \le \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{k}\) for every \(r\).
  3. By lemma 10.53, \(1 - \cos\theta \le \theta^2/2\), so \(2(1 - \cos\theta) \le \theta^2\).
  4. By equation (10.72), \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{z}} = \tfrac{c^2}{h^2}\cdot 2(1 - \cos\theta_{\mathrm{r}}) \le \tfrac{c^2}{h^2}\,\theta_{\mathrm{r}}^2 = c^2k_{\mathrm{r}}^2 = \omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2\), because \(\theta_{\mathrm{r}} = k_{\mathrm{r}} h\). The lumped frequency is therefore below the exact one.

Source: GR (PDF p. 389) states the second inequality for this bar: the frequencies (4.146) (PDF p. 265) of the lumped mass model are lower than the exact ones. For the lumped mass in general COOK (PDF p. 397) says only "Lumped mass matrices usually, but not always, underestimate natural frequencies". The proof is ours.

For the consistent mass we have a reason, but no proof for every \(r\) in this chapter. By COOK (PDF pp. 396 and 397) the computed frequencies are upper bounds of the exact ones when the elements are compatible (the field is continuous at the junction of elements, as with our hat functions), the stiffness is not softened by incomplete integration and the masses are consistent. The reason is Ritz's. The quotient \(\vek d^\T\mat K\vek d/\vek d^\T\mat M\vek d\) equals, by lemma 10.50 and point 3 of theorem 7.13, the quotient of the energies of the continuous bar computed on the field \(u_{\mathrm{h}}\), and the smallest value of a quotient on a smaller set of fields is not smaller than the smallest on a larger one (for matrices this is the left inequality \(\lambda_1 \le R(\vek v)\) of theorem 10.42: on every smaller set of columns the quotient is at least \(\lambda_1\)). The statement that for the continuous bar every eigenfrequency of such a model lies above the exact one is therefore proved in chapter 13, where the Ritz method of chapter 6 is stated for eigenfrequencies. We do not use it anywhere in this chapter. GR (PDF p. 389) says that a non-consistent mass matrix may lose the bound, and COOK (PDF p. 397) that lumped masses "usually, but not always" underestimate the frequencies. 10.8.6 Both ends fixed and a chain of masses between two walls (veriga mas med stenama)

So far the right end of the bar was free. What changes if we fix both ends? The question is not only about the bar. The lumped mass model is something very familiar: a row of point masses joined by springs. A bar element without mass behaves as a spring of stiffness \(k = EA/h\), since by lemma 7.5 the forces at its nodes are proportional to the difference of the displacements, \(\tfrac{EA}{h}(d_2 - d_1)\). The lumped mass puts a point mass \(m = \rho A h\) at every interior node (lemma 10.50). Let us look at such a chain on its own, because it is the basic model of every system of equal links, and then translate it back to the bar.

We build the chain piece by piece. On the left and on the right are two walls, which are at rest. Between them are \(N\) equal masses \(m\), in the figure \(N = 4\), numbered \(j = 1, \dots, N\). They are joined by \(N + 1\) equal springs of stiffness \(k\), the first and the last hold the end masses to the walls. The coordinate \(q_{\mathrm{j}}\) is the displacement of mass \(j\) along the chain from its equilibrium position, the walls have \(q_0 = q_{\mathrm{N}+1} = 0\). The model makes three assumptions.

  1. The masses move only along the chain. This holds when a track guides them, and does not hold when the chain can bend sideways. A small transverse offset \(w\) of a mass stretches a spring of length \(a\) only by about \(w^2/(2a)\), at \(w = 0{,}01\,a\) by \(0{,}00005\,a\), so it does not change the longitudinal frequencies in the linear computation.
  2. The springs are linear, the force is proportional to the stretch. This is right for small displacements, as in theorem 10.3, and fails when a spring is compressed solid or passes its elastic limit. With a nonlinear spring the frequency depends on the amplitude, and equation (10.81) holds only in the limit of small amplitudes.
  3. The springs have no mass. This is right when the mass of a spring \(m_{\mathrm{v}}\) is small against \(m\). A spring with mass has, by lemma 10.47, the mass matrix \(\tfrac{m_{\mathrm{v}}}{6}\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\), and at \(m_{\mathrm{v}} = 0{,}02\,m\) this addition lowers the first frequency of the chain of four masses by \(0{,}92\) % (NumPy).

Now we write the equation of motion of one mass. The spring on the left of mass \(j\) is stretched by \(q_{\mathrm{j}} - q_{\mathrm{j}-1}\) and pulls the mass back with the force \(k\,(q_{\mathrm{j}} - q_{\mathrm{j}-1})\). The spring on its right is stretched by \(q_{\mathrm{j}+1} - q_{\mathrm{j}}\) and pulls it forward with the force \(k\,(q_{\mathrm{j}+1} - q_{\mathrm{j}})\). Newton's law for mass \(j\) is

\[m\,\ddot q_{\mathrm{j}} = -k\,(q_{\mathrm{j}} - q_{\mathrm{j}-1}) + k\,(q_{\mathrm{j}+1} - q_{\mathrm{j}}) = -k\,\big(-q_{\mathrm{j}-1} + 2q_{\mathrm{j}} - q_{\mathrm{j}+1}\big) . \tag{10.79}\]

The mass matrix is therefore \(\mat M = m\,\mat I\) and the stiffness matrix \(\mat K = k\,\mathrm{tridiag}(-1, 2, -1)\) of order \(N\), with the number \(2\) on the whole diagonal, because every mass, the end ones too, has a spring on both sides. In the bar with a free end the last node had an element on one side only, which is why the diagonal had \(1\) there. Here too we guess the eigenpairs: samples of a sine, only that now the sample at each wall must be zero.

Corollary 10.56 Chain of equal masses between two walls and the bar with both ends fixed (veriga enakih mas med stenama in palica z obema pritrjenima koncema)

Let \(N \ge 1\) and let \(\mat K_{\mathrm{N}} = \mathrm{tridiag}(-1, 2, -1)\) be the matrix of order \(N\) with the number \(2\) on the whole diagonal and \(-1\) just above and below it. For \(r = 1, \dots, N\) let

\[\theta_{\mathrm{r}} = \frac{r\pi}{N + 1} , \qquad \vek\phi_{\mathrm{r}} = \big(\sin\theta_{\mathrm{r}},\ \sin 2\theta_{\mathrm{r}},\ \dots,\ \sin N\theta_{\mathrm{r}}\big) . \tag{10.80}\]
  1. The chain. A system of \(N\) equal masses \(m > 0\) between two walls at rest, joined by \(N + 1\) equal linear springs \(k > 0\), has \(\mat M = m\,\mat I\) and \(\mat K = k\,\mat K_{\mathrm{N}}\). Its eigenpairs are \((\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}, \vek\phi_{\mathrm{r}})\) with
    \[\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} = \frac{2k}{m}\,\big(1 - \cos\theta_{\mathrm{r}}\big) , \qquad \sqrt{\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}} = 2\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}\,\sin\frac{\theta_{\mathrm{r}}}{2} = 2\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}\,\sin\frac{r\pi}{2(N + 1)} . \tag{10.81}\]
    We have \(0 < \lambda_1 < \dots < \lambda_{\mathrm{N}} < 4k/m\), these are all the eigenvalues and each is simple.
  2. The bar. A uniform bar of length \(L\) with constant \(EA\) and \(\rho A\), fixed at \(x = 0\) and at \(x = L\) and divided into \(N + 1\) equal elements of length \(h = L/(N + 1)\), has for the unknowns \(d_1, \dots, d_{\mathrm{N}}\) the matrices \(\mat K = \tfrac{EA}{h}\,\mat K_{\mathrm{N}}\), \(\mat M^{\mathrm{z}} = \rho A h\,\mat I\) and \(\mat M = \tfrac{\rho A h}{6}\,\mathrm{tridiag}(1, 4, 1)\) with the number \(4\) on the whole diagonal. The vector \(\vek\phi_{\mathrm{r}}\) is an eigenvector of the pair \((\mat K, \mat M^{\mathrm{z}})\) and of the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\), and the eigenvalues are as in equation (10.72), with the angle \(\theta_{\mathrm{r}}\) of equation (10.80):
    \[\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{z}} = \frac{2c^2}{h^2}\,\big(1 - \cos\theta_{\mathrm{r}}\big) , \qquad \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{k}} = \frac{6c^2}{h^2}\,\frac{1 - \cos\theta_{\mathrm{r}}}{2 + \cos\theta_{\mathrm{r}}} , \qquad c^2 = \frac{E}{\rho} . \tag{10.82}\]
  3. The error. Let \(\omega_{\mathrm{r}} = r\pi c/L\) be the exact frequencies of the continuous bar with both ends fixed. Then \(\theta_{\mathrm{r}} = \omega_{\mathrm{r}} h/c\) and the estimates of corollary 10.54 hold with this angle: \(1 - \theta_{\mathrm{r}}^2/12 \le \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{z}}/\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2 \le 1\) for every \(r\), and \(1 \le \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{k}}/\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2 \le 6/(6 - \theta_{\mathrm{r}}^2)\) at \(\theta_{\mathrm{r}}^2 < 6\). The frequencies with the lumped mass are therefore below the exact ones.
  4. Sign changes. In the sequence of components \(\phi_1, \dots, \phi_{\mathrm{N}}\) of the vector \(\vek\phi_{\mathrm{r}}\), with any zeros left out, the sign changes exactly \(r - 1\) times.
Proof

We must show four points. With \(\theta = \theta_{\mathrm{r}}\) we write \(\phi_{\mathrm{j}} = \sin(j\theta)\) for every integer \(j\), also for \(j = 0\) and \(j = N + 1\), where the walls are.

1 and 2. Eigenpairs.

  1. By the addition theorem (corollary 8.16) and the values \(\cos\pi = -1\), \(\sin\pi = 0\) (lemma 8.11, point 2), \(\sin(t + \pi) = \sin t\cos\pi + \cos t\sin\pi = -\sin t\) for every \(t\). From \(\sin 0 = 0\) it follows in turn that \(\sin\pi = \sin 2\pi = \dots = \sin(r\pi) = 0\). So \(\phi_{\mathrm{N}+1} = \sin(r\pi) = 0\) and \(\phi_0 = 0\).
  2. As in step 1 of the proof of theorem 10.52, for every integer \(j\) the addition theorem gives \(\phi_{\mathrm{j}-1} + \phi_{\mathrm{j}+1} = 2\cos\theta\,\phi_{\mathrm{j}}\) (the middle of the chord), so \(-\phi_{\mathrm{j}-1} + 2\phi_{\mathrm{j}} - \phi_{\mathrm{j}+1} = (2 - 2\cos\theta)\,\phi_{\mathrm{j}}\).
  3. Row \(j\) of the matrix \(\mat K_{\mathrm{N}}\) on \(\vek\phi_{\mathrm{r}}\) is the left expression of step 2, except that row \(1\) has no term \(\phi_0\) and row \(N\) no term \(\phi_{\mathrm{N}+1}\). Both missing terms are zero by step 1. So \(\mat K_{\mathrm{N}}\vek\phi_{\mathrm{r}} = (2 - 2\cos\theta)\,\vek\phi_{\mathrm{r}}\), with no special row at the end.
  4. In the same way row \(j\) of the matrix \(\mathrm{tridiag}(1, 4, 1)\) on \(\vek\phi_{\mathrm{r}}\) equals \(\phi_{\mathrm{j}-1} + 4\phi_{\mathrm{j}} + \phi_{\mathrm{j}+1} = (4 + 2\cos\theta)\,\phi_{\mathrm{j}}\).
  5. The vector \(\vek\phi_{\mathrm{r}}\) is not zero: since \(r \le N\), we have \(0 < \theta < \pi\) and \(\phi_1 = \sin\theta > 0\) (lemma 8.11, point 3).
  6. The chain: by step 3, \(\mat K\vek\phi_{\mathrm{r}} = k\,(2 - 2\cos\theta)\,\vek\phi_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda\,m\,\vek\phi_{\mathrm{r}}\) at \(\lambda = \tfrac{2k}{m}(1 - \cos\theta)\). This is an eigenpair (definition 10.13).
  7. The bar with the lumped mass: the same calculation with \(k = EA/h\) and \(m = \rho A h\) gives \(\lambda^{\mathrm{z}} = \tfrac{2EA}{\rho A h^2}(1 - \cos\theta) = \tfrac{2c^2}{h^2}(1 - \cos\theta)\). With the consistent mass, by steps 3 and 4, \(\tfrac{EA}{h}(2 - 2\cos\theta)\,\vek\phi_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda\,\tfrac{\rho A h}{6}(4 + 2\cos\theta)\,\vek\phi_{\mathrm{r}}\), which \(\lambda^{\mathrm{k}}\) of equation (10.82) satisfies.
  8. The half angle: the addition theorem with \(\alpha = \beta = \theta/2\) gives \(\cos\theta = \cos^2(\theta/2) - \sin^2(\theta/2) = 1 - 2\sin^2(\theta/2)\) (lemma 8.11, point 1), so \(2(1 - \cos\theta) = 4\sin^2(\theta/2)\). Since \(0 < \theta/2 < \pi/2\), we have \(\sin(\theta/2) > 0\) and \(\sqrt{\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}} = 2\sqrt{k/m}\,\sin(\theta/2)\).
  9. The angles \(\theta_1 < \dots < \theta_{\mathrm{N}} = \pi - \pi/(N + 1)\) lie in \((0, \pi)\). The functions \(1 - \cos\theta\) and \((1 - \cos\theta)/(2 + \cos\theta)\) increase strictly there (step 7 of the proof of theorem 10.52), so the \(N\) eigenvalues of each pair are distinct, and by step 8 of the same proof these are all of them, each simple. On \((0, \pi)\) we have \(\cos\theta > \cos\pi = -1\) (lemma 8.11, point 3), so \(1 - \cos\theta < 2\) and \(\lambda_{\mathrm{N}} < 4k/m\).

3. The error.

  1. From \(\omega_{\mathrm{r}} = r\pi c/L\) and \(h = L/(N + 1)\) we get \(\omega_{\mathrm{r}} h/c = r\pi/(N + 1) = \theta_{\mathrm{r}}\), so \(\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2 = c^2\theta_{\mathrm{r}}^2/h^2\). The ratios \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{z}}/\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2\) and \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{k}}/\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2\) are therefore the expressions of equation (10.73) with \(u = 1 - \cos\theta_{\mathrm{r}}\).
  2. The proof of corollary 10.54 uses only these two ratios and \(0 < \theta < \pi\), not the value of the angle itself. Its steps 1 to 4 therefore hold unchanged.

4. Sign changes.

  1. Component \(j\) has the angle \(t_{\mathrm{j}} = j\theta\). The angles increase from \(t_0 = 0\) to \(t_{\mathrm{N}+1} = r\pi\) in steps of \(\theta < \pi\).
  2. By step 1, \(\sin(t + \pi) = -\sin t\), and on \((0, \pi)\) the sine is positive. On the interval \(I_{\mathrm{i}} = (i\pi, (i + 1)\pi)\) the sine therefore has the sign \((-1)^i\), and at the ends of the intervals it is zero.
  3. Each of the intervals \(I_0, \dots, I_{\mathrm{r}-1}\) contains at least one angle \(t_{\mathrm{j}}\) with \(1 \le j \le N\). Let \(j\) be the largest index with \(t_{\mathrm{j}} \le i\pi\). It exists, because \(t_0 = 0\), and it is at most \(N\), because \(t_{\mathrm{N}+1} = r\pi > i\pi\). Then \(t_{\mathrm{j}+1} > i\pi\) and \(t_{\mathrm{j}+1} = t_{\mathrm{j}} + \theta < i\pi + \pi\), so \(t_{\mathrm{j}+1}\) lies in \(I_{\mathrm{i}}\), and since \(t_{\mathrm{j}+1} < (i + 1)\pi \le t_{\mathrm{N}+1}\), we have \(j + 1 \le N\).
  4. The non-zero components are those with angles inside the intervals. Since the angles increase, they pass through \(I_0, I_1, \dots, I_{\mathrm{r}-1}\) in turn. Inside an interval the sign does not change, between consecutive intervals it flips by step 13, and by step 14 no interval is empty. The sign therefore changes \(r - 1\) times.

10.8.7 A free bar (prosta palica)

Section 10.5 showed the zero frequency of a structure that has a displacement without energy. We see the same on a bar that is not fixed anywhere. Take two elements (\(h = L/2\), nodes \(0, 1, 2\), all three unknowns):

\[\mat K = \frac{EA}{h}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 & 0 \\ -1 & 2 & -1 \\ 0 & -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} , \qquad \mat M = \frac{\rho A h}{6}\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 4 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} . \tag{10.84}\]

The sum of each row of the matrix \(\mat K\) is zero, so \(\mat K\vek 1 = \vek 0 = 0\cdot\mat M\vek 1\) for \(\vek 1 = (1, 1, 1)\). This is an eigenpair with \(\lambda_1 = 0\) (definition 10.13), the rigid displacement of the bar without deformation (theorem 10.34). Let us draw all three modes as the field \(u_{\mathrm{h}}\) over the bar.

The horizontal axis is the position \(x\) on the bar, the displacement is drawn upwards. In the rigid displacement \((1, 1, 1)\) the field is the same everywhere and no element is stretched. The remaining modes are \((1, 0, -1)\) and \((1, -1, 1)\) with frequencies \(2763{,}70\) and \(5527{,}41\) Hz (NumPy). The exact ones are \(rc/(2L) = 2506{,}40\) and \(5012{,}80\) Hz, with the shapes \(\cos(r\pi x/L)\) (GR table 4.2, PDF p. 264, free bar, which counts the rigid displacement too and therefore writes \(r - 1\)), both errors \(+10{,}27\) %. The first error is the same as for one element of the fixed bar, because in the mode \((1, 0, -1)\) the middle is at rest and each half behaves as a fixed bar of length \(L/2\) with one element (\(\theta = \pi/2\)). The second error is the same for the same reason. In the mode \((1, -1, 1)\) the field \(u_{\mathrm{h}}\) is zero at the middle of each element, so each of the four pieces of length \(L/4\) between the middle of an element and a node behaves as a fixed bar of length \(L/4\) with one element (\(\theta = \pi/2\)), and the exact \(\omega = 2\pi c/L\) is by equation (10.70) exactly the first frequency of a fixed bar of length \(L/4\). The lumped mass gives \(2256{,}55\) and \(3191{,}25\) Hz. The total mass is \(\vek 1^\T\mat M\,\vek 1 = \rho A L = 0{,}78\) kg. By point 3 of theorem 10.34, since \(\mat K\vek 1 = \vek 0\), both modes with non-zero frequency are \(\mat M\)-orthogonal to \(\vek 1\): \(\vek 1^\T\mat M\,(1, 0, -1)^\T = \tfrac{\rho A h}{6}\,(2 - 2) = 0\), which means that the centre of mass of the bar stays in place in each of them. Why, we explain now, as section 10.5 promised for a mass matrix that is not diagonal.

For \(\vek s = \vek 1\) and the field \(u_{\mathrm{h}} = \sum_{\mathrm{I}} d_{\mathrm{I}}\psi_{\mathrm{I}}\),

\[\vek 1^\T\mat M\,\vek d = \int_0^L\rho A\,u_{\mathrm{h}}\,\mathrm{d}x . \tag{10.85}\]

On an element, namely, \(\vek 1^\T\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = \int\rho A\,(\mat\psi_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek 1)^\T\mat\psi_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mathrm{d}x\) and \(\mat\psi_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek 1 = \psi_1^e + \psi_2^e = 1\) (lemma 7.3), so this is \(\int\rho A\,u_{\mathrm{h}}\,\mathrm{d}x\) over the element. The selection matrix \(\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}\) maps the vector \(\vek 1\) into the vector \(\vek 1\) of order \(2\), so the sum over the elements (lemma 10.50) gives the equation. If the nodes of the bar are displaced by \(\vek d\), each point of a cross-section is displaced by \(u_{\mathrm{h}}\) and the centre of mass (definition 8.81, \(x_{\mathrm{T}} = \tfrac1m\int\rho A\,x\,\mathrm{d}x\)) is displaced by \(\tfrac1m\int\rho A\,u_{\mathrm{h}}\,\mathrm{d}x = \vek 1^\T\mat M\,\vek d/m\). For a diagonal \(\mat M\) this is the sum of the masses multiplied by the nodal displacements, divided by the mass \(m\), and for ours the sum is replaced by an integral over the field \(u_{\mathrm{h}}\). The condition \(\vek 1^\T\mat M\,\vek\phi = 0\) means that the mean displacement of the field, weighted by the mass, is zero: the centre of mass is at rest in this mode. At constant \(\rho A\) the integral is the area between the field and the axis, with its sign. In the mode \((1, 0, -1)\) the field \(u_{\mathrm{h}}\) is antisymmetric about the middle: the triangle above the axis and the triangle below it each have the area \(\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{L}{2}\cdot 1 = L/4\) and the integral is indeed zero. In the mode \((1, -1, 1)\) there are two triangles above the axis with the area \(\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{L}{4}\cdot 1 = L/8\) and one below it with the area \(\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{L}{2}\cdot 1 = L/4\), again zero in total. In the rigid displacement the whole area \(L\cdot 1\) is above the axis and the centre of mass is displaced by \(1\), like every point of the bar.

10.8.8 Large eigenproblems (veliki lastni problemi)

A bar of sixteen elements has \(16 \times 16\) matrices. For a uniform bar the eigenvalues are written down, but for a general bar or truss the eigenproblem \(\mat K\vek\phi = \lambda\mat M\vek\phi\) must be solved. The lecture notes of this chapter reduce it to an ordinary symmetric problem. The matrix \(\mat M\) is positive definite (lemma 10.50), so elimination has positive pivots (theorem 6.29) and writes \(\mat M = \mat L\mat D\mat L^\T\) with a positive diagonal \(\mat D\) (equation (7.45)). With \(\mat C = \mat L\sqrt{\mat D}\) we have \(\mat M = \mat C\mat C^\T\). With \(\vek y = \mat C^\T\vek\phi\) the equation \(\mat K\vek\phi = \lambda\mat C\mat C^\T\vek\phi\) becomes \(\mat C^{-1}\mat K\,\mat C^{-\T}\vek y = \lambda\vek y\) with a matrix that is symmetric, and NumPy computes all eigenvalues of a symmetric matrix. CK (D.32)-(D.36) (PDF p. 713) describes the same method with the Cholesky factorisation \(\mat M = \mat C\mat C^\T\), only the way to \(\mat C\) through the factorisation \(\mat L\mat D\mat L^\T\) of equation (7.45) is ours. For a large number of unknowns, thousands and more, it is not suitable, because the matrix is full and costs of the order of \(n^3\) operations (equation (7.43)). For such problems there are other methods: vector iteration (CK §15.2, PDF p. 489), subspace iteration and the Lanczos procedure (CK §15.3 and §15.4, PDF p. 498 onwards), and for full matrices tridiagonalisation with QR (KREYSZIG §20.9, PDF p. 914). Here we only name them, their material is in chapter 21. The next section shows a way that replaces them for frequencies: we choose only a few shapes and obtain an upper bound with them.
