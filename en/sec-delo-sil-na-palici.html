Chapter 4 Energy principles and virtual work

4.1 The work of forces on a bar

In this section we answer two questions. How much work do the forces do that load a bar slowly? And where is this work stored? We look for the answer first in a bar pulled at its end, then in the hanging bar, and at the end in a bracket of two bars that will accompany us through the whole chapter. We assume four things, and the same will hold in all following sections of the chapter.

  1. The material is linear elastic (definition 3.3), and the stresses are below the yield stress. So in a bar the stress is proportional to the strain, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), and the material returns to its original shape on unloading. In every example we will check that the stress is indeed below the yield stress.
  2. The displacements are small compared with the lengths. We therefore compute equilibrium and lengths on the shape before loading, as in chapter 2.
  3. We load slowly. The forces grow from zero to their final values so slowly that the body is in equilibrium at every moment and the velocities of its points are negligible. In section 3.6 we called such loading quasi-static. What happens when we do not load slowly we will see on the bar.
  4. The loads keep their direction during loading. The weight points down and the force on the end of a bar along its axis, however the body deforms.

4.1.1 The work of a force

In section 3.5 we computed work for a force that points in the direction of motion. The force on the end of the bar pulled along the axis, and the end moved along the axis. In structures, however, a force often does not point in the direction in which its point of application moves, the point at which the force acts. The load at the end of a bracket points vertically down, but the end of the bracket also moves a little sideways. How do we count the work then?

Take a box on the floor that we pull with a rope (figure 4.1). Let the axis \(x\) point horizontally in the direction of motion and the axis \(y\) vertically up. The rope pulls obliquely upward with the force \(\vek F = (8, 6, 0)\) N, and the box moves horizontally by \(\Delta\vek u = (2, 0, 0)\) m. We split the force into a part along the displacement and a part perpendicular to it (lemma 2.14 on splitting a vector). The direction of the displacement is given by the unit vector \(\vek d = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). The projection of the force on it is \(\vek F\cdot\vek d = 8\) N, the part along the displacement is \((8, 0, 0)\) N, and the perpendicular part is \((0, 6, 0)\) N. The perpendicular part only partly lifts the box and so relieves the floor, but it neither speeds up nor slows down the motion along the floor. Only the part along the displacement does work. It stays the same throughout and points in the direction of motion, so by the school formula of section 3.5 the work is \(8\ \text{N} \cdot 2\ \text{m} = 16\) N m. We get the same number as the scalar product of the force and the displacement,

\[\vek F\cdot\Delta\vek u = 8 \cdot 2 + 6 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot 0 = 16\ \text{N m} .\]

This is no accident. The displacement is its length times the unit vector of its direction, \(\Delta\vek u = |\Delta\vek u|\,\vek d\), so by item 3 of the properties of the scalar product (lemma 2.12) \(\vek F\cdot\Delta\vek u = (\vek F\cdot\vek d)\,|\Delta\vek u|\). This is the projection of the force times the distance travelled.

The sign has a meaning. If we pushed the box back during the same displacement, with the force \((-8, 0, 0)\) N, the scalar product would be \(-16\) N m. A force that acts against the motion does negative work, like friction that slows the box. A force perpendicular to the displacement does zero work. Such is the weight of the box, which points vertically down while the box moves horizontally.

And if the force changes during the displacement? Then we proceed as in section 3.5. We cut the path into short pieces, estimate the work on each piece with the projection of the force, which is almost the same there, and add the estimates. The projection of the force on the direction of motion is a number that depends on the distance travelled \(s\), and the work is its integral (figure 4.2). Where the projection becomes negative, the force slows the motion, and that part of the integral decreases the work.

Definition 4.1 Work of a force (delo sile)

Let a force act on a point that moves along a straight segment of length \(\ell\) in the direction of the unit vector \(\vek d\).

  1. If the force is the same throughout the displacement, \(\vek F\), and the displacement of the point is \(\Delta\vek u = \ell\,\vek d\), the work (delo) of the force is the number
    \[\vek F\cdot\Delta\vek u . \tag{4.1}\]
  2. If \(\vek F(s)\) is the force at the moment when the point has travelled the distance \(s\), and the projection \(\vek F(s)\cdot\vek d\) is a continuous function of the variable \(s\) on the interval \([0, \ell]\), the work of the force is the number
    \[\int_0^\ell \vek F(s)\cdot\vek d\ \mathrm{d}s . \tag{4.2}\]

Source: KREYSZIG §9.2, Example 2, (9) (PDF p. 390), EP (3.1.1), (3.1.2) (p. 97), KREYSZIG §10.1 (PDF p. 442).

Work is a number. Its unit is the newton metre, which is called the joule, \(1\ \text{J} = 1\ \text{N m}\) (KREYSZIG §10.1, Example 3, PDF p. 443). The two items of the definition agree. If the force is the same throughout, its projection is the constant number \(\vek F\cdot\vek d\), and by item 3 of the properties of the integral (lemma 3.39) the integral equals \((\vek F\cdot\vek d)\,\ell\). By item 3 of the properties of the scalar product this is \(\vek F\cdot(\ell\,\vek d) = \vek F\cdot\Delta\vek u\). The second item asks for a continuous projection because by theorem 3.38 we know that every continuous function on a closed interval has an integral. When several forces act on the same point, the sum of their works equals the work of their resultant, because the scalar product splits a sum (item 2 of the properties of the scalar product) and so does the integral (item 1 of the properties of the integral).

The definition describes only a displacement along a straight segment, which is a special case. A point moving along a curve contributes on every short piece of its path the projection of the force on the direction of that piece times its length, and the work is an integral along the curve, which books call a line integral (krivuljni integral) (KREYSZIG §10.1, PDF p. 442, EP (3.1.2), p. 97). We will need it only in section 4.8.3, for loads whose ratio changes during loading. When a linear elastic body is loaded slowly and all loads grow in the same proportion, every point moves along a straight segment. For the hanging bar we will see this by computation. In general we will prove in section 4.8.2 that in a body whose supports prevent rigid displacements an \(\alpha\) times larger load causes an \(\alpha\) times larger displacement of every point. When the load has reached the fraction \(\alpha\) of its final value, every point has therefore moved by \(\alpha\) times its final displacement and lies on the segment from its original to its final position.

Often several forces act on a body at different points. The sum of the works of all external forces acting on a body we will denote by \(W_{\mathrm{zun}}\), with the index zun from the Slovenian word zunanji, external. The plain letter \(W\) is already the weight of the hanging bar in this book (equation (2.1)). EP denotes the work of the external forces by \(W_{\mathrm{E}}\) and counts it with the opposite sign, as work spent on the body, \(W_{\mathrm{E}} = -W_{\mathrm{zun}}\) (EP (3.1.7) and the equation before it, p. 99).

4.1.2 Work and energy of a bar

Let us return to the bar of sections 3.5 and 3.6. The steel bar is \(L = 10\) m long and has a round cross section with diameter 10 mm and area \(A = 78{,}54\ \text{mm}^2\), and Young's modulus of the steel is \(E = 196\) GPa. The left end is held by a fixed grip, and we pull the right end along the axis with a force that we increase slowly from zero to \(F = 10\) kN (figure 4.3, left). We neglect the weight of the bar, since it is 60 N, 0,6 % of the force 10 kN. The stress at the force 10 kN is 127,3 MPa (section 2.2), below the yield stress 220 MPa. Let the axis \(x\) point from the fixed end along the bar. When the right end has moved by \(e\), the bar is stretched by \(e\), and the force is \(k\,e\) with \(k = EA/L = 1{,}539 \cdot 10^6\) N/m. The number \(k\) says how many newtons of force are needed for every metre of elongation. The final elongation is \(\Delta L = F/k = 6{,}496\) mm.

The right end moves along a straight segment in the direction \(\vek d = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). When it has travelled the distance \(s\), the force is \(k\,s\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and its projection on \(\vek d\) is \(k\,s\). By item 2 of definition 4.1 the work of the force is the integral of this projection, which we already computed in section 3.5. The function \(\tfrac12 k\,s^2\) has by the rules of differentiation (lemma 1.81) the derivative \(k\,s\), so by item 1 of the fundamental theorem of calculus 3.40

\[\int_0^{\Delta L} k\,s\,\mathrm{d}s = \tfrac12 k\,\Delta L^2 = \tfrac12 F\,\Delta L = \tfrac12 \cdot 10\,000\ \text{N} \cdot 6{,}496 \cdot 10^{-3}\ \text{m} = 32{,}48\ \text{J} ,\]

where we used \(k\,\Delta L = F\). On the graph of the force against the elongation this is the area of the triangle under the line (figure 4.3, right). The half appears because the force grows evenly from zero to \(F\), so the average force along the way is half of the final one.

Where this work goes we saw in section 3.6. When we decrease the force slowly to zero, the bar returns along the same line of force and elongation, pulls the grip with which we hold its end with the force \(k\,e\), while the grip gives way in the direction in which the bar pulls, and so gives back all 32,48 J. The bar stored the work as the strain energy \(U\). Per unit volume this is the strain energy density \(w\) (definition 3.44), which for a bar of a linear elastic material with the strain \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) along the axis is

\[w = \tfrac12\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \tfrac12\,E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}^2\]

(section 3.6). In our bar the strain is the same in all cross sections, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \Delta L/L = 6{,}496 \cdot 10^{-4}\), so

\[w = \tfrac12 \cdot 196 \cdot 10^9\ \text{Pa} \cdot (6{,}496 \cdot 10^{-4})^2 = 41\,356\ \text{J/m}^3\]

at every point of the bar. The volume of the bar is \(A L = 7{,}854 \cdot 10^{-4}\ \text{m}^3\), and the energy of the whole bar is \(w\,A L = 32{,}48\) J, exactly the work of the force.

In the hanging bar, however, the strain is not the same everywhere, and neither is \(w\). Then we cut the bar into short pieces. The piece between the cross sections at \(x_{\mathrm{i}-1}\) and \(x_{\mathrm{i}}\) has the volume \(A\,\Delta x_{\mathrm{i}}\), and since the energy density on it is almost the same, it stores approximately the energy \(w\,A\,\Delta x_{\mathrm{i}}\). The sum over all pieces is a Riemann sum of the function that assigns to every number \(x\) of the interval \([0, L]\) the number \(w(x)\,A\), the energy per unit length of the bar at \(x\) (definition 3.32). As the pieces get shorter, such a sum approaches an integral. So we define:

Definition 4.2 Strain energy of a bar (deformacijska energija palice)

Let a bar lie along the axis \(x\) from \(x = 0\) to \(x = L\), let the area of its cross section before loading be \(A\), and let the strain energy density in the cross section at \(x\) be \(w(x)\), a continuous function on \([0, L]\). The strain energy (deformacijska energija) of the bar is the number

\[U = \int_0^L w(x)\,A\,\mathrm{d}x . \tag{4.3}\]

Source: EP pp. 104-105, CM (7.4.6) (PDF p. 316).

The definition assumes that the energy density is the same at all points of a cross section, as in the bar model, in which the whole cross section moves by the same \(u(x)\). For a body that is not a bar, the strain energy is the integral of the density \(w\) over the whole volume of the body (EP (3.2.8), p. 106). We get to it in section 4.6, after we have learned integrals over a body in section 4.2. Now we can state the claim about the pulled bar in general, for a bar of any length, area and material, pulled or compressed.

Theorem 4.3 Work and energy of a bar (delo in energija palice)

A straight bar of length \(L\) with a cross section of area \(A\) is made of a linear elastic material with Young's modulus \(E\). Let the axis \(x\) point along the bar from one end, which is fixed, towards the other. A force in the direction of the axis \(x\) acts on the other end, and its component \(x\) changes slowly and without turning back from zero to the final value \(N\). Apart from this force and the force with which the fixed end holds the bar, no force acts on the bar. Then the work of this force equals the strain energy of the bar in the final state,

\[\tfrac12\,N\,\Delta L = U = \frac{N^2 L}{2EA} , \tag{4.4}\]

where \(\Delta L\) is the final elongation of the bar.

Proof

We must show that the work and the energy equal the same number \(N^2 L/(2EA)\). We compute the work with the definition of work and the fundamental theorem of calculus, the energy with the definition of strain energy. For \(N = 0\) there is neither force nor strain, and both sides are zero, so let \(N \neq 0\).

  1. Let \(G\) be the component \(x\) of the force on the end at some moment of the loading. The bar is in equilibrium, so the fixed end acts on it with the force \(-G\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). As for the bar pulled at both ends (section 2.1), the axial force in every cross section is therefore \(G\).
  2. The stress in all cross sections is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = G/A\) (definition 2.6). By the definition of Young's modulus 3.1 the strain is \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = G/(EA)\), the same in all cross sections.
  3. The elongation of the bar is therefore \(e = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\,L = GL/(EA)\) (definition 1.2), and the force is \(G = k\,e\) with \(k = EA/L\). The fixed end does not move, so the loaded end moves by \(e\) along the axis \(x\). The final elongation is \(\Delta L = N/k = NL/(EA)\).
  4. Let \(N > 0\). The end moves along a segment of length \(\Delta L\) in the direction \(\vek d = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). When it has travelled the distance \(s\), \(e = s\), and the projection of the force on \(\vek d\) is \(k\,s\). By item 2 of definition 4.1 the work is \(\int_0^{\Delta L} k\,s\,\mathrm{d}s\).
  5. Let \(N < 0\). We compress the bar, and the end moves in the direction \(\vek d = -\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), along a segment of length \(|\Delta L|\). When it has travelled the distance \(s\), \(e = -s\), the force is \(k\,e\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = -k\,s\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), and its projection on \(\vek d\) is \((-k\,s\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\cdot(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = k\,s\). The work is \(\int_0^{|\Delta L|} k\,s\,\mathrm{d}s\).
  6. The function \(\tfrac12 k\,s^2\) has by the rules of differentiation (lemma 1.81) the derivative \(k\,s\). By item 1 of the fundamental theorem of calculus 3.40 the work in steps 4 and 5 is therefore \(\tfrac12 k\,\Delta L^2\), since \(|\Delta L|^2 = \Delta L^2\). Since \(k\,\Delta L = N\), this is \(\tfrac12 N\,\Delta L\), and with \(\Delta L = NL/(EA)\) we get \(\tfrac12 N\,\Delta L = N^2 L/(2EA)\).
  7. In the final state, by step 2 taken at \(G = N\), the strain in all cross sections is \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = N/(EA)\) and the energy density is \(w = \tfrac12 E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}^2 = N^2/(2EA^2)\), the same number in all cross sections.
  8. By item 3 of the properties of the integral (lemma 3.39) and by definition 4.2, \(U = \int_0^L w\,A\,\mathrm{d}x = w\,A\,L = N^2 L/(2EA)\), the same as in step 6.

Source: CM, Example 7.4.2, (7.4.6) (PDF p. 316), EP p. 100 (spring) and pp. 103-105 (bar), SADD §6.1, (6.1.1) (PDF p. 128).

The proof has shown why the two numbers are equal. The work is the area of the triangle under the line of the force, the energy is the area of the triangle under the line of stress and strain times the volume. The elongation is the strain times the length and the force is the stress times the area, so both triangles are the same area in different units. The theorem also holds for compression. For \(N < 0\) the axial force and the elongation are negative, but their product is positive. A compressed bar stores energy just as a stretched one does, like a compressed spring. The energy is proportional to the square of the force. A twice larger force stretches the bar twice as much and acts along this twice longer way with a twice larger average force, so it does four times as much work.

Where do the assumptions fail? The theorem needs a linear elastic material. Above the yield stress the bar no longer returns along the same line on unloading (figure 2.11), part of the work is not given back, and the work no longer equals the energy that the bar returns. It also needs slow loading. In section 3.6 we saw that in slow pulling the kinetic energy of the bar is negligible compared with the work. But what happens if the force \(F\) starts to act on the end of the bar at once, with its full size from the start (figure 4.4)? Then the force \(F\) does the work \(F\,\Delta L = 64{,}96\) J along the way \(\Delta L\), while the bar at the elongation \(\Delta L\) stores only \(\tfrac12 F\,\Delta L = 32{,}48\) J. The difference, the area of the blue triangle in the figure, does not vanish. It becomes kinetic energy, the energy of motion of the end of the bar and of everything attached to it, which are still moving at the elongation \(\Delta L\). The bar therefore stretches beyond \(\Delta L\) until this energy is used up, at the elongation \(2\,\Delta L = 12{,}99\) mm, where the blue and the grey areas are equal, and then it oscillates about \(\Delta L\). CK shows exactly this for a spring with a mass that is loaded at once: without damping it reaches twice the static displacement (CK §5.1, PDF p. 137). Our bar, however, would not take this elastically. At the elongation \(2\,\Delta L\) the stress would be twice as large as under slow loading, \(2 \cdot 127{,}3\ \text{MPa} = 254{,}6\) MPa, which is above the yield stress 220 MPa, so the bar would deform permanently before the elongation reached \(2\,\Delta L\). It would oscillate like this only under a load for which twice the stress stays below the yield stress, that is, under a force of at most \(220\ \text{MPa} \cdot 78{,}54\ \text{mm}^2/2 = 8{,}64\) kN. A sudden load can therefore be dangerous even when the same load applied slowly is safe. When the force grows over a much longer time than one oscillation of the bar lasts, there is almost no oscillation (CK §5.2, PDF p. 140). This is the precise meaning of the word slowly. SADD asks for the same when it equates work with strain energy: the stress should grow so slowly that inertia effects are negligible (SADD §6.1, PDF p. 128).

4.1.3 The hanging bar

In the bar pulled at its end, a single external force does work, and the strain is the same everywhere. In the hanging bar the weight is spread over the whole bar, and the strain changes with depth. Is the work of the weight equal to the strain energy here too?

Let us recall the setting. A steel bar of length \(L = 10\) m with diameter 10 mm and cross-section area \(A = 78{,}54\ \text{mm}^2\), with density \(\rho = 7800\ \text{kg/m}^3\) and Young's modulus \(E = 196\) GPa, hangs under its own weight. The axis \(x\) points vertically down from the centre \(O\) of the top face. The bar is held by the idealized holding of section 1.7.1: only the point \(O\) is fixed, and the top face is pulled up evenly by a force that carries the whole bar (figure 4.5). A real ceiling does not hold a bar like this. We choose the model because it gives a simple displacement field, and in it the displacement along the axis is

\[u(x) = a\,(2Lx - x^2) , \qquad a = \frac{\rho g}{2E} = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1}\]

(section 3.1). The strain is its derivative, \(\varepsilon(x) = u'(x) = 2a\,(L - x)\), and the stress is \(\sigma(x) = \rho g\,(L - x)\) (equation (2.5)). At the ends \(x = 0\) and \(x = L\) the derivative is one-sided (definition 1.9), since the displacement is defined only on the bar. The stress at the top is \(0{,}765\) MPa, far below the yield stress.

In this section we describe the bar only by the displacement along the axis, \(u(x)\), as if every cross section moved as a whole. This is a simplification. By the field (1.18) the cross sections also get thinner and curve slightly, because the component \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) has the additional term \(-0{,}3\,a\,(y^2 + z^2)\), where \(0{,}3\) is Poisson's ratio of steel \(\nu\) (definition 3.2). This term does not change the energy. The strain along the axis is \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial x = 2a\,(L - x)\), because the term \(-0{,}3\,a\,(y^2 + z^2)\) does not depend on \(x\), and so it is the same at all points of a cross section. The only stress that is not zero is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) (section 3.2), so the energy density is \(\tfrac12\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) as in a bar. The thinning of the bar does not increase the energy, because there is no stress in the directions \(y\) and \(z\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\), so the thinning needs no work. The work of the weight, however, is decreased a little by this term, since the points at the rim of a cross section move a little less than the points on the axis. The work of the pull on the top face, which rises at the rim, is increased by exactly as much. For this computation we would need the integral over a body with a round cross section, which the book develops only in section 4.2.8, so here we stay with the bar model.

Energy. The energy density at the depth \(x\) is

\[w(x) = \tfrac12\,E\,\varepsilon(x)^2 = \tfrac12\,E\,\big(2a\,(L - x)\big)^2 = 2Ea^2\,(L - x)^2 .\]

At the top \(w(0) = 2Ea^2 L^2 = 1{,}494\ \text{J/m}^3\), at the middle \(w(L/2) = 0{,}3734\ \text{J/m}^3\), a quarter of that, and at the bottom zero (figure 4.5). Why a quarter? The strain at the middle is half of that at the top, the energy density is proportional to the square of the strain, and \((1/2)^2 = 1/4\). By definition 4.2 the energy of the bar is \(U = \int_0^L 2Ea^2\,(L - x)^2\,A\,\mathrm{d}x\). We compute it in three steps.

  1. We expand the square, \((L - x)^2 = L^2 - 2Lx + x^2\).
  2. The function \(H(x) = L^2 x - L x^2 + x^3/3\) has by the rules of differentiation (lemma 1.81) the derivative \(L^2 - 2Lx + x^2\). By item 1 of the fundamental theorem of calculus 3.40, \(\int_0^L (L - x)^2\,\mathrm{d}x = H(L) - H(0) = L^3 - L^3 + L^3/3 = L^3/3\).
  3. By item 1 of the properties of the integral (lemma 3.39) we may take the constant factor \(2Ea^2 A\) out, and with \(a = \rho g/(2E)\) we get
    \[U = 2Ea^2 A\,\frac{L^3}{3} = \frac{2}{3}\,E A L^3\,\frac{\rho^2 g^2}{4E^2} = \frac{\rho^2 g^2 A L^3}{6E} = 3{,}910 \cdot 10^{-4}\ \text{J} .\]

This is \(391{,}03\) microjoules, µJ. The energy is not spread evenly. In the upper half of the bar \(\int_0^{L/2} (L - x)^2\,\mathrm{d}x = H(L/2) - H(0) = L^3/2 - L^3/4 + L^3/24 = 7L^3/24 = \tfrac78 \cdot L^3/3\), so seven eighths of all the energy, \(342{,}15\) µJ, and in the lower half only one eighth, \(48{,}88\) µJ. The energy is gathered where the material is stretched most.

Work of the weight. The weight does not grow during loading. The Earth pulls the bar with its full weight for as long as the bar exists. We therefore compute the work of the weight for an imagined process in which the weight of every piece of the bar grows slowly from zero to its full value, for all pieces at the same time and in the same proportion. Such an idea is allowed for two reasons. The final state of a linear elastic bar depends only on the final forces, not on the way by which we reached them, since the strain depends only on the stress, not on how we loaded the material before (section 3.1). And the energy that an ideal elastic material stores depends only on the final strain (definition 3.43). So we may compare the work in this imagined process with the energy \(U\) in the final state.

How do the points of the bar move during such loading? At some moment let the weight of every piece be half of its full weight. We repeat the computation of section 2.1 with half the weight per unit length. The axial force is then \(\tfrac12\,\rho g A\,(L - x)\), half of the final one, so the stress and the strain are also halves of the final ones, and the integral of the strains gives half the displacement, \(\tfrac12\,u(x)\). The same holds for every other fraction of the full weight. So every point moves straight down, from zero to \(u(x)\), and when it has moved by the distance \(s\), the weight of every piece is \(s/u(x)\) times its full weight (figure 4.6). At a given moment this number is the same for all points.

We compute the work of the weight in four steps.

  1. Take a short little part of the bar of length \(\Delta t\) at the depth \(t\), whose full weight is \(\rho g A\,\Delta t\). When the little part has moved by the distance \(s\), its weight is \((s/u(t))\,\rho g A\,\Delta t\) in the direction \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), the direction of motion. By item 2 of definition 4.1 and by the fundamental theorem of calculus, used as in step 6 of the proof of theorem 4.3 on the work and energy of a bar, the work of its weight is
    \[\int_0^{u(t)} \frac{s}{u(t)}\,\rho g A\,\Delta t\ \mathrm{d}s = \tfrac12\,\rho g A\,\Delta t\,u(t) ,\]
    half of the full weight times the final displacement, the area of the blue triangle on the right of figure 4.6. At the top, where \(u(0) = 0\), the little part does not move, and the work is zero.
  2. We cut the bar into pieces by a partition \(0 = x_0 < x_1 < \dots < x_{\mathrm{n}} = L\). The displacement \(u\) grows with depth, since its derivative \(2a\,(L - x)\) is positive, so the points of the piece \([x_{\mathrm{i}-1}, x_{\mathrm{i}}]\) move at least by \(u(x_{\mathrm{i}-1})\) and at most by \(u(x_{\mathrm{i}})\). Step 1 treated the little part as if all its points moved by the same \(u(t)\). For a piece whose points move by different distances, definition 4.1 does not fix the work, since it speaks of a force on one point. We therefore assume what is true for the little part of step 1: the work of the weight of the piece lies between half its weight times the smallest final displacement of its points and half its weight times the largest. Step 3 shows that this assumption fixes the work of the whole bar uniquely. The work of the weight of the piece is therefore between \(\tfrac12\,\rho g A\,\Delta x_{\mathrm{i}}\,u(x_{\mathrm{i}-1})\) and \(\tfrac12\,\rho g A\,\Delta x_{\mathrm{i}}\,u(x_{\mathrm{i}})\).
  3. We add over all pieces. The work of the weight of the whole bar lies between the lower and the upper sum of the function \(\tfrac12\,\rho g A\,u(x)\) for the partition (definition 3.32), and this for every partition. So it is not smaller than the supremum of the lower sums and not larger than the infimum of the upper sums. The function is continuous, so by theorem 3.38 it is integrable, and both equal the integral (definition 3.34). The work of the weight is therefore \(\tfrac12 \int_0^L \rho g A\,u(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\).
  4. The pull on the top face acts at points that do not move in the bar model, \(u(0) = 0\), so it does no work. The work of all external forces is
    \[W_{\mathrm{zun}} = \tfrac12 \int_0^L \rho g A\,a\,(2Lx - x^2)\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac12\,\rho g A\,a\,\Big(L^3 - \frac{L^3}{3}\Big) = \frac{\rho g A\,a\,L^3}{3} ,\]
    where we computed the integral by the fundamental theorem of calculus with the function \(L x^2 - x^3/3\), whose derivative is \(2Lx - x^2\).

With \(a = \rho g/(2E)\),

\[W_{\mathrm{zun}} = \frac{\rho g A L^3}{3} \cdot \frac{\rho g}{2E} = \frac{\rho^2 g^2 A L^3}{6E} = U .\]

The work of the weight equals the strain energy, \(391{,}03\) µJ, here too. The result has a simple meaning. The weight of the whole bar is \(\rho g A L = 60{,}10\) N, and its average displacement is \(\frac1L \int_0^L u(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac23 a L^2 = 13{,}01\) µm. The work is half of the weight times the average displacement, \(\tfrac12 \cdot 60{,}10\ \text{N} \cdot 13{,}01 \cdot 10^{-6}\ \text{m} = 391{,}0\) µJ. The half appears for the same reason as in the pulled bar: in the imagined process the weight of every little part grows in proportion to its displacement.

Why are they equal? The equality is not self-evident. The weight of the lower pieces does much work, since they move the most, but they store almost no energy, because they are hardly stretched. How does the work get from the lower part of the bar into the upper part, where the energy is stored? Let us cut the bar in thought at the middle, at \(x = L/2 = 5\) m, and look at the two halves (figure 4.7). The lower half pulls the upper half down with the force \(N(L/2) = \rho g A\,L/2 = 30{,}05\) N (equation (2.3)), and the upper half pulls the lower half up with a force of the same size. Both forces act on the same cross section, which moves down by \(u(L/2) = 14{,}64\) µm. In the imagined process both grow in proportion to the displacement of the cross section, so each does work of size \(\tfrac12 \cdot 30{,}05\ \text{N} \cdot 14{,}64\ \mu\text{m} = 219{,}95\) µJ. On the upper half the force points in the direction of motion and the work is positive, on the lower half it points against the motion and the work is negative. The table shows the work of all forces on each half and the energy that the half stores, all in microjoules.

half work of its weight work of the force across the cut at \(L/2\) work of all forces stored energy
upper, from \(0\) to \(L/2\) \(122{,}20\) \(+219{,}95\) \(342{,}15\) \(342{,}15\)
lower, from \(L/2\) to \(L\) \(268{,}83\) \(-219{,}95\) \(48{,}88\) \(48{,}88\)

We computed the work of the weight as in step 4, only with the integral from \(0\) to \(L/2\) and from \(L/2\) to \(L\), and the energy as above. The pull on the top face does no work here either. The table shows the mechanism. Each half stores exactly as much energy as the work done by all forces acting on it, its weight and the force of the other half across the cut. SADD (6.1.1) (PDF p. 128) says the same for a small piece. The force across the cut carries \(219{,}95\) µJ of work from the lower half to the upper half. When we add the two rows, the works of the forces across the cut cancel, because the two forces are opposite and act on the same cross section with the same displacement. What remains is the work of the weight, \(391{,}03\) µJ, equal to all the stored energy. The same reasoning holds for every cross section, so however we cut the bar into pieces, the internal forces only carry work, and all the work of the external forces is stored. That the work of the external forces in slow loading equals the strain energy for every linear elastic body we will prove in section 4.6 (CM (7.4.4), PDF p. 315).

4.1.4 Trusses

Let us return to the bracket from the beginning of the chapter. Structures of straight bars joined at their ends are all around us: roof structures, bridges, cranes, power line towers. We compute them with a model that has its own name.

Definition 4.4 Truss (paličje)

A truss (paličje) is a model of a structure of straight bars joined at their ends by pins (zatiči). The places where the bars meet are called joints (vozlišča). In the model:

  1. a pin does not prevent the bars at a joint from turning about it, and there is no friction between a bar and a pin,
  2. external forces act only at the joints,
  3. the weight of the bars is negligible.

Source: EP §3.2.3 (p. 109), FELIPPA §2.2-2.3 (PDF pp. 29-30).

A truss is an idealization. Real bars at the joints are usually bolted, riveted or welded to a plate, so they cannot turn freely and also carry some bending. Experience shows, however, that the stresses and displacements computed with the truss model are usually good enough for choosing the cross sections of the bars (FELIPPA §2.3, PDF p. 30). How bending is computed we will see in chapter 5. Why does the model ask for pins and forces only at the joints? Because then every bar carries only an axial force. A bar then has forces only at its two ends, where the pins hold it, and equilibrium leaves it nothing else (figure 4.8).

Lemma 4.5 Force in a bar of a truss (sila v palici paličja)

Only the forces \(\vek F_1\) at \(P_1\) and \(\vek F_2\) at \(P_2\) act on a straight bar with the ends \(P_1\) and \(P_2\), and the bar is in equilibrium. Let \(\vek n\) be the unit vector from \(P_1\) towards \(P_2\) and \(N = \vek F_2\cdot\vek n\). Then \(\vek F_1 = -\vek F_2\), the force \(\vek F_2\) points along the bar, \(\vek F_2 = N\,\vek n\), and the axial force in every cross section of the bar is \(N\).

Proof

We must show three things: that the forces are opposite, that they point along the bar and that the axial force is \(N\) everywhere. The first follows from the condition for forces, the second from the condition for moments, the third from the equilibrium of a part of the bar.

  1. By the first condition of equilibrium (2.2), \(\vek F_1 + \vek F_2 = \vek 0\), so \(\vek F_1 = -\vek F_2\).
  2. We take moments about the point \(P_1\). The vector from \(P_1\) to \(P_1\) is the zero vector, and the vector from \(P_1\) to \(P_2\) is \(\vek r = L\,\vek n\), where \(L\) is the length of the bar. By the second condition of equilibrium (2.35), \(\vek 0\times\vek F_1 + \vek r\times\vek F_2 = \vek 0\), and since \(\vek 0\times\vek F_1 = \vek 0\), we get \(\vek r\times\vek F_2 = \vek 0\).
  3. By item 5 of lemma 2.31 on the properties of the vector product, \(|\vek r\times\vek F_2|^2 = |\vek r|^2\,|\vek F_2|^2 - (\vek r\cdot\vek F_2)^2\). We insert \(\vek r = L\,\vek n\) and \(|\vek r| = L\) and take \(L\) out by item 3 of the properties of the scalar product: \(0 = L^2\,\big(|\vek F_2|^2 - (\vek F_2\cdot\vek n)^2\big)\).
  4. Since \(L > 0\), \(|\vek F_2|^2 - (\vek F_2\cdot\vek n)^2 = 0\). By item 3 of lemma 2.14 on splitting a vector this difference is the square of the length of the perpendicular part, \(|\vek F_2 - (\vek F_2\cdot\vek n)\,\vek n|^2\). So the perpendicular part has length zero and is the zero vector, and \(\vek F_2 = (\vek F_2\cdot\vek n)\,\vek n = N\,\vek n\).
  5. We lay the axis \(x\) along the bar with the origin at \(P_1\), in the direction \(\vek n\), and cut the bar in thought at any cross section. On the part between \(P_1\) and the cut act the force \(\vek F_1\) and the force \(\vek R\) with which the part on the side of larger \(x\) acts on it across the cut. This part is in equilibrium, so \(\vek R = -\vek F_1 = \vek F_2 = N\,\vek n\).
  6. By definition 2.5 the axial force is the component \(x\) of the force \(\vek R\), so \(\vek R\cdot\vek n = N\,(\vek n\cdot\vek n) = N\).

Source: EP §3.2.3 (p. 109), FELIPPA §2.2 (PDF p. 29). The proof is not from the sources.

The lemma says that the forces at the ends of a bar point along it. If they did not, they would still be equal and opposite, but they would turn the bar, like the forces across the ruler (figure 4.8, left). For \(N > 0\), \(\vek F_2\) points away from \(P_1\), the two forces stretch the bar, and the bar is in tension. For \(N < 0\) they compress it. The lemma holds only when nothing else acts on the bar, neither a load between the joints nor the weight. This is exactly what items 2 and 3 of the definition of a truss ask for.

Now we set up the bracket that will accompany us through the whole chapter (figure 4.9). It lies in a vertical plane. The axis \(x\) points horizontally to the right, the axis \(y\) vertically up, and the axis \(z\) towards the viewer, so that the coordinate system is right-handed. All points have the coordinate \(z\) equal to zero. The origin is at the joint \(C = (0, 0, 0)\). The bar \(AC\) is horizontal, with the pin \(A = (-4, 0, 0)\) m on the wall. The bar \(BC\) is inclined, with the pin \(B = (-4, 3, 0)\) m on the wall. The wall does not let the pins move, but the bars can turn about them. The length of the bar \(AC\) is \(L_{\mathrm{AC}} = 4\) m, and that of the bar \(BC\) is by the length formula (1.10)

\[L_{\mathrm{BC}} = |C - B| = |(4, -3, 0)|\ \text{m} = \sqrt{16 + 9 + 0}\ \text{m} = 5\ \text{m} .\]

A load hangs at the joint \(C\) and acts on it with the force \(\vek F = (0, -F, 0)\), \(F = 10\) kN, which is the weight of a body with a mass of about 1019 kg. Both bars are steel, with \(E = 196\) GPa, and have a cross section of area \(A_{\mathrm{AC}} = A_{\mathrm{BC}} = 1\ \text{cm}^2 = 10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2\). The areas carry the name of the bar as an index, so that we do not confuse them with the point \(A\). The weight of the bar \(BC\) is \(\rho g A_{\mathrm{BC}} L_{\mathrm{BC}} = 38{,}26\) N and that of the bar \(AC\) is \(30{,}61\) N, each less than 0,4 % of the load, so we neglect them, as the truss model asks.

The unit vectors along the bars, from the pin on the wall towards the joint \(C\), are

\[\vek n_{\mathrm{AC}} = \frac{C - A}{L_{\mathrm{AC}}} = (1, 0, 0) , \qquad \vek n_{\mathrm{BC}} = \frac{C - B}{L_{\mathrm{BC}}} = \tfrac15\,(4, -3, 0) .\]

Only the pins at its ends act on each bar, so by lemma 4.5 on the force in a bar of a truss each carries only an axial force, \(N_{\mathrm{AC}}\) and \(N_{\mathrm{BC}}\). For the bar \(BC\) we take \(P_1 = B\) and \(P_2 = C\) in the lemma. The pin \(C\) then acts on the bar with the force \(N_{\mathrm{BC}}\,\vek n_{\mathrm{BC}}\), and the bar acts on the pin, by the law of action and reaction (section 2.1), with the force \(-N_{\mathrm{BC}}\,\vek n_{\mathrm{BC}}\). In the same way the bar \(AC\) acts on the pin with the force \(-N_{\mathrm{AC}}\,\vek n_{\mathrm{AC}}\). In tension, \(N > 0\), a bar pulls the pin towards the wall, in compression it pushes it away from the wall.

The pin \(C\) is a small body on which three forces act: the load and the forces of the two bars (figure 4.10). All three act at the same point, so the sum of their moments about this point is zero, and the condition for forces (2.2) remains.

  1. Equilibrium of the pin \(C\):
    \[(0, -F, 0) - N_{\mathrm{AC}}\,(1, 0, 0) - N_{\mathrm{BC}}\,\tfrac15\,(4, -3, 0) = (0, 0, 0) .\]
  2. Component \(y\): \(-F + \tfrac35 N_{\mathrm{BC}} = 0\), so \(N_{\mathrm{BC}} = \tfrac53 F = 16{,}67\) kN.
  3. Component \(x\): \(-N_{\mathrm{AC}} - \tfrac45 N_{\mathrm{BC}} = 0\), so \(N_{\mathrm{AC}} = -\tfrac45 \cdot \tfrac53 F = -\tfrac43 F = -13{,}33\) kN.
  4. Component \(z\) gives \(0 = 0\).

The bar \(BC\) is in tension, the bar \(AC\) is compressed. This agrees with what we expect. The load pulls the joint down. The inclined bar holds it up by pulling it towards the point \(B\), obliquely up and towards the wall. The part of this force that points towards the wall must be balanced by the horizontal bar, which therefore pushes the joint away from the wall and is compressed. Why are both forces larger than the load? Only part of the force in the bar \(BC\) points up, the component \(y\) of the vector \(-N_{\mathrm{BC}}\,\vek n_{\mathrm{BC}}\) is \(\tfrac35 N_{\mathrm{BC}}\), and this must equal \(F\). The bar \(BC\) is shallow, so this part is small and the force in the bar is large. The three forces on the pin form a closed triangle (figure 4.10, right) with sides 10 kN, 13,33 kN and 16,67 kN in the ratio \(3 : 4 : 5\), like the triangle \(ABC\) with sides 3 m, 4 m and 5 m. The two triangles are similar because every force is parallel to one side of the triangle \(ABC\): the load to the wall \(AB\), the force of the bar \(AC\) to the bar \(AC\) and the force of the bar \(BC\) to the bar \(BC\).

The stresses in the bars are by definition 2.6

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{BC}} = \frac{N_{\mathrm{BC}}}{A_{\mathrm{BC}}} = \frac{16\,667\ \text{N}}{10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2} = 166{,}7\ \text{MPa} , \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{AC}} = \frac{N_{\mathrm{AC}}}{A_{\mathrm{AC}}} = -133{,}3\ \text{MPa} .\]

The first is 76 % of the yield stress 220 MPa, the second in size 61 %, so the material is linear elastic, as assumption 1 asks. The compressed bar \(AC\), however, has one more danger. A long thin bar that we compress can suddenly bend sideways at a force much smaller than the one that would bring it to the yield stress. This is called buckling (uklon). BOWER states that a straight bar with ends free to turn is stable only below a limiting force that depends on the material, on the length and on how the cross section resists bending (BOWER §10.4.3, PDF p. 677). We do not treat buckling in this chapter and assume that the bar \(AC\) stays straight, as if something held it at the sides. This is a very strong idealization. If the bar \(AC\) has a solid round cross section of area 1 cm², this limiting force for its length of 4 m is by BOWER only about 0,1 kN, more than a hundred times less than 13,33 kN. In a real bracket we would choose for the compressed bar a larger cross section that resists bending much more, for example a tube.

The bars carry constant axial forces, so the strain along each bar is the same, and the elongations are, as in step 3 of the proof of theorem 4.3 on the work and energy of a bar,

\[\begin{aligned} \Delta L_{\mathrm{BC}} &= \frac{N_{\mathrm{BC}}\,L_{\mathrm{BC}}}{E\,A_{\mathrm{BC}}} = \frac{16\,667\ \text{N} \cdot 5\ \text{m}}{196 \cdot 10^9\ \text{Pa} \cdot 10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2} = 4{,}252\ \text{mm} , \\ \Delta L_{\mathrm{AC}} &= \frac{N_{\mathrm{AC}}\,L_{\mathrm{AC}}}{E\,A_{\mathrm{AC}}} = -2{,}721\ \text{mm} . \end{aligned}\]

The bar \(BC\) gets longer, the bar \(AC\) shorter. The strains are \(8{,}503 \cdot 10^{-4}\) and \(-6{,}803 \cdot 10^{-4}\), in size less than a thousandth, as assumption 2 asks.

How much energy does the bracket store? Each bar is a bar with a constant axial force, so its strain energy is \(N^2 L/(2EA)\) by step 8 of the proof of theorem 4.3 on the work and energy of a bar. The pins of the truss model store no energy, since they do not deform and there is no friction between them and the bars. The strain energy of a truss is therefore the sum of the energies of its bars. We label the bars by an index \(i\), and let \(N_{\mathrm{i}}\), \(L_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(A_{\mathrm{i}}\) be the axial force, the length and the cross-section area of the bar \(i\). If all bars are made of the same material with Young's modulus \(E\),

\[U = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \frac{N_{\mathrm{i}}^2\,L_{\mathrm{i}}}{2\,E\,A_{\mathrm{i}}} , \tag{4.5}\]

where the sum runs over all bars (EP (3.2.28), p. 109, where the energy density of each bar is multiplied by its volume). In our bracket \(i\) is one of the labels \(AC\) and \(BC\), and

\[\begin{aligned} U &= \frac{(16\,667\ \text{N})^2 \cdot 5\ \text{m}}{2 \cdot 196 \cdot 10^9\ \text{Pa} \cdot 10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2} + \frac{(13\,333\ \text{N})^2 \cdot 4\ \text{m}}{2 \cdot 196 \cdot 10^9\ \text{Pa} \cdot 10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2} \\ &= 35{,}43\ \text{J} + 18{,}14\ \text{J} = 53{,}57\ \text{J} . \end{aligned}\]

The compressed bar \(AC\) also stores energy, because the formula contains the square of the axial force. Each term equals \(\tfrac12 N_{\mathrm{i}}\,\Delta L_{\mathrm{i}}\), half the product of the axial force and the elongation, for example \(\tfrac12 \cdot 16\,667\ \text{N} \cdot 4{,}252\ \text{mm} = 35{,}43\) J.

And where does the joint \(C\) move? We do not know yet. For a single bar the displacement of the end was simply the elongation. Here the bar \(AC\) gets shorter and the bar \(BC\) longer, and their ends, which met at \(C\), no longer meet (figure 4.11). The joint moves to where the two bars, which can turn about the pins \(A\) and \(B\), meet again. How this displacement is computed we will see in sections 4.4 and 4.6. Energy, however, gives us part of the answer already now, if we accept that also in a truss all the work of the load is stored in the bars, as in a single bar. The wall does no work, because the pins \(A\) and \(B\) do not move, and the pins use up no energy, because there is no friction. Let us write the displacement of the joint by its components, \(\vek u_{\mathrm{C}} = (u, v, 0)\), where \(u\) is the horizontal part, positive to the right, and \(v\) the vertical part, positive upward. The load \(\vek F = (0, -F, 0)\) points vertically down, so by definition 4.1 only the vertical part of the displacement counts, \(\vek F\cdot\vek u_{\mathrm{C}} = -F\,v\). If the displacement of the joint grows in proportion to the load, as in a bar, the joint moves along a straight segment, and the work of the load is, as for a little part of the hanging bar, half the product of the final force and the final displacement, \(\tfrac12\,\vek F\cdot\vek u_{\mathrm{C}} = -\tfrac12 F\,v\). From \(-\tfrac12 F\,v = U\) we get

\[v = -\frac{2U}{F} = -\frac{2 \cdot 53{,}57\ \text{J}}{10\,000\ \text{N}} = -10{,}71\ \text{mm} .\]

So the joint sinks by a good centimetre, more than the length of any bar changes. This is possible because the bars also turn about the pins, and turning does not change the length of a bar. We will get this number in another way in section 4.4, and we will prove the assumption that all the work of the load is stored in the bars in section 4.6. The horizontal part \(u\) is not given by the equality of work and energy, because the load does no work in the horizontal direction. We will compute both parts of the displacement in section 4.4.

The section has shown the following. The work of a force that is not the same throughout the displacement of its point of application, or does not point in the direction of motion, is the integral of its projection on the direction of motion. In a bar of a linear elastic material that is loaded slowly, the work of the force on the end is stored as strain energy, \(\tfrac12 N\,\Delta L = N^2 L/(2EA)\), which we proved by computing both sides. In the hanging bar we checked the same with two integrals, and with the two halves of the bar we saw how the internal forces carry work without using any of it up. In a truss every bar carries only an axial force, we got the forces in the bars of the bracket from the equilibrium of the joint \(C\), and the energy of a truss is the sum of the energies of its bars. Of the displacement of the joint we got only the vertical part, and even that with an assumption we have not proved. The answer will come from the new form of equilibrium, which everything that follows also needs. For it we must first be able to add up over a body.
