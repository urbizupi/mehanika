Chapter 12 Beam and frame finite elements

12.4 The plane frame (ravninski okvir)

In chapter 5 we computed plane frames three times and each time differently. We computed the L-frame with unit loads (section 5.14.2), the truss with welded joints with three unknowns at the joint \(C\), whose matrix we assembled by hand (section 5.14.3), and the statically indeterminate frame with Castigliano's theorem, where we had to choose an unknown reaction (section 5.14.4). At the end of the section on the welded truss we called the procedure that assembles the matrix for any number of elements and nodes the finite element method and promised it for these chapters. Does one single procedure solve all three frames, and with that every plane frame?

The answer is yes, because we have all the ingredients. The bar element of chapter 7 carries the axial force, the beam element of section 12.1 carries bending, and assembly by elements and the reduced system we know from chapter 7 and from section 12.2. What is missing is only that the element lies obliquely and that it has three unknowns at a node instead of one or two. This section therefore first builds the frame element from the bar and the beam and turns it into the global axes. Then it shows that the solution of the assembled system is really the solution of the frame of chapter 5, exactly at the nodes, and at the end it computes all three frames of chapter 5 and two more examples, a load on an element and a hinge.

The model is the model of chapter 5. The frame lies in the plane \(xy\) together with the loads (definition 5.90), its elements are straight beams by the Euler-Bernoulli theory (definition 5.14) with the axis through the centroids of the sections, a linearly elastic material and constant \(EA\) and \(EI\) on each element, the joints are rigid, and displacements and rotations are small. The joint has no size, the weld has no compliance and shear deformation is neglected. For our elements, 1 m long and 40 mm high, the shear part of the displacement by section 5.13 is 0,125 % of the bending part. With a real weld the joint would be slightly compliant, and for thick and short elements shear deformation would count (section 12.7). The data are the data of chapter 5: steel with \(E = 196\) GPa, a strip \(20 \times 40\) mm on its edge with \(EA = 1{,}568 \cdot 10^8\) N and \(EI = 20\,906{,}7\) N m\(^2\) (the L-frame and the statically indeterminate frame) and bars \(10 \times 10\) mm with \(EA = 1{,}96 \cdot 10^7\) N and \(EI = 163{,}3\) N m\(^2\) (the welded truss).

12.4.1 Frame element (element okvirja)

How is an element that lies obliquely built from a bar and a beam? Let us first look at the element itself. In section 12.1 the beam element has two unknowns at each node, the transverse displacement and the rotation, and in chapter 7 the bar element has one, the axial displacement. The frame element has three at each node. The local axes are, as in the section on the rotation of the bar element (figure 7.16), the axis \(\bar x\) along the element from node 1 towards node 2 and the axis \(\bar y\), which is the axis \(\bar x\) turned by a right angle counterclockwise (definition 5.90, where we called the coordinate along the element \(s\)). If the nodes are at the points \((x_1, y_1)\) and \((x_2, y_2)\) and \(h\) is the length of the element, the unit vector of the axis \(\bar x\) is \((c, s) = \big(\tfrac{x_2 - x_1}{h}, \tfrac{y_2 - y_1}{h}\big)\) with \(c = \cos\alpha\), \(s = \sin\alpha\) for the angle \(\alpha\) between the axis \(x\) and the element, and the unit vector of the axis \(\bar y\) is \((-s, c)\). The rows of the rotation matrix (definition 2.37, plane part) are exactly these two vectors,

\[\mat Q = \begin{bmatrix} c & s \\ -s & c \end{bmatrix} , \tag{12.11}\]

as in lemma 7.29. Figure 12.11 shows the element and its six unknowns in both systems.

We build the displacement field of the element from the fields of the two elements that we already have. The axis of the element passes through the centroids of the sections, so by lemma 5.33 \(S = 0\) and the axial force \(N = EA\,\bar u'\) and the bending moment \(M = EI\,\bar v''\) are independent, where \(\bar u\) and \(\bar v\) are the axial and transverse displacement of the axis in the local axes. The axial displacement can therefore be interpolated separately from the transverse one.

Definition 12.17 Frame element (element okvirja)

Let there be a plane frame (definition 5.90) and let there be a straight element with nodes 1 and 2, length \(h\), constant \(EA\) and \(EI\) and the axis through the centroids of the sections. Let \(\bar x \in [0, h]\) be the distance from node 1 along the element, \(\bar u(\bar x)\) the axial and \(\bar v(\bar x)\) the transverse displacement of the axis in the local axes \(\bar x\), \(\bar y\), and \(\varphi = \bar v'\) the rotation of the section counterclockwise. The frame element (element okvirja) is this element together with a column of six real numbers, the local displacements of the nodes,

\[\bar{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}} = (\bar u_1,\ \bar v_1,\ \varphi_1,\ \bar u_2,\ \bar v_2,\ \varphi_2)^\T ,\]

and the fields \(\bar u_{\mathrm{h}}(\bar x) = \big(1 - \tfrac{\bar x}{h}\big)\bar u_1 + \tfrac{\bar x}{h}\,\bar u_2\) (the shape functions of definition 7.2) and \(\bar v_{\mathrm{h}}(\bar x) = \mat H_{\mathrm{e}}(\bar x)\,(\bar v_1, \varphi_1, \bar v_2, \varphi_2)^\T\) (the Hermite functions of the beam element, definition 12.3). In the global axes \(x\), \(y\) the element has the column \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = (u_1, v_1, \varphi_1, u_2, v_2, \varphi_2)^\T\) of node displacements.

Source: COOK §2.4 (PDF pp. 45-46), CK (14.27) and (14.28) (PDF p. 453), ONATE1 (1.37)-(1.43) (PDF pp. 56-57). The notation is ours.

We obtain the matrix of the element from energy and from forces, as for the bar and the beam. For the notation we use the order of unknowns \((\bar u_1, \bar v_1, \varphi_1, \bar u_2, \bar v_2, \varphi_2)\). The axial displacements \(\bar u_1\), \(\bar u_2\) are in rows 1 and 4, the transverse displacements and rotations in rows 2, 3, 5, 6.

Lemma 12.18 Stiffness matrix of a frame element (togostna matrika elementa okvirja)

Let there be a frame element (definition 12.17) with length \(h\), angle \(\alpha\), \(c = \cos\alpha\), \(s = \sin\alpha\) and \(\mat Q\) from equation (12.11). Let \(\mat R = \mathrm{diag}(\mat Q, 1, \mat Q, 1)\) be the \(6 \times 6\) matrix with one block \(\mat Q\) for each node and the number 1 for the rotation. Then:

  1. If no load acts along the element, the strain energy of the element is \(U_{\mathrm{e}} = \tfrac12\,\bar{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,\bar{\mat k}_{\mathrm{e}}\,\bar{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}\), and the forces and moments that the surroundings exert on the nodes of the element, in local components (force along \(\bar x\), force along \(\bar y\), moment counterclockwise), are \(\bar{\vek F}_{\mathrm{e}} = \bar{\mat k}_{\mathrm{e}}\,\bar{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}\), with
    \[\bar{\mat k}_{\mathrm{e}} = \begin{bmatrix} \tfrac{EA}{h} & 0 & 0 & -\tfrac{EA}{h} & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \tfrac{12EI}{h^3} & \tfrac{6EI}{h^2} & 0 & -\tfrac{12EI}{h^3} & \tfrac{6EI}{h^2} \\ 0 & \tfrac{6EI}{h^2} & \tfrac{4EI}{h} & 0 & -\tfrac{6EI}{h^2} & \tfrac{2EI}{h} \\ -\tfrac{EA}{h} & 0 & 0 & \tfrac{EA}{h} & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -\tfrac{12EI}{h^3} & -\tfrac{6EI}{h^2} & 0 & \tfrac{12EI}{h^3} & -\tfrac{6EI}{h^2} \\ 0 & \tfrac{6EI}{h^2} & \tfrac{2EI}{h} & 0 & -\tfrac{6EI}{h^2} & \tfrac{4EI}{h} \end{bmatrix} . \tag{12.12}\]
  2. The local and global displacements are related by \(\bar{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat R\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\). For an element without a load the forces of the surroundings on the nodes in global components are \(\vek F_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat R^\T\,\bar{\vek F}_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) and the energy is \(U_{\mathrm{e}} = \tfrac12\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\), where
    \[\mat k_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat R^\T\,\bar{\mat k}_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mat R \tag{12.13}\]
    is a symmetric matrix (the stiffness matrix of a frame element in the global axes, definition 7.4).
  3. If there is a load on the element, let \(\bar{\vek f}_{\mathrm{e}}\) be the column of consistent forces and moments of the element in the local axes (the axial load by definition 7.7 at places 1 and 4, the transverse one by definition 12.6 at places 2, 3, 5, 6). Then the column of consistent forces and moments in the global axes is \(\vek f_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat R^\T\,\bar{\vek f}_{\mathrm{e}}\).
Proof

We must show that the energy is the sum of the axial and the bending energy, that the matrix and the forces follow from it, and that the rotation of the axes converts the local quantities into global ones with the matrix \(\mat R\).

  1. By definition 5.75 the energy of the element is \(U_{\mathrm{e}} = \tfrac12\int_0^h \big(EA\,\bar u_{\mathrm{h}}'^2 + EI\,\bar v_{\mathrm{h}}''^2\big)\,\mathrm{d}\bar x\), because \(S = 0\) (lemma 5.33) gives \(N = EA\,\bar u'\) and \(M = EI\,\bar v''\) without a mixed term.
  2. By lemma 7.5 (point 2) the first term equals \(\tfrac12\,\tfrac{EA}{h}(\bar u_2 - \bar u_1)^2\), by lemma 12.4 (point 1) the second equals \(\tfrac12\,\vek d_{\mathrm{b}}^\T\,\mat k_{\mathrm{b}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{b}}\) with \(\vek d_{\mathrm{b}} = (\bar v_1, \varphi_1, \bar v_2, \varphi_2)^\T\) and \(\mat k_{\mathrm{b}}\) the matrix of the beam element. The unknowns \(\bar u_1\), \(\bar u_2\) are not in the second term and the unknowns \(\bar v_1\), \(\varphi_1\), \(\bar v_2\), \(\varphi_2\) are not in the first, so the sum is a quadratic form with the matrix \(\bar{\mat k}_{\mathrm{e}}\) from equation (12.12), in which rows and columns 1 and 4 are the matrix of the bar, the others the matrix \(\mat k_{\mathrm{b}}\), and all other entries are zero.
  3. The forces of the surroundings on the nodes are by lemma 7.5 (point 3) for the axial component \((F_1, F_4) = \tfrac{EA}{h}(\bar u_1 - \bar u_2,\ \bar u_2 - \bar u_1)\) and by lemma 12.4 (point 2) for the other components \(\mat k_{\mathrm{b}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{b}}\). Put into a column they give \(\bar{\vek F}_{\mathrm{e}} = \bar{\mat k}_{\mathrm{e}}\,\bar{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}\).
  4. The displacement of a node in the local axes is \((\bar u, \bar v)^\T = \mat Q\,(u, v)^\T\) by lemma 2.38 (point 1). The rotation \(\varphi\) is the same number in both systems: by definition 1.116 the only nonzero component of the small rotation tensor in the plane is \(\omega_{\mathrm{xy}} = -\varphi\) (a point with the displacement \(\vek u = (-\varphi\,y,\ \varphi\,x)\) has \(\omega_{\mathrm{xy}} = \tfrac12(-\varphi - \varphi)\)), and in turned axes \(\bar{\ten\omega} = \mat Q\,\ten\omega\,\mat Q^\T\) (section 2.9.4, with the plane part \(\mat Q\) of the rotation matrix). Two plane rotations commute, \(\mat Q\,\mat J = \mat J\,\mat Q\) for \(\mat J = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\), because we write both matrices as \(a\,\mat I + b\,\mat J\) with numbers \(a\) and \(b\). Therefore \(\mat Q\,\mat J\,\mat Q^\T = \mat J\,\mat Q\,\mat Q^\T = \mat J\) and \(\bar\omega_{\mathrm{xy}} = \omega_{\mathrm{xy}}\), so \(\bar\varphi = \varphi\). Altogether \(\bar{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat R\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\).
  5. The force of a node is a vector and by lemma 2.38 (point 2) its global components are \(\mat Q^\T\) times the local ones, while the moment (the \(z\) component) does not change under a rotation about the axis \(z\) (step 2 of the proof of lemma 5.91). Therefore \(\vek F_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat R^\T\,\bar{\vek F}_{\mathrm{e}}\) and by steps 3 and 4 \(\vek F_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat R^\T\bar{\mat k}_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mat R\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\). Energy is a scalar, and by lemma 1.101 \(U_{\mathrm{e}} = \tfrac12\,(\mat R\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}})^\T\bar{\mat k}_{\mathrm{e}}\,(\mat R\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}) = \tfrac12\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T(\mat R^\T\bar{\mat k}_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mat R)\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\). The matrix \(\mat R^\T\bar{\mat k}_{\mathrm{e}}\mat R\) is symmetric, because \(\bar{\mat k}_{\mathrm{e}}\) is symmetric and transposing a product reverses the order of the factors.
  6. The load on the element. Consistent forces and moments are forces and moments at the nodes, so under a rotation of the axes they map as in step 5, \(\vek f_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat R^\T\bar{\vek f}_{\mathrm{e}}\).

Source: COOK §2.4 (PDF pp. 45-46, equations (2.4-8) and (2.4-9) for the rotation of the beam element), CK (14.27), (14.35) (PDF pp. 453-455, transformation by the principle of virtual work), ONATE1 (1.41)-(1.45) (PDF p. 57). The sources rotate the matrix with the same product. The proof with the rotation tensor (step 4) and with energy (step 5) is not from the sources.

The matrix \(\bar{\mat k}_{\mathrm{e}}\) has the bar matrix in rows and columns 1 and 4 and the beam matrix in the others, so its entries are already computed. For the L-frame both elements have \(h = 1\) m, so \(EA/h = 1{,}568\cdot 10^8\) N/m, \(12EI/h^3 = 250\,880\) N/m, \(6EI/h^2 = 125\,440\) N, \(4EI/h = 83\,626{,}7\) N m and \(2EI/h = 41\,813{,}3\) N m. The axial stiffness is \(625\) times larger than the transverse one. Therefore the axial part of the displacement is small at the same load: in chapter 5 the axial force \(500\) N shortens the column by only \(F a/EA = 0{,}0032\) mm, while the bending of the column moves the end \(C\) of the crossbeam by \(F b^2 a/EI = 23{,}9\) mm, because the moment \(500\) N m acts along the whole length of the column. Therefore the axial term did not count in the displacements of the frame.

We compute the product \(\mat R^\T\bar{\mat k}_{\mathrm{e}}\mat R\) by node blocks. The block of node 2, which we shall need for the truss with welded joints, is the submatrix \(\mat k_{22}\) of the element in the global axes and equals

\[\mat k_{22} = \begin{bmatrix} c^2\,\dfrac{EA}{h} + s^2\,\dfrac{12EI}{h^3} & c\,s\Big(\dfrac{EA}{h} - \dfrac{12EI}{h^3}\Big) & \dfrac{6EI}{h^2}\,s \\ c\,s\Big(\dfrac{EA}{h} - \dfrac{12EI}{h^3}\Big) & s^2\,\dfrac{EA}{h} + c^2\,\dfrac{12EI}{h^3} & -\dfrac{6EI}{h^2}\,c \\ \dfrac{6EI}{h^2}\,s & -\dfrac{6EI}{h^2}\,c & \dfrac{4EI}{h} \end{bmatrix} . \tag{12.14}\]

We obtain it by the multiplication \(\mat R_2^\T\,\bar{\mat k}_{22}\,\mat R_2\), where \(\bar{\mat k}_{22}\) is the lower right \(3 \times 3\) block of the matrix (12.12) and \(\mat R_2 = \mathrm{diag}(\mat Q, 1)\). The upper left \(2 \times 2\) block is \(\mat Q^\T\,\mathrm{diag}(a, b)\,\mat Q\) with \(a = EA/h\), \(b = 12EI/h^3\) and \(\mat Q^\T = \begin{bmatrix} c & -s \\ s & c \end{bmatrix}\). The entry \(-6EI/h^2\) in the row of the transverse force and the column of the rotation is split by \(\mat Q^\T\) into the components \((-s, c)\cdot(-6EI/h^2)\), which is the third column of the block, and symmetrically the third row.

12.4.2 Uniqueness and exactness (enoličnost in točnost)

Is the solution of the assembled system really the solution of the frame of chapter 5? The computer solves a system of equations, not a frame, so we must show this. For the beam (section 12.3) we showed it with an error that is orthogonal to the finite element fields. For the frame we choose a shorter way: from the solution of the system we build a field that satisfies all conditions of the frame, and we show that the conditions allow at most one solution. First let us say what the solution of a frame is.

The solution of the frame (rešitev okvirja) for a given load is a set of fields \(\bar u\) (twice differentiable) and \(\bar v\) (four times differentiable) on the elements, with \(N = EA\,\bar u'\), \(M = EI\,\bar v''\) and \(V = -M'\), for which:

  1. On each element \(N' = -\bar q_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(V' = -\bar q\) (section 5.2, corollary 5.11), where \(\bar q_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(\bar q\) are the continuous axial and transverse load on the element in the local axes.
  2. In each node all elements meeting in it have the same displacement \((u, v)\) and the same rotation \(\varphi\) (definition 5.90).
  3. In each node the equilibrium of the joint (lemma 5.91) holds with the external force \(\vek F_{\mathrm{J}}\) and moment \(M_{\mathrm{J}}\) that the node carries (the given load and, in a supported node, also the reaction of the support). A node that lies inside a beam between two elements has the same equilibrium.
  4. The supports forbid the corresponding components of displacement or rotation, which are zero there, and the reactions of the supports act only in these directions.

The load on an element is continuous, because we cut the element wherever the load has a jump. Point forces and moments are therefore only in the nodes. A point force inside an element would by section 12.3 give the same nodal results, but we do not need it here.

Lemma 12.19 Uniqueness of the frame solution (enoličnost rešitve okvirja)

Let there be a plane frame (definition 5.90), divided into frame elements (definition 12.17) with constant \(EA\) and \(EI\) on each element, with a continuous load on the elements, point forces and moments in the nodes and supports in the nodes, and let it be connected (from each node we reach every other node through the elements). A small rigid motion of the frame is a displacement of all its points by the field \(\vek u = \vek a + \varphi\,\mat J\,\vek r\) with the same \(\vek a\) and \(\varphi\) (theorem 1.134, in the plane \(\mat W = \varphi\,\mat J\)). An admissible virtual displacement is a set of fields \(\delta\bar u\) and \(\delta\bar v\) on the elements, in which \(\delta\bar u\) is continuously differentiable and \(\delta\bar v\) twice continuously differentiable on each element, the elements have the same displacement and rotation in each node, and the components that a support forbids are zero. Then:

  1. For every solution of the frame and every admissible virtual displacement
    \[\begin{aligned} &\sum_{\mathrm{e}} \int_0^{h_{\mathrm{e}}} \big(N\,\delta\bar u' + M\,\delta\bar v''\big)\,\mathrm{d}\bar x \\ &\quad = \sum_{\mathrm{e}} \int_0^{h_{\mathrm{e}}} \big(\bar q_{\mathrm{x}}\,\delta\bar u + \bar q\,\delta\bar v\big)\,\mathrm{d}\bar x \\ &\quad + \sum_{\mathrm{J}} \big(\vek F_{\mathrm{J}}\cdot\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{J}} + M_{\mathrm{J}}\,\delta\varphi_{\mathrm{J}}\big) , \end{aligned} \tag{12.15}\]
    where \(\vek F_{\mathrm{J}}\) is the given load of the node \(J\) (without reactions), \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{J}}\) is the virtual displacement and \(\delta\varphi_{\mathrm{J}}\) the virtual rotation of the node \(J\).
  2. If the only small rigid motion of the whole frame in which the components forbidden at the supports are zero is the zero one, the frame has at most one solution.
Proof

We must show that the boundary terms of the elements add up in the nodes to the work of the node loads (point 1) and that the difference of two solutions is a rigid motion (point 2).

  1. On an element the assumptions of theorem 5.62 hold (continuous functions, \(N' + \bar q_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\), \(V' + \bar q = 0\), \(M' + V = 0\)), so by steps 1 to 4 of its proof (the conditions at the ends are needed only for step 5) \(\int_0^{h}(N\,\delta\bar u' + M\,\delta\bar v'')\,\mathrm{d}\bar x = \int_0^{h}(\bar q_{\mathrm{x}}\,\delta\bar u + \bar q\,\delta\bar v)\,\mathrm{d}\bar x + \big[N\,\delta\bar u + V\,\delta\bar v + M\,\delta\bar v'\big]_0^{h}\).
  2. The boundary term of the element is the work of the forces of the surroundings on the nodes on the virtual displacements. By theorem 5.62 the force of the surroundings on node 2 is \((N, V, M)\) at \(\bar x = h\) and on node 1 it is \((-N, -V, -M)\) at \(\bar x = 0\) (definition of the internal force, section 5.2). The boundary term is therefore \(\bar{\vek F}_{\mathrm{e}}\cdot\delta\bar{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}\), the scalar product of the column of forces and the column of virtual displacements of the element, both local.
  3. The scalar product has the same value in every orthogonal Cartesian system (proof of lemma 2.38, point 2), and the moment and the rotation do not change under a rotation of the axes (steps 4 and 5 of the proof of lemma 12.18). Therefore \(\bar{\vek F}_{\mathrm{e}}\cdot\delta\bar{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}} = \vek F_{\mathrm{e}}\cdot\delta\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) in global components.
  4. Let us add over the elements and collect the terms by nodes. By step 2 of the proof of lemma 5.91 an element with the node \(J\) at its start acts on the piece of the frame around \(J\) with the force \(\bar{\vek F}(0)\), which is the opposite of the force of the surroundings on node 1 of the element. An element with the node \(J\) at its end acts on the piece with the force \(-\bar{\vek F}(h)\), the opposite of the force of the surroundings on node 2. In both cases the force of the element on the piece is the opposite of the force \(\vek F_{\mathrm{e}}\) of the surroundings on the element at \(J\). The equilibrium of the piece (lemma 5.91) is therefore \(\sum_{\mathrm{e} \ni \mathrm{J}}\vek F_{\mathrm{e}} = \vek F_{\mathrm{J}}\), and the same for moments. The sum of boundary terms at \(J\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{e} \ni \mathrm{J}}\big(\vek F_{\mathrm{e}}\cdot\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{J}} + M_{\mathrm{e}}\,\delta\varphi_{\mathrm{J}}\big) = \vek F_{\mathrm{J}}\cdot\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{J}} + M_{\mathrm{J}}\,\delta\varphi_{\mathrm{J}}\), because all elements at \(J\) have the same \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{J}}\) and \(\delta\varphi_{\mathrm{J}}\) (\(\vek F_{\mathrm{e}}\) and \(M_{\mathrm{e}}\) are here the force and moment of the element at \(J\)).
  5. In a node with a support \(\vek F_{\mathrm{J}}\) is the sum of the given force and the reaction. The reaction acts only in the forbidden direction, in which the virtual displacement is zero, so it does no work and the term \(\vek F_{\mathrm{J}}\cdot\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{J}}\) contains only the given load. This proves equation (12.15).
  6. Let there be two solutions with the same load and let \(w\) be their difference: fields \(\bar u_{\mathrm{w}}\) and \(\bar v_{\mathrm{w}}\) on the elements. The difference has no load and is an admissible virtual displacement (continuity, supports). Equation (12.15) with \(\delta = w\) and a zero right side gives \(\sum_{\mathrm{e}}\int_0^{h_{\mathrm{e}}}\big(EA\,\bar u_{\mathrm{w}}'^2 + EI\,\bar v_{\mathrm{w}}''^2\big)\,\mathrm{d}\bar x = 0\). The integrand is nonnegative and continuous, so \(\bar u_{\mathrm{w}}' = 0\) and \(\bar v_{\mathrm{w}}'' = 0\) on each element (as in step 2 of lemma 6.7), hence \(\bar u_{\mathrm{w}}\) is a constant and \(\bar v_{\mathrm{w}}\) a linear function (corollary 1.85).
  7. An element with a constant \(\bar u_{\mathrm{w}}\) and a linear \(\bar v_{\mathrm{w}}\) moves as a rigid body (definition 1.11): the point of its axis at \(\bar x\) is displaced by \((\bar u_{\mathrm{w}},\ \bar v_{\mathrm{w}}(0) + \varphi\,\bar x)\) with \(\varphi = \bar v_{\mathrm{w}}'\), which is the displacement of the point at \(\bar x = 0\) plus the rotation \(\varphi\) about it, that is a field of the form \(\vek a + \mat W\,\vek r\) from theorem 1.134, in the plane \(\vek u = (a_1 - \varphi\,y,\ a_2 + \varphi\,x)\). Such a field is determined by the displacement of one point and the rotation \(\varphi\). Two elements in a common node have the same displacement and rotation of the node, so their rigid motion is the same. A connected frame therefore moves as one whole, that is as a small rigid motion of the frame, which by assumption is zero because of the supports. The difference \(w\) is zero, so the solution is unique.

Source: The proof is not from a source. The principle of virtual displacements of the frame is in chapter 5 (corollary 5.92, steps 1 to 4), and uniqueness from energy has the form of the proof of theorem 5.49.

Equation (12.15) is the frame form of the principle of virtual displacements of a beam (theorem 5.62): we collected the boundary terms of the elements by nodes, as for the unit load (corollary 5.92), only here it holds for every admissible virtual displacement. Now we show that the assembled system is really the frame.

Theorem 12.20 Finite element method for a plane frame (metoda končnih elementov za ravninski okvir)

Let a plane frame (definition 5.90) be divided into frame elements (definition 12.17) with constant \(EA\) and \(EI\) on each element, let it be connected, and let it have supports and loads as in lemma 12.19. Let the only small rigid motion of the frame in which the forbidden components are zero be the zero one. Let \(\mat K\) and \(\vek f\) be the matrix and the right side of the frame from theorem 7.13 with the matrices \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) from lemma 12.18 and the consistent forces \(\vek f_{\mathrm{e}}\) (point 3 of the lemma), and let \(\vek P\) be the column of external forces and moments of the nodes in the directions of the unknowns. For each element let \(\vek F_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} - \vek f_{\mathrm{e}}\). Then:

  1. The row of a free displacement or rotation of the node \(J\) of the system \(\mat K\,\vek d = \vek f\) is the equilibrium of the joint from lemma 5.91, \(\sum_{\mathrm{e} \ni \mathrm{J}}\vek F_{\mathrm{e}} = \vek F_{\mathrm{J}}\) (component by component).
  2. The reduced system (theorem 7.18) has exactly one solution \(\vek d\).
  3. The displacements and rotations of the nodes of this solution are exactly the displacements and rotations of the solution of the frame, and \(\vek F_{\mathrm{e}}\) is the column of forces and moments that the surroundings exert on the nodes of the element in the solution of the frame, so \(\mat R\,\vek F_{\mathrm{e}} = (-N, -V, -M, N, V, M)^\T\) in local components, with the values at \(\bar x = 0\) for the first three and at \(\bar x = h\) for the last three. If there is no load on the elements, the field of the element \(\bar u_{\mathrm{h}}\), \(\bar v_{\mathrm{h}}\) is a solution of the frame, so the displacement is exact along the whole length.
Proof

We must show that the rows of the system give the equilibrium of the nodes (point 1), that the matrix \(\mat K_{\mathrm{ff}}\) is positive definite (point 2), and that by recomputing the end forces and correcting the field we obtain a solution of the frame, which is unique by lemma 12.19 (point 3).

  1. By point 1 of theorem 7.13 row \(I\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{e}}(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}})_{\mathrm{i}} = f_{\mathrm{I}} = \sum_{\mathrm{e}} (\vek f_{\mathrm{e}})_{\mathrm{i}} + P_{\mathrm{I}}\), where \(P_{\mathrm{I}}\) is the component of the external force or moment of the node in the direction of the unknown \(I\). So \(\sum_{\mathrm{e}}(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} - \vek f_{\mathrm{e}})_{\mathrm{i}} = P_{\mathrm{I}}\), which is \(\sum_{\mathrm{e} \ni \mathrm{J}}\vek F_{\mathrm{e}} = \vek F_{\mathrm{J}}\) by components. By step 4 of the proof of lemma 12.19 this is the equilibrium of the joint.
  2. Let \(\vek d\) be a column with zero prescribed unknowns and \(\vek d^\T\mat K\,\vek d = 0\). By point 2 of theorem 7.13 \(\sum_{\mathrm{e}}\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = 0\), each term is \(2U_{\mathrm{e}} \ge 0\) (lemma 12.18, point 2, with \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) symmetric, so the hypothesis of corollary 7.14 holds for \(\mat K\)), so \(U_{\mathrm{e}} = 0\) for every element. The energy is the sum \(\tfrac12\int(EA\,\bar u_{\mathrm{h}}'^2 + EI\,\bar v_{\mathrm{h}}''^2)\), so \(\bar u_{\mathrm{h}}\) is constant and \(\bar v_{\mathrm{h}}\) linear, as in steps 6 and 7 of lemma 12.19, the elements move rigidly, the frame as a whole rigidly, and by assumption \(\vek d = \vek 0\). By theorem 7.18 the matrix \(\mat K_{\mathrm{ff}}\) is positive definite and the system has exactly one solution.
  3. Let \(\vek d\) be this solution and \(\bar{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat R\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) the local displacements of each element. For each element let us define the fields \(\bar u = \bar u_{\mathrm{h}} + \bar u_0\) and \(\bar v = \bar v_{\mathrm{h}} + \bar v_0\), where \(\bar u_{\mathrm{h}}\), \(\bar v_{\mathrm{h}}\) are the finite element fields from \(\bar{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}\), \(\bar u_0\) is the solution of the equation \(EA\,\bar u_0'' = -\bar q_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(\bar v_0\) the solution of the equation \(EI\,\bar v_0'''' = \bar q\), both with the value zero at both ends of the element, \(\bar v_0\) also with zero slope at both ends. Such functions exist and are unique: the second or fourth derivative is known, by theorem 5.45 the solution is determined up to a polynomial of degree at most 1 or 3, and the conditions determine the polynomial exactly (a line through two points, a cubic polynomial by lemma 12.2). Without a load \(\bar u_0 = 0\), \(\bar v_0 = 0\).
  4. The fields \(\bar u\), \(\bar v\) satisfy condition 1 of the solution of the frame: \(\bar u_{\mathrm{h}}'' = 0\) and \(\bar v_{\mathrm{h}}'''' = 0\) (a linear and a cubic function), so \(N' = EA\,\bar u'' = EA\,\bar u_0'' = -\bar q_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(V' = -M'' = -EI\,\bar v'''' = -EI\,\bar v_0'''' = -\bar q\).
  5. Condition 2: \(\bar u_0\) and \(\bar v_0\) are zero at both ends of the element with zero slope, so \(\bar u\) and \(\bar v\) have at the ends the same values and slope as \(\bar u_{\mathrm{h}}\) and \(\bar v_{\mathrm{h}}\), that is the displacements and rotations of the nodes of the solution \(\vek d\). These are equal for elements in the same node. Supports: the forbidden components are zero in \(\vek d\).
  6. The forces of the surroundings on the nodes of the element for the fields \(\bar u\), \(\bar v\) are the sum of the forces of the fields \(\bar u_{\mathrm{h}}\), \(\bar v_{\mathrm{h}}\) and the forces of the fields \(\bar u_0\), \(\bar v_0\). The first are by lemma 12.18 (point 1) equal to \(\bar{\mat k}_{\mathrm{e}}\,\bar{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}\), because \(\bar u_{\mathrm{h}}\) and \(\bar v_{\mathrm{h}}\) are fields of an element without a load. The second are \(-\bar{\vek f}_{\mathrm{e}}\). For the axial part this follows from lemma 7.9 applied to \(\bar u_0\) with zero node displacements (\(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\cdot\vek 0 = \vek f_{\mathrm{e}} + \vek F_{\mathrm{e}}\)). For the bending part \(\int_0^h EI\,\bar v_0''\,H_{\mathrm{i}}''\,\mathrm{d}\bar x = 0\) holds for each Hermite function \(H_{\mathrm{i}}\): after integration by parts a boundary term with \(\bar v_0'\) remains, which is zero at the ends, and the integral \(-EI\,H_{\mathrm{i}}'''\int_0^h \bar v_0'\,\mathrm{d}\bar x = -EI\,H_{\mathrm{i}}'''\,\big(\bar v_0(h) - \bar v_0(0)\big) = 0\), since \(H_{\mathrm{i}}'''\) is constant. Therefore by theorem 5.62 with the virtual displacement \(H_{\mathrm{i}}\) the work of the forces of the surroundings at the ends is \(-\int_0^h \bar q\,H_{\mathrm{i}}\,\mathrm{d}\bar x = -(\bar f_{\mathrm{e}})_{\mathrm{i}}\) (corollary 12.15, point 3). The sum is \(\bar{\mat k}_{\mathrm{e}}\,\bar{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}} - \bar{\vek f}_{\mathrm{e}}\), and by lemma 12.18 (points 2 and 3) this is in global components \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} - \vek f_{\mathrm{e}} = \vek F_{\mathrm{e}}\).
  7. By point 1 and step 6 \(\sum_{\mathrm{e} \ni \mathrm{J}}\vek F_{\mathrm{e}} = \vek F_{\mathrm{J}}\) holds in the nodes, so condition 3 of the solution of the frame. By steps 4 to 6 \((\bar u, \bar v)\) is therefore a solution of the frame. By lemma 12.19 it is unique, so this is the solution of the frame, its node displacements and rotations are \(\vek d\) and the forces at the ends of the elements are \(\vek F_{\mathrm{e}}\). Without a load \(\bar u_0 = 0\) and \(\bar v_0 = 0\), so the solution is the finite element field.

Source: COOK §2.5 (PDF pp. 46-47), where each node is a small joint in equilibrium under the forces of the elements and the external load, ONATE2 (PDF pp. 49-50), where for a beam with forces only in the nodes the finite element field is exact. The proof is not from a source.

The theorem has two conditions that we must not forget. The first is a constant \(EI\) (and \(EA\)) on each element, the second consistent forces for a load on an element (section 12.2). If \(EI\) varied inside an element or if we replaced the load by crude forces in the nodes, for example by half of the weight on each node without moments, the nodal values would no longer be exact, as section 12.3 shows. The proof also showed what remains inside the element: the true displacement is the finite element field plus the displacement \(\bar v_0\) of an element clamped at both ends, so the field itself is exact only without a load on the element.

We treat supports as for the bar, with the division of the unknowns into free and prescribed (theorem 7.18). The reaction in a prescribed unknown is the value \((\mat K\,\vek d - \vek f)_{\mathrm{I}}\) in the row of this unknown (section 7.3.4), that is the sum of the forces of the surroundings \(\vek F_{\mathrm{e}}\) on the elements in the node in this direction, which the support must provide. A hinge (členek) between two elements is modelled so that each element has its own rotation unknown in the node, and the displacements are common: the connectivity table (definition 7.10) has two numbers for the rotation in the node. The row of each of these two unknowns is by point 1 of theorem 7.13 the moment of one element and demands that the moment of this element in the hinge is zero, as figure 5.78 shows. Theorem 12.20 is proved for rigid joints, so we do not prove uniqueness of the solution with a hinge, but we check it with statics in the example at the end of the section.

The rotations of the nodes as unknowns have their history, which Timoshenko tells and which begins with reinforced concrete.

From history

The theory of structures was developed in the nineteenth century principally for trusses, since for trusses satisfactory solutions were obtained by assuming that the joints are hinged and that all bars are loaded only axially (TIMO, PDF p. 361). With the introduction of reinforced concrete into structural engineering, various frame structures spread. These are usually highly statically indeterminate and their elements work mostly in bending, so the previously developed methods soon proved unsuitable and new ones were introduced, based on deformations (TIMO, PDF p. 361). Timoshenko points out that already in the computation of secondary stresses it is useful to take the rotations of rigid joints as unknowns (TIMO, PDF p. 361). The systematic use of rotations in the analysis of frames, however, was introduced by Axel Bendixen, who developed the slope-deflection method (metoda zasukov), and his book was published in Berlin in 1914 (TIMO, PDF p. 361, note 1). His equation writes the moment at the end \(m\) of the element \(mn\) as twice the stiffness factor \(k = EI/l\) multiplied by the sum of twice the rotation of the end \(m\) and the rotation of the end \(n\), plus the fixed-end moment. This is the moment that would act on the end \(m\) if both ends were fixed and only transverse forces acted on the element. Rotations and moments are positive clockwise in this equation (TIMO, PDF p. 361). He uses the equation for frames in which the displacements of the joints can be neglected, and treats more general cases in his paper (TIMO, PDF p. 361, note 2). If at the joint \(a\), where four elements meet, we took the moments at the ends of the elements as unknowns, we would have to derive and solve seven equations, and with the rotation \(\theta_{\mathrm{a}}\) of the joint as the unknown the computation reduces to one equation, which we obtain by setting the sum of the four moments at the ends of the elements in the joint equal to zero (TIMO, PDF p. 362). With several joints one such equation is written for each, the number of equations equals the number of unknown rotations, and when the rotations are computed, all the moments at the ends are known from the equation for the moment (TIMO, PDF p. 362). The next step of simplification was made by K. A. Čališev, who used successive approximations and so solved in each step one equation with one unknown (TIMO, PDF p. 362). He first assumed that all joints are fixed and do not rotate under load, calculated the fixed-end moments for all loaded elements and summed them by joints. This sum with a negative sign is the unbalanced moment of the joint (TIMO, PDF p. 362). Then he unlocked one joint at a time, leaving the others fixed, and for the unlocked joint computed the first approximation of the rotation with an equation similar to Bendixen's (TIMO, PDF pp. 362-363). He recommended beginning at the joint with the largest unbalanced moment (TIMO, PDF p. 362, note 2). The same aim can also be reached by distributing the unbalanced moments directly while unlocking one joint at a time and holding the others fixed. This procedure of successive approximations was proposed by Hardy Cross and found wide use in America (TIMO, PDF p. 363, note).

We were looking at how frames with several rigid joints were computed with the rotations of the joints as unknowns. Our row of the system for the rotation of a joint is exactly Bendixen's joint equation: the sum of the moments at the ends of the elements in the joint equals the external moment (point 1 of theorem 12.20). The moment that the surroundings exert on end 1 of the element when \(\bar v_1 = \bar v_2\) is the third row of the matrix (12.12), \(\tfrac{4EI}{h}\,\varphi_1 + \tfrac{2EI}{h}\,\varphi_2 = 2k\,(2\varphi_1 + \varphi_2)\) with \(k = EI/h\). We wrote the equation with rotations counterclockwise, and when the direction is reversed the signs of rotations and moments are reversed and the form of the equation does not change. The stiffness factors of the classical method are therefore the entries \(4EI/h\) and \(2EI/h\) of the element matrix. The fixed-end moment is for us the moment that the surroundings exert on an element fixed at both ends, that is \(-\bar f_3\) at \(\bar{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}} = \vek 0\) (corollary 12.15, point 3).

These are in N and N m \(\bar{\vek F}_{\mathrm{AB}} = (273{,}41,\ 0,\ 23{,}410,\ -273{,}41,\ 0,\ -23{,}410)\), \(\bar{\vek F}_{\mathrm{BD}} = (0,\ 273{,}41,\ 23{,}410,\ 0,\ -273{,}41,\ 113{,}295)\) and \(\bar{\vek F}_{\mathrm{DC}} = (0,\ -226{,}59,\ -113{,}295,\ 0,\ 226{,}59,\ 0)\). By lemma 12.18 we therefore have in the column \(N = -273{,}41\) N (compression, as \(R - F\) in chapter 5), \(V = 0\) and \(M = -23{,}410\) N m. In the left half of the crossbeam \(V = -273{,}41\) N and the moment grows from \(-23{,}410\) N m at \(B\) to \(113{,}295\) N m under the load, because \(M' = -V\). In the right half \(V = +226{,}59\) N and the moment falls to \(0\) at \(C\). The jump of the transverse force under the load is \(226{,}59 - (-273{,}41) = 500\) N, as lemma 5.12 demands for a force \(500\) N downward.

The difference between \(226{,}590\) and \(226{,}5625\) N tells what the model of chapter 5 left out: the axial extension of the column and the crossbeam. The finite element method takes it along without extra work, because the axial stiffness \(EA\) is part of the matrix of each element. The price is eight unknowns instead of one, which is no trouble for a computer.

12.4.6 Load on an element and a hinge (breme na elementu in členek)

Until now the load was always at a node. What if it acts along an element? Let us take the L-frame of section 12.4.3 with a constant horizontal load \(w = 1000\) N/m in the direction \(+x\) (wind) along the whole height \(a = 1\) m of the column \(AB\) and no force at \(C\). The column is one element, the crossbeam another. The local axis \(\bar y\) of the column points in the direction \(-x\), so the local transverse load is \(\bar q = -w\). The consistent forces from section 12.2 are \(\bar{\vek f}_{\mathrm{e}} = -w\,(0,\ h/2,\ h^2/12,\ 0,\ h/2,\ -h^2/12) = (0,\ -500,\ -83{,}33,\ 0,\ -500,\ 83{,}33)\) in N and N m, and by lemma 12.18 (point 3) \(\vek f_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat R^\T\bar{\vek f}_{\mathrm{e}} = (500,\ 0,\ -83{,}33,\ 500,\ 0,\ 83{,}33)\). At the nodes \(A\) and \(B\) there are therefore forces of \(500\) N each in the direction \(+x\), half of the load \(w\,a\) on each node, and the moments \(\mp 83{,}33\) N m, which ensure that the forces and couples on every Hermite field of the element do the same work as the load (lemma 12.7).

The solution (NumPy) gives \(u_{\mathrm{B}} = 5{,}9790\) mm, \(\varphi_{\mathrm{B}} = -7{,}9719\cdot 10^{-3}\) and \(v_{\mathrm{C}} = -7{,}9719\) mm. The exact values follow from chapter 5. The moment of the load on the part of the column above the section at \(s\) is \(M(s) = -w\,(a - s)^2/2\). A unit moment at \(B\) (\(m = 1\)) gives \(\varphi_{\mathrm{B}} = \int_0^a M/EI\,\mathrm{d}s = -w\,a^3/(6EI) = -7{,}9719\cdot 10^{-3}\), a unit horizontal force at \(B\) (\(m = -(a - s)\)) gives \(u_{\mathrm{B}} = \int_0^a m\,M/EI\,\mathrm{d}s = w\,a^4/(8EI) = 5{,}9790\) mm. The crossbeam without a load rotates as a rigid body, so \(v_{\mathrm{C}} = \varphi_{\mathrm{B}}\,b = -7{,}9719\) mm. The forces on node 1 of the column are \(\bar{\vek F}_{\mathrm{e}} = \bar{\mat k}_{\mathrm{e}}\,\bar{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}} - \bar{\vek f}_{\mathrm{e}}\), locally \(\bar F_2 = 1000\) N and \(\bar F_3 = 500\) N m. Therefore \(V(0) = -\bar F_2 = -w\,a = -1000\) N and \(M(0) = -\bar F_3 = -w\,a^2/2 = -500\) N m, which are the transverse force and the moment of the load above the section at \(s = 0\). Theorem 12.20 (point 3) therefore holds up to rounding: the nodal values and the forces at the ends of the elements are exact.

Inside the element the finite element field is not exact. At the middle of the column the field \(\bar v_{\mathrm{h}}\) moves in the direction \(+x\) by \(1{,}9930\) mm, and the true displacement is \(w\,s^2(6a^2 - 4a\,s + s^2)/(24EI) = 2{,}1175\) mm at \(s = a/2\). The difference \(0{,}1246\) mm is \(w\,a^4/(384EI)\), which is the correction \(\bar v_0\) from the proof of theorem 12.20 at the middle of the element. If we divide the column into two elements, the error at the middle of each is 16 times smaller, because \(h^4\) decreases by 16 (section 12.3).

What remains is the hinge. Let us take the statically indeterminate frame of the previous section, but with a hinge at \(B\) between the column and the crossbeam. The node \(B\) now has four unknowns: \(u_{\mathrm{B}}\), \(v_{\mathrm{B}}\), the rotation of the column \(\varphi_{\mathrm{B}}^{(1)}\) and the rotation of the crossbeam \(\varphi_{\mathrm{B}}^{(2)}\), so there are 13 unknowns in all, the same four are prescribed as before, and nine are free. The connectivity table of the column is \((1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6)\), of the first half of the crossbeam \((4, 5, 7, 8, 9, 10)\) and of the second \((8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13)\). The solution (NumPy) is \(R = 250{,}000\) N, \(\varphi_{\mathrm{B}}^{(1)} = 0\), \(\varphi_{\mathrm{B}}^{(2)} = -1{,}4931\cdot 10^{-3}\) and the moment under the load \(125{,}000\) N m. Statics confirms this. At the hinge the moment is zero, so the crossbeam is a simply supported beam of length 1 m with the force \(F\) in the middle, with the reaction \(F/2 = 250\) N at each support and the moment \(F\,L/4 = 125\) N m under the force. The column carries only the axial force \(-250\) N and does not bend, so \(\varphi_{\mathrm{B}}^{(1)} = 0\). The rotation of the crossbeam at \(B\) for a simply supported beam is \(-F L^2/(16EI) = -1{,}4947\cdot 10^{-3}\). The difference \(1{,}6\cdot 10^{-6}\) is the vertical displacement of the node \(B\), \(v_{\mathrm{B}} = -0{,}00159\) mm (the column shortens by \(250\ \text{N}\cdot 1\ \text{m}/EA\)), divided by the length of the crossbeam, because the chord of the crossbeam therefore rotates.

Let us summarize. The frame element is the sum of the bar element and the beam element, rotated with the matrix \(\mat R\) (lemma 12.18). In each node it has three unknowns, a displacement and a rotation, as the frame demands (definition 5.90). The rows of the assembled system are the equilibrium of the nodes, the solution is unique and is the solution of the frame exactly at the nodes, and for loads only in the nodes along the whole length (theorem 12.20). The same procedure gave all three frames of chapter 5 and did not require us to choose a reaction or to compute a displacement with a unit force. A hinge, a rigid connection and a support differ only in the connectivity table and in the prescribed unknowns, the element matrices are the same everywhere.
