Chapter 8 Analytical dynamics (analitična dinamika)

8.4 Constraints and generalized coordinates (vezi in posplošene koordinate)

In section 1.5.1 we wrote that the position of a mechanism is described by as many numbers as the mechanism has independent ways of moving, and that these numbers will be given a name. For the truss of section 4.1.4 two numbers were enough, the displacements of the node \(C\) in the directions \(x\) and \(y\). There we only announced the numbers. Now we answer the three questions that this promise opens. What is the position of a system that has several points and bodies and links between them? How many independent numbers do we need, and how do we read this count from the links? And how do we choose the numbers so that a virtual displacement of the system (section 4.4.2) is written with them?

8.4.1 Motion of a rigid body

We start with one body. How many numbers describe the position of a rigid body (definition 1.11), for example of a bar of mechanism D? Let us take a plate in the shape of the letter L and move and turn it on a table (figure 8.55, left). We draw the position before the motion grey dashed, the position after it orange. A rigid body keeps the distances between all pairs of points: the distance \(d\) between two points of the plate is the same after the motion as before it.

To be able to follow each point, we imagine the body built into a rigid frame that fills all of space and moves with it, like a coordinate system fixed to the body. We denote a point of the frame by the position vector (krajevni vektor) \(\vek X\) that it has at time \(t = 0\), and by \(\vek x(t)\) the position vector of the same point at time \(t\). For every \(t\) the map \(\vek X \mapsto \vek x(t)\) keeps all distances. Which such maps exist at all? A translation by a vector \(\vek c\) keeps them, since all vectors between points stay the same. A rotation with an orthogonal matrix \(\mat Q\) (definition 2.46) keeps them too, because it keeps the scalar product, \((\mat Q\vek a)\cdot(\mat Q\vek b) = \vek a^\T\mat Q^\T\mat Q\,\vek b = \vek a\cdot\vek b\), and the distance is the root of the scalar product of the difference with itself (lemma 2.9). The next lemma says that there are no others.

Why are there no others? Let us show the idea on figure 8.56 before we write it in steps. Panel a shows the triangle, panel b the parallelogram and panel c the basis vectors. First we shift the map so that the point \(\vek 0\) stays in place. Then the triangle with the vertices \(\vek 0\), \(\vek x\) and \(\vek y\) goes into a triangle with pairwise equal sides: the two sides from \(\vek 0\) are kept because the distance to \(\vek 0\) is kept, and the third side because the distance between \(\vek x\) and \(\vek y\) is kept. The scalar product \(\vek x\cdot\vek y\) is determined by these three lengths (lemma 2.9), so it is kept too, and with it the angle at \(\vek 0\). Since lengths and angles are kept, the parallelogram on \(\vek x\) and \(\vek y\) goes into a parallelogram, so a sum into a sum, and the point \(k\,\vek x\) on the ray through \(\vek x\) into a point on the mapped ray. Such a map is linear, so it is determined by the images of the basis vectors. These images are the columns of the matrix \(\mat Q\): three unit vectors, pairwise perpendicular like the basis vectors themselves.

Lemma 8.38 Distance-preserving map (preslikava, ki ohranja razdalje)

Let \(\vek f\) be a map that assigns to every position vector \(\vek x\) in space a position vector \(\vek f(\vek x)\), and let it keep distances, that is, let \(|\vek f(\vek x) - \vek f(\vek y)| = |\vek x - \vek y|\) hold for all \(\vek x\) and \(\vek y\). Then there are a vector \(\vek c\) and an orthogonal \(3 \times 3\) matrix \(\mat Q\) with

\[\vek f(\vek x) = \vek c + \mat Q\,\vek x \qquad \text{for every } \vek x . \tag{8.25}\]
Proof

We must show that \(\vek f\) is, up to the translation \(\vek c\), a linear map with an orthogonal matrix. First we get scalar products from the distances, then linearity, finally the matrix.

  1. Let \(\vek c = \vek f(\vek 0)\) and \(\vek g(\vek x) = \vek f(\vek x) - \vek c\). The difference \(\vek g(\vek x) - \vek g(\vek y) = \vek f(\vek x) - \vek f(\vek y)\) does not contain \(\vek c\), so \(\vek g\) keeps distances and \(\vek g(\vek 0) = \vek 0\): the point \(\vek 0\) stays in place.
  2. For \(\vek y = \vek 0\) step 1 gives \(|\vek g(\vek x)| = |\vek x|\). These are the sides from \(\vek 0\).
  3. By the formula for the square of a difference (lemma 2.9), \(|\vek g(\vek x) - \vek g(\vek y)|^2 = |\vek g(\vek x)|^2 - 2\,\vek g(\vek x)\cdot\vek g(\vek y) + |\vek g(\vek y)|^2\), and likewise \(|\vek x - \vek y|^2 = |\vek x|^2 - 2\,\vek x\cdot\vek y + |\vek y|^2\). By step 1 the left sides are equal, and by step 2 the squares of the lengths are equal, hence \(\vek g(\vek x)\cdot\vek g(\vek y) = \vek x\cdot\vek y\). In the figure: the third sides are equal, so the angles at \(\vek 0\) are equal.
  4. Let \(\vek d = \vek g(\vek x + \vek y) - \vek g(\vek x) - \vek g(\vek y)\), the difference between the image of the vertex \(\vek x + \vek y\) and the fourth vertex of the mapped parallelogram. We expand the square \(|\vek d|^2\) into six terms. We replace the squares of lengths and the scalar products by steps 2 and 3 with those without \(\vek g\):
    \[|\vek d|^2 = |\vek x + \vek y|^2 + |\vek x|^2 + |\vek y|^2 - 2\,(\vek x + \vek y)\cdot\vek x - 2\,(\vek x + \vek y)\cdot\vek y + 2\,\vek x\cdot\vek y .\]
    The terms \(-2\,(\vek x + \vek y)\cdot\vek x - 2\,(\vek x + \vek y)\cdot\vek y\) together give \(-2\,|\vek x + \vek y|^2\), so \(|\vek d|^2 = |\vek x|^2 + |\vek y|^2 + 2\,\vek x\cdot\vek y - |\vek x + \vek y|^2 = 0\). A vector of length zero is the zero vector, hence \(\vek g(\vek x + \vek y) = \vek g(\vek x) + \vek g(\vek y)\).
  5. For a number \(k\) we expand in the same way \(|\vek g(k\,\vek x) - k\,\vek g(\vek x)|^2 = |k\,\vek x|^2 - 2k\,(k\,\vek x)\cdot\vek x + k^2\,|\vek x|^2 = 0\), hence \(\vek g(k\,\vek x) = k\,\vek g(\vek x)\) (the point \(k\,\vek x\) and its image).
  6. Let \(\mat Q\) be the matrix whose columns are \(\vek g(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\), \(\vek g(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}})\) and \(\vek g(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}})\), the images of the basis vectors. By steps 4 and 5 the vector \(\vek x = x_1\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} + x_2\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} + x_3\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) is mapped to \(x_1\,\vek g(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) + x_2\,\vek g(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}) + x_3\,\vek g(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}) = \mat Q\,\vek x\). Hence \(\vek f(\vek x) = \vek c + \mat Q\,\vek x\).
  7. The element \((i, j)\) of the product \(\mat Q^\T\mat Q\) is the scalar product of columns \(i\) and \(j\) of \(\mat Q\), so by step 3 the scalar product \(\vek e_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\), which is \(1\) for \(i = j\) and \(0\) otherwise: the columns are unit vectors and pairwise perpendicular. Hence \(\mat Q^\T\mat Q = \mat I\).
  8. By theorem 2.74 and points 5 and 6 of lemma 2.73, \((\det\mat Q)^2 = \det\mat Q^\T\det\mat Q = \det\mat I = 1\), so \(\det\mat Q \neq 0\) and by theorem 2.76 \(\mat Q\) has an inverse matrix. From step 7 it follows that \(\mat Q^\T = \mat Q^\T(\mat Q\,\mat Q^{-1}) = (\mat Q^\T\mat Q)\,\mat Q^{-1} = \mat Q^{-1}\), so also \(\mat Q\,\mat Q^\T = \mat I\), and \(\mat Q\) is orthogonal.

Source: The proof is not from the source. SHABANA §2.1-2.2 (PDF pp. 48-50) uses a transformation matrix \(\mat A\) with \(\mat A\mat A^\T = \mat I\) and does not prove that every motion of a rigid body has this form.

Where did rigidity enter the lemma? In the only assumption, that distances are kept. For a body that deforms the assumption does not hold: the stretched rubber band of chapter 1 changes the distances between its points, so its motion is not described by one vector \(\vek c\) and one matrix \(\mat Q\), it needs the displacement of every point separately.

Step 8 also shows that \(\det\mat Q = 1\) or \(\det\mat Q = -1\). A reflection, for example \(\mathrm{diag}(-1, 1, 1)\), which swaps left and right, is orthogonal with determinant \(-1\) and also keeps distances. On the right of figure 8.55 are the plate and its mirror image across the dashed line. All distances in both are equal, yet no moving and turning on the table brings the plate into its mirror image, just as a right glove cannot be turned into a left one. We therefore exclude reflection in the written form of the motion and prove this afterwards.

Definition 8.39 Motion of a rigid body (gibanje togega telesa)

Let \(\vek X\) be the position vector of a point of the rigid frame at time \(0\). The motion of a rigid body on the interval \(0 \le t \le t_1\) is the expression of the position vector of the same point at time \(t\),

\[\vek x(t) = \vek c(t) + \mat Q(t)\,\vek X , \tag{8.26}\]

where \(\vek c(t)\) is a vector, \(\mat Q(t)\) is an orthogonal \(3 \times 3\) matrix (definition 2.46), \(\vek c(0) = \vek 0\), \(\mat Q(0) = \mat I\), and the elements of the vector \(\vek c\) and the matrix \(\mat Q\) are twice continuously differentiable functions of time.

Source: SHABANA §2.1 (PDF p. 48), where \(\vek r = \vek R + \mat A\bar{\vek u}\) writes the position of a point of a rigid body with a vector and a transformation matrix. Orthogonality \(\mat A\mat A^\T = \mat I\) is in §2.2 (PDF pp. 49-50).

The vector \(\vek c(t)\) is the position of the point of the frame that is at the origin at time \(0\), and the matrix \(\mat Q(t)\) tells how the frame has rotated up to time \(t\). If we put the origin at the point \(O\) of the plate in figure 8.55, \(\vek c(t)\) is simply the displacement of the point \(O\), and the matrix \(\mat Q(t)\) tells by what angle the plate has turned. That this form is really the only possible one is stated by lemma 8.38: at every time the map \(\vek X \mapsto \vek x(t)\) keeps distances. We assume twice differentiability because in the second part of the chapter we will need accelerations, the second derivatives of the position with respect to time. The assumption fails at the instant of an impact, for example at the blow of a hammer, when the velocity jumps. At such an instant the acceleration does not exist, so we treat the motion only on the intervals between impacts.

The idea of the proof is in figure 8.57, panel a. On the axes we draw the determinant against time. Only the height \(1\) and the height \(-1\) are allowed. The determinant starts at the height \(1\). To get to the height \(-1\), a continuous path would have to cross the height \(0\) somewhere, and it may not take this value. So it stays at the height \(1\).

Lemma 8.40 A rigid motion does not reflect (gibanje togega telesa ne zrcali)

In a motion of a rigid body as in definition 8.39, \(\det\mat Q(t) = 1\) for every \(t\).

Proof

We must show that the determinant, which equals \(1\) at time \(0\), cannot jump to \(-1\). We use that a continuous function that passes between two values must also stop at every value in between.

  1. By step 8 of the proof of lemma 8.38, \((\det\mat Q(t))^2 = 1\), so \(\det\mat Q(t)\) equals \(1\) or \(-1\) for every \(t\) (the heights \(1\) and \(-1\)).
  2. The elements of \(\mat Q(t)\) are differentiable functions of time, hence continuous (lemma 1.80). The determinant is a sum of products of elements of the matrix (definition 2.72), so by point 1 of corollary 1.65 on the sum and product of continuous functions, \(t \mapsto \det\mat Q(t)\) is continuous: its graph has no jump.
  3. At time \(0\), \(\det\mat Q(0) = \det\mat I = 1\) (lemma 2.73, point 5). This is the starting point of the graph.
  4. Suppose \(\det\mat Q(t_2) = -1\) for some \(t_2\) (the orange path). The continuous function equals \(1\) at time \(0\) and \(-1\) at time \(t_2\), so by theorem 2.79 on the intermediate value it takes the value \(0\) in between. This contradicts step 1. Hence \(\det\mat Q(t) = 1\) for every \(t\).

Source: The proof is not from the source.

The proof has shown that a rotation keeps orientation at every time. In plane motion this means that the rotation is described by a single angle. Let us see why (figure 8.57, panel b). The column \(\mat Q\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) is a unit vector in the plane \(xy\), so it points to a point on the unit circle, and such a point is determined by one angle \(\varphi\). The second column is a unit vector and perpendicular to the first. There are only two such vectors: the first gives a rotation, the second a reflection, which lemma 8.40 excludes.

Lemma 8.41 Position of a rigid body in the plane (lega togega telesa v ravnini)

Let a rigid body move according to definition 8.39 in the plane \(xy\): the vector \(\vek c(t)\) lies in the plane \(xy\) and \(\mat Q(t)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) for every \(t\). Then for every \(t\) there is an angle \(\varphi\), measured in radians, such that

\[\mat Q(t) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\varphi & -\sin\varphi & 0 \\ \sin\varphi & \cos\varphi & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} . \tag{8.27}\]

Let \(O\) be a point of the body in the plane \(xy\) with position \(\vek o\) at time \(t\), and \(\vek r_0\) the vector in the plane \(xy\) from \(O\) to a point of the body at time \(0\). At time \(t\) this point has the position

\[\vek x = \vek o + \mat R(\varphi)\,\vek r_0 , \qquad \mat R(\varphi) = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\varphi & -\sin\varphi \\ \sin\varphi & \cos\varphi \end{bmatrix} , \tag{8.28}\]

where we write vectors of the plane \(xy\) with two components.

Proof

We must show that the matrix \(\mat Q\) is a rotation about the axis \(z\), and then express the position of the point by the point \(O\) and the angle.

  1. By step 7 of the proof of lemma 8.38 the columns \(\mat Q\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\mat Q\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\mat Q\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) are unit vectors and pairwise perpendicular. The first two are perpendicular to \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), so their component \(z\) is zero. We write \(\mat Q\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = (u_1, u_2, 0)\) and \(\mat Q\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} = (w_1, w_2, 0)\).
  2. We have \(u_1^2 + u_2^2 = 1\), so \(-1 \le u_1 \le 1\) (lemma 1.29). By definition 8.10, item 1, the cosine on \([0, \pi]\) is the inverse function of the function from lemma 8.7, which is defined on \([-1, 1]\). Hence there is \(\theta_0 \in [0, \pi]\) with \(\cos\theta_0 = u_1\), and \(\sin\theta_0 = \sqrt{1 - u_1^2} = |u_2|\). For \(u_2 \ge 0\) let \(\varphi = \theta_0\). For \(u_2 < 0\) we have \(u_1^2 < 1\) and \(\theta_0 > 0\), and we let \(\varphi = -\theta_0\): by item 2 of the same definition \(\cos\varphi = u_1\) and \(\sin\varphi = -|u_2| = u_2\). In both cases \((u_1, u_2) = (\cos\varphi, \sin\varphi)\): a point on the unit circle at the angle \(\varphi\).
  3. Let \(\lambda = -w_1\sin\varphi + w_2\cos\varphi\). The columns are perpendicular, \(w_1\cos\varphi + w_2\sin\varphi = 0\), and \(\cos^2\varphi + \sin^2\varphi = 1\) (lemma 8.11, item 1). Hence \(-\lambda\sin\varphi = w_1\sin^2\varphi - w_2\sin\varphi\cos\varphi = w_1\sin^2\varphi + w_1\cos^2\varphi = w_1\) and in the same way \(\lambda\cos\varphi = w_2\), so \((w_1, w_2) = \lambda\,(-\sin\varphi, \cos\varphi)\). The column is a unit vector, so \(\lambda^2 = \lambda^2(\sin^2\varphi + \cos^2\varphi) = w_1^2 + w_2^2 = 1\) and \(\lambda\) is \(1\) or \(-1\). These are both unit vectors perpendicular to the first column.
  4. For \(\lambda = -1\) we would have \(\mat Q\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} = (\sin\varphi, -\cos\varphi, 0)\) and by (2.70) \(\det\mat Q = -\cos^2\varphi - \sin^2\varphi = -1\), which lemma 8.40 excludes (the orange vector drops out). So \(\lambda = 1\), and the columns \(\mat Q\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\mat Q\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) give the matrix (8.27).
  5. Let \(\vek X_{\mathrm{O}}\) be the position of the point \(O\) at time \(0\) and \(\vek X = \vek X_{\mathrm{O}} + \vek r_0\) the position of the point of the body. By (8.26), \(\vek x - \vek o = (\vek c + \mat Q\,\vek X) - (\vek c + \mat Q\,\vek X_{\mathrm{O}}) = \mat Q\,\vek r_0\). For \(\vek r_0 = (r_1, r_2, 0)\), by (8.27) \(\mat Q\,\vek r_0 = (r_1\cos\varphi - r_2\sin\varphi,\ r_1\sin\varphi + r_2\cos\varphi,\ 0)\), which in the components \(x\) and \(y\) is exactly \(\mat R(\varphi)\,\vek r_0\). This is (8.28): from the point \(O\) we go by the rotated vector \(\mat R(\varphi)\,\vek r_0\), which came from the vector \(\vek r_0\).

Source: The proof is not from a source.

The lemma has shown that the position of a rigid body in the plane is determined by three numbers, the two coordinates of the point \(O\) and the angle \(\varphi\), and that the position of every other point is computed from them with (8.28). Let us test this on the plate of figure 8.55. Let the three numbers be the position of the point \(O\) after the motion, \(\vek o = (520,\ 90)\) mm, measured from the axes, and the angle \(\varphi = 35° = 0{,}6109\) rad. The point of the plate with \(\vek r_0 = (160,\ 0)\) mm has after the motion the position

\[\vek x = (520 + 160\cos\varphi,\ 90 + 160\sin\varphi) \approx (651,\ 182)\ \text{mm} ,\]

that is the tip of the orange arrow. A second point, with \(\vek r_0 = (0,\ 200)\) mm, goes approximately to \((405,\ 254)\) mm. The distance between the two points is \(\sqrt{160^2 + 200^2} = 256{,}1\) mm before the motion and, from the unrounded coordinates, also \(256{,}1\) mm after it (NumPy), as rigidity requires. The rotation matrix of the axes from definition 2.44 is the transpose of the matrix \(\mat R(\varphi)\), because there the rows are the new axes.

8.4.2 Position of a system and constraints

The first system on which we test this is a pendulum (nihalo), which we call pendulum \(A\). A point of mass \(m = 1\) kg, that is, a body so small that its position is described by the position of one point, hangs at the end of a rigid massless rod of length \(l = 1\) m. The other end of the rod is fixed in a pin at the point \(O\). The motion is in a plane, gravity points down. We could describe the position of the point with Cartesian coordinates \(x\) and \(y\) with the origin at \(O\) and the axis \(y\) pointing up (definition 1.12). The two numbers are not independent, however, because the rod keeps the point at distance \(l\) from \(O\):

\[x^2 + y^2 = l^2 . \tag{8.29}\]

The same position is described by one number, the angle \(\theta\) of the rod from the vertical downward, measured in radians (definition 8.8). The point has the coordinates \(x = l\sin\theta\) and \(y = -l\cos\theta\), which satisfy equation (8.29) for every \(\theta\) (figure 8.58). At \(\theta = \pi/6 = 0{,}5236\) rad, that is \(30°\), we have \((x, y) \approx (500,\ -866)\) mm. Two numbers and one relation thus leave one free number. That the rod is rigid and massless are idealizations. Rigidity makes the constraint exact: a rod that stretched would add one more free number, its length. The mass of the rod changes nothing in the count: a rod with mass is a rigid body with three numbers, which the pin ties with two equations, and the free number is again one. It will matter only in the equations of motion.

The condition (8.29), which keeps the point on the circle, is an example of a constraint (vez, section 4.4.2). To count constraints for an arbitrary system, we must first say what the position of a system is, and then what a written constraint looks like. First the position.

Definition 8.42 Configuration of a system (konfiguracija sistema)

Let a system consist of finitely many mass points, where we imagine all the mass gathered in a point, and rigid bodies (definition 1.11) in a plane. The configuration (konfiguracija) of the system is a column \(\vek x \in \mathbb{R}^N\) of numbers that determine the position of all points and bodies. A point has two numbers, the coordinates \((x, y)\) in a Cartesian coordinate system. A rigid body has three numbers, the coordinates of a chosen point \(O\) of the body and the angle of rotation \(\varphi\) of the body about this point, measured in radians (definition 8.8), from which by lemma 8.41 we compute the position of every point of the body. Points in space have three numbers. The number \(N\) is the count of all these numbers, so \(N = 2p + 3b\) for \(p\) points and \(b\) bodies in the plane. A configuration is an element of the space \(\mathbb{R}^N\) (section 1.5.1), not a vector in the space in which the system lies.

Source: GR §1.2.2 (PDF p. 36) writes the configuration of \(N\) particles with \(3N\) displacements. The extension to rigid bodies with a point and an angle is not from the sources. That the position of a rigid body in the plane is really determined by these three numbers is proved by lemma 8.41.

A configuration is thus a list of numbers, not a picture. For the pendulum \(N = 2\) and \(\vek x = (x, y)\), the coordinates of the point. For the truss of section 4.1.4, where the nodes \(A\) and \(B\) stand still, the position of the node \(C\) is determined by \(\vek x = (u, v)\), the displacements from the position before loading, so \(N = 2\) and there is no constraint that links \(u\) and \(v\).

We write a constraint as an equation between the numbers of the configuration. For the pendulum it is equation (8.29), the circle. For a mechanism with a support that we move it may also depend on time.

Definition 8.43 Holonomic, scleronomic and rheonomic constraint (holonomna, skleronomna in reonomna vez)

Let \(\vek x \in \mathbb{R}^N\) be the configuration of a system and \(t\) the time. A holonomic constraint (holonomna vez) is an equation

\[f(\vek x, t) = 0 , \tag{8.30}\]

where \(f\) is a continuously differentiable function with \(N + 1\) numbers as input and one number as output. A system with \(m\) holonomic constraints \(f_1, \dots, f_{\mathrm{m}}\) has a column \(\vek f = (f_1, \dots, f_{\mathrm{m}})\) and an \(m \times N\) matrix of partial derivatives \(\partial\vek f/\partial\vek x\), which has the number \(\partial f_{\mathrm{r}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{s}}\) at the place \((r, s)\). The constraints are independent (neodvisne) in the configuration \(\vek x\) at time \(t\) when this matrix at \((\vek x, t)\) has \(m\) pivots under Gauss elimination (section 6.3.4), where we swap rows if a pivot would be zero and skip a column without a pivot. A constraint is scleronomic (skleronomna) if \(f\) does not contain the time \(t\), like the circle of a pendulum with a support at rest, and rheonomic (reonomna) if it does.

Source: GR §1.2.2 (PDF p. 36), equation (1.12) and the definitions of scleronomic and rheonomic constraints. The requirement of \(m\) pivots is the condition of theorem 8.35, not from the source.

Why do we require independence? We count constraints in order to subtract their number from the number of numbers in the configuration. If one constraint were a consequence of the others, we would subtract too much. For the pendulum we could, for example, also write \(2x^2 + 2y^2 = 2l^2\). Both equations describe the same circle. These are two equations but one constraint, and the matrix of derivatives \(\left[\begin{smallmatrix} 2x & 2y \\ 4x & 4y \end{smallmatrix}\right]\) has a zero second row after the first step of elimination, so one pivot, not two.

Pendulum \(A\) has \(f = x^2 + y^2 - l^2\). The matrix of derivatives is the row \((2x, 2y)\). It is zero only at \(x = y = 0\), which is not on a circle with \(l > 0\), so in every configuration that satisfies the constraint there is one pivot. The constraint is scleronomic. If we move the support \(O\) horizontally with constant speed \(v\), then \(O\) is at the point \((vt, 0)\) at time \(t\) and the constraint \((x - vt)^2 + y^2 - l^2 = 0\) is rheonomic.

What does a constraint prevent when we move the system? For a small displacement \(\delta\vek x\) from a configuration that satisfies the constraint, \(f\) changes by \(\delta f = (\partial f/\partial\vek x)\,\delta\vek x\) in first order (theorem 8.28 on the change of a function of \(N\) variables over a short step, applied at constant \(t\)). Definition 8.1 requires that we respect constraints up to terms of first order, so the constraint is satisfied in the first approximation when \(\delta f = 0\). What this approximation costs we see on the pendulum. A displacement along the tangent of length \(s = 10\) mm moves the point to the distance \(\sqrt{l^2 + s^2} = 1000{,}05\) mm from \(O\) (NumPy), so only by \(0{,}05\) mm, which is about \(s^2/(2l)\), second order in \(s\). For a displacement ten times shorter the error is a hundred times smaller. The approximation is therefore good for displacements that are small compared with the length of the rod \(l\), and fails for displacements comparable with it. The time stays at the value \(t\), which we will justify with an example of a rheonomic constraint at the end of the section. For \(m\) constraints a virtual displacement that satisfies them is therefore a solution of the system

\[\frac{\partial\vek f}{\partial\vek x}(\vek x, t)\,\delta\vek x = \vek 0 . \tag{8.31}\]

Such virtual displacements are called admissible (dopustni, compatible with the constraints). For the pendulum the equation is \(2x\,\delta x + 2y\,\delta y = 0\), that is \(\delta\vek x \perp \vek x\), a displacement perpendicular to the rod, as figure 8.58 shows.

8.4.3 Nonholonomic constraints

Not every constraint has the form (8.30). The simplest system with a different constraint is a disk. A disk of radius \(r = 0{,}1\) m, standing upright, rolls on a horizontal plane without slipping. Its configuration has four numbers (figure 8.60): the coordinates \((x, y)\) of the contact point with the ground, the angle \(\psi\) between the axis \(x\) and the direction of rolling (the direction in which the disk moves if it rolls forward), and the rolling angle \(\varphi\) shown by a marked point on the rim of the disk, measured in radians so that it grows when the disk rolls forward. Such a system has \(N = 4\).

The point of the disk that touches the ground is at rest relative to the ground (this is rolling without slipping from section 8.1.2). Why does the centre move at all, then? Let us look at the disk from the side (figure 8.59, right). The disk stands on the ground and turns with the angular velocity \(\dot\varphi\). A point on the rim is at the distance of the radius \(r\) from the centre. When the disk turns by an angle \(\Delta\varphi\), measured in radians, a point on the rim travels relative to the centre along an arc of length \(r\,\Delta\varphi\) (definition 8.8). So the point of the disk at the contact moves backward relative to the centre with the speed \(r\dot\varphi\), in the direction opposite to the direction of rolling. Relative to the ground, however, it is at rest. This is possible only if the centre carries it forward with the same speed \(r\dot\varphi\), so that the two velocities cancel. Therefore the centre moves relative to the ground with the speed \(r\dot\varphi\) in the direction of rolling, and with it the contact point \((x, y)\), which is always vertically below the centre. The point of the disk that touches the ground is at rest, but at every instant a different point of the disk touches the ground. In components:

\[\dot x = r\,\dot\varphi\,\cos\psi , \qquad \dot y = r\,\dot\varphi\,\sin\psi . \tag{8.32}\]

A dot means the derivative with respect to time. These are two equations between velocities, not between coordinates. We can write them as \(\mat A\,\dot{\vek x} = \vek 0\) with the \(2 \times 4\) matrix

\[\mat A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 & -r\cos\psi \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & -r\sin\psi \end{bmatrix} ,\]

which has two pivots in every configuration (in the first two columns).

Can the equations (8.32) be written as a holonomic constraint between \(x\), \(y\), \(\psi\) and \(\varphi\)? If they could, \(\varphi\) would be determined by the position and the direction, and a return to the same position and direction would return the same \(\varphi\). We test this on two paths. A path along a circle of radius \(R = 1{,}05\) m, which the disk rolls once, returns the contact point to the same point with the same direction of rolling. The angle \(\psi\) changes by \(2\pi\) on the way, so a turn in place by \(-2\pi\), which leaves \(\varphi\) unchanged, returns the number \(\psi\) to its initial value as well. The path has length \(2\pi R\), the disk rolls it with \(\Delta\varphi = 2\pi R/r\), that is \(65{,}97\) rad or \(10{,}5\) turns (computed in NumPy). The marked point on the rim is after the path on the opposite side of the disk compared with before it (figure 8.60, right). If the disk stays in place, \(\varphi\) does not change. Two paths that start and end in the same position and direction therefore end with different \(\varphi\).

We chose the radius \(R = 1{,}05\) m on purpose. At \(R = 1\) m we would have \(\Delta\varphi = 20\pi\), exactly ten turns, and the marked point would be on the same side of the disk after the path as before it. The number \(\varphi\) would still differ from its initial value by \(20\pi\), but the figure would not show the difference.

Two paths show that \(\varphi\) is not a function of position and direction. They do not show, however, that there is no other holonomic constraint that would replace the rolling constraint. That is shown by the lemma that follows. We must say what it means that a holonomic constraint replaces constraints on velocities.

Definition 8.44 Nonholonomic constraint (neholonomna vez)

Let a motion (gibanje) of a system be a continuously differentiable function \(t \mapsto \vek x(t)\) with output in the space of configurations \(\mathbb{R}^N\). Velocity constraints (vezi na hitrosti), like the two equations for the velocity of the centre of the disk, are equations \(\mat A(\vek x, t)\,\dot{\vek x} + \vek b(\vek x, t) = \vek 0\), where \(\mat A\) is an \(m \times N\) matrix with \(m\) pivots and \(\vek b\) a column of \(m\) numbers, both continuous in \(\vek x\) and \(t\). Only the motions that satisfy them are allowed. The velocity constraints are integrable (integrabilne) if there exist \(k \ge 0\) holonomic constraints \(\vek f(\vek x, t) = \vek 0\) (definition 8.43), independent in every configuration and at every time at which they hold, which at some time are satisfied by at least one configuration, and for every motion \(\vek x(t)\) that satisfies \(\vek f = \vek 0\) at some time \(t_0\) the following holds: the motion satisfies the velocity constraints exactly when it satisfies \(\vek f = \vek 0\) at every time. A velocity constraint that is not integrable is nonholonomic (neholonomna).

Source: GR §1.2.2 (PDF p. 37): a constraint that cannot be written in the form (1.12) is nonholonomic, and velocity constraints are "generally not integrable". GR Example 1.2 (PDF p. 37-38) is the disk. The formulation of integrability with the same motions is not from the source.

What it would mean that a holonomic constraint replaces the rolling constraint is shown in figure 8.61. On the left is the holonomic constraint of the pendulum, the circle \(f = x^2 + y^2 - l^2 = 0\). The pendulum starts at the position \(\vek x_0\) on the circle and moves along it, so no motion reaches the position \(\vek x_1\) off the circle. Inside the circle \(f < 0\), outside \(f > 0\), so \(f\) changes across the circle and its gradient \(\nabla f\), the row of the matrix \(\partial f/\partial\vek x\), is not zero. This is the pivot that independence of the constraint requires. On the right is the disk, of which we draw only two of the four numbers, the position \(x\) and the rolling angle \(\varphi\). A turn in place changes only \(\psi\), so it does not move the point in this figure. The disk starts at the position \(\vek x_0\). When rolling straight in the direction of the axis \(x\), by (8.32) \(\dot x = r\dot\varphi\), so \(\varphi - x/r\) does not change and the point stays on the line \(\varphi - x/r = 0{,}5\). This line looks like a holonomic constraint. With one round of a small circle, however, the disk raises \(\varphi\) by \(\Delta = 3\) rad at the same \(x\) and arrives at the position \(\vek x_1\), which does not lie on the line. The lemma that follows shows that it reaches every position in this way. Every holonomic constraint that the motions respect would therefore hold in every position and would have no gradient that is not zero.

Lemma 8.45 Rolling of a disk is a nonholonomic constraint (kotaljenje diska je neholonomna vez)

Let the configuration of a disk be \(\vek x = (x, y, \psi, \varphi) \in \mathbb{R}^4\), where \(\psi\) and \(\varphi\) are numbers (whole turns are counted too), and let a disk of radius \(r > 0\) satisfy the constraints (8.32). Then for every configuration \(\vek x_0\), time \(t_0\) and time \(t_1 > t_0\) and every configuration \(\vek x_1 \in \mathbb{R}^4\) there is a continuously differentiable motion that satisfies (8.32), is in \(\vek x_0\) at \(t_0\) and in \(\vek x_1\) at \(t_1\). The constraint (8.32) is nonholonomic.

Proof

First we reach every configuration, then the statement on holonomic constraints follows. Three kinds of motion satisfy (8.32): a turn in place (\(\dot x = \dot y = \dot\varphi = 0\), \(\dot\psi\) arbitrary), rolling in the same direction (\(\dot\psi = 0\), \(\dot\varphi\) arbitrary) and rest. We compose the motions from pieces that each start and end at rest (\(\dot{\vek x} = \vek 0\)), so the composed motion is continuously differentiable. A piece may last as long as we wish: if \(\vek x(t)\) satisfies (8.32), then for every smooth increasing \(\tau(t)\) with \(\tau' = 0\) at the end points \(\vek x(\tau(t))\) satisfies it too, because (8.32) is linear and homogeneous in the velocities (both sides are multiplied by \(\tau'\)). We therefore run through the path piece by piece in any time, and rest is allowed in between.

  1. Let the target be the configuration \(\vek x_1 = (x_1, y_1, \psi_1, \varphi_1)\). Let \(d \ge 0\) be the distance from \((x_0, y_0)\) to \((x_1, y_1)\). If \(d > 0\), with a turn in place we turn \(\psi\) from the initial direction \(\psi_0\) into the direction from \((x_0, y_0)\) toward \((x_1, y_1)\). If \(d = 0\), we skip this turn and step 2.
  2. With \(\dot\varphi\) constant and \(\dot\psi = 0\) we roll the distance \(d\) along a straight line to \((x_1, y_1)\). By equation (8.32) the length of the path is \(\int r\,|\dot\varphi|\,\mathrm{d}t\), so \(\varphi\) changes by \(d/r\).
  3. With a turn in place we turn the direction into \(\psi_1\). Now we are in \((x_1, y_1, \psi_1, \varphi_0 + d/r)\).
  4. What remains is the difference \(\Delta = \varphi_1 - \varphi_0 - d/r\), which may be positive or negative. If \(\Delta = 0\) we are at the target. Otherwise we choose \(R = r\,|\Delta|/(2\pi)\) and the circle of radius \(R\) through \((x_1, y_1)\). At \(\dot\varphi > 0\) the contact point moves by (8.32) in the direction \(\psi\), at \(\dot\varphi < 0\) in the opposite direction. So we turn \(\psi\) in place into the direction of the tangent of the circle (for \(\Delta > 0\) into the direction of travel, for \(\Delta < 0\) into the opposite one) and roll the circle once, forward for \(\Delta > 0\) and backward for \(\Delta < 0\), changing \(\psi\) along the way so that it keeps the same direction relative to the travel (\(\dot\psi\) is not restricted by the constraint (8.32)). The path has length \(2\pi R\), so by step 2 the change of \(\varphi\) is \(\pm 2\pi R/r = \Delta\). The contact point returns to \((x_1, y_1)\), and the angle \(\psi\) changes by a multiple of \(2\pi\). With a turn in place we return it to \(\psi_1\). After that the configuration is \(\vek x_1\).
  5. Let the constraint (8.32) be integrable, that is, holonomic in disguise, with \(k\) constraints \(\vek f(\vek x, t) = \vek 0\). Let \(\vek x_0\) be a configuration and \(t_0\) a time that satisfy \(\vek f(\vek x_0, t_0) = \vek 0\) (these exist by the definition). By definition 8.44 every motion from \(\vek x_0\) that satisfies (8.32) satisfies \(\vek f = \vek 0\) at every time (the direction "exactly when" from left to right). By step 4 such a motion arrives in every configuration \(\vek x_1\) at every time \(t_1 > t_0\) (the target may be anywhere, also off the line that would look like a constraint), so \(\vek f(\vek x_1, t_1) = \vek 0\) for every \(\vek x_1 \in \mathbb{R}^4\) and every \(t_1 > t_0\). The function \(\vek f\) is therefore constantly zero on the open set \(\mathbb{R}^4 \times (t_0, \infty)\), so all partial derivatives \(\partial\vek f/\partial\vek x\) are zero at every \(\vek x_1\). The matrix \(\partial\vek f/\partial\vek x\) has no pivot at \((\vek x_1, t_1)\), which contradicts independence in this configuration, where the constraints hold, if \(k \ge 1\). With independent constraints, each constraint has a nonzero gradient in every position where the constraints hold, like the circle of the pendulum.
  6. For \(k = 0\) the condition \(\vek f = \vek 0\) is satisfied by every motion. By the definition every motion would have to satisfy the velocity constraints, also the motion with \(\dot x = 1\), \(\dot y = 0\) and \(\dot\varphi = 0\), in which the disk does not turn but its contact point slides on the ground. Such a motion does not satisfy (8.32) (the first equation gives \(1 = 0\) at \(\dot\varphi = 0\)). So \(k = 0\) is not possible either.

Source: GR Example 1.2 (PDF p. 37-38) says that the rolling constraints are not integrable, and understands them as "behaviour constraints": they do not restrict positions, only the way to reach them. The proof by reachability is not from the source.

The lemma says exactly what GR says: the rolling constraints do not restrict the positions that the disk can take, only the way to reach them. The disk therefore has \(N = 4\) numbers and no holonomic constraint, yet only two independent directions of motion in each position, rolling forward or backward and a turn in place, because two equations tie four velocities. For velocity constraints we take, by convention, the virtual displacements to be the solutions of the equation \(\mat A\,\delta\vek x = \vek 0\) with the same matrix, written for every \(\delta\vek x\) as (8.31) (SHABANA (3.114), PDF p. 134). For the disk \(\vek b = \vek 0\), so the same equation also determines the allowed velocities, and the virtual displacements are exactly the displacements of rolling and of a turn in place and their sums. Section 8.9.2 will show that this suffices for the equations of motion, because there we use only the condition \(\mat A\,\delta\vek x = \vek 0\) on virtual displacements. We will not compute an example of a nonholonomic system by its motion: none of the sources we use has an example of that size.

8.4.4 Degrees of freedom and generalized coordinates

For the pendulum we saw that two numbers, the coordinates \(x\) and \(y\), and one constraint, the circle, give one number, the angle \(\theta\). Does the same hold in general? With \(m\) independent holonomic constraints we expect \(N - m\) free numbers, and the theorem of section 8.3 says that this is true. Now we name this count and say how we choose the numbers that describe it.

Definition 8.46 Degrees of freedom and generalized coordinates (prostostne stopnje in posplošene koordinate)

Let a system have a configuration \(\vek x \in \mathbb{R}^N\) and \(m\) holonomic constraints \(\vek f(\vek x, t) = \vek 0\), independent in every configuration and at every time under consideration at which they hold. The number of degrees of freedom (prostostne stopnje) is \(n = N - m\), for the pendulum \(n = 2 - 1 = 1\). Generalized coordinates (posplošene koordinate) are numbers \(q_1, \dots, q_{\mathrm{n}}\), gathered in a column \(\vek q \in \mathbb{R}^n\), together with a map \(\vek X\) that has \(n + 1\) numbers \((\vek q, t)\) as input and a configuration \(\vek x = \vek X(\vek q, t) \in \mathbb{R}^N\) as output, such that:

  1. \(\vek X\) is continuously differentiable on an open set, as the pendulum point moves smoothly along the circle when the angle \(\theta\) grows,
  2. \(\vek f\big(\vek X(\vek q, t), t\big) = \vek 0\) for every \((\vek q, t)\) of this set, so every position that \(\vek X\) describes satisfies the constraints, as every position \((l\sin\theta,\ -l\cos\theta)\) lies on the circle,
  3. the \(N \times n\) matrix of partial derivatives \(\partial\vek X/\partial\vek q\) has \(n\) pivots at every \((\vek q, t)\), so every nonzero \(\delta\vek q\) moves the position in first order, as \(\delta\theta\) moves the pendulum along the tangent.

Source: GR §1.2.3 (PDF p. 38), equation (1.14): \(n = 3N - R\) configuration parameters, the generalized coordinates. The requirement of pivots and the writing with a map \(\vek X\) with time are not from the source.

Why the third requirement? The matrix \(\partial\vek X/\partial\vek q\) tells how the configuration changes when we change one coordinate. For the pendulum with the coordinate \(\theta\) it is the column \(l\,(\cos\theta,\ \sin\theta)\): a change \(\delta\theta\) moves the point by \(l\,\delta\theta\) along the tangent. If the matrix had fewer than \(n\) pivots, there would be a nonzero \(\delta\vek q\) for which the configuration does not change in first order. The coordinates would be redundant: we count \(n\) of them, but the system moves in first order in fewer than \(n\) independent directions.

We get such a coordinate already for the pendulum if we write \(\theta = q^3\). Figure 8.62 shows the graph of this function above the axes \(q\) and \(\theta\). By the chain rule (lemma 8.30) then \(\partial\vek X/\partial q = 3q^2\,l\,(\cos q^3,\ \sin q^3)\), where \(3q^2\) is the slope of the graph. At \(q = 0{,}8\) the slope is \(1{,}92\), but at \(q = 0\) the graph is horizontal, the slope is \(3q^2 = 0\) and the column is zero, without a pivot. A change \(\delta q\) does not move the point in first order, so in this position \(q\) does not write the displacement along the tangent, although the pendulum can make it, and lemma 8.48 below would not hold there. With the toggle mechanism we will see another wrong choice, a coordinate at which the map \(\vek X\) is not differentiable.

The definition requires a map \(\vek X\). Does it exist? For the pendulum we guessed it, \(\vek X(\theta) = (l\sin\theta,\ -l\cos\theta)\) with the angle \(\theta\). Theorem 8.35 says that it always exists when the constraints are independent.

Lemma 8.47 Existence of generalized coordinates (obstoj posplošenih koordinat)

Let \(f_1, \dots, f_{\mathrm{m}}\) be independent holonomic constraints of a system with configuration \(\vek x \in \mathbb{R}^N\) in the configuration \(\vek x_0\) at time \(t_0\), and let \(\vek f(\vek x_0, t_0) = \vek 0\). Then we can choose generalized coordinates \(\vek q \in \mathbb{R}^{N - m}\) and a map \(\vek X\) of definition 8.46 that describe all configurations near \(\vek x_0\) which satisfy the constraints at times near \(t_0\), as \(x\) describes the arc of the circle near the point \(T\).

Proof

We must show that the constraints determine \(m\) coordinates by the remaining \(N - m\) coordinates and the time, and that the remaining coordinates are true generalized coordinates.

  1. By assumption the matrix \(\partial\vek f/\partial\vek x\) has \(m\) pivots at \((\vek x_0, t_0)\). We renumber the coordinates so that the pivots are in the last \(m\) columns. We write \(\vek x = (\vek y, \vek z)\) with \(\vek y \in \mathbb{R}^{N - m}\) and \(\vek z \in \mathbb{R}^m\). For the circle the pivot at the point \(T\) is in the column of the coordinate \(y\), because \(\partial f/\partial y = 2y = 1{,}6\) m \(\neq 0\), so \(y\) plays the role of \(\vek z\) and \(x\) the role of \(\vek y\).
  2. We treat the function \(\vek F(\vek y, t, \vek z) = \vek f\big((\vek y, \vek z), t\big)\) as a function of \(N + 1\) variables, where time counts among the first ones. The last \(m\) columns of its matrix of derivatives are the same as in \(\partial\vek f/\partial\vek x\), so they hold \(m\) pivots. Theorem 8.35 gives a continuously differentiable map \(\vek g(\vek y, t)\) with values in \(\mathbb{R}^m\) such that near \((\vek x_0, t_0)\) the solutions of the equation \(\vek F = \vek 0\) are exactly the points \(\vek z = \vek g(\vek y, t)\). By item 2 of the theorem the matrix of these \(m\) columns is invertible also at every point \(\big(\vek y, \vek g(\vek y, t)\big)\), so the constraints are independent in every configuration we obtain, as the definition requires. For the circle this is the arc near the point \(T\), the graph of the function \(y = g(x) = \sqrt{l^2 - x^2}\): to every \(x\) near \(0{,}6\) m belongs exactly one point of the circle near \(T\).
  3. We take \(\vek q = \vek y\) and \(\vek X(\vek q, t) = \big(\vek q, \vek g(\vek q, t)\big)\). The map is continuously differentiable (requirement 1), it satisfies the constraints by step 2 (requirement 2) and describes all nearby configurations that the constraints allow. For the circle \(q = x\) and \(\vek X(x) = (x, g(x))\), the point of the circle above \(x\).
  4. The matrix \(\partial\vek X/\partial\vek q\) is stacked from the identity matrix of order \(N - m\) and the matrix \(\partial\vek g/\partial\vek q\). The pivots of the identity matrix stand in the first \(N - m\) rows, so \(\partial\vek X/\partial\vek q\) has \(n = N - m\) pivots (requirement 3). For the circle this is the column \((1,\ g'(x))\) in the direction of the tangent, which because of the first component \(1\) is never zero.

Source: The proof is not from the source, GR (PDF p. 38) assumes the existence of the coordinates. We use theorem 8.35.

The lemma says that the number of numbers the system needs is really \(n = N - m\), and that the coordinates may be some of the original ones. For the pendulum this is for example \(q = x\) with \(y = -\sqrt{l^2 - x^2}\) (the lower arc of the circle), which fails at \(|x| = l\), at the leftmost and the rightmost point of the circle, where the rod is horizontal, the tangent vertical and the derivative \(\partial y/\partial x\) does not exist there (it grows beyond all bounds as \(|x|\) tends to \(l\)), or the angle \(\theta\), which is none of the original coordinates and fails nowhere. The choice is not unique.

Lemma 8.48 Virtual displacements in generalized coordinates (virtualni pomiki v posplošenih koordinatah)

Let \(\vek q\) be generalized coordinates of a system with \(m\) holonomic constraints \(\vek f(\vek x, t) = \vek 0\), independent in every configuration \(\vek X(\vek q, t)\), and \(\vek x = \vek X(\vek q, t)\). For every \(\delta\vek q \in \mathbb{R}^n\)

\[\delta\vek x = \sum_{\mathrm{s}=1}^n \frac{\partial\vek X}{\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}}\,\delta q_{\mathrm{s}} = \frac{\partial\vek X}{\partial\vek q}\,\delta\vek q \tag{8.33}\]

is an admissible virtual displacement, which satisfies (8.31), for the pendulum a displacement along the tangent. Every admissible virtual displacement \(\delta\vek x\) has the form (8.33) for exactly one \(\delta\vek q\). The derivatives are computed at the chosen time \(t\), which does not change in a virtual displacement.

Proof

We must show two things: that the displacement (8.33) satisfies (8.31) and that we obtain all solutions of this equation with it. We denote \(\mat A = \partial\vek f/\partial\vek x\) and \(\mat U = \partial\vek X/\partial\vek q\).

  1. At constant \(t\) we have \(\vek f\big(\vek X(\vek q, t), t\big) = \vek 0\) for every \(\vek q\). The derivative with respect to \(\vek q\) is zero. By the chain rule (lemma 8.30, item 1, applied to every component \(f_{\mathrm{r}}\), where the input \(t\) has zero derivatives with respect to \(\vek q\)) \(\mat A\,\mat U = \mat 0\), an \(m \times n\) matrix. Hence for every \(\delta\vek q\) we have \(\mat A\,(\mat U\,\delta\vek q) = \vek 0\), that is (8.31). On the circle of figure 8.52 this means that the gradient of the constraint, the row \(\mat A\), is perpendicular to the tangent, the column \(\mat U\), the direction in which the coordinate moves the point.
  2. The matrix \(\mat A\) has \(m\) pivots in the configuration \(\vek X(\vek q, t)\), because the constraints are independent there. In the elimination of the system \(\mat A\,\delta\vek x = \vek 0\), \(m\) unknowns are determined by the remaining \(n = N - m\), which we call the free unknowns. With the values of the free unknowns \(\delta\vek x\) is determined uniquely (back substitution, KREYSZIG §7.3 PDF p. 300-302). For the circle of figure 8.63 \(m = 1\) and \(n = 1\). At the point \(T\) the row \(\mat A\) is the gradient \(\nabla f = (1{,}2,\ 1{,}6)\) m. We take the pivot in the column \(\delta y\), because \(2y = 1{,}6\) m \(\neq 0\), so the free unknown is \(\delta x\), which we read on the horizontal line \(\delta x\). The solutions lie on the tangent, and once we choose \(\delta x\), \(\delta y\) is determined.
  3. Let \(\mat H\) be the square matrix of the rows of \(\mat U\) that correspond to the free unknowns. If \(\mat H\,\vek c = \vek 0\), the free unknowns of \(\mat U\,\vek c\) are zero. The column \(\mat U\,\vek c\) solves \(\mat A\,\delta\vek x = \vek 0\) (step 1), so \(\mat U\,\vek c = \vek 0\) (step 2). Since \(\mat U\) has \(n\) pivots, \(\vek c = \vek 0\). For the circle at the point \(T\), \(\mat U\) is the column \((1,\ g'(x)) = (1,\ -0{,}75)\) along the tangent, and \(\mat H\) is its first row, the shadow on the line \(\delta x\), \(\mat H = 1\). A nonzero arrow along the tangent has a nonzero shadow here. It is essential that the free unknowns are chosen by elimination as in step 2. At the point \(S = (l,\ 0)\) of the same circle the gradient is horizontal and the pivot is in the column \(\delta x\). The arrow along the tangent is vertical there, its shadow on the line \(\delta x\) is a point, \(\mat H = 0\), and \(\mat H\,c = 0\) would hold also for \(c \neq 0\) if we took \(\delta x\) as the free unknown.
  4. A square matrix for which \(\mat H\,\vek c = \vek 0\) has only the solution \(\vek c = \vek 0\) has \(n\) pivots in elimination, so for every column \(\vek s\) the system \(\mat H\,\vek c = \vek s\) has exactly one solution (back substitution).
  5. Let \(\delta\vek x\) be any solution of equation (8.31) and \(\vek s\) the column of its free unknowns. By step 4 we choose \(\vek c\) with \(\mat H\,\vek c = \vek s\). The difference \(\delta\vek x - \mat U\,\vek c\) solves (8.31) (step 1) and has zero free unknowns, so by step 2 it is \(\vek 0\). Hence \(\delta\vek x = \mat U\,\vek c\), and \(\vek c\) is unique because \(\mat U\) has \(n\) pivots. For the circle, the displacement \(\delta\vek x\) along the tangent has the shadow \(s\), so \(c = s/\mat H = s\) and \(\delta\vek x = s\,\mat U\), a multiple of the column \((1,\ g'(x))\).

Source: GR (1.15) (PDF p. 39) writes the virtual displacements \(\delta u_{\mathrm{ik}}\) with generalized coordinates and says that the \(\delta q_{\mathrm{s}}\) are independent. The proof with pivots is not from the source.

The lemma says that the coordinates \(\delta q_{\mathrm{s}}\) write every virtual displacement and that we may choose them freely, as GR says (PDF p. 39). We therefore no longer need to compute the constraint, the displacements allowed by it are already built into the coordinates: for the pendulum \(\delta\theta\) alone gives a displacement along the tangent, which respects the constraint. The virtual work of forces on the system, written with \(\delta q_{\mathrm{s}}\), is treated in section 8.5.

Why are the derivatives \(\partial\vek X/\partial t\) absent from a virtual displacement in a rheonomic constraint, although they are in the real one? Along a real motion \(\vek q(t)\) the point has the velocity \(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\vek X(\vek q(t), t) = \sum_{\mathrm{s}} (\partial\vek X/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}})\,\dot q_{\mathrm{s}} + \partial\vek X/\partial t\) (chain rule, lemma 8.30, the same as GR (1.24), PDF p. 42). The last term is there because the constraint itself moves too, in figure 8.64 the support, which carries the point along also at an unchanged angle. A virtual displacement compares two positions at the same time, because the forces with which we test it are counted for this position (section 4.4.2), so \(\delta\vek x = \sum_{\mathrm{s}} (\partial\vek X/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}})\,\delta q_{\mathrm{s}}\) and \(\partial\vek X/\partial t\) does not enter it (GR (1.15), PDF p. 39, SHABANA, PDF p. 107: a virtual displacement is consistent with the constraints at the given instant \(t\)). Let us show that this is not only a notation (figure 8.64). We hang pendulum \(A\) on a support that slides along a horizontal rail with the speed \(v = 0{,}5\) m/s, so \(\vek X(\theta, t) = (vt + l\sin\theta,\ -l\cos\theta)\). We draw the position at time \(t\) grey dashed, the position at time \(t + \Delta t\) at the same angle \(\theta = \pi/6\) orange. In the time \(\Delta t = 0{,}1\) s the support moves by \(v\,\Delta t = 0{,}05\) m and with it the point by the same horizontal displacement, the part of the real displacement caused by the support, \(\vek X(\theta, t + \Delta t) - \vek X(\theta, t) = (v\,\Delta t,\ 0)\), greatly enlarged in the figure. The rod pulls the point toward the support with a force of magnitude \(S = 10\) N, that is with the force \(-S\,(\sin\theta,\ -\cos\theta)\). On the horizontal displacement only its horizontal component \(S\sin\theta\) does work, and it points against the displacement, so the real work is

\[-S\sin\theta \cdot v\,\Delta t = -10 \cdot 0{,}5 \cdot 0{,}05 = -0{,}25\ \text{J} .\]

The virtual displacement \(l\,(\cos\theta,\ \sin\theta)\,\delta\theta\), however, lies along the tangent, perpendicular to the rod, and the force does on it the work \(-S\,l\,(\sin\theta\cos\theta - \cos\theta\sin\theta)\,\delta\theta = 0\). If the angle \(\theta\) changes meanwhile as well, to first order only a displacement along the tangent is added to the real displacement, and on it the force of the rod does no work, as we have just computed. To first order the real work is therefore the same, \(-0{,}25\) J (SymPy). The rod is thus an ideal constraint (definition 8.3): the virtual work is zero, but the real work is not, for a rheonomic constraint.

8.4.5 Examples

Each of the previous definitions and lemmas is written in general. We test them on the systems that will accompany us through the chapter: on pendulum \(A\), the rod on two springs \(C\), the toggle mechanism \(D\), and on the double pendulum and the free rigid body.

Pendulum \(A\). \(N = 2\), \(m = 1\), \(n = 1\), the coordinate \(q = \theta\) and \(\vek X(\theta) = (l\sin\theta,\ -l\cos\theta)\). By theorem 8.13 on the derivatives of sine and cosine \(\partial\vek X/\partial\theta = l\,(\cos\theta,\ \sin\theta)\), which has one pivot (at least one component is nonzero). At \(\theta = \pi/6\) we have \(\partial\vek X/\partial\theta \approx (866,\ 500)\) mm. The matrix of derivatives of the constraint is \((2x, 2y) \approx (1000,\ -1732)\) mm and their product is \(2l^2(\sin\theta\cos\theta - \cos\theta\sin\theta) = 0\), as lemma 8.48 claims. The virtual displacement \(\delta\vek x = l\,(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\,\delta\theta\) is perpendicular to the rod, as figure 8.58 shows.

Rod on two springs \(C\). A rigid rod of length \(L = 2\) m lies horizontally on two vertical springs, one at each end. A guide holds its centroid \(G\) on a vertical line, so it excludes horizontal motion (this is our idealization, made to keep two numbers, and not a restriction of the rod itself). The configuration is \(\vek x = (x_{\mathrm{G}}, y_{\mathrm{G}}, \varphi)\) with the centroid \((x_{\mathrm{G}}, y_{\mathrm{G}})\) and the rotation \(\varphi\) of the rod, so \(N = 3\). The guide gives one constraint \(f = x_{\mathrm{G}} = 0\), so \(m = 1\) and \(n = 2\). The coordinates are the vertical displacement \(y\) of the centroid and the rotation \(\varphi\), \(\vek X(y, \varphi) = (0, y, \varphi)\) (figure 8.65). The matrix \(\partial\vek X/\partial\vek q\) is \(\left[\begin{smallmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\right]\) with two pivots, the matrix of the constraint \((1, 0, 0)\) has one pivot and \((1, 0, 0)\,\partial\vek X/\partial\vek q = (0, 0)\). The virtual displacement is \(\delta\vek x = (0, \delta y, \delta\varphi)\), the guide does not allow a horizontal displacement.

Example 8.49 Toggle mechanism \(D\) (kolenasti mehanizem)

Two bars \(AB\) and \(BC\), each of length \(a = 0{,}5\) m, are joined in the hinge \(B\). The bar \(AB\) is fixed by a pin at \(A = (0, 0)\), and the end \(C\) of the bar \(BC\) slides along a horizontal line through \(A\) (figure 8.65). We describe each bar with the coordinates of its centroid and an angle: \((x_1, y_1, \varphi_1)\) and \((x_2, y_2, \varphi_2)\), as for a rigid body in definition 8.42, where \(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\) is the angle of the bar from the axis \(x\) in the direction from the first toward the second end. The configuration has \(N = 6\) numbers. There are five constraints.

  1. The pin \(A\) holds the first end of the bar \(AB\) at the origin. The centroid is at \(a/2\) from it in the direction \(\varphi_1\): \(f_1 = x_1 - \tfrac a2\cos\varphi_1 = 0\) and \(f_2 = y_1 - \tfrac a2\sin\varphi_1 = 0\).
  2. The hinge \(B\) is the second end of the bar \(AB\), which is also the first end of the bar \(BC\): \(f_3 = x_2 - a\cos\varphi_1 - \tfrac a2\cos\varphi_2 = 0\) and \(f_4 = y_2 - a\sin\varphi_1 - \tfrac a2\sin\varphi_2 = 0\).
  3. The slider \(C\), the second end of the bar \(BC\), is at height zero: \(f_5 = y_2 + \tfrac a2\sin\varphi_2 = 0\).
  4. For \(\varphi_1 = \theta\) the constraints 3 to 5 are satisfied with \(\varphi_2 = -\theta\): \(f_5 = 0\) gives \(y_2 = -\tfrac a2\sin\varphi_2\), which we insert into \(f_4\) and get \(\sin\varphi_2 = -\sin\varphi_1\), so \(\varphi_2 = -\varphi_1\) for angles between \(-\pi/2\) and \(\pi/2\), where the sine is strictly increasing, because its derivative \(\cos\theta\) is positive there (theorem 8.13). We leave out the branch where the bar \(BC\) points backward in this example. The remaining coordinates are \(x_2 = \tfrac32 a\cos\theta\) and \(y_2 = \tfrac12 a\sin\theta\). At \(\theta = \pi/6\) this gives for the centroid of the bar \(BC\) \(x_2 = 0{,}6495\) m and \(y_2 = 0{,}125\) m.
  5. The matrix of derivatives \(\partial\vek f/\partial\vek x\) (\(5 \times 6\)) has, in the columns \(x_1\), \(y_1\), \(x_2\), \(y_2\) and \(\varphi_1\), after subtracting the fourth row from the fifth, the pivots \(1\), \(1\), \(1\), \(1\) and \(a\cos\theta\) (SymPy). For \(-\pi/2 < \theta < \pi/2\), \(a\cos\theta \neq 0\), so the constraints are independent in every position of this branch. At \(\theta = \pm\pi/2\), where slider \(C\) reaches pin \(A\), there are only four pivots. So \(m = 5\) and \(n = 6 - 5 = 1\). One coordinate suffices. We choose \(q = \theta\), \(\vek X(\theta) = \big(\tfrac a2\cos\theta,\ \tfrac a2\sin\theta,\ \theta,\ \tfrac32 a\cos\theta,\ \tfrac12 a\sin\theta,\ -\theta\big)\).
  6. The matrix \(\partial\vek X/\partial\theta\) is a column of six numbers, the displacements of the centroids and the changes of the angles of the bars per unit \(\delta\theta\), at \(\theta = \pi/6\) it is \((-0{,}125,\ 0{,}2165,\ 1,\ -0{,}375,\ 0{,}2165,\ -1)\) (SymPy), and has one pivot. The product \(\partial\vek f/\partial\vek x\,\partial\vek X/\partial\theta\) is a zero column with five zeros (SymPy), as lemma 8.48 requires.

Why did we choose the angle \(\theta\) as the coordinate and not the horizontal position of the slider \(x_{\mathrm{C}} = 2a\cos\theta\), which seems more natural? Section 8.3.4 has already given the answer with pivots: at \(\theta = \pm 0{,}1\) rad the slider \(C\) has the same position \(x_{\mathrm{C}} = 0{,}9950\) m, while the hinge \(B\) is once above the guide and once below it. Step 2 of the proof of lemma 8.47, applied to the system with the added equation \(x_{\mathrm{C}} = c\), gives \(x_{\mathrm{C}}\) as a coordinate where that system has six pivots. At \(\theta = 0\) it has only five, and there the lemma says nothing. That \(x_{\mathrm{C}}\) really cannot be a coordinate there we show with the graph.

On the axes we draw \(x_{\mathrm{C}}\) against \(\theta\). The curve \(x_{\mathrm{C}} = 2a\cos\theta\) has its top \(2a = 1\) m at \(\theta = 0\), where the bars lie on one line. There the tangent of the curve is horizontal, because the derivative \(\mathrm{d}x_{\mathrm{C}}/\mathrm{d}\theta = -2a\sin\theta\) is zero at \(\theta = 0\). A horizontal line at the height \(c\) below the top therefore cuts the curve twice, at \(\theta\) and at \(-\theta\): one value of \(x_{\mathrm{C}}\), two positions of the mechanism. How far from zero are the angles? By lemma 8.20 on small angles the drop is

\[2a - c = 2a\,(1 - \cos\theta) \le a\,\theta^2 , \qquad\text{so}\qquad |\theta| \ge \sqrt{\frac{2a - c}{a}} .\]

Already a drop of \(1\) mm, to \(c = 0{,}999\) m, gives \(|\theta| \ge \sqrt{0{,}001/0{,}5} = 0{,}0447\) rad, that is \(2{,}56°\) (exactly \(0{,}04473\) rad, NumPy). At the dead position the dependence of \(\theta\) on \(x_{\mathrm{C}}\) is a square root, not linear: a drop a hundred times smaller gives an angle only ten times smaller. So \(x_{\mathrm{C}}\) cannot be a coordinate at \(\theta = 0\). If it were, the angle \(\theta\), one of the components of the map \(\vek X\), would be a continuously differentiable function of \(x_{\mathrm{C}}\) with \(x_{\mathrm{C}} = 2a\cos\theta\). The derivative of this equation with respect to \(x_{\mathrm{C}}\) (chain rule, lemma 8.30) would give \(1 = -2a\sin\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta/\mathrm{d}x_{\mathrm{C}}\), so \(1 = 0\) at \(\theta = 0\). There the first requirement of definition 8.46 fails. The angle \(\theta\), however, meets all three requirements in every position. The choice of the coordinate is therefore not a matter of taste: where it fails, the pivot condition shows.

Example 8.50 Double pendulum and the choice of coordinates (dvojno nihalo in izbira koordinat)

Two mass points, \(P_1\) and \(P_2\), hang one on the other. Panel (a) shows the pendulum with both choices of the second coordinate. The point \(P_1\) is on a rigid massless rod of length \(l_1 = 1\) m from the pin \(O\), the point \(P_2\) on a rod of length \(l_2 = 0{,}5\) m from \(P_1\). The motion is in a plane. The configuration has four numbers, the coordinates of both points, \(N = 4\), and two constraints, the lengths of the rods, so \(n = 2\) (GR Example 1.4, PDF p. 40-41).

  1. As coordinates we choose the angles \(\theta_1\) and \(\theta_2\) of both rods from the vertical downward. Then \(\vek X(\theta_1, \theta_2) = \big(l_1\sin\theta_1,\ -l_1\cos\theta_1,\ l_1\sin\theta_1 + l_2\sin\theta_2,\ -l_1\cos\theta_1 - l_2\cos\theta_2\big)\).
  2. At \(\theta_1 = \pi/6\) and \(\theta_2 = \pi/3\) the points are approximately at \((500,\ -866)\) mm and \((933,\ -1116)\) mm. The matrix of derivatives \(\partial\vek X/\partial\vek q\) is
    \[\begin{bmatrix} 0{,}8660 & 0 \\ 0{,}5000 & 0 \\ 0{,}8660 & 0{,}2500 \\ 0{,}5000 & 0{,}4330 \end{bmatrix} \text{m}\]
    and has two pivots, in the first and second column (the first column is nonzero and the second is not parallel to it). The constraints are independent too: the matrix of derivatives \(\left[\begin{smallmatrix} 2x_1 & 2y_1 & 0 & 0 \\ -2(x_2 - x_1) & -2(y_2 - y_1) & 2(x_2 - x_1) & 2(y_2 - y_1) \end{smallmatrix}\right]\) has at these numbers approximately the rows \((1000,\ -1732,\ 0,\ 0)\) mm and \((-866,\ 500,\ 866,\ -500)\) mm, of which the second is nonzero also in the last two columns and the first is not, so they have two pivots. The product of this matrix with the matrix of derivatives \(\partial\vek X/\partial\vek q\) is a zero matrix (SymPy), as lemma 8.48 requires.
  3. GR chooses the second angle relative to the first, \(\alpha = \theta_2 - \theta_1\), the angle between the extension of the first rod and the second rod. The new coordinates \((\theta_1, \alpha)\) are obtained from the old ones by \(\theta_2 = \theta_1 + \alpha\). The derivative \(\partial\vek X/\partial(\theta_1, \alpha)\) is the product of the old matrix with \(\left[\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\right]\) (chain rule, lemma 8.30, item 2), so with numbers
    \[\begin{bmatrix} 0{,}8660 & 0 \\ 0{,}5000 & 0 \\ 1{,}1160 & 0{,}2500 \\ 0{,}9330 & 0{,}4330 \end{bmatrix} \text{m} ,\]
    which also has two pivots, since the determinant of \(\left[\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\right]\) is \(1\). Both choices are generalized coordinates of the same system, only the writings of the same virtual displacement differ.
  4. The same change \(\delta\theta_1\), however, means a different motion in the two choices. We raise the first rod from the position before the shift by \(15°\), enlarged in the figure. If \(\theta_2\) stays the same (panel b), the second rod moves parallel to itself and both points get the same displacement, \(261\) mm long. If \(\alpha\) stays the same (panel c), the whole pendulum turns about \(O\) as one rigid body. The point \(P_1\) moves by the same \(261\) mm, the point \(P_2\), which is further from \(O\), by \(380\) mm (NumPy). So the columns \(\partial\vek X/\partial\theta_1\) of the two matrices differ (\(0{,}8660\) and \(1{,}1160\) in the third row), although they describe the same system. We therefore always state a coordinate together with which other coordinates stay constant when it changes.

Free rigid body. A body in the plane without constraints has \(N = 3\), \(m = 0\), \(n = 3\) (the point \(O\) and the angle). In space we write the configuration of a body with the point \(O\) and the matrix \(\mat Q\) (a \(3 \times 3\) orthogonal matrix, definition 2.46) that rotates the body from the initial position, so with \(3 + 9 = 12\) numbers. The constraint is \(\mat Q^\T\mat Q = \mat I\): six equations, because the matrix \(\mat Q^\T\mat Q\) is symmetric and has \(3 + 2 + 1 = 6\) different elements. In figure 8.56, panel c, these are the three columns of the matrix \(\mat Q\) with length \(1\) (three equations on the diagonal) and the three right angles between them (three equations off the diagonal). Independence is simple. The derivative of the left side in the direction \(\delta\mat Q\) is \(\mat Q^\T\delta\mat Q + \delta\mat Q^\T\mat Q\). For every symmetric matrix \(\mat S\) we choose \(\delta\mat Q = \tfrac12\mat Q\,\mat S\) and get \(\tfrac12\mat S + \tfrac12\mat S^\T = \mat S\), so the \(6 \times 9\) system is solvable for every right side, which is possible only if elimination gives six pivots (otherwise, with a zero row, some right sides would turn out unsolvable). At \(\mat Q = \mat I\) the same is seen directly: the \(6 \times 9\) matrix has six pivots (SymPy). So \(n = 12 - 6 = 6\), three displacements and three rotations. We do not choose three angles that would describe the rotation by three numbers in this chapter: section 8.8 works directly with the matrix \(\mat Q\).
