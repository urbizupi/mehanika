Chapter 8 Analytical dynamics (analitična dinamika)

8.4 Constraints and generalized coordinates (vezi in posplošene koordinate)

In section 1.5.1 we wrote that the position of a mechanism is described by as many numbers as the mechanism has independent ways of moving, and that these numbers will be given a name. For the truss of section 4.1.4 two numbers were enough, the displacements of the node \(C\) in the directions \(x\) and \(y\). There we only announced the numbers. Now we answer the three questions that this promise opens. What is the position of a system that has several points and bodies and links between them? How many independent numbers do we need, and how do we read this count from the links? And how do we choose the numbers so that a virtual displacement of the system (section 4.4.2) is written with them?

8.4.1 Motion of a rigid body

We start with one body. How many numbers describe the position of a rigid body (definition 1.11), for example of a bar of mechanism D? A rigid body keeps the distances between all pairs of points. To be able to follow each point, we imagine the body built into a rigid frame that fills all of space and moves with it, like a coordinate system fixed to the body. We denote a point of the frame by the position vector (krajevni vektor) \(\vek X\) that it has at time \(t = 0\), and by \(\vek x(t)\) the position vector of the same point at time \(t\). For every \(t\) the map \(\vek X \mapsto \vek x(t)\) keeps all distances. Which such maps exist at all? A translation by a vector \(\vek c\) keeps them, since all vectors between points stay the same. A rotation with an orthogonal matrix \(\mat Q\) (definition 2.39) keeps them too, because it keeps the scalar product, \((\mat Q\vek a)\cdot(\mat Q\vek b) = \vek a^\T\mat Q^\T\mat Q\,\vek b = \vek a\cdot\vek b\), and the distance is the root of the scalar product of the difference with itself (lemma 2.9). The next lemma says that there are no others.

Lemma 8.37 Distance-preserving map (preslikava, ki ohranja razdalje)

Let \(\vek f\) be a map that assigns to every position vector \(\vek x\) in space a position vector \(\vek f(\vek x)\), and let \(|\vek f(\vek x) - \vek f(\vek y)| = |\vek x - \vek y|\) hold for all \(\vek x\) and \(\vek y\). Then there are a vector \(\vek c\) and an orthogonal \(3 \times 3\) matrix \(\mat Q\) with

\[\vek f(\vek x) = \vek c + \mat Q\,\vek x \qquad \text{for every } \vek x . \tag{8.23}\]
Proof

We must show that \(\vek f\) is, up to the translation \(\vek c\), a linear map with an orthogonal matrix. First we get scalar products from the distances, then linearity, finally the matrix.

  1. Let \(\vek c = \vek f(\vek 0)\) and \(\vek g(\vek x) = \vek f(\vek x) - \vek c\). The difference \(\vek g(\vek x) - \vek g(\vek y) = \vek f(\vek x) - \vek f(\vek y)\) does not contain \(\vek c\), so \(\vek g\) keeps distances and \(\vek g(\vek 0) = \vek 0\).
  2. For \(\vek y = \vek 0\) step 1 gives \(|\vek g(\vek x)| = |\vek x|\).
  3. By the formula for the square of a difference (lemma 2.9), \(|\vek g(\vek x) - \vek g(\vek y)|^2 = |\vek g(\vek x)|^2 - 2\,\vek g(\vek x)\cdot\vek g(\vek y) + |\vek g(\vek y)|^2\), and likewise \(|\vek x - \vek y|^2 = |\vek x|^2 - 2\,\vek x\cdot\vek y + |\vek y|^2\). By step 1 the left sides are equal, and by step 2 the squares of the lengths are equal, hence \(\vek g(\vek x)\cdot\vek g(\vek y) = \vek x\cdot\vek y\).
  4. Let \(\vek d = \vek g(\vek x + \vek y) - \vek g(\vek x) - \vek g(\vek y)\). We expand the square \(|\vek d|^2\) into six terms. We replace the squares of lengths and the scalar products by steps 2 and 3 with those without \(\vek g\):
    \[|\vek d|^2 = |\vek x + \vek y|^2 + |\vek x|^2 + |\vek y|^2 - 2\,(\vek x + \vek y)\cdot\vek x - 2\,(\vek x + \vek y)\cdot\vek y + 2\,\vek x\cdot\vek y .\]
    The terms \(-2\,(\vek x + \vek y)\cdot\vek x - 2\,(\vek x + \vek y)\cdot\vek y\) together give \(-2\,|\vek x + \vek y|^2\), so \(|\vek d|^2 = |\vek x|^2 + |\vek y|^2 + 2\,\vek x\cdot\vek y - |\vek x + \vek y|^2 = 0\). A vector of length zero is the zero vector, hence \(\vek g(\vek x + \vek y) = \vek g(\vek x) + \vek g(\vek y)\).
  5. For a number \(k\) we expand in the same way \(|\vek g(k\,\vek x) - k\,\vek g(\vek x)|^2 = |k\,\vek x|^2 - 2k\,(k\,\vek x)\cdot\vek x + k^2\,|\vek x|^2 = 0\), hence \(\vek g(k\,\vek x) = k\,\vek g(\vek x)\).
  6. Let \(\mat Q\) be the matrix whose columns are \(\vek g(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\), \(\vek g(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}})\) and \(\vek g(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}})\). By steps 4 and 5 the vector \(\vek x = x_1\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} + x_2\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} + x_3\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) is mapped to \(x_1\,\vek g(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) + x_2\,\vek g(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}) + x_3\,\vek g(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}) = \mat Q\,\vek x\). Hence \(\vek f(\vek x) = \vek c + \mat Q\,\vek x\).
  7. The element \((i, j)\) of the product \(\mat Q^\T\mat Q\) is the scalar product of columns \(i\) and \(j\) of \(\mat Q\), so by step 3 the scalar product \(\vek e_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\), which is \(1\) for \(i = j\) and \(0\) otherwise. Hence \(\mat Q^\T\mat Q = \mat I\).
  8. By theorem 2.52 and points 5 and 6 of lemma 2.51, \((\det\mat Q)^2 = \det\mat Q^\T\det\mat Q = \det\mat I = 1\), so \(\det\mat Q \neq 0\) and by theorem 2.54 \(\mat Q\) has an inverse matrix. From step 7 it follows that \(\mat Q^\T = \mat Q^\T(\mat Q\,\mat Q^{-1}) = (\mat Q^\T\mat Q)\,\mat Q^{-1} = \mat Q^{-1}\), so also \(\mat Q\,\mat Q^\T = \mat I\), and \(\mat Q\) is orthogonal.

Source: The proof is not from the source. SHABANA §2.1-2.2 (PDF pp. 48-50) uses a transformation matrix \(\mat A\) with \(\mat A\mat A^\T = \mat I\) and does not prove that every motion of a rigid body has this form.

Step 8 also shows that \(\det\mat Q = 1\) or \(\det\mat Q = -1\). A reflection, for example \(\mathrm{diag}(1, 1, -1)\), is orthogonal with determinant \(-1\) and also keeps distances. But a body that is the mirror image of another cannot be brought into the original position by a continuous motion, just as a right glove cannot be turned into a left one. We therefore exclude reflection in the written form of the motion and prove this afterwards.

Definition 8.38 Motion of a rigid body (gibanje togega telesa)

Let \(\vek X\) be the position vector of a point of the rigid frame at time \(0\). The motion of a rigid body on the interval \(0 \le t \le t_1\) is the expression of the position vector of the same point at time \(t\),

\[\vek x(t) = \vek c(t) + \mat Q(t)\,\vek X , \tag{8.24}\]

where \(\vek c(t)\) is a vector, \(\mat Q(t)\) is an orthogonal \(3 \times 3\) matrix (definition 2.39), \(\vek c(0) = \vek 0\), \(\mat Q(0) = \mat I\), and the elements of the vector \(\vek c\) and the matrix \(\mat Q\) are twice continuously differentiable functions of time.

Source: SHABANA §2.1 (PDF p. 48), where \(\vek r = \vek R + \mat A\bar{\vek u}\) writes the position of a point of a rigid body with a vector and a transformation matrix. Orthogonality \(\mat A\mat A^\T = \mat I\) is in §2.2 (PDF pp. 49-50).

The vector \(\vek c(t)\) is the position of the point of the frame that is at the origin at time \(0\), and the matrix \(\mat Q(t)\) tells how the frame has rotated up to time \(t\). That this form is really the only possible one is stated by lemma 8.37: at every time the map \(\vek X \mapsto \vek x(t)\) keeps distances. Twice differentiability will be needed for the accelerations in the second part of the chapter.

Lemma 8.39 A rigid motion does not reflect (gibanje togega telesa ne zrcali)

In a motion of a rigid body as in definition 8.38, \(\det\mat Q(t) = 1\) for every \(t\).

Proof

We must show that the determinant, which equals \(1\) at time \(0\), cannot jump to \(-1\). We use that a continuous function that passes between two values must also stop at every value in between.

  1. By step 8 of the proof of lemma 8.37, \((\det\mat Q(t))^2 = 1\), so \(\det\mat Q(t)\) equals \(1\) or \(-1\) for every \(t\).
  2. The elements of \(\mat Q(t)\) are differentiable functions of time, hence continuous (lemma 1.79). The determinant is a sum of products of elements of the matrix (definition 2.50), so by point 1 of corollary 1.64 on the sum and product of continuous functions, \(t \mapsto \det\mat Q(t)\) is continuous.
  3. At time \(0\), \(\det\mat Q(0) = \det\mat I = 1\) (lemma 2.51, point 5).
  4. Suppose \(\det\mat Q(t_2) = -1\) for some \(t_2\). The continuous function equals \(1\) at time \(0\) and \(-1\) at time \(t_2\), so by theorem 2.57 on the intermediate value it takes the value \(0\) in between. This contradicts step 1. Hence \(\det\mat Q(t) = 1\) for every \(t\).

Source: The proof is not from the source.

The proof has shown that a rotation keeps orientation at every time. In plane motion this means that the rotation is described by a single angle.

Lemma 8.40 Position of a rigid body in the plane (lega togega telesa v ravnini)

Let a rigid body move according to definition 8.38 in the plane \(xy\): the vector \(\vek c(t)\) lies in the plane \(xy\) and \(\mat Q(t)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) for every \(t\). Then for every \(t\) there is an angle \(\varphi\), measured in radians, such that

\[\mat Q(t) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\varphi & -\sin\varphi & 0 \\ \sin\varphi & \cos\varphi & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} . \tag{8.25}\]

Let \(O\) be a point of the body in the plane \(xy\) with position \(\vek o\) at time \(t\), and \(\vek r_0\) the vector in the plane \(xy\) from \(O\) to a point of the body at time \(0\). At time \(t\) this point has the position

\[\vek x = \vek o + \mat R(\varphi)\,\vek r_0 , \qquad \mat R(\varphi) = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\varphi & -\sin\varphi \\ \sin\varphi & \cos\varphi \end{bmatrix} , \tag{8.26}\]

where we write vectors of the plane \(xy\) with two components.

Proof

We must show that the matrix \(\mat Q\) is a rotation about the axis \(z\), and then express the position of the point by the point \(O\) and the angle.

  1. By step 7 of the proof of lemma 8.37 the columns \(\mat Q\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\mat Q\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\mat Q\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) are unit vectors and pairwise perpendicular. The first two are perpendicular to \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), so their component \(z\) is zero. We write \(\mat Q\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = (u_1, u_2, 0)\) and \(\mat Q\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} = (w_1, w_2, 0)\).
  2. We have \(u_1^2 + u_2^2 = 1\), so \(-1 \le u_1 \le 1\) (lemma 1.28). By definition 8.9, item 1, the cosine on \([0, \pi]\) is the inverse function of the function from lemma 8.6, which is defined on \([-1, 1]\). Hence there is \(\theta_0 \in [0, \pi]\) with \(\cos\theta_0 = u_1\), and \(\sin\theta_0 = \sqrt{1 - u_1^2} = |u_2|\). For \(u_2 \ge 0\) let \(\varphi = \theta_0\). For \(u_2 < 0\) we have \(u_1^2 < 1\) and \(\theta_0 > 0\), and we let \(\varphi = -\theta_0\): by item 2 of the same definition \(\cos\varphi = u_1\) and \(\sin\varphi = -|u_2| = u_2\). In both cases \((u_1, u_2) = (\cos\varphi, \sin\varphi)\).
  3. Let \(\lambda = -w_1\sin\varphi + w_2\cos\varphi\). The columns are perpendicular, \(w_1\cos\varphi + w_2\sin\varphi = 0\), and \(\cos^2\varphi + \sin^2\varphi = 1\) (lemma 8.10, item 1). Hence \(-\lambda\sin\varphi = w_1\sin^2\varphi - w_2\sin\varphi\cos\varphi = w_1\sin^2\varphi + w_1\cos^2\varphi = w_1\) and in the same way \(\lambda\cos\varphi = w_2\), so \((w_1, w_2) = \lambda\,(-\sin\varphi, \cos\varphi)\). The column is a unit vector, so \(\lambda^2 = \lambda^2(\sin^2\varphi + \cos^2\varphi) = w_1^2 + w_2^2 = 1\) and \(\lambda\) is \(1\) or \(-1\).
  4. For \(\lambda = -1\) we would have \(\mat Q\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} = (\sin\varphi, -\cos\varphi, 0)\) and by (2.51) \(\det\mat Q = -\cos^2\varphi - \sin^2\varphi = -1\), which lemma 8.39 excludes. So \(\lambda = 1\), and the columns \(\mat Q\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\mat Q\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) give the matrix (8.25).
  5. Let \(\vek X_{\mathrm{O}}\) be the position of the point \(O\) at time \(0\) and \(\vek X = \vek X_{\mathrm{O}} + \vek r_0\) the position of the point of the body. By (8.24), \(\vek x - \vek o = (\vek c + \mat Q\,\vek X) - (\vek c + \mat Q\,\vek X_{\mathrm{O}}) = \mat Q\,\vek r_0\). For \(\vek r_0 = (r_1, r_2, 0)\), by (8.25) \(\mat Q\,\vek r_0 = (r_1\cos\varphi - r_2\sin\varphi,\ r_1\sin\varphi + r_2\cos\varphi,\ 0)\), which in the components \(x\) and \(y\) is exactly \(\mat R(\varphi)\,\vek r_0\). This is (8.26).

Source: The proof is not from a source.

The lemma has shown that the position of a rigid body in the plane is determined by three numbers, the two coordinates of the point \(O\) and the angle \(\varphi\), and that the position of every other point is computed from them with (8.26). The rotation matrix of the axes from definition 2.37 is the transpose of the matrix \(\mat R(\varphi)\), because there the rows are the new axes.

8.4.2 Position of a system and constraints

The first system on which we test this is a pendulum (nihalo), which we call pendulum \(A\). A point of mass \(m = 1\) kg, that is, a body so small that its position is described by the position of one point, hangs at the end of a rigid massless rod of length \(l = 1\) m. The other end of the rod is fixed in a pin at the point \(O\). The motion is in a plane, gravity points down. We could describe the position of the point with Cartesian coordinates \(x\) and \(y\) with the origin at \(O\) and the axis \(y\) pointing up (definition 1.12). The two numbers are not independent, however, because the rod keeps the point at distance \(l\) from \(O\):

\[x^2 + y^2 = l^2 . \tag{8.27}\]

The same position is described by one number, the angle \(\theta\) of the rod from the vertical downward, measured in radians (definition 8.7). The point has the coordinates \(x = l\sin\theta\) and \(y = -l\cos\theta\), which satisfy equation (8.27) for every \(\theta\) (figure 8.10). At \(\theta = \pi/6 = 0{,}5236\) rad, that is \(30°\), we have \((x, y) = (0{,}5000,\ -0{,}8660)\) m. Two numbers and one relation thus leave one free number.

The condition (8.27) is an example of a constraint (vez, section 4.4.2). To count constraints for an arbitrary system, we must first say what the position of a system is, and then what a written constraint looks like. First the position.

Definition 8.41 Configuration of a system (konfiguracija sistema)

Let a system consist of finitely many mass points, where we imagine all the mass gathered in a point, and rigid bodies (definition 1.11) in a plane. The configuration (konfiguracija) of the system is a column \(\vek x \in \mathbb{R}^N\) of numbers that determine the position of all points and bodies. A point has two numbers, the coordinates \((x, y)\) in a Cartesian coordinate system. A rigid body has three numbers, the coordinates of a chosen point \(O\) of the body and the angle of rotation \(\varphi\) of the body about this point, measured in radians (definition 8.7), from which by lemma 8.40 we compute the position of every point of the body. Points in space have three numbers. The number \(N\) is the count of all these numbers, so \(N = 2p + 3b\) for \(p\) points and \(b\) bodies in the plane. A configuration is an element of the space \(\mathbb{R}^N\) (section 1.5.1), not a vector in the space in which the system lies.

Source: GR §1.2.2 (PDF p. 36) writes the configuration of \(N\) particles with \(3N\) displacements. The extension to rigid bodies with a point and an angle is not from the sources. That the position of a rigid body in the plane is really determined by these three numbers is proved by lemma 8.40.

A configuration is thus a list of numbers, not a picture. For the pendulum \(N = 2\) and \(\vek x = (x, y)\). For the truss of section 4.1.4, where the nodes \(A\) and \(B\) stand still, the position of the node \(C\) is determined by \(\vek x = (u, v)\), the displacements from the position before loading, so \(N = 2\) and there is no constraint that links \(u\) and \(v\).

We write a constraint as an equation between the numbers of the configuration. For the pendulum it is equation (8.27). For a mechanism with a support that we move it may also depend on time.

Definition 8.42 Holonomic, scleronomic and rheonomic constraint (holonomna, skleronomna in reonomna vez)

Let \(\vek x \in \mathbb{R}^N\) be the configuration of a system and \(t\) the time. A holonomic constraint (holonomna vez) is an equation

\[f(\vek x, t) = 0 , \tag{8.28}\]

where \(f\) is a continuously differentiable function with \(N + 1\) numbers as input and one number as output. A system with \(m\) holonomic constraints \(f_1, \dots, f_{\mathrm{m}}\) has a column \(\vek f = (f_1, \dots, f_{\mathrm{m}})\) and an \(m \times N\) matrix of partial derivatives \(\partial\vek f/\partial\vek x\), which has the number \(\partial f_{\mathrm{r}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{s}}\) at the place \((r, s)\). The constraints are independent (neodvisne) in the configuration \(\vek x\) at time \(t\) when this matrix at \((\vek x, t)\) has \(m\) pivots under Gauss elimination (section 6.3.4), where we swap rows if a pivot would be zero and skip a column without a pivot. A constraint is scleronomic (skleronomna) if \(f\) does not contain the time \(t\), and rheonomic (reonomna) if it does.

Source: GR §1.2.2 (PDF p. 36), equation (1.12) and the definitions of scleronomic and rheonomic constraints. The requirement of \(m\) pivots is the condition of theorem 8.34, not from the source.

Why do we require independence? We count constraints in order to subtract their number from the number of numbers in the configuration. If one constraint were a consequence of the others, we would subtract too much. For the pendulum we could, for example, also write \(2x^2 + 2y^2 = 2l^2\). These are two equations but one constraint, and the matrix of derivatives \(\left[\begin{smallmatrix} 2x & 2y \\ 4x & 4y \end{smallmatrix}\right]\) has a zero second row after the first step of elimination, so one pivot, not two.

Pendulum \(A\) has \(f = x^2 + y^2 - l^2\). The matrix of derivatives is the row \((2x, 2y)\). It is zero only at \(x = y = 0\), which is not on a circle with \(l > 0\), so in every configuration that satisfies the constraint there is one pivot. The constraint is scleronomic. If we move the support \(O\) horizontally with constant speed \(v\), then \(O\) is at the point \((vt, 0)\) at time \(t\) and the constraint \((x - vt)^2 + y^2 - l^2 = 0\) is rheonomic.

What does a constraint prevent when we move the system? For a small displacement \(\delta\vek x\) from a configuration that satisfies the constraint, \(f\) changes by \(\delta f = (\partial f/\partial\vek x)\,\delta\vek x\) in first order (theorem 8.27 on the change of a function of \(N\) variables over a short step, applied at constant \(t\)). Definition 8.1 requires that we respect constraints up to terms of first order, so the constraint is satisfied in the first approximation when \(\delta f = 0\). The time stays at the value \(t\), which we will justify with an example of a rheonomic constraint at the end of the section. For \(m\) constraints a virtual displacement that satisfies them is therefore a solution of the system

\[\frac{\partial\vek f}{\partial\vek x}(\vek x, t)\,\delta\vek x = \vek 0 . \tag{8.29}\]

Such virtual displacements are called admissible (dopustni, compatible with the constraints). For the pendulum the equation is \(2x\,\delta x + 2y\,\delta y = 0\), that is \(\delta\vek x \perp \vek x\), a displacement perpendicular to the rod, as figure 8.10 shows.

8.4.3 Nonholonomic constraints

Not every constraint has the form (8.28). The simplest system with a different constraint is a disk. A disk of radius \(r = 0{,}1\) m, standing upright, rolls on a horizontal plane without slipping. Its configuration has four numbers (figure 8.11): the coordinates \((x, y)\) of the contact point with the ground, the angle \(\psi\) between the axis \(x\) and the direction of rolling (the direction in which the disk moves if it rolls forward), and the rolling angle \(\varphi\) shown by a marked point on the rim of the disk, measured in radians so that it grows when the disk rolls forward. Such a system has \(N = 4\).

The point of the disk that touches the ground is at rest relative to the ground (this is rolling without slipping from section 8.1.2). Because of the rotation of the disk, this point moves relative to the centre with the speed \(r\dot\varphi\) in the direction opposite to the direction of rolling, so the centre, and with it the contact point, moves relative to the ground with the speed \(r\dot\varphi\) in the direction of rolling. In components:

\[\dot x = r\,\dot\varphi\,\cos\psi , \qquad \dot y = r\,\dot\varphi\,\sin\psi . \tag{8.30}\]

A dot means the derivative with respect to time. These are two equations between velocities, not between coordinates. We can write them as \(\mat A\,\dot{\vek x} = \vek 0\) with the \(2 \times 4\) matrix

\[\mat A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 & -r\cos\psi \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & -r\sin\psi \end{bmatrix} ,\]

which has two pivots in every configuration (in the first two columns).

Can the equations (8.30) be written as a holonomic constraint between \(x\), \(y\), \(\psi\) and \(\varphi\)? If they could, \(\varphi\) would be determined by the position and the direction, and a return to the same position and direction would return the same \(\varphi\). We test this on two paths. A path along a circle of radius \(R = 1{,}05\) m, which the disk rolls once, returns the contact point to the same point with the same direction of rolling (the angle \(\psi\) changes by \(2\pi\), which is the same direction). The path has length \(2\pi R\), the disk rolls it with \(\Delta\varphi = 2\pi R/r\), that is \(65{,}97\) rad or \(10{,}5\) turns (computed in NumPy). The marked point on the rim is after the path on the opposite side of the disk compared with before it (figure 8.11, right). If the disk stays in place, \(\varphi\) does not change. Two paths that start and end in the same position and direction therefore end with different \(\varphi\).

We chose the radius \(R = 1{,}05\) m on purpose. At \(R = 1\) m we would have \(\Delta\varphi = 20\pi\), exactly ten turns, and the marked point would be on the same side of the disk after the path as before it.

Two paths show that \(\varphi\) is not a function of position and direction. They do not show, however, that there is no other holonomic constraint that would replace the rolling constraint. That is shown by the lemma that follows. We must say what it means that a holonomic constraint replaces constraints on velocities.

Definition 8.43 Nonholonomic constraint (neholonomna vez)

Let a motion (gibanje) of a system be a continuously differentiable function \(t \mapsto \vek x(t)\) with output in the space of configurations \(\mathbb{R}^N\). Velocity constraints (vezi na hitrosti) are equations \(\mat A(\vek x, t)\,\dot{\vek x} + \vek b(\vek x, t) = \vek 0\), where \(\mat A\) is an \(m \times N\) matrix with \(m\) pivots and \(\vek b\) a column of \(m\) numbers, both continuous in \(\vek x\) and \(t\). Only the motions that satisfy them are allowed. The velocity constraints are integrable (integrabilne) if there exist \(k \ge 0\) independent holonomic constraints \(\vek f(\vek x, t) = \vek 0\) (definition 8.42), which at some time are satisfied by at least one configuration, and for every motion \(\vek x(t)\) that satisfies \(\vek f = \vek 0\) at some time \(t_0\) the following holds: the motion satisfies the velocity constraints exactly when it satisfies \(\vek f = \vek 0\) at every time. A velocity constraint that is not integrable is nonholonomic (neholonomna).

Source: GR §1.2.2 (PDF p. 37): a constraint that cannot be written in the form (1.12) is nonholonomic, and velocity constraints are "generally not integrable". GR Example 1.2 (PDF p. 37-38) is the disk. The formulation of integrability with the same motions is not from the source.

Lemma 8.44 Rolling of a disk is a nonholonomic constraint (kotaljenje diska je neholonomna vez)

Let the configuration of a disk be \(\vek x = (x, y, \psi, \varphi) \in \mathbb{R}^4\), where \(\psi\) and \(\varphi\) are numbers (whole turns are counted too), and let a disk of radius \(r > 0\) satisfy the constraints (8.30). Then for every configuration \(\vek x_0\), time \(t_0\) and time \(t_1 > t_0\) and every configuration \(\vek x_1 \in \mathbb{R}^4\) there is a continuously differentiable motion that satisfies (8.30), is in \(\vek x_0\) at \(t_0\) and in \(\vek x_1\) at \(t_1\). The constraint (8.30) is nonholonomic.

Proof

First we reach every configuration, then the statement on holonomic constraints follows. Three kinds of motion satisfy (8.30): a turn in place (\(\dot x = \dot y = \dot\varphi = 0\), \(\dot\psi\) arbitrary), rolling in the same direction (\(\dot\psi = 0\), \(\dot\varphi\) arbitrary) and rest. We compose the motions from pieces that each start and end at rest (\(\dot{\vek x} = \vek 0\)), so the composed motion is continuously differentiable. A piece may last as long as we wish: if \(\vek x(t)\) satisfies (8.30), then for every smooth increasing \(\tau(t)\) with \(\tau' = 0\) at the end points \(\vek x(\tau(t))\) satisfies it too, because (8.30) is linear and homogeneous in the velocities (both sides are multiplied by \(\tau'\)). We therefore run through the path piece by piece in any time, and rest is allowed in between.

  1. Let the target be the configuration \(\vek x_1 = (x_1, y_1, \psi_1, \varphi_1)\). Let \(d \ge 0\) be the distance from \((x_0, y_0)\) to \((x_1, y_1)\). If \(d > 0\), we turn \(\psi\) in place into the direction from \((x_0, y_0)\) toward \((x_1, y_1)\). If \(d = 0\), we skip this turn and step 2.
  2. With \(\dot\varphi\) constant and \(\dot\psi = 0\) we roll the distance \(d\) along a straight line to \((x_1, y_1)\). By equation (8.30) the length of the path is \(\int r\,|\dot\varphi|\,\mathrm{d}t\), so \(\varphi\) changes by \(d/r\).
  3. With a turn in place we turn the direction into \(\psi_1\). Now we are in \((x_1, y_1, \psi_1, \varphi_0 + d/r)\).
  4. What remains is the difference \(\Delta = \varphi_1 - \varphi_0 - d/r\), which may be positive or negative. If \(\Delta = 0\) we are at the target. Otherwise we choose \(R = r\,|\Delta|/(2\pi)\) and the circle of radius \(R\) through \((x_1, y_1)\). At \(\dot\varphi > 0\) the contact point moves by (8.30) in the direction \(\psi\), at \(\dot\varphi < 0\) in the opposite direction. So we turn \(\psi\) in place along the tangent of the circle (for \(\Delta > 0\) into the direction of travel, for \(\Delta < 0\) into the opposite one) and roll the circle once, forward for \(\Delta > 0\) and backward for \(\Delta < 0\), changing \(\psi\) along the way so that it keeps the same direction relative to the travel (\(\dot\psi\) is not restricted by the constraint (8.30)). The path has length \(2\pi R\), so by step 2 the change of \(\varphi\) is \(\pm 2\pi R/r = \Delta\). The contact point returns to \((x_1, y_1)\), and the angle \(\psi\) changes by a multiple of \(2\pi\). With a turn in place we return it to \(\psi_1\). After that the configuration is \(\vek x_1\).
  5. Let the constraint (8.30) be integrable, that is, holonomic in disguise, with \(k\) constraints \(\vek f(\vek x, t) = \vek 0\). Let \(\vek x_0\) be a configuration and \(t_0\) a time that satisfy \(\vek f(\vek x_0, t_0) = \vek 0\) (these exist by the definition). By definition 8.43 every motion from \(\vek x_0\) that satisfies (8.30) satisfies \(\vek f = \vek 0\) at every time (the direction "exactly when" from left to right). By step 4 such a motion arrives in every configuration \(\vek x_1\) at every time \(t_1 > t_0\), so \(\vek f(\vek x_1, t_1) = \vek 0\) for every \(\vek x_1 \in \mathbb{R}^4\) and every \(t_1 > t_0\). The function \(\vek f\) is therefore constantly zero on the open set \(\mathbb{R}^4 \times (t_0, \infty)\), so all partial derivatives \(\partial\vek f/\partial\vek x\) are zero at every \(\vek x_1\). The matrix \(\partial\vek f/\partial\vek x\) has no pivot, which contradicts independence if \(k \ge 1\).
  6. For \(k = 0\) the condition \(\vek f = \vek 0\) is satisfied by every motion. By the definition every motion would have to satisfy the velocity constraints, also the motion with \(\dot x = 1\), \(\dot y = 0\) and \(\dot\varphi = 0\), which does not satisfy (8.30) (the first equation gives \(1 = 0\) at \(\dot\varphi = 0\)). So \(k = 0\) is not possible either.

Source: GR Example 1.2 (PDF p. 37-38) says that the rolling constraints are not integrable, and understands them as "behaviour constraints": they do not restrict positions, only the way to reach them. The proof by reachability is not from the source.

The lemma says exactly what GR says: the rolling constraints do not restrict the positions that the disk can take, only the way to reach them. The disk therefore has \(N = 4\) numbers and no holonomic constraint, yet only two independent directions of motion in each position, because two equations tie four velocities. The virtual displacements that satisfy the constraints in the first approximation are the solutions of the equation \(\mat A\,\delta\vek x = \vek 0\) with the same matrix, written for every \(\delta\vek x\) as (8.29). Section 8.9 will show that this suffices to describe the motion, because there we use only the condition \(\mat A\,\delta\vek x = \vek 0\) on virtual displacements. We will not compute an example of a nonholonomic system by its motion: none of the sources we use has an example of that size.

8.4.4 Degrees of freedom and generalized coordinates

For the pendulum we saw that two numbers and one constraint give one number. Does the same hold in general? With \(m\) independent holonomic constraints we expect \(N - m\) free numbers, and the theorem of section 8.3 says that this is true. Now we name this count and say how we choose the numbers that describe it.

Definition 8.45 Degrees of freedom and generalized coordinates (prostostne stopnje in posplošene koordinate)

Let a system have a configuration \(\vek x \in \mathbb{R}^N\) and \(m\) independent holonomic constraints \(\vek f(\vek x, t) = \vek 0\). The number of degrees of freedom (prostostne stopnje) is \(n = N - m\). Generalized coordinates (posplošene koordinate) are numbers \(q_1, \dots, q_{\mathrm{n}}\), gathered in a column \(\vek q \in \mathbb{R}^n\), together with a map \(\vek X\) that has \(n + 1\) numbers \((\vek q, t)\) as input and a configuration \(\vek x = \vek X(\vek q, t) \in \mathbb{R}^N\) as output, such that:

  1. \(\vek X\) is continuously differentiable on an open set,
  2. \(\vek f\big(\vek X(\vek q, t), t\big) = \vek 0\) for every \((\vek q, t)\) of this set, so every position that \(\vek X\) describes satisfies the constraints,
  3. the \(N \times n\) matrix of partial derivatives \(\partial\vek X/\partial\vek q\) has \(n\) pivots at every \((\vek q, t)\).

Source: GR §1.2.3 (PDF p. 38), equation (1.14): \(n = 3N - R\) configuration parameters, the generalized coordinates. The requirement of pivots and the writing with a map \(\vek X\) with time are not from the source.

Why the third requirement? The matrix \(\partial\vek X/\partial\vek q\) tells how the configuration changes when we change one coordinate. If it had fewer than \(n\) pivots, there would be a nonzero \(\delta\vek q\) for which the configuration does not change in first order. The coordinates would be redundant: we count \(n\) of them, but the system moves in first order in fewer than \(n\) independent directions. How such coordinates arise we will see with the toggle mechanism.

The definition requires a map \(\vek X\). Does it exist? For the pendulum we guessed it. Theorem 8.34 says that it always exists when the constraints are independent.

Lemma 8.46 Existence of generalized coordinates (obstoj posplošenih koordinat)

Let \(\vek f_1, \dots, \vek f_{\mathrm{m}}\) be independent holonomic constraints of a system with configuration \(\vek x \in \mathbb{R}^N\) in the configuration \(\vek x_0\) at time \(t_0\), and let \(\vek f(\vek x_0, t_0) = \vek 0\). Then we can choose generalized coordinates \(\vek q \in \mathbb{R}^{N - m}\) and a map \(\vek X\) of definition 8.45 that describe all configurations near \(\vek x_0\) which satisfy the constraints at times near \(t_0\).

Proof

We must show that the constraints determine \(m\) coordinates by the remaining \(N - m\) coordinates and the time, and that the remaining coordinates are true generalized coordinates.

  1. By assumption the matrix \(\partial\vek f/\partial\vek x\) has \(m\) pivots at \((\vek x_0, t_0)\). We renumber the coordinates so that the pivots are in the last \(m\) columns. We write \(\vek x = (\vek y, \vek z)\) with \(\vek y \in \mathbb{R}^{N - m}\) and \(\vek z \in \mathbb{R}^m\).
  2. We treat the function \(\vek F(\vek y, t, \vek z) = \vek f\big((\vek y, \vek z), t\big)\) as a function of \(N + 1\) variables, where time counts among the first ones. Its matrix of derivatives has the same last \(m\) columns as \(\partial\vek f/\partial\vek x\), so \(m\) pivots in them. Theorem 8.34 gives a continuously differentiable map \(\vek g(\vek y, t)\) with values in \(\mathbb{R}^m\) such that near \((\vek x_0, t_0)\) the solutions of the equation \(\vek F = \vek 0\) are exactly the points \(\vek z = \vek g(\vek y, t)\).
  3. We take \(\vek q = \vek y\) and \(\vek X(\vek q, t) = \big(\vek q, \vek g(\vek q, t)\big)\). The map is continuously differentiable (requirement 1), it satisfies the constraints by step 2 (requirement 2) and describes all nearby configurations that the constraints allow.
  4. The matrix \(\partial\vek X/\partial\vek q\) is stacked from the identity matrix of order \(N - m\) and the matrix \(\partial\vek g/\partial\vek q\). The pivots of the identity matrix stand in the first \(N - m\) rows, so \(\partial\vek X/\partial\vek q\) has \(n = N - m\) pivots (requirement 3).

Source: The proof is not from the source, GR (PDF p. 38) assumes the existence of the coordinates. We use theorem 8.34.

The lemma says that the number of numbers the system needs is really \(n = N - m\), and that the coordinates may be some of the original ones. For the pendulum this is for example \(q = x\) with \(y = -\sqrt{l^2 - x^2}\) (the lower arc of the circle), which fails at \(|x| = l\), when the rod is horizontal and the derivative \(\partial y/\partial x\) is infinite, or the angle \(\theta\), which is none of the original coordinates and fails nowhere. The choice is not unique.

Lemma 8.47 Virtual displacements in generalized coordinates (virtualni pomiki v posplošenih koordinatah)

Let \(\vek q\) be generalized coordinates of a system with \(m\) independent holonomic constraints \(\vek f(\vek x, t) = \vek 0\) and \(\vek x = \vek X(\vek q, t)\). For every \(\delta\vek q \in \mathbb{R}^n\)

\[\delta\vek x = \sum_{\mathrm{s}=1}^n \frac{\partial\vek X}{\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}}\,\delta q_{\mathrm{s}} = \frac{\partial\vek X}{\partial\vek q}\,\delta\vek q \tag{8.31}\]

is an admissible virtual displacement, which satisfies (8.29). Every admissible virtual displacement \(\delta\vek x\) has the form (8.31) for exactly one \(\delta\vek q\). The derivatives are computed at the chosen time \(t\), which does not change in a virtual displacement.

Proof

We must show two things: that the displacement (8.31) satisfies (8.29) and that we obtain all solutions of this equation with it. We denote \(\mat A = \partial\vek f/\partial\vek x\) and \(\mat U = \partial\vek X/\partial\vek q\).

  1. At constant \(t\) we have \(\vek f\big(\vek X(\vek q, t), t\big) = \vek 0\) for every \(\vek q\). The derivative with respect to \(\vek q\) is zero. By the chain rule (lemma 8.29, item 1, applied to every component \(f_{\mathrm{r}}\), where the input \(t\) has zero derivatives with respect to \(\vek q\)) \(\mat A\,\mat U = \mat 0\), an \(m \times n\) matrix. Hence for every \(\delta\vek q\) we have \(\mat A\,(\mat U\,\delta\vek q) = \vek 0\), that is (8.29).
  2. The matrix \(\mat A\) has \(m\) pivots. In the elimination of the system \(\mat A\,\delta\vek x = \vek 0\), \(m\) unknowns are determined by the remaining \(n = N - m\), which we call the free unknowns. With the values of the free unknowns \(\delta\vek x\) is determined uniquely (back substitution, KREYSZIG §7.3 PDF p. 300-302).
  3. The matrix \(\mat U\) has \(n\) pivots, so \(\mat U\,\vek c = \vek 0\) holds only for \(\vek c = \vek 0\). Let \(\mat H\) be the square matrix of order \(n\) made of the rows of the matrix \(\mat U\) that correspond to the free unknowns. If \(\mat H\,\vek c = \vek 0\), then \(\mat U\,\vek c\) has zero free unknowns by step 1 and solves (8.29), so by step 2 it is \(\vek 0\), and hence \(\vek c = \vek 0\).
  4. A square matrix for which \(\mat H\,\vek c = \vek 0\) has only the solution \(\vek c = \vek 0\) has \(n\) pivots in elimination, so for every column \(\vek s\) the system \(\mat H\,\vek c = \vek s\) has exactly one solution (back substitution).
  5. Let \(\delta\vek x\) be any solution of equation (8.29) and \(\vek s\) the column of its free unknowns. By step 4 we choose \(\vek c\) with \(\mat H\,\vek c = \vek s\). The difference \(\delta\vek x - \mat U\,\vek c\) solves (8.29) (step 1) and has zero free unknowns, so by step 2 it is \(\vek 0\). Hence \(\delta\vek x = \mat U\,\vek c\), and \(\vek c\) is unique because \(\mat U\) has \(n\) pivots.

Source: GR (1.15) (PDF p. 39) writes the virtual displacements \(\delta u_{\mathrm{ik}}\) with generalized coordinates and says that the \(\delta q_{\mathrm{s}}\) are independent. The proof with pivots is not from the source.

The lemma says that the coordinates \(\delta q_{\mathrm{s}}\) write every virtual displacement and that we may choose them freely, as GR says (PDF p. 39). We therefore no longer need to compute the constraint, the displacements allowed by it are already built into the coordinates. The virtual work of forces on the system, written with \(\delta q_{\mathrm{s}}\), is treated in section 8.5.

Why are the derivatives \(\partial\vek X/\partial t\) absent from a virtual displacement in a rheonomic constraint, although they are in the real one? Along a real motion \(\vek q(t)\) the point has the velocity \(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\vek X(\vek q(t), t) = \sum_{\mathrm{s}} (\partial\vek X/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}})\,\dot q_{\mathrm{s}} + \partial\vek X/\partial t\) (chain rule, lemma 8.29, the same as GR (1.24), PDF p. 42). The last term is there because the constraint itself moves too. A virtual displacement compares two positions at the same time, because the forces with which we test it are counted for this position (section 4.4.2), so \(\delta\vek x = \sum_{\mathrm{s}} (\partial\vek X/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}})\,\delta q_{\mathrm{s}}\) and \(\partial\vek X/\partial t\) does not enter it (GR (1.15), PDF p. 39, SHABANA, PDF p. 107: a virtual displacement is consistent with the constraints at the given instant \(t\)). Let us show that this is not only a notation. Pendulum \(A\) with a support that moves horizontally with the speed \(v = 0{,}5\) m/s has \(\vek X(\theta, t) = (vt + l\sin\theta,\ -l\cos\theta)\). The rod acts on the point with a force of magnitude \(N = 10\) N toward the support, that is with the force \(-N\,(\sin\theta,\ -\cos\theta)\). At \(\theta = \pi/6\) the force does on the real displacement in the time \(\Delta t = 0{,}1\) s at unchanged \(\theta\), that is on the displacement \(\vek X(\theta, t + \Delta t) - \vek X(\theta, t) = (v\,\Delta t,\ 0)\), the work \(-10 \cdot 0{,}5 \cdot 0{,}5 \cdot 0{,}1 = -0{,}25\) J, and on the virtual displacement \(l\,(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\,\delta\theta\) the work \(-N\,l\,(\sin\theta\cos\theta - \cos\theta\sin\theta)\,\delta\theta = 0\). The rod is thus an ideal constraint (definition 8.2): the virtual work is zero, but the real work is not, for a rheonomic constraint.

8.4.5 Examples

Each of the previous definitions and lemmas is written in general. We test them on the systems that will accompany us through the chapter: on pendulum \(A\), the rod on two springs \(C\), the toggle mechanism \(D\), and on the double pendulum and the free rigid body.

Pendulum \(A\). \(N = 2\), \(m = 1\), \(n = 1\), the coordinate \(q = \theta\) and \(\vek X(\theta) = (l\sin\theta,\ -l\cos\theta)\). By theorem 8.12 on the derivatives of sine and cosine \(\partial\vek X/\partial\theta = l\,(\cos\theta,\ \sin\theta)\), which has one pivot (at least one component is nonzero). At \(\theta = \pi/6\) we have \(\partial\vek X/\partial\theta = (0{,}8660,\ 0{,}5000)\) m. The matrix of derivatives of the constraint is \((2x, 2y) = (1{,}000,\ -1{,}732)\) m and their product is \(2l^2(\sin\theta\cos\theta - \cos\theta\sin\theta) = 0\), as lemma 8.47 claims. The virtual displacement \(\delta\vek x = l\,(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\,\delta\theta\) is perpendicular to the rod, as figure 8.10 shows.

Rod on two springs \(C\). A rigid rod of length \(L = 2\) m lies horizontally on two vertical springs, one at each end. A guide holds its centroid \(G\) on a vertical line, so it excludes horizontal motion (this is our idealization, made to keep two numbers, and not a restriction of the rod itself). The configuration is \(\vek x = (x_{\mathrm{G}}, y_{\mathrm{G}}, \varphi)\) with the centroid \((x_{\mathrm{G}}, y_{\mathrm{G}})\) and the rotation \(\varphi\) of the rod, so \(N = 3\). The guide gives one constraint \(f = x_{\mathrm{G}} = 0\), so \(m = 1\) and \(n = 2\). The coordinates are the vertical displacement \(y\) of the centroid and the rotation \(\varphi\), \(\vek X(y, \varphi) = (0, y, \varphi)\) (figure 8.12). The matrix \(\partial\vek X/\partial\vek q\) is \(\left[\begin{smallmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\right]\) with two pivots, the matrix of the constraint \((1, 0, 0)\) has one pivot and \((1, 0, 0)\,\partial\vek X/\partial\vek q = (0, 0)\). The virtual displacement is \(\delta\vek x = (0, \delta y, \delta\varphi)\), the guide does not allow a horizontal displacement.

Example 8.48 Toggle mechanism \(D\) (kolenasti mehanizem)

Two bars \(AB\) and \(BC\), each of length \(a = 0{,}5\) m, are joined in the hinge \(B\). The bar \(AB\) is fixed by a pin at \(A = (0, 0)\), and the end \(C\) of the bar \(BC\) slides along a horizontal line through \(A\) (figure 8.12). We describe each bar with the coordinates of its centroid and an angle: \((x_1, y_1, \varphi_1)\) and \((x_2, y_2, \varphi_2)\), as for a rigid body in definition 8.41, where \(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\) is the angle of the bar from the axis \(x\) in the direction from the first toward the second end. The configuration has \(N = 6\) numbers. There are five constraints.

  1. The pin \(A\) holds the first end of the bar \(AB\) at the origin. The centroid is at \(a/2\) from it in the direction \(\varphi_1\): \(f_1 = x_1 - \tfrac a2\cos\varphi_1 = 0\) and \(f_2 = y_1 - \tfrac a2\sin\varphi_1 = 0\).
  2. The hinge \(B\) is the second end of the bar \(AB\), which is also the first end of the bar \(BC\): \(f_3 = x_2 - a\cos\varphi_1 - \tfrac a2\cos\varphi_2 = 0\) and \(f_4 = y_2 - a\sin\varphi_1 - \tfrac a2\sin\varphi_2 = 0\).
  3. The slider \(C\), the second end of the bar \(BC\), is at height zero: \(f_5 = y_2 + \tfrac a2\sin\varphi_2 = 0\).
  4. For \(\varphi_1 = \theta\) the constraints 3 to 5 are satisfied with \(\varphi_2 = -\theta\): \(f_5 = 0\) gives \(y_2 = -\tfrac a2\sin\varphi_2\), which we insert into \(f_4\) and get \(\sin\varphi_2 = -\sin\varphi_1\), so \(\varphi_2 = -\varphi_1\) for angles between \(-\pi/2\) and \(\pi/2\), where the sine is strictly increasing, because its derivative \(\cos\theta\) is positive there (theorem 8.12). We leave out the branch where the bar \(BC\) points backward in this example. The remaining coordinates are \(x_2 = \tfrac32 a\cos\theta\) and \(y_2 = \tfrac12 a\sin\theta\). At \(\theta = \pi/6\) this gives \(x_2 = 0{,}6495\) m and \(y_2 = 0{,}125\) m.
  5. The matrix of derivatives \(\partial\vek f/\partial\vek x\) has in the form \(5 \times 6\) five pivots at \(\theta = \pi/6\), the same at \(\theta = 0\) and \(\theta = 0{,}1\) rad (SymPy), so the constraints are independent, \(m = 5\) and \(n = 6 - 5 = 1\). One coordinate suffices. We choose \(q = \theta\), \(\vek X(\theta) = \big(\tfrac a2\cos\theta,\ \tfrac a2\sin\theta,\ \theta,\ \tfrac32 a\cos\theta,\ \tfrac12 a\sin\theta,\ -\theta\big)\).
  6. The matrix \(\partial\vek X/\partial\theta\) is a column of six numbers, at \(\theta = \pi/6\) it is \((-0{,}125,\ 0{,}2165,\ 1,\ -0{,}375,\ 0{,}2165,\ -1)\) (SymPy), and has one pivot. The product \(\partial\vek f/\partial\vek x\,\partial\vek X/\partial\theta\) is a zero column with five zeros (SymPy), as lemma 8.47 requires.

Source: The mechanism is our model, not from a source.

Why did we choose the angle \(\theta\) as the coordinate and not the horizontal position of the slider \(x_{\mathrm{C}} = 2a\cos\theta\), which seems more natural? The derivative \(\mathrm{d}x_{\mathrm{C}}/\mathrm{d}\theta = -2a\sin\theta\) is zero at \(\theta = 0\). The bars then lie exactly on one line. At \(\theta = \pm 0{,}1\) rad \(x_{\mathrm{C}} = 0{,}9950\) m for both angles, so two different positions of the mechanism (the hinge \(B\) above the guide or below it) with the same value of \(x_{\mathrm{C}}\). At \(\theta = 0\) we have \(x_{\mathrm{C}} = 2a = 1\) m. Even a small change of \(x_{\mathrm{C}}\) by \(1\) mm, to \(0{,}999\) m, changes \(\theta\) by \(\pm 0{,}0447\) rad, that is by \(2{,}56°\): at the dead position the dependence of \(\theta\) on \(x_{\mathrm{C}}\) is a square root, not linear. The pivots show the same. If we add to the constraints the equation \(x_{\mathrm{C}} = c\) with a given number \(c\), we have six equations for six unknowns. At \(\theta = \pi/6\) the \(6 \times 6\) matrix of derivatives has six pivots, at \(\theta = 0\) only five (SymPy). By step 2 of the proof of lemma 8.46 we could take \(x_{\mathrm{C}}\) as a coordinate only if the extended system had six pivots. At \(\theta = 0\) it does not, and requirement 3 of definition 8.45 could be met in this position only by another choice. The angle \(\theta\) meets it in every position. The choice of the coordinate is therefore not a matter of taste: where it fails, the pivot condition shows.

Example 8.49 Double pendulum and the choice of coordinates (dvojno nihalo in izbira koordinat)

Two mass points, \(P_1\) and \(P_2\), hang one on the other. The point \(P_1\) is on a rigid massless rod of length \(l_1 = 1\) m from the pin \(O\), the point \(P_2\) on a rod of length \(l_2 = 0{,}5\) m from \(P_1\). The motion is in a plane. The configuration has four numbers, the coordinates of both points, \(N = 4\), and two constraints, the lengths of the rods, so \(n = 2\) (GR Example 1.4, PDF p. 40-41).

  1. As coordinates we choose the angles \(\theta_1\) and \(\theta_2\) of both rods from the vertical downward. Then \(\vek X(\theta_1, \theta_2) = \big(l_1\sin\theta_1,\ -l_1\cos\theta_1,\ l_1\sin\theta_1 + l_2\sin\theta_2,\ -l_1\cos\theta_1 - l_2\cos\theta_2\big)\).
  2. At \(\theta_1 = \pi/6\) and \(\theta_2 = \pi/3\) the points are at \((0{,}5000,\ -0{,}8660)\) m and \((0{,}9330,\ -1{,}1160)\) m. The matrix of derivatives \(\partial\vek X/\partial\vek q\) is
    \[\begin{bmatrix} 0{,}8660 & 0 \\ 0{,}5000 & 0 \\ 0{,}8660 & 0{,}2500 \\ 0{,}5000 & 0{,}4330 \end{bmatrix} \text{m}\]
    and has two pivots, in the first and second column (the first column is nonzero and the second is not parallel to it). The constraints are independent too: the matrix of derivatives \(\left[\begin{smallmatrix} 2x_1 & 2y_1 & 0 & 0 \\ -2(x_2 - x_1) & -2(y_2 - y_1) & 2(x_2 - x_1) & 2(y_2 - y_1) \end{smallmatrix}\right]\) has at these numbers the rows \((1{,}000,\ -1{,}732,\ 0,\ 0)\) m and \((-0{,}866,\ 0{,}500,\ 0{,}866,\ -0{,}500)\) m, of which the second is nonzero also in the last two columns and the first is not, so they have two pivots. The product of this matrix with the matrix of derivatives \(\partial\vek X/\partial\vek q\) is a zero matrix (SymPy), as lemma 8.47 requires.
  3. GR chooses the second angle relative to the first, \(\alpha = \theta_2 - \theta_1\). The new coordinates \((\theta_1, \alpha)\) are obtained from the old ones by \(\theta_2 = \theta_1 + \alpha\). The derivative \(\partial\vek X/\partial(\theta_1, \alpha)\) is the product of the old matrix with \(\left[\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\right]\) (chain rule, lemma 8.29, item 2), so with numbers
    \[\begin{bmatrix} 0{,}8660 & 0 \\ 0{,}5000 & 0 \\ 1{,}1160 & 0{,}2500 \\ 0{,}9330 & 0{,}4330 \end{bmatrix} \text{m} ,\]
    which also has two pivots, since the determinant of \(\left[\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\right]\) is \(1\). Both choices are generalized coordinates of the same system, only the writings of the same virtual displacement differ.

Source: GR Example 1.4 (PDF p. 40-41). The numbers are from SymPy.

Free rigid body. A body in the plane without constraints has \(N = 3\), \(m = 0\), \(n = 3\) (the point \(O\) and the angle). In space we write the configuration of a body with the point \(O\) and the matrix \(\mat Q\) (a \(3 \times 3\) orthogonal matrix, definition 2.39) that rotates the body from the initial position, so with \(3 + 9 = 12\) numbers. The constraint is \(\mat Q^\T\mat Q = \mat I\): six equations, because the matrix \(\mat Q^\T\mat Q\) is symmetric and has \(3 + 2 + 1 = 6\) different elements. Independence is simple. The derivative of the left side in the direction \(\delta\mat Q\) is \(\mat Q^\T\delta\mat Q + \delta\mat Q^\T\mat Q\). For every symmetric matrix \(\mat S\) we choose \(\delta\mat Q = \tfrac12\mat Q\,\mat S\) and get \(\tfrac12\mat S + \tfrac12\mat S^\T = \mat S\), so the \(6 \times 9\) system is solvable for every right side, which is possible only if elimination gives six pivots (otherwise, with a zero row, some right sides would turn out unsolvable). At \(\mat Q = \mat I\) the same is seen directly: the \(6 \times 9\) matrix has six pivots (SymPy). So \(n = 12 - 6 = 6\), three displacements and three rotations. We do not choose three angles that would describe the rotation by three numbers in this chapter: section 8.8 works directly with the matrix \(\mat Q\).

Summary of the examples: for the pendulum \(N = 2\), \(m = 1\), \(n = 1\), for the rod on springs \(N = 3\), \(m = 1\), \(n = 2\), for the toggle mechanism \(N = 6\), \(m = 5\), \(n = 1\), for the double pendulum \(N = 4\), \(m = 2\), \(n = 2\), for the free body in space \(N = 12\), \(m = 6\), \(n = 6\), for the disk \(N = 4\), \(m = 0\) (holonomic constraints) and two velocity constraints.

Now we have positions, constraints and coordinates. In section 8.5 we compute the virtual work of forces with them. The displacements \(\delta q_{\mathrm{s}}\) are independent and we may choose them freely, so in equilibrium every expression beside them must be zero by itself.
