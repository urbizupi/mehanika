So far we have solved every structure completely: with all nodes, all elements and all unknowns. The finite elements of chapters 7 and 12 have two troubles here. The first is size: a portal frame with four elements on each column and eight on the beam already has \(51\) unknowns, and symmetry can save us half of the work. The second is that the conditions between unknowns do not end with a support that forbids a single unknown: a support slides along an incline, a beam is rigidly attached to a column that belongs to another element. This chapter gives three tools for both troubles. With mirror and rotational symmetry we compute only a piece of the structure, with periodicity we use repetition, and with constraints we tie together the unknowns that the mesh does not tie by itself.

Each word of the title has its reason. Symmetry (simetrija) is a Greek word from syn, together, and metron, measure: what is symmetric has a common measure with another part, so that it can be made to cover it. Periodicity (periodičnost) comes from the Greek periodos, a going round, from peri, around, and hodos, way: what is periodic repeats, returning to the same state after each round. A constraint (vez) is a condition that ties unknowns to each other, as a rope ties one body to another so that it cannot move freely.

The chapter keeps four promises of earlier chapters. Chapter 5 promised the symmetry of frames, chapter 7 the symmetric and antisymmetric load, chapter 11 the conditions of symmetry on a plane, and chapter 12 the constraints between several nodes and the joining of different elements. The path is this: first we prove that a symmetric structure under a symmetric or antisymmetric load gives a symmetric or antisymmetric solution, then what this means on the plane of symmetry and how an arbitrary load is split. Next comes why this does not suffice for vibration and buckling. Then constraints: elimination of unknowns, rigid links, joining of different elements. At the end repetition and rotation, where the symmetry is not a mirror but a shift or a turn. Where none of the books we have gives a proof, we derive it ourselves and say so.
