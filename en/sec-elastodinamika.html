Chapter 13 Vibration of continuous systems: string, shaft and beam

13.4 Elastodynamics

The string and the bar are models that we wrote down directly in one dimension: the string with tension \(T\) and mass per length, the bar with axial stiffness \(EA\) (theorem 13.3, equation (8.97)). From which equations for a three-dimensional body do they follow? Chapter 11 gave three equations for the displacements of a body in equilibrium (theorem 11.6). Chapter 2 already said what happens when the body accelerates: the density times the acceleration appears on the right side of the equilibrium equations, giving the equation of motion of a body (definition 8.70). The same term appears as the inertial force \(-\rho\,\vek a\) in d'Alembert's principle for a body (theorem 8.71). In this section we put it into the Navier-Lamé equation with inertia added (elastodynamics, elastodinamika), show that it has solutions in the form of a wave travelling in one direction, with two speeds, and obtain the speed of a wave in a bar as a limiting case of a slender bar.

13.4.1 A finite part of the body

What does Newton's law say for a part of a body, not only for a cube around a point? Let \(V\) be a part of the body with surface \(S\) and outward unit normal \(\vek n\), as in section 4.2.13. The rest of the body acts on it through the surface with the stress vector \(\vek t(\vek n)\) per unit area, and a body force \(\vek b\) per unit volume acts on all its material. At rest the sum of these forces is zero (corollary 4.41). In motion it must equal mass times acceleration, but every piece of material has its own acceleration, so we add the mass over the volume: the sum is the integral \(\int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\vek a\,\mathrm{d}V\). Figure 13.25 shows these three quantities.

We will obtain the equation for every part \(V\) from the equation at a point, just as corollary 4.41 obtains the equilibrium of a part from the equilibrium equations. For this we need the acceleration in terms of displacements. Figure 13.26 shows how we get it from the displacement.

On the left the dashed line shows the body at time zero, the solid line the same body at time \(t\). Label a point of the body by the position \(X\) it had at time zero, and its displacement at time \(t\) by \(\vek u(X, t)\). The position of the point is \(X + \vek u(X, t)\). On the right is the displacement of this point as a function of time. Its velocity is the derivative in \(t\) at fixed \(X\), the slope of the displacement curve, and its acceleration is the second derivative, the curvature of that curve:

\[\vek a = \frac{\partial^2\vek u}{\partial t^2} = \vek u_{\mathrm{tt}} . \tag{13.21}\]

This is exact if displacements and stresses are treated as functions of the original position \(X\). The equation of motion, however, holds at the current position \(X + \vek u\). If we evaluate the fields at \(X\) instead of at \(X + \vek u\), we make an error. We bound it for a field that varies like a sine along the direction of travel, with amplitude \(A\) and wavelength \(\ell\). Let \(k = 2\pi/\ell\) be the wave number. The derivative of such a field in any direction is at most \(k\,A\) in absolute value, so over a distance \(|\vek u|\) the field changes by at most \(k\,A\,|\vek u|\) (theorem 1.84 on the segment from \(X\) to \(X + \vek u\)). The error is therefore at most the fraction \(k\,|\vek u|\) of the amplitude. The wavelength is the speed of the wave divided by the frequency, and we compute the speeds in the next section. For steel (\(E = 196\) GPa, \(\nu = 0{,}3\), \(\rho = 7800\) kg/m³) at 1 kHz the wavelength is \(5{,}82\) m for displacement along the direction of travel and \(3{,}11\) m for displacement perpendicular to it. With a displacement amplitude of 1 \(\mu\)m this gives \(k\,|\vek u| = 1{,}1 \cdot 10^{-6}\) for the first and \(2{,}0 \cdot 10^{-6}\) for the second displacement. This is the same smallness as the strains of linear theory, so we accept it together with that theory. Two further idealizations: the stress at each instant is determined by the strain at the same instant through Hooke's law (no memory, no heat flow and no damping, which this chapter does not treat), and the density \(\rho\) is constant.

Theorem 13.24 Navier-Lamé equations with inertia (Navier-Laméjeve enačbe z vztrajnostjo)

Let the material be homogeneous, isotropic and linearly elastic, with Lamé constants \(\lambda\) and \(\mu\) (\(\nu \neq -1\), \(\nu \neq \tfrac12\)) and constant density \(\rho > 0\). Let \(D\) be an open set of points of the body, \(I\) an open time interval and \(\vek u(\vek x, t)\) a displacement field with continuous second partial derivatives in the coordinates and in \(t\) (also mixed) on \(D \times I\). Let \(\ten\sigma\) be the stresses obtained from \(\vek u\) at every \(t\) as in theorem 11.6, \(\vek b\) the body force density and \(\vek a = \vek u_{\mathrm{tt}}\). Then:

  1. At a point \(P\) of \(D\) at time \(t\) of \(I\) the stresses satisfy the equation of motion of a body (8.59) if and only if at \(P\) at time \(t\)
    \[\mu\,\nabla^2\vek u + (\lambda + \mu)\,\nabla(\operatorname{div}\vek u) + \vek b = \rho\,\vek u_{\mathrm{tt}} . \tag{13.22}\]
  2. Let \(V\) be a part of the body that satisfies the assumptions of corollary 4.41 (a body of boxes with surface \(S\), every point of \(V\) an interior point, continuously differentiable stresses on an open set \(D \supset V\), continuous \(\vek b\), the Cauchy assumption at every point of the surface). Let (13.22) hold at every point of \(V\) at time \(t\). Then
    \[\int_{\mathrm{S}} \vek t(\vek n)\,\mathrm{d}A + \int_{\mathrm{V}} \vek b\,\mathrm{d}V = \int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\vek u_{\mathrm{tt}}\,\mathrm{d}V , \tag{13.23}\]
    where \(\vek t(\vek n)\) is the stress vector at a point of the surface \(S\) for the outward normal \(\vek n\).
Proof

We must show two things: that the equation of motion of a body at a point is the same equation as (13.22), and that the equation for a part of the body follows from it. The first is the computation of theorem 11.6 with a different right side, the second the steps of corollary 4.41 with a different right side.

  1. At fixed \(t\) the field \(\vek u(\cdot, t)\) has continuous second derivatives in the coordinates. Steps 1 to 6 of the proof of theorem 11.6 use only the definition of strain, Hooke's law and the interchange of mixed derivatives, not the equilibrium equations. Hence at \(P\) at \(t\), for \(\ix{i} = x, y, z\),

    \[\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{ji}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} = \mu\,\nabla^2 u_{\mathrm{i}} + (\lambda + \mu)\,\frac{\partial(\operatorname{div}\vek u)}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}} .\]

  2. The equation of motion (8.59) with the index names interchanged is \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{ji}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}} + b_{\mathrm{i}} = \rho\,a_{\mathrm{i}}\). By (13.21), \(a_{\mathrm{i}} = \partial^2 u_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial t^2\).

  3. In the left side of step 2 we insert the right side of step 1. This gives \(\mu\,\nabla^2 u_{\mathrm{i}} + (\lambda + \mu)\,\partial(\operatorname{div}\vek u)/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}} + b_{\mathrm{i}} = \rho\,\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial t^2\).

    Steps 1 and 3 are equalities, so one equation holds exactly when the other holds. The three equations together are the vector equation (13.22), which proves part 1.

  4. Part 2. By part 1 the first equation of motion holds at every point of \(V\), \(\dfrac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}}{\partial x} + \dfrac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}}{\partial y} + \dfrac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}}{\partial z} + b_{\mathrm{x}} = \rho\,\dfrac{\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial t^2}\). All five terms are continuous on \(V\), since derivatives of stresses are linear combinations of second derivatives of displacements.

  5. The integral of a sum is the sum of the integrals (lemma 4.10, part 1). By the divergence theorem 4.14 with the field \((\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}})\) the integral of the first three terms equals \(\int_{\mathrm{S}} (\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}n_{\mathrm{x}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}n_{\mathrm{y}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}n_{\mathrm{z}})\,\mathrm{d}A\). By Cauchy's formula (theorem 2.24) this is \(\int_{\mathrm{S}} t_{\mathrm{x}}(\vek n)\,\mathrm{d}A\), as in step 4 of the proof of corollary 4.41. The \(x\) component of (13.23) is therefore \(\int_{\mathrm{S}} t_{\mathrm{x}}\,\mathrm{d}A + \int_{\mathrm{V}} b_{\mathrm{x}}\,\mathrm{d}V = \int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial t^2\,\mathrm{d}V\).

  6. For the components \(y\) and \(z\) we repeat steps 4 and 5 with the second and third equation of motion and with the fields \((\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{zy}})\) and \((\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}})\).

Source: Equation of motion: BOWER §2.3.1 (PDF p. 76), §2.4.4.1 (PDF p. 86), CM (5.3.12) (PDF p. 228) (definition 8.70). Equation (13.22) in index form \(C_{\mathrm{ijkl}}\,\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{k}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}\partial x_{\mathrm{l}} = \rho_0\,\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial t^2\): BOWER §4.4.4 (PDF p. 267), with the isotropic \(C_{\mathrm{ijkl}}\) of BOWER (PDF p. 268), which has \(2\mu\nu/(1 - 2\nu) = \lambda\). The proof is ours, written out from the proof of theorem 11.6.

Equation (13.22) is completed by boundary conditions as in the boundary value problem (displacement on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\), stress vector on \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\)) and by initial conditions, the displacement \(\vek u(\cdot, 0)\) and the velocity \(\vek u_{\mathrm{t}}(\cdot, 0)\). Figure 13.27 shows the body with all three. Whether such an initial-boundary value problem has exactly one solution for reasonable data, the book does not prove. We wrote down and checked the solutions for the string and the bar ourselves (sections 13.2 and 13.3).

13.4.2 Plane waves

What motions does equation (13.22) have without body force, in an unbounded body? The simplest are motions in which the displacement field moves as a whole. Let \(\vek n\) be a unit vector of direction, \(\vek e\) a unit vector along which the points move, and \(f\) a function of one variable. The field

\[\vek u(\vek x, t) = f(\vek n\cdot\vek x - c\,t)\,\vek e , \qquad c > 0 , \tag{13.24}\]

has at time \(t\) the same displacement at all points of the plane \(\vek n\cdot\vek x = s_0\), since there the argument of \(f\) equals \(s_0 - ct\). After a time \(\tau\) the same displacement belongs to the plane \(\vek n\cdot\vek x = s_0 + c\,\tau\), so the pattern moves in the direction \(\vek n\) with speed \(c\), as on the string (definition 13.4). We call such a motion a wave travelling in the direction \(\vek n\) (the lemma that follows calls it a plane wave, ravninski val, because it has the same displacement over the whole plane \(\vek n\cdot\vek x = s_0\)). Figure 13.28 shows points on a line in the direction \(\vek n\): empty circles are points at rest, filled circles displaced points. It shows two possibilities: displacement along \(\vek n\) (longitudinal, vzdolžno) and displacement perpendicular to it (transverse, prečno). Below is the same line with the pattern at two times.

For which \(\vek e\) and \(c\) is the field (13.24) a solution of (13.22)? Differentiate by the chain rule: \(\partial u_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}} = f'\,n_{\mathrm{j}} e_{\mathrm{i}}\), so the sum of the second derivatives in all coordinates gives \(f''\,e_{\mathrm{i}}\), the divergence is \(f'\,(\vek n\cdot\vek e)\) and its gradient \(f''\,(\vek n\cdot\vek e)\,\vek n\), and the second time derivative is \(c^2 f''\,\vek e\). The condition on \(\vek e\) and \(c\) must not touch the shape of \(f\), so we write it in a lemma.

The function \(f\) can be a wave, which bends, so the second derivative is not zero everywhere, or a straight line, which never bends and has second derivative zero. Both are drawn on the whole axis, since the argument \(\vek n\cdot\vek x - ct\) takes all real values.

On every panel \(\vek n\) is the grey horizontal arrow and \(\vek e\) the blackwhite one. On the left panel \(\vek e = \vek n\) and there is no part perpendicular to \(\vek n\). On the middle panel \(\vek e\) is perpendicular to \(\vek n\) and there is no part along \(\vek n\). On the right panel \(\vek e\) is at an angle: its part along \(\vek n\) is the blue horizontal line, its part perpendicular to it the blue vertical one.

Lemma 13.25 Plane waves in an isotropic body (Ravninski valovi v izotropnem telesu)

Let the material be as in theorem 13.24, with modulus of elasticity \(E > 0\) and Poisson's ratio \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\) (theorem 3.48), without body force, \(\vek b = \vek 0\). Let \(\vek n\) be a unit vector, \(\vek e\) a unit vector, \(c > 0\) and \(f\) a twice continuously differentiable function that is not everywhere linear (\(f''\) is not everywhere zero). Then the field (13.24) is a solution of (13.22) at all points of space and at all times if and only if one of the following holds:

  1. \(\vek e = \pm\vek n\) and \(c = c_{\mathrm{L}}\) (longitudinal wave, speed \(c_{\mathrm{L}}\)), where
    \[c_{\mathrm{L}}^2 = \frac{\lambda + 2\mu}{\rho} = \frac{E\,(1 - \nu)}{\rho\,(1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)} , \tag{13.25}\]
  2. \(\vek e\cdot\vek n = 0\) and \(c = c_{\mathrm{T}}\) (transverse wave, speed \(c_{\mathrm{T}}\)), where
    \[c_{\mathrm{T}}^2 = \frac{\mu}{\rho} = \frac{E}{2\rho\,(1 + \nu)} . \tag{13.26}\]

Moreover \(c_{\mathrm{L}} > c_{\mathrm{T}}\).

Proof

We must show that equation (13.22) for the field (13.24) reduces to a condition on \(\vek e\) and \(c\). We compute the derivatives, insert them and split the vector into a component along \(\vek n\) and one perpendicular to it.

  1. Let \(s = \vek n\cdot\vek x - c\,t\). By the chain rule \(\partial s/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}} = n_{\mathrm{j}}\) and \(\partial s/\partial t = -c\), so \(\partial u_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}} = f'(s)\,n_{\mathrm{j}} e_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}^2 = f''(s)\,n_{\mathrm{j}}^2 e_{\mathrm{i}}\). Since \(\vek n\) is a unit vector, \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} n_{\mathrm{j}}^2 = 1\) and \(\nabla^2 u_{\mathrm{i}} = f''(s)\,e_{\mathrm{i}}\).
  2. The divergence is \(\operatorname{div}\vek u = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} f'(s)\,n_{\mathrm{j}} e_{\mathrm{j}} = f'(s)\,(\vek n\cdot\vek e)\), and its derivative in \(x_{\mathrm{i}}\) is \(f''(s)\,(\vek n\cdot\vek e)\,n_{\mathrm{i}}\). The second time derivative is \(\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial t^2 = c^2 f''(s)\,e_{\mathrm{i}}\).
  3. By steps 1 and 2, equation (13.22) with \(\vek b = \vek 0\) is
    \[f''(s)\,\Big( (\mu - \rho c^2)\,\vek e + (\lambda + \mu)\,(\vek n\cdot\vek e)\,\vek n \Big) = \vek 0 .\]
    We denote the vector in the large brackets by \(\vek w\). It does not depend on \(\vek x\) and \(t\).
  4. If \(\vek w = \vek 0\), the equation holds for every \(f\). If \(\vek w \neq \vek 0\), we need \(f''(s) = 0\) for every \(s\) that is reached, and these are all real numbers, since \(\vek n\cdot\vek x - ct\) takes all values. Then \(f''\) would be zero everywhere, which we excluded. The equation therefore holds exactly when \(\vek w = \vek 0\).
  5. Split \(\vek e = (\vek e\cdot\vek n)\,\vek n + \vek e_\perp\) with \(\vek e_\perp\cdot\vek n = 0\). Since \(\vek n\cdot\vek n = 1\), \(\vek w = (\mu - \rho c^2)\,\vek e_\perp + \big((\mu - \rho c^2) + (\lambda + \mu)\big)(\vek e\cdot\vek n)\,\vek n\). The vectors \(\vek e_\perp\) and \(\vek n\) are perpendicular, so \(\vek w = \vek 0\) exactly when both terms are zero: \((\mu - \rho c^2)\,\vek e_\perp = \vek 0\) and \((\lambda + 2\mu - \rho c^2)\,(\vek e\cdot\vek n) = 0\).
  6. If \(\vek e_\perp = \vek 0\), then \(\vek e = \pm\vek n\), \(\vek e\cdot\vek n = \pm 1 \neq 0\) and the second condition gives \(\rho c^2 = \lambda + 2\mu\), which is possibility 1.
  7. If \(\vek e_\perp \neq \vek 0\), the first condition gives \(\rho c^2 = \mu\). The second condition is then \((\lambda + \mu)\,(\vek e\cdot\vek n) = 0\). By equation (3.11), \(\lambda + \mu = \dfrac{\nu E}{(1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)} + \dfrac{E}{2(1 + \nu)} = \dfrac{E}{2(1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)}\), which is positive for \(E > 0\) and \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\). Hence \(\vek e\cdot\vek n = 0\), which is possibility 2.
  8. Conversely, in possibilities 1 and 2 both conditions of step 5 hold, so \(\vek w = \vek 0\) and the field (13.24) is a solution.
  9. Expressions with \(E\) and \(\nu\): \(\lambda + 2\mu = \dfrac{\nu E}{(1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)} + \dfrac{E}{1 + \nu} = \dfrac{E\,(1 - \nu)}{(1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)}\) and \(\mu = \dfrac{E}{2(1 + \nu)}\) by (3.11).
  10. Since \(\lambda + \mu > 0\) (step 7), \(\lambda + 2\mu = \mu + (\lambda + \mu) > \mu > 0\), so \(c_{\mathrm{L}}^2 > c_{\mathrm{T}}^2\).

Source: BOWER §4.4.4 (PDF p. 267-268): plane wave with direction \(\vek p\) and displacement \(\vek a\), speeds \(c_{\mathrm{L}}\) and \(c_{\mathrm{S}}\) from the eigenvalues of the acoustic tensor. BOWER's \(c_{\mathrm{S}}\) is our \(c_{\mathrm{T}}\). The proof by substitution and splitting is ours.

The two speeds differ, and their ratio depends only on Poisson's ratio: \(c_{\mathrm{L}}^2/c_{\mathrm{T}}^2 = (\lambda + 2\mu)/\mu = 2(1 - \nu)/(1 - 2\nu)\). For steel with \(E = 196\) GPa, \(\nu = 0{,}3\) and \(\rho = 7800\) kg/m³, \(\lambda = 113{,}08\) GPa, \(\mu = G = 75{,}38\) GPa, \(\lambda + 2\mu = 263{,}85\) GPa and

\[c_{\mathrm{L}} = 5816 \text{ m/s} , \qquad c_{\mathrm{T}} = 3109 \text{ m/s} , \qquad \frac{c_{\mathrm{L}}^2}{c_{\mathrm{T}}^2} = 3{,}5 .\]

The reason lies in what holds the material back during the motion. In a longitudinal wave the displacement changes only along \(\vek n\), so the material does not contract sideways when it stretches, and \(\lambda\) raises the stiffness: besides the stress \(2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) there is \(\lambda\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) if the axis \(x\) points along \(\vek n\). A transverse wave deforms the material in shear and \(\lambda\) does not enter.

The lemma allows only two speeds, and each is set by the direction of the displacement relative to the direction of travel. Let us look at three fields, each missing one of the conditions of the lemma. For steel \(\lambda + 2\mu = 263{,}85\) GPa and \(\lambda + \mu = 188{,}46\) GPa. The vector \(\vek w\) is the one from step 3 of the proof of the lemma, and the field is a solution exactly when \(\vek w = \vek 0\) (step 4).

  1. A longitudinal wave with the speed of the bar. Let \(\vek e = \vek n\) and \(c = \sqrt{E/\rho} = 5012{,}80\) m/s, which is \(0{,}862\,c_{\mathrm{L}}\), so that \(\rho c^2 = E = 196{,}00\) GPa. Then \(\vek w = (\mu - \rho c^2 + \lambda + \mu)\,\vek n = (\lambda + 2\mu - \rho c^2)\,\vek n = (263{,}85 - 196{,}00)\,\vek n = 67{,}85\ \text{GPa}\ \vek n \ne \vek 0\). The equation misses \(25{,}7\) % of the term \((\lambda + 2\mu)\,f''\). The field depends only on \(\vek n\cdot\vek x\), so there are no lateral deformations, the material cannot contract sideways, and the stiffness is \(\lambda + 2\mu\), not \(E\). The speed \(\sqrt{E/\rho}\) appears only for a slender bar with free sides, as an approximation for long waves (section 13.4.3).
  2. A displacement at an angle. Let \(\vek n = (1, 0, 0)\) and \(\vek e = (\cos 45^\circ, \sin 45^\circ, 0)\). At \(c = c_{\mathrm{L}}\) we have \(\mu - \rho c^2 = -(\lambda + \mu)\) and \(\vek w = (0,\ -133{,}26,\ 0)\) GPa. At \(c = c_{\mathrm{T}}\) we have \(\mu - \rho c^2 = 0\) and \(\vek w = (133{,}26,\ 0,\ 0)\) GPa. In both cases \(|\vek w| = 133{,}26\) GPa, never zero. Step 5 of the proof requires two things: \((\mu - \rho c^2)\,\vek e_\perp = \vek 0\) gives \(c = c_{\mathrm{T}}\), because \(\vek e_\perp \ne \vek 0\), and \((\lambda + 2\mu - \rho c^2)\,(\vek e\cdot\vek n) = 0\) gives \(c = c_{\mathrm{L}}\), because \(\vek e\cdot\vek n \ne 0\). One speed cannot be both, so a mixed field is not one wave.
  3. A straight line instead of a wave. Let \(f(s) = a\,s\), so \(f'' = 0\). The field \(a\,(\vek n\cdot\vek x - ct)\,\vek e\) satisfies the equation for every \(\vek e\) and every \(c\), because \(\vek w\) is multiplied by \(f'' = 0\). No speed is selected, so the lemma requires \(f''\) not everywhere zero.

13.4.3 The bar as a limiting case

The bar in chapter 8 (equation (8.97)) has wave speed \(\sqrt{E/\rho}\) and not \(c_{\mathrm{L}}\). Why, if the bar is part of a body? The difference is in the boundary conditions. The longitudinal wave (13.25) was obtained in an unbounded body, where the material cannot deform sideways (contract or expand) because the neighbouring material holds it back. The sides of a slender bar are free and the material contracts sideways. GR §4.3.1 (PDF p. 262) treats the bar under the assumption that displacements and stresses are constant over the cross-section, which holds for a slowly varying cross-section and loading in the axis only. From the body we obtain it as a model with two choices. The state of stress has only the component \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), all other components are zero. Of the three equations of motion we keep only the one in the direction of the axis \(x\), and so neglect the inertia of the transverse motion. This is not an exact consequence of the equations of the body. What the model neglects we estimate after the lemma.

The bar lies along the axis \(x\), drawn dashed. The cross-sections at \(x\) and \(x + \Delta x\) at time zero are dashed, the displaced cross-sections are solid. Their displacements \(u\) and \(u + \Delta u\) are drawn as distances above the bar. The stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) pulls the piece between the cross-sections outward on both sides. The sides of the bar are free, so nothing holds them and the bar contracts sideways.

Lemma 13.26 Speed of a longitudinal wave in a slender bar (Hitrost vzdolžnega vala v tanki palici)

Let a bar of homogeneous, isotropic, linearly elastic material with modulus of elasticity \(E > 0\), Poisson's ratio \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\) and density \(\rho > 0\) have axis \(x\). Let the state of stress in the bar be uniaxial: \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(x, t)\) is the only nonzero component of stress. Let \(u(x, t)\) be the displacement in the direction of the axis, equal at all points of the cross-section, with continuous second partial derivatives. Let the first equation of motion (8.59) (in the direction of the axis \(x\)) hold. Then

\[E\,\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} + b_{\mathrm{x}} = \rho\,\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial t^2} . \tag{13.27}\]

Without body force this is the wave equation with speed \(c_{\mathrm{B}}\), where

\[c_{\mathrm{B}}^2 = \frac{E}{\rho} , \tag{13.28}\]

and \(c_{\mathrm{B}} \le c_{\mathrm{L}}\) with equality exactly when \(\nu = 0\).

Proof

We must show that (13.27) follows from the uniaxial state of stress and the equation of motion in the direction \(x\), and compare the speed with \(c_{\mathrm{L}}\).

  1. By Hooke's law (theorem 3.11) in uniaxial stress \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/E\), so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\).
  2. By the definition of the small strain tensor (definition 1.115), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \partial u/\partial x\), so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\,\partial u/\partial x\).
  3. The first equation of motion (8.59) is \(\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/\partial x + \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}/\partial y + \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}/\partial z + b_{\mathrm{x}} = \rho\,a_{\mathrm{x}}\). Since \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}} = 0\) by the assumption of uniaxial stress (on the free sides only the \(x\) component of the stress vector is zero, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}n_{\mathrm{y}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}n_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\), elsewhere we assume it), what remains is \(\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/\partial x + b_{\mathrm{x}} = \rho\,a_{\mathrm{x}}\), and by (13.21) \(a_{\mathrm{x}} = \partial^2 u/\partial t^2\). Inserting step 2 gives (13.27).
  4. For \(b_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\), \(\partial^2 u/\partial t^2 = c_{\mathrm{B}}^2\,\partial^2 u/\partial x^2\) with \(c_{\mathrm{B}}^2 = E/\rho\), an equation of the same form as for the string (theorem 13.3) with speed \(c_{\mathrm{B}}\) in place of \(c\).
  5. Comparison: \(c_{\mathrm{B}}^2/c_{\mathrm{L}}^2 = E/(\lambda + 2\mu) = (1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)/(1 - \nu)\) by (13.25). Since \(1 - \nu > 0\), this ratio is less than 1 exactly when \((1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu) < 1 - \nu\), that is \(1 - \nu - 2\nu^2 < 1 - \nu\), so \(\nu \neq 0\). At \(\nu = 0\) it equals 1. The speeds \(c_{\mathrm{B}}\) and \(c_{\mathrm{L}}\) are positive, so \(c_{\mathrm{B}}^2 \le c_{\mathrm{L}}^2\) gives \(c_{\mathrm{B}} \le c_{\mathrm{L}}\).

Source: GR §4.3.1 (PDF p. 262): the assumption of constant displacements and stresses over the cross-section. BOWER §4.4.3 (PDF p. 266): \(c_{\mathrm{B}}^2 = E/\rho_0\) with uniaxial stress, with the remark that the equation is exact for \(\nu = 0\) and otherwise cannot hold, since it neglects the transverse motion. The proof is ours.

For steel \(c_{\mathrm{B}} = 5013\) m/s, which is \(0{,}862\,c_{\mathrm{L}}\), because \(E/(\lambda + 2\mu) = 0{,}743\). A longitudinal wave in a bar is slower than a wave in an unbounded body, because the sides can move.

What does the lemma neglect? In uniaxial stress, by Hooke's law the transverse strain is \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = -\nu\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/E\) (definition 3.2), so the points of the cross-section also move sideways. With uniaxial stress the equations of motion for \(y\) and \(z\) would require \(0 = \rho\,a_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(0 = \rho\,a_{\mathrm{z}}\), but the accelerations \(a_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(a_{\mathrm{z}}\) are not zero. This remainder is the inertia of the transverse motion, which the lemma neglects. The assumptions of uniaxial stress and of \(u\) equal over the cross-section do not fit exactly either: with the lateral displacement \(-\nu\,y\,\partial u/\partial x\) the shear strain would be \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = -\tfrac12\nu\,y\,\partial^2 u/\partial x^2\), not zero, so the cross-section bulges slightly in the wave. Compared with \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) this strain is of order \(\nu\,k\) times the size of the cross-section. We estimate its order of magnitude by the ratio of kinetic energies. The transverse velocity of the point with cross-section coordinates \(y\), \(z\), measured from the axis, is \(-\nu\,y\,\partial^2 u/\partial x\partial t\) and \(-\nu\,z\,\partial^2 u/\partial x\partial t\), and at wave number \(k\) the quantity \(\partial^2 u/\partial x\partial t\) has size \(k\) times \(\partial u/\partial t\). The average of \(y^2 + z^2\) over a rectangle \(b \times h\) is \((b^2 + h^2)/12\). The ratio of the kinetic energy of transverse motion to that of axial motion is therefore \(\nu^2 k^2 (b^2 + h^2)/12\). For a bar with cross-section \(20 \times 40\) mm and the first mode of a fixed-free bar of length 1 m, where the wavelength is \(4\) m, this is \(3{,}7 \cdot 10^{-5}\), negligible. For shorter waves, for example a wavelength of 8 cm, it is not negligible (\(\nu^2 k^2 (b^2 + h^2)/12 = 0{,}09 \cdot 6{,}2 \cdot 10^{3} \cdot 1{,}67 \cdot 10^{-4} \approx 0{,}09\) at \(k = 2\pi/0{,}08 = 78{,}5\) 1/m), and then the one-dimensional equation is no longer good. The natural frequencies of the bar from theorem 13.18, which use the speed \(c_{\mathrm{B}}\), are therefore never exact for \(\nu \neq 0\) (BOWER §4.4.3, PDF p. 266). They are a good approximation only for waves long compared with the size of the cross-section, where the ratio \(\nu^2 k^2 (b^2 + h^2)/12\) is small, and this is the condition of a slender bar.
