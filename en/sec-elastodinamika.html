Chapter 13 Vibration of continuous systems: string, shaft and beam

13.4 Elastodynamics

The string and the bar are models that we wrote down directly in one dimension: the string with tension \(T\) and mass per length, the bar with axial stiffness \(EA\) (theorem 13.3, equation (8.129)). From which equations for a three-dimensional body do they follow? Chapter 11 gave three equations for the displacements of a body in equilibrium (theorem 11.6). Chapter 2 already said what happens when the body accelerates: the density times the acceleration appears on the right side of the equilibrium equations, giving the equation of motion of a body (definition 8.78). The same term appears as the inertial force \(-\rho\,\vek a\) in d'Alembert's principle for a body (theorem 8.79). In this section we put it into the Navier-Lamé equation with inertia added (elastodynamics, elastodinamika), show that it has solutions in the form of a wave travelling in one direction, with two speeds, and obtain the speed of a wave in a bar as a limiting case of a slender bar.

13.4.1 A finite part of the body

What does Newton's law say for a part of a body, not only for a cube around a point? Let \(V\) be a part of the body with surface \(S\) and outward unit normal \(\vek n\), as in section 4.2.13. The rest of the body acts on it through the surface with the stress vector \(\vek t(\vek n)\) per unit area, and a body force \(\vek b\) per unit volume acts on all its material. At rest the sum of these forces is zero (corollary 4.41). In motion it must equal mass times acceleration, but every piece of material has its own acceleration, so we add the mass over the volume: the sum is the integral \(\int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\vek a\,\mathrm{d}V\). Figure 13.48 shows these three quantities.

We will obtain the equation for every part \(V\) from the equation at a point, just as corollary 4.41 obtains the equilibrium of a part from the equilibrium equations. For this we need the acceleration in terms of displacements. Figure 13.49 shows how we get it from the displacement.

On the left we draw the body at time zero grey dashed and the same body at time \(t\) orange. Label a point of the body by the position \(X\) it had at time zero. By time \(t\) it has moved to the position \(X + \vek u(X, t)\), and the arrow from \(X\) to the new position, drawn blue and greatly exaggerated, is its displacement \(\vek u(X, t)\). On the right is the displacement of this point as a function of time. Its velocity is the derivative in \(t\) at fixed \(X\), the slope of the displacement curve, and its acceleration is the second derivative, the curvature of that curve:

\[\vek a = \frac{\partial^2\vek u}{\partial t^2} = \vek u_{\mathrm{tt}} . \tag{13.32}\]

This is exact if displacements and stresses are treated as functions of the original position \(X\). The equation of motion, however, holds at the current position \(X + \vek u\). If we evaluate the fields at \(X\) instead of at \(X + \vek u\), we make an error. How large it is, figure 13.50 shows.

Let us draw a field that varies along the direction of travel \(s\) like a sine with amplitude \(A\) and wavelength \(\ell\), for example one component of the displacement in a wave. Let \(k = 2\pi/\ell\) be the wave number, so that the field is \(A\sin(k s)\). Its derivative \(k A\cos(k s)\) is at most \(k A\) in absolute value, and it is that large where the sine crosses the axis. The triangle with horizontal leg \(1\) and vertical leg \(k A\) has this slope. Now take a point at its original position \(X\), drawn grey dashed, and its current position \(X + \vek u\), drawn orange. The two positions are a distance \(|\vek u|\) apart. By theorem 1.85 on the segment from \(X\) to \(X + \vek u\) the field changes between them by at most \(k A\,|\vek u|\). If \(\vek u\) does not point in the direction of travel, the field changes even more slowly along \(\vek u\), since in the direction of travel we cover only the projection of \(\vek u\) on that direction. The error is therefore at most the fraction \(k\,|\vek u|\) of the amplitude.

The wavelength is the speed of the wave divided by the frequency \(f\). In one oscillation, which lasts \(1/f\), the pattern moves by \(c/f\), and every point is then in the same state as at the start of the oscillation. So the pattern repeats over the distance \(c/f\). We compute the speeds in the next section. For steel (\(E = 196\) GPa, \(\nu = 0{,}3\), \(\rho = 7800\) kg/m³) at 1 kHz the wavelength is \(5{,}82\) m for displacement along the direction of travel and \(3{,}11\) m for displacement perpendicular to it. With a displacement of 1 \(\mu\)m this gives \(k\,|\vek u| = 1{,}1 \cdot 10^{-6}\) for the first and \(2{,}0 \cdot 10^{-6}\) for the second displacement. In the figure the gap \(|\vek u|\) is about an eighth of the wavelength, in steel it is \(1{,}7 \cdot 10^{-7}\) of the wavelength.

This is the same smallness as the strains of linear theory, and that is no accident. The strain of a wave is the derivative of the displacement with respect to position, which by the same computation is at most \(k A\). Since \(|\vek u| \le A\), the error is at most the same fraction as the largest strain. When linear theory neglects strains compared with \(1\), it neglects this error with them.

The model has two more idealizations. For each we say why it is good here, when it is not, and how much it changes the result.

  1. The density \(\rho\) is constant. The mass of a small piece does not change, but its volume changes by the fraction \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = \operatorname{div}\vek u\) (section 2.10.5), so the density changes by about the same fraction. For a field that varies only along the direction of travel, \(\operatorname{div}\vek u\) is the derivative of the displacement component in that direction, so at most \(k A\), for steel at 1 kHz and 1 \(\mu\)m at most \(1{,}1 \cdot 10^{-6}\). The term \(\rho\,\vek u_{\mathrm{tt}}\) therefore changes by at most \(1{,}1 \cdot 10^{-6}\) of its value. This fails at large deformations, when the volume changes by percents.
  2. The stress at each instant is determined by the strain at the same instant through Hooke's law: the material has no memory, no heat flows and nothing damps the motion. Without damping an oscillation never dies out. Metal structures have a damping ratio (definition 9.5) of about \(0{,}005\), losses in the joints included (FPGL, PDF p. 46). With damping each peak of the oscillation is smaller than the one before (the figure shows the ratio \(0{,}01\)), and at the ratio \(0{,}005\) it is \(0{,}969\) times as large (section 9.5). After ten oscillations \(73\) % of the amplitude is left, after a hundred \(4{,}3\) %, and at 1 kHz a hundred oscillations take a tenth of a second. The model without damping is therefore good for a few oscillations, but for a long-lasting oscillation we must add damping to it.
Theorem 13.33 Navier-Lamé equations with inertia (Navier-Laméjeve enačbe z vztrajnostjo)

Let the material be homogeneous, isotropic and linearly elastic, with Lamé constants \(\lambda\) and \(\mu\) (\(\nu \neq -1\), \(\nu \neq \tfrac12\)) and constant density \(\rho > 0\). Let \(D\) be an open set of points of the body, \(I\) an open time interval and \(\vek u(\vek x, t)\) a displacement field with continuous second partial derivatives in the coordinates and in \(t\) (also mixed) on \(D \times I\). Let \(\ten\sigma\) be the stresses obtained from \(\vek u\) at every \(t\) as in theorem 11.6, \(\vek b\) the body force density and \(\vek a = \vek u_{\mathrm{tt}}\). Then:

  1. At a point \(P\) of \(D\) at time \(t\) of \(I\) the stresses satisfy the equation of motion of a body (8.74) if and only if at \(P\) at time \(t\)
    \[\mu\,\nabla^2\vek u + (\lambda + \mu)\,\nabla(\operatorname{div}\vek u) + \vek b = \rho\,\vek u_{\mathrm{tt}} . \tag{13.33}\]
  2. Let \(V\) be a part of the body that satisfies the assumptions of corollary 4.41 (a body of boxes with surface \(S\), every point of \(V\) an interior point, continuously differentiable stresses on an open set \(D \supset V\), continuous \(\vek b\), the Cauchy assumption at every point of the surface). Let (13.33) hold at every point of \(V\) at time \(t\). Then
    \[\int_{\mathrm{S}} \vek t(\vek n)\,\mathrm{d}A + \int_{\mathrm{V}} \vek b\,\mathrm{d}V = \int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\vek u_{\mathrm{tt}}\,\mathrm{d}V , \tag{13.34}\]
    where \(\vek t(\vek n)\) is the stress vector at a point of the surface \(S\) for the outward normal \(\vek n\).
Proof

We must show two things: that the equation of motion of a body at a point is the same equation as (13.33), and that the equation for a part of the body follows from it. The first is the computation of theorem 11.6 with a different right side, the second the steps of corollary 4.41 with a different right side.

  1. At fixed \(t\) the field \(\vek u(\cdot, t)\) has continuous second derivatives. Steps 1 to 6 of the proof of theorem 11.6 use only strain, Hooke's law and mixed derivatives, not equilibrium. Hence at \(P\) at \(t\), for \(\ix{i} = x, y, z\),

    \[\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{ji}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} = \mu\,\nabla^2 u_{\mathrm{i}} + (\lambda + \mu)\,\frac{\partial(\operatorname{div}\vek u)}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}} .\]

  2. The equation of motion (8.74) with the index names interchanged is \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{ji}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}} + b_{\mathrm{i}} = \rho\,a_{\mathrm{i}}\). By (13.32), \(a_{\mathrm{i}} = \partial^2 u_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial t^2\).

  3. In the left side of step 2 we insert the right side of step 1. This gives \(\mu\,\nabla^2 u_{\mathrm{i}} + (\lambda + \mu)\,\partial(\operatorname{div}\vek u)/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}} + b_{\mathrm{i}} = \rho\,\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial t^2\).

    Steps 1 and 3 are equalities, so one equation holds exactly when the other holds. The three equations together are the vector equation (13.33), which proves part 1.

  4. Part 2. By part 1 the first equation of motion holds at every point of \(V\), \(\dfrac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}}{\partial x} + \dfrac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}}{\partial y} + \dfrac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}}{\partial z} + b_{\mathrm{x}} = \rho\,\dfrac{\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial t^2}\). All five terms are continuous on \(V\), since derivatives of stresses are linear combinations of second derivatives of displacements.

  5. The integral of a sum is the sum of the integrals (lemma 4.10, part 1). By the divergence theorem 4.14 with the field \((\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}})\) the integral of the first three terms equals \(\int_{\mathrm{S}} (\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}n_{\mathrm{x}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}n_{\mathrm{y}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}n_{\mathrm{z}})\,\mathrm{d}A\). By Cauchy's formula (theorem 2.26) this is \(\int_{\mathrm{S}} t_{\mathrm{x}}(\vek n)\,\mathrm{d}A\), as in step 4 of the proof of corollary 4.41. The \(x\) component of (13.34) is therefore \(\int_{\mathrm{S}} t_{\mathrm{x}}\,\mathrm{d}A + \int_{\mathrm{V}} b_{\mathrm{x}}\,\mathrm{d}V = \int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial t^2\,\mathrm{d}V\).

  6. For the components \(y\) and \(z\) we repeat steps 4 and 5 with the second and third equation of motion and with the fields \((\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{zy}})\) and \((\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}})\).

Source: Equation of motion: BOWER §2.3.1 (PDF p. 76), §2.4.4.1 (PDF p. 86), CM (5.3.12) (PDF p. 228) (definition 8.78). Equation (13.33) in index form \(C_{\mathrm{ijkl}}\,\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{k}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}\partial x_{\mathrm{l}} = \rho_0\,\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial t^2\): BOWER §4.4.4 (PDF p. 267), with the isotropic \(C_{\mathrm{ijkl}}\) of BOWER (PDF p. 268), which has \(2\mu\nu/(1 - 2\nu) = \lambda\). The proof is ours, written out from the proof of theorem 11.6.

Equation (13.33) is completed by boundary conditions as in the boundary value problem (displacement on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\), stress vector on \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\)) and by initial conditions, the displacement \(\vek u(\cdot, 0)\) and the velocity \(\vek u_{\mathrm{t}}(\cdot, 0)\). Figure 13.51 shows the body with all three. Whether such an initial-boundary value problem has exactly one solution for reasonable data, the book does not prove. We wrote down and checked the solutions for the string and the bar ourselves (sections 13.2 and 13.3).

13.4.2 Plane waves

What motions does equation (13.33) have without body force, in an unbounded body? The simplest are motions in which the displacement field moves as a whole. Let \(\vek n\) be a unit vector of direction, \(\vek e\) a unit vector along which the points move, and \(f\) a function of one variable. The field

\[\vek u(\vek x, t) = f(\vek n\cdot\vek x - c\,t)\,\vek e , \qquad c > 0 , \tag{13.35}\]

has at time \(t\) the same displacement at all points of the plane \(\vek n\cdot\vek x = s_0\), since there the argument of \(f\) equals \(s_0 - ct\). After a time \(\tau\) the same displacement belongs to the plane \(\vek n\cdot\vek x = s_0 + c\,\tau\), so the pattern moves in the direction \(\vek n\) with speed \(c\), as on the string (definition 13.4). We call such a motion a wave travelling in the direction \(\vek n\) (the lemma that follows calls it a plane wave, ravninski val, because it has the same displacement over the whole plane \(\vek n\cdot\vek x = s_0\)). In figure 13.52 we take points on a line in the direction \(\vek n\) and draw them, where they are at rest, as empty circles. The wave moves each point, and we draw the moved point as a filled circle. The points can move along \(\vek n\) (longitudinal, vzdolžno), and then they crowd together and thin out, or perpendicular to it (transverse, prečno). On the same line below we draw the transverse displacement as a curve at two times.

For which \(\vek e\) and \(c\) is the field (13.35) a solution of (13.33)? Every term of the equation is a second derivative of the field, in position or in time, so every term is proportional to \(f''\) (steps 1 to 3 of the proof of the lemma that follows). The condition on \(\vek e\) and \(c\) therefore does not touch the shape of \(f\), as long as \(f''\) is not zero everywhere.

The function \(f\) can be a wave, which bends, so the second derivative is not zero everywhere, or a straight line, which never bends and has second derivative zero. Both are drawn on the whole axis, since the argument \(\vek n\cdot\vek x - ct\) takes all real values.

On every panel we draw the direction of travel \(\vek n\) grey and horizontal, and the direction of displacement \(\vek e\) blackwhite. On the left panel \(\vek e = \vek n\) and there is no part perpendicular to \(\vek n\). On the middle panel \(\vek e\) is perpendicular to \(\vek n\) and there is no part along \(\vek n\). On the right panel \(\vek e\) is at an angle. Its part along \(\vek n\) is the shadow that \(\vek e\) casts on the horizontal direction, its part perpendicular to it the shadow on the vertical direction, and we draw both blue.

Steps 1 and 2 of the proof below differentiate by the chain rule. What \(\partial s/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}} = n_{\mathrm{j}}\) means, let us draw in figure 13.55 in the plane \(xy\), where \(n_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\). At a chosen time the points with the same value \(s = \vek n\cdot\vek x - ct\) are lines perpendicular to \(\vek n\), the wavefronts (valovne fronte). We draw them grey, each half a unit of \(s\) further in the direction of \(\vek n\). From point \(P\) we make a step \(h\) along the \(x\) axis. Its end lies on the same front as the end of the step \(n_{\mathrm{x}} h\) along \(\vek n\), because the front is perpendicular to \(\vek n\) and \(n_{\mathrm{x}} h\) is the shadow of the step on the direction \(\vek n\). The phase therefore increases by \(n_{\mathrm{x}} h\), which means \(\partial s/\partial x = n_{\mathrm{x}}\). For \(n_{\mathrm{x}} = 0{,}8\) and \(h = 2{,}5\) this is 2, four fronts. The step \(h\) along the \(y\) axis ends on the front of the step \(n_{\mathrm{y}} h = 1{,}5\), three fronts. The second derivative in \(x_{\mathrm{j}}\) uses this factor once more, so \(n_{\mathrm{j}}^2\) appears. With time the fronts move in the direction \(\vek n\) with the speed \(c\) (as the pattern in figure 13.52), so \(s\) at a fixed point decreases with the rate \(c\) and \(\partial s/\partial t = -c\).

The Laplace operator adds the second derivatives in all three axes, so \(f''(s)\,e_{\mathrm{i}}\) multiplied by \(n_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + n_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + n_{\mathrm{z}}^2\). On the right we draw the right triangle with hypotenuse \(\vek n\) and legs \(n_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(n_{\mathrm{y}}\). The square on the leg \(n_{\mathrm{x}}\) has the area \(n_{\mathrm{x}}^2 = 0{,}64\), the square on the leg \(n_{\mathrm{y}}\) the area \(n_{\mathrm{y}}^2 = 0{,}36\). By the Pythagorean theorem together they have the area of the square on the hypotenuse, \(|\vek n|^2 = 1\). In space a third square \(n_{\mathrm{z}}^2\) is added and the sum is again \(|\vek n|^2 = 1\). The sum of the second derivatives along the axes is therefore just the second derivative along \(\vek n\), \(\nabla^2 u_{\mathrm{i}} = f''(s)\,e_{\mathrm{i}}\).

Step 5 of the proof below writes the vector \(\vek w\) as the sum of a part along \(\vek n\) and a part along \(\vek e_\perp\), each with its own coefficient. In figure 13.56 we draw the axis \(\rho c^2\) and above it both coefficients with the numbers of steel. The coefficient of the part along \(\vek e_\perp\), \(\mu - \rho c^2\), is a line that falls by as much as \(\rho c^2\) grows, and it crosses the axis at \(\rho c^2 = \mu\). The coefficient of the part along \(\vek n\), \(\lambda + 2\mu - \rho c^2\), falls equally fast and crosses the axis at \(\rho c^2 = \lambda + 2\mu\). The lines are parallel, so the gap between them is the same everywhere, \(\lambda + \mu = 188{,}46\) GPa, and they are never zero at the same time.

Below we draw the directions \(\vek n\) and \(\vek e\) for a slanted \(\vek e\) at 45 degrees and from them the vector \(\vek w\) at three values of \(\rho c^2\). Each part of \(\vek w\) is a blue side, and \(\vek w\) is a blackwhite arrow that adds the two parts. At \(\rho c^2 = \mu\) the part along \(\vek e_\perp\) is zero, but the part along \(\vek n\) remains, \((\lambda + \mu)(\vek e\cdot\vek n) = 133{,}3\) GPa. At \(\rho c^2 = 170\) GPa, dashed in the upper picture, both parts are nonzero, 66,4 GPa along \(\vek n\) and 66,9 GPa opposite to \(\vek e_\perp\). At \(\rho c^2 = \lambda + 2\mu\) the part opposite to \(\vek e_\perp\) remains, 133,3 GPa long. A slanted \(\vek e\) therefore gives \(\vek w = \vek 0\) at no speed. The remaining part vanishes only if \(\vek e\) has no part in its direction: at \(\rho c^2 = \lambda + 2\mu\) we need \(\vek e_\perp = \vek 0\), as on the left panel of figure 13.54, and at \(\rho c^2 = \mu\) we need \(\vek e\cdot\vek n = 0\), as on the middle panel.

Lemma 13.34 Plane waves in an isotropic body (Ravninski valovi v izotropnem telesu)

Let the material be as in theorem 13.33, with modulus of elasticity \(E > 0\) and Poisson's ratio \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\) (theorem 3.64), without body force, \(\vek b = \vek 0\). Let \(\vek n\) be a unit vector, \(\vek e\) a unit vector, \(c > 0\) and \(f\) a twice continuously differentiable function that is not everywhere linear (\(f''\) is not everywhere zero). Then the field (13.35) is a solution of (13.33) at all points of space and at all times if and only if one of the following holds:

  1. \(\vek e = \pm\vek n\) and \(c = c_{\mathrm{L}}\) (longitudinal wave, speed \(c_{\mathrm{L}}\)), where
    \[c_{\mathrm{L}}^2 = \frac{\lambda + 2\mu}{\rho} = \frac{E\,(1 - \nu)}{\rho\,(1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)} , \tag{13.36}\]
  2. \(\vek e\cdot\vek n = 0\) and \(c = c_{\mathrm{T}}\) (transverse wave, speed \(c_{\mathrm{T}}\)), where
    \[c_{\mathrm{T}}^2 = \frac{\mu}{\rho} = \frac{E}{2\rho\,(1 + \nu)} . \tag{13.37}\]

Moreover \(c_{\mathrm{L}} > c_{\mathrm{T}}\).

Proof

We must show that equation (13.33) for the field (13.35) reduces to a condition on \(\vek e\) and \(c\). We compute the derivatives, insert them and split the vector into a component along \(\vek n\) and one perpendicular to it.

  1. Let \(s = \vek n\cdot\vek x - c\,t\). By the chain rule \(\partial s/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}} = n_{\mathrm{j}}\) and \(\partial s/\partial t = -c\), so \(\partial u_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}} = f'(s)\,n_{\mathrm{j}} e_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}^2 = f''(s)\,n_{\mathrm{j}}^2 e_{\mathrm{i}}\). Since \(\vek n\) is a unit vector, \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} n_{\mathrm{j}}^2 = 1\) and \(\nabla^2 u_{\mathrm{i}} = f''(s)\,e_{\mathrm{i}}\).
  2. The divergence is \(\operatorname{div}\vek u = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} f'(s)\,n_{\mathrm{j}} e_{\mathrm{j}} = f'(s)\,(\vek n\cdot\vek e)\), and its derivative in \(x_{\mathrm{i}}\) is \(f''(s)\,(\vek n\cdot\vek e)\,n_{\mathrm{i}}\). The second time derivative is \(\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial t^2 = c^2 f''(s)\,e_{\mathrm{i}}\).
  3. By steps 1 and 2, equation (13.33) with \(\vek b = \vek 0\) is
    \[f''(s)\,\Big( (\mu - \rho c^2)\,\vek e + (\lambda + \mu)\,(\vek n\cdot\vek e)\,\vek n \Big) = \vek 0 .\]
    We denote the vector in the large brackets by \(\vek w\). It does not depend on \(\vek x\) and \(t\).
  4. If \(\vek w = \vek 0\), the equation holds for every \(f\). If \(\vek w \neq \vek 0\), we need \(f''(s) = 0\) for every \(s\) that is reached, and these are all real numbers, since \(\vek n\cdot\vek x - ct\) takes all values. Then \(f''\) would be zero everywhere, which we excluded. The equation therefore holds exactly when \(\vek w = \vek 0\).
  5. Split \(\vek e = (\vek e\cdot\vek n)\,\vek n + \vek e_\perp\) with \(\vek e_\perp\cdot\vek n = 0\). Since \(\vek n\cdot\vek n = 1\), \(\vek w = (\mu - \rho c^2)\,\vek e_\perp + \big((\mu - \rho c^2) + (\lambda + \mu)\big)(\vek e\cdot\vek n)\,\vek n\). The vectors \(\vek e_\perp\) and \(\vek n\) are perpendicular, so \(\vek w = \vek 0\) exactly when both terms are zero: \((\mu - \rho c^2)\,\vek e_\perp = \vek 0\) and \((\lambda + 2\mu - \rho c^2)\,(\vek e\cdot\vek n) = 0\).
  6. If \(\vek e_\perp = \vek 0\), then \(\vek e = \pm\vek n\), \(\vek e\cdot\vek n = \pm 1 \neq 0\) and the second condition gives \(\rho c^2 = \lambda + 2\mu\), which is possibility 1.
  7. If \(\vek e_\perp \neq \vek 0\), the first condition gives \(\rho c^2 = \mu\). The second condition is then \((\lambda + \mu)\,(\vek e\cdot\vek n) = 0\). By equation (3.26), \(\lambda + \mu = \dfrac{\nu E}{(1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)} + \dfrac{E}{2(1 + \nu)} = \dfrac{E}{2(1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)}\), which is positive for \(E > 0\) and \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\). Hence \(\vek e\cdot\vek n = 0\), which is possibility 2.
  8. Conversely, in possibilities 1 and 2 both conditions of step 5 hold, so \(\vek w = \vek 0\) and the field (13.35) is a solution.
  9. Expressions with \(E\) and \(\nu\): \(\lambda + 2\mu = \dfrac{\nu E}{(1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)} + \dfrac{E}{1 + \nu} = \dfrac{E\,(1 - \nu)}{(1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)}\) and \(\mu = \dfrac{E}{2(1 + \nu)}\) by (3.26).
  10. Since \(\lambda + \mu > 0\) (step 7), \(\lambda + 2\mu = \mu + (\lambda + \mu) > \mu > 0\), so \(c_{\mathrm{L}}^2 > c_{\mathrm{T}}^2\).

Source: BOWER §4.4.4 (PDF p. 267-268): plane wave with direction \(\vek p\) and displacement \(\vek a\), speeds \(c_{\mathrm{L}}\) and \(c_{\mathrm{S}}\) from the eigenvalues of the acoustic tensor. BOWER's \(c_{\mathrm{S}}\) is our \(c_{\mathrm{T}}\). The proof by substitution and splitting is ours.

The two speeds differ, and their ratio depends only on Poisson's ratio: \(c_{\mathrm{L}}^2/c_{\mathrm{T}}^2 = (\lambda + 2\mu)/\mu = 2(1 - \nu)/(1 - 2\nu)\). For steel with \(E = 196\) GPa, \(\nu = 0{,}3\) and \(\rho = 7800\) kg/m³, by (3.26) \(\mu = G = 75{,}38\) GPa, \(\lambda + \mu = 188{,}46\) GPa, \(\lambda + 2\mu = 263{,}85\) GPa and

\[c_{\mathrm{L}} = 5816 \text{ m/s} , \qquad c_{\mathrm{T}} = 3109 \text{ m/s} , \qquad \frac{c_{\mathrm{L}}^2}{c_{\mathrm{T}}^2} = 3{,}5 .\]

The ratio \(3{,}5\) is the ratio of the lengths of the segment \(\lambda + 2\mu\) and the segment \(\mu\).

The reason lies in what holds the material back during the motion. At the bottom of the figure we take a small element, before the wave a square, which we draw grey dashed, and in the wave we draw it orange. In a longitudinal wave the element gets longer (blue), but its height stays. The displacement depends only on the position along \(\vek n\), so the neighbours above and below it move exactly as it does, and the element cannot contract sideways. Put the axis \(x\) in the direction \(\vek n\). Hooke's law (3.28) gives \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \lambda\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}) + 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), and since \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = (\lambda + 2\mu)\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\). Besides \(2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) there is \(\lambda\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), which raises the stiffness. A transverse wave shifts the element (blue) and turns it from a square into a parallelogram. Why does \(\lambda\) not enter here? Because \(\lambda\) multiplies the sum \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\), which is the relative change of volume (section 2.10.5), and the parallelogram has the same base and the same height as the square, so the same area. Shear does not change the volume, so \(\lambda\) adds nothing to the stiffness and only \(\mu\) remains.

So one blow reaches a distant point twice. Lamb computed in 1904 that in a body struck briefly at one place, the motion far from the blow begins after the time the longitudinal wave needs, and a second stage of the motion after the time the transverse wave needs (LOVE, PDF p. 328).

Take a large steel block and strike it briefly at the point \(O\). We measure the motion at the point \(S\), 1 m away, and the time since the blow on the time axis below, in microseconds. The longitudinal wave gets there after \(t_{\mathrm{L}} = 1\ \text{m}/c_{\mathrm{L}} = 172\ \mu\)s, the transverse after \(t_{\mathrm{T}} = 1\ \text{m}/c_{\mathrm{T}} = 322\ \mu\)s, \(150\ \mu\)s later. The delay grows in proportion to the distance, at 2 m it is twice as large, so from the delay and the two speeds we compute how far away the blow was.

The lemma allows only two speeds, and each is set by the direction of the displacement relative to the direction of travel. Let us look at three fields, each missing one of the conditions of the lemma. For steel \(\lambda + 2\mu = 263{,}85\) GPa and \(\lambda + \mu = 188{,}46\) GPa. The vector \(\vek w\) is the one from step 3 of the proof of the lemma, and the field is a solution exactly when \(\vek w = \vek 0\) (step 4).

  1. A longitudinal wave with the speed of the bar. Let \(\vek e = \vek n\) and \(c = \sqrt{E/\rho} = 5012{,}80\) m/s, which is \(0{,}862\,c_{\mathrm{L}}\), so that \(\rho c^2 = E = 196{,}00\) GPa. Then \(\vek w = (\mu - \rho c^2 + \lambda + \mu)\,\vek n = (\lambda + 2\mu - \rho c^2)\,\vek n = (263{,}85 - 196{,}00)\,\vek n = 67{,}85\ \text{GPa}\ \vek n \ne \vek 0\). The equation misses \(25{,}7\) % of the term \((\lambda + 2\mu)\,f''\). The field depends only on \(\vek n\cdot\vek x\), so there are no lateral deformations, the material cannot contract sideways, and the stiffness is \(\lambda + 2\mu\), not \(E\). The speed \(\sqrt{E/\rho}\) appears only for a slender bar with free sides, as an approximation for long waves (section 13.4.3).
  2. A displacement at an angle. Let \(\vek n = (1, 0, 0)\) and \(\vek e = (\cos 45^\circ, \sin 45^\circ, 0)\). At \(c = c_{\mathrm{L}}\) we have \(\mu - \rho c^2 = -(\lambda + \mu)\) and \(\vek w = (0,\ -133{,}26,\ 0)\) GPa. At \(c = c_{\mathrm{T}}\) we have \(\mu - \rho c^2 = 0\) and \(\vek w = (133{,}26,\ 0,\ 0)\) GPa. In both cases \(|\vek w| = 133{,}26\) GPa, never zero. Step 5 of the proof requires two things: \((\mu - \rho c^2)\,\vek e_\perp = \vek 0\) gives \(c = c_{\mathrm{T}}\), because \(\vek e_\perp \ne \vek 0\), and \((\lambda + 2\mu - \rho c^2)\,(\vek e\cdot\vek n) = 0\) gives \(c = c_{\mathrm{L}}\), because \(\vek e\cdot\vek n \ne 0\). One speed cannot be both, so a mixed field is not one wave.
  3. A straight line instead of a wave. Let \(f(s) = a\,s\), so \(f'' = 0\). The field \(a\,(\vek n\cdot\vek x - ct)\,\vek e\) satisfies the equation for every \(\vek e\) and every \(c\), because \(\vek w\) is multiplied by \(f'' = 0\). No speed is selected, so the lemma requires \(f''\) not everywhere zero.

13.4.3 The bar as a limiting case

The bar in chapter 8 (equation (8.129)) has wave speed \(\sqrt{E/\rho}\) and not \(c_{\mathrm{L}}\). Why, if the bar is part of a body? The difference is in the boundary conditions. The longitudinal wave (13.36) was obtained in an unbounded body, where the material cannot deform sideways (contract or expand) because its neighbours move exactly as it does. The sides of a slender bar are free and the material contracts sideways. GR §4.3.1 (PDF p. 262) treats the bar under the assumption that displacements and stresses are constant over the cross-section, which holds for a slowly varying cross-section and loading in the axis only. From the body we obtain it as a model with two choices. The state of stress has only the component \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), all other components are zero. Why is this good here? Nothing touches a free side, so the stress vector on it is zero, and with it the stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}\) on a side with normal in the direction \(y\), and likewise on the sides with normal in the direction \(z\). In a slender bar every point is close to a side, so on the short way from one side to the other these stresses stay small, if the state changes slowly along the bar compared with the height \(h\). When this fails, we show after the lemma. Of the three equations of motion we keep only the one in the direction of the axis \(x\), and so neglect the inertia of the transverse motion. This is not an exact consequence of the equations of the body. What the model neglects we estimate after the lemma.

Let us draw a bar of height \(h\) along the axis \(x\) as it is before the wave, grey dashed, and in it the cross-sections at \(x\) and \(x + \Delta x\). The wave moves the cross-sections along the axis. We draw the moved ones orange, and their displacements \(u\) and \(u + \Delta u\) blue above the bar. The second cross-section has moved more, so the piece between them has got longer. The stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) pulls it outward on both sides. The sides of the piece are free and nothing holds them, so the piece gets thinner: each side moves inward by \(\nu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\,h/2\) (definition 3.8).

Lemma 13.35 Speed of a longitudinal wave in a slender bar (Hitrost vzdolžnega vala v tanki palici)

Let a bar of homogeneous, isotropic, linearly elastic material with modulus of elasticity \(E > 0\), Poisson's ratio \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\) and density \(\rho > 0\) have axis \(x\). Let the state of stress in the bar be uniaxial: \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(x, t)\) is the only nonzero component of stress. Let \(u(x, t)\) be the displacement in the direction of the axis, equal at all points of the cross-section, with continuous second partial derivatives. Let the first equation of motion (8.74) (in the direction of the axis \(x\)) hold. Then

\[E\,\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} + b_{\mathrm{x}} = \rho\,\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial t^2} . \tag{13.38}\]

Without body force this is the wave equation with speed \(c_{\mathrm{B}}\), where

\[c_{\mathrm{B}}^2 = \frac{E}{\rho} , \tag{13.39}\]

and \(c_{\mathrm{B}} \le c_{\mathrm{L}}\) with equality exactly when \(\nu = 0\).

Proof

We must show that (13.38) follows from the uniaxial state of stress and the equation of motion in the direction \(x\), and compare the speed with \(c_{\mathrm{L}}\).

  1. By Hooke's law (theorem 3.19) in uniaxial stress \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/E\), so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\).
  2. By the definition of the small strain tensor (definition 1.121), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \partial u/\partial x\), so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\,\partial u/\partial x\).
  3. The first equation of motion (8.74) is \(\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/\partial x + \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}/\partial y + \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}/\partial z + b_{\mathrm{x}} = \rho\,a_{\mathrm{x}}\). Since \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}} = 0\) by the assumption of uniaxial stress (on the free sides only the \(x\) component of the stress vector is zero, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}n_{\mathrm{y}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}n_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\), elsewhere we assume it), what remains is \(\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/\partial x + b_{\mathrm{x}} = \rho\,a_{\mathrm{x}}\), and by (13.32) \(a_{\mathrm{x}} = \partial^2 u/\partial t^2\). Inserting step 2 gives (13.38).
  4. For \(b_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\), \(\partial^2 u/\partial t^2 = c_{\mathrm{B}}^2\,\partial^2 u/\partial x^2\) with \(c_{\mathrm{B}}^2 = E/\rho\), an equation of the same form as for the string (theorem 13.3) with speed \(c_{\mathrm{B}}\) in place of \(c\).
  5. Comparison: \(c_{\mathrm{B}}^2/c_{\mathrm{L}}^2 = E/(\lambda + 2\mu) = (1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)/(1 - \nu)\) by (13.36). Since \(1 - \nu > 0\), this ratio is less than 1 exactly when \((1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu) < 1 - \nu\), that is \(1 - \nu - 2\nu^2 < 1 - \nu\), so \(\nu \neq 0\). At \(\nu = 0\) it equals 1. The speeds \(c_{\mathrm{B}}\) and \(c_{\mathrm{L}}\) are positive, so \(c_{\mathrm{B}}^2 \le c_{\mathrm{L}}^2\) gives \(c_{\mathrm{B}} \le c_{\mathrm{L}}\).

Source: GR §4.3.1 (PDF p. 262): the assumption of constant displacements and stresses over the cross-section. BOWER §4.4.3 (PDF p. 266): \(c_{\mathrm{B}}^2 = E/\rho_0\) with uniaxial stress, with the remark that the equation is exact for \(\nu = 0\) and otherwise cannot hold, since it neglects the transverse motion. The proof is ours.

For steel \(c_{\mathrm{B}} = 5013\) m/s, which is \(0{,}862\,c_{\mathrm{L}}\), because \(E/(\lambda + 2\mu) = 0{,}743\). This is the speed of the first example after lemma 13.34, which was not a solution in an unbounded body. There the material cannot get thinner, because its neighbours move as it does, but in a bar the sides allow it, so the bar is softer and the wave in it slower.

What does the lemma neglect? In uniaxial stress, by Hooke's law the transverse strain is \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = -\nu\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/E\) (definition 3.8), so the points of the cross-section also move sideways. With uniaxial stress the equations of motion for \(y\) and \(z\) would require \(0 = \rho\,a_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(0 = \rho\,a_{\mathrm{z}}\), but the accelerations \(a_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(a_{\mathrm{z}}\) are not zero. This remainder is the inertia of the transverse motion, which the lemma neglects. How large it is, figure 13.60 shows.

Take a piece of the bar of height \(h = 40\) mm and width \(b = 20\) mm, 160 mm long, drawn grey dashed before the wave. A point at height \(y\) from the axis moves sideways in the wave by \(-\nu\,y\,\partial u/\partial x\), a side, where \(y = h/2\), by \(\nu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\,h/2\), the same in both parts of the figure. At a wavelength of 4 m the strain hardly changes over 160 mm, so both sides move by the same amount and stay straight. At a wavelength of 8 cm the bar gets thinner and thicker within one wavelength, and the sides undulate. As the wave passes, every point of a side goes in and out once in each oscillation. An oscillation lasts \(\ell/c_{\mathrm{B}}\), so the short wave moves the points of a side \(50\) times faster than the long one, over the same sideways distance. Kinetic energy is proportional to the square of the velocity, so the short wave has \(2500\) times more of the sideways motion.

Let us compute the ratio. The transverse velocity of the point with cross-section coordinates \(y\), \(z\), measured from the axis, is \(-\nu\,y\,\partial^2 u/\partial x\partial t\) and \(-\nu\,z\,\partial^2 u/\partial x\partial t\), and at wave number \(k\) the quantity \(\partial^2 u/\partial x\partial t\) has size \(k\) times \(\partial u/\partial t\). The average of \(y^2 + z^2\) over a rectangle \(b \times h\) is \((b^2 + h^2)/12\). The ratio of the kinetic energy of transverse motion to that of axial motion is therefore \(\nu^2 k^2 (b^2 + h^2)/12\). At a wavelength of 4 m, for example in the first mode of a fixed-free bar of length 1 m, this is \(3{,}7 \cdot 10^{-5}\), negligible. At a wavelength of 8 cm, \(k = 2\pi/0{,}08 = 78{,}5\) 1/m and the ratio is \(0{,}09 \cdot 6{,}2 \cdot 10^{3} \cdot 1{,}67 \cdot 10^{-4} \approx 0{,}09\), \(2500\) times more. This is not negligible, and then the one-dimensional equation is no longer good. Then the assumptions of uniaxial stress and of \(u\) equal over the cross-section do not fit either: with the lateral displacement \(-\nu\,y\,\partial u/\partial x\) the shear strain would be \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = -\tfrac12\nu\,y\,\partial^2 u/\partial x^2\), not zero, so the cross-section bulges in the wave, most where the strain changes fastest along the bar. Compared with \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) this strain is of order \(\nu\,k\) times the size of the cross-section, the same order as the square root of the energy ratio. The natural frequencies of the bar from theorem 13.24, which use the speed \(c_{\mathrm{B}}\), are therefore never exact for \(\nu \neq 0\) (BOWER §4.4.3, PDF p. 266). They are a good approximation only for waves long compared with the size of the cross-section, where the ratio \(\nu^2 k^2 (b^2 + h^2)/12\) is small, and this is the condition of a slender bar.
