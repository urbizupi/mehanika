Chapter 14 Heat conduction

14.2 The heat equation

Section 14.1 said how heat flows through matter when the temperature is known: the flux is proportional to the temperature gradient, \(\vek q = -k\,\nabla T\) (definition 14.3). But we do not know the temperature, we are looking for it. We want to know how the temperature at every point of a body changes with time. Figure 14.11 shows an experiment that poses this question.

Take a steel bar with one end held in hot water of temperature \(T_{\mathrm{v}}\) and the other in the air. At first the bar has everywhere the temperature of the surroundings \(T_0\). At the places \(x_1\), \(x_2\) and \(x_3\) we push thermometers into it. What they show we draw below: on the horizontal axis is the position \(x\) along the bar, on the vertical one the temperature \(T\). At time \(t_1\) only a little heat has entered the bar, so the temperature is high only near the water. Later, at time \(t_2\), it is higher everywhere. The places warm up one after another: at \(x_1\) near the water the temperature rose a lot between the two times, at \(x_2\) less, at \(x_3\) hardly at all. What decides how fast a place in the middle warms, say \(x_2\)? The answer must connect the flow of heat with the calculation of temperature: the more heat a piece of the bar receives than it gives away, the more its temperature rises. This equation is the goal of the section. The answer to the question about the place \(x_2\) is given by figure 14.19 after corollary 14.10.

From history

To write how much heat there is in a piece of a body between two points \(a\) and \(b\) we need an integral over that piece with its limits in the notation (this is our reason for the story). The modern notation for a definite integral, with the limits written beside the integral sign, was introduced by Fourier in his book on the conduction of heat of 1822 (CAJNOT2, PDF p. 273). In the book he says that the sign denotes the integral that begins when the variable equals \(a\) and is complete when it equals \(b\) (CAJNOT2, PDF p. 274). The reason this notation fits the balance of a piece is ours: the piece is given in advance by \(a\) and \(b\), so every quantity of the piece must be written with the two limits. Fourier had used the notation earlier, in the Memoirs of the Paris Academy for 1819 and 1820, from which the beginning of the 1822 book is reprinted (CAJNOT2, PDF p. 274). The lower part of figure 14.12 shows such an integral as an area under a curve.

The piece of the body that we shall look at is thus the part of the bar between the limits \(a\) and \(b\) on the axis \(x\), which runs along the bar. The integral over it is the area under the curve of a quantity that this section has still to determine. We do the first calculation for the simplest body. Take a straight bar of constant cross-section, with cross-section area \(A\). We wrap the whole mantle of the bar in insulation, a material through which practically no heat flows, as in a thermos flask. Heat can then flow only along the bar, and the temperature is the same across every cross-section, \(T = T(x, t)\), where \(x\) is the position of the cross-section along the bar and \(t\) the time. At the top of figure 14.12 is the bar.

From the bar we take the piece between the cross-sections \(a\) and \(b\), \(a < b\). The piece is arbitrary, not necessarily short. We shall get the equation at a point from the balance holding for every piece, without ever making the piece shorter, as theorem 14.8 shows. The hatching along the mantle means that no heat enters or leaves there. Through the cross-section at \(a\) heat flows into the piece, through the cross-section at \(b\) out of it. The heat flux \(q(x, t)\) is by definition 14.2 the power of heat per unit area, in W/m\(^2\), and it is positive when heat flows in the direction of increasing \(x\). The power with which heat enters the piece is therefore \(A\,q(a, t)\), and the power with which it leaves is \(A\,q(b, t)\). Both powers have the unit W, because \(\text{W/m}^2\cdot\text{m}^2 = \text{W}\).

What happens to the difference between the power that enters and the power that leaves, \(A\,q(a, t) - A\,q(b, t)\)? It is like the state of an account: what comes in, goes out or stays, and that is why such an accounting is called a balance. We use a law the reader knows: energy is neither lost nor created, it changes only by as much as has entered the piece or left it (the first law of thermodynamics, CM §5.4.2, PDF p. 236, where it is written for a body as a whole: the rate of change of the total energy equals the power of the external forces plus the heat supplied per unit time). In a bar at rest there is no kinetic energy and the external forces do no work, so the difference of the fluxes is stored as internal energy of the piece. It is called internal because it is energy inside the matter, not the energy of the motion of the body as a whole. CM (PDF p. 236) says that internal energy is the sum of the energy of the microscopic motions of the molecules, the elastic strain energy and other forms of energy that we do not see as motion of the body. For us only this matters: when heat is supplied to a piece, its temperature rises. By how much is a property of the material that we measure: we supply heat to a piece of mass \(m\) and measure how much its temperature rises, if it is the same throughout the piece. For different materials the response is different, and we write it as a definition.

Definition 14.6 Specific heat (specifična toplota)
Motivation

A piece of material of mass \(m\) that has the same temperature \(T\) at every point gets a higher temperature when heat is supplied. The quotient of the supplied heat and the product \(m\,\Delta T\) is a number for the material, independent of the size of the piece.

Let \(e\) be the internal energy per unit mass of the material, in J/kg. The material has specific heat \(c\) (in J/(kg K)) if \(e\) depends only on the temperature \(T\) and \(\mathrm{d}e/\mathrm{d}T = c\). For constant \(c\) this is the same as \(e(T) = e(T_0) + c\,(T - T_0)\). A piece of mass \(m\) with uniform temperature whose temperature rises from \(T_0\) to \(T_1\) therefore receives the heat \(m\,c\,(T_1 - T_0)\). The word "specific" means "per unit mass": \(c\) is the heat that we must supply to one kilogram of the material to raise its temperature by one kelvin.

Source: CM §8.3.1 (PDF p. 402, read): \(e = e(T)\) and \(\mathrm{d}e/\mathrm{d}T = c_{\mathrm{v}}\), the specific heat at constant volume, which CM replaces by \(c_{\mathrm{p}}\) at constant pressure for solids. SADD §12.1 (PDF p. 379, read) uses \(c\) at constant volume. The sources differ and neither estimates the difference, so we take \(c\) as one measured number of the material. The unit J/(kg K) and the range \(10^2 < c < 10^4\) for all solids and fluids are given by COOK (PDF p. 469, read). The reason for the name "specific heat" is ours.

From now on \(\rho c\) means the product of density and specific heat. It has the unit \(\text{kg/m}^3\cdot\text{J/(kg K)} = \text{J/(m}^3\,\text{K)}\) and says how much heat a volume of \(1\ \text{m}^3\) of the material needs for its temperature to rise by one kelvin (BARBER §14.2, PDF p. 235). The heat that must be added to the piece with a uniform temperature for its temperature to rise is thus the product of the mass, \(c\) and the change of \(T\). For a piece with cross-section area \(A\) and temperature \(T(x, t)\) the mass is spread along the length with density \(\rho A\), so the internal energy of the piece is \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \rho A\,e(T)\,\mathrm{d}x\), and its derivative with respect to time is the stored power \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \rho c A\,T_{\mathrm{t}}\,\mathrm{d}x\) (derivative of an integral with respect to a parameter, lemma 4.19). The law of conservation of energy for the piece then gives the equation

\[\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \rho c A\,T_{\mathrm{t}}(x, t)\,\mathrm{d}x = A\,q(a, t) - A\,q(b, t) + \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b A\,Q(x, t)\,\mathrm{d}x , \tag{14.4}\]

in which we have added a possible source: \(Q\) is the power of heat generated in the material (for example by electric heating) per unit volume, in W/m\(^3\) (BARBER §14.2, PDF p. 235, CM §5.4.2, PDF p. 237). All terms have the unit W.

This is the balance for a bar. A body in more than one dimension has no cross-section that the flow crosses only twice. So we repeat the same calculation for a part \(V\) of an arbitrary body. Figure 14.13 shows such a part.

The part of the body \(V\) is a body made of boxes (definition 4.11), as in the divergence theorem. It has a surface \(S\) and an outward normal \(\vek n\) (a unit vector perpendicular to the surface, pointing out). The heat flux \(\vek q\) is a vector, so the amount of heat flowing outward through the surface per unit time is the integral of the component of the flux in the direction of the normal, \(\int_{\mathrm{S}} \vek q\cdot\vek n\,\mathrm{d}A\) (definition 14.2). Where heat enters, \(\vek q\cdot\vek n < 0\), where it leaves, \(\vek q\cdot\vek n > 0\). Inside are the sources \(Q\).

Before we write the calculation we say what the model assumes. We name each idealization, say why it holds here and when it does not, and give it a measure.

  1. The body is at rest: every part \(V\) consists at all times of the same material with the same density \(\rho\), and the mechanical work is zero. Because of thermal expansion the density changes at \(\Delta T = 30\) K by \(3\alpha\,\Delta T = 3\cdot 15\cdot 10^{-6}\cdot 30 = 1{,}35\cdot 10^{-3}\) (relative, steel of chapter 3, definition 3.21: for small strains the volumetric strain is three times the linear one), that is \(0{,}14\) %. The work of the stresses in this motion, which the model neglects, is the coupling term of thermoelasticity. SADD (§12.1, PDF p. 379, read) says that for most materials it is small under static or slow loading, and so we neglect it (the uncoupled problem). We do not prove an estimate of its size here, because we would need the specific heat of steel, which the project's books do not give.
  2. Heat is transferred only by conduction according to Fourier's law (definition 14.3). Inside a solid body this is true: heat flows in it by conduction, while by convection of the surrounding fluid and by radiation it passes only through its surface (COOK §12.1, PDF p. 468, read). On the surface \(S\) the idealization therefore does not hold, and there we write radiation and convection as boundary conditions in section 14.3, not into the equation for the interior. What this changes is given by a measure. The flux by radiation depends on the difference of the fourth powers of the absolute temperatures (COOK §12.1, PDF p. 469, read, treated in §12.3). At the same difference of \(10\) K it carries between \(600\) K and \(610\) K \((610^4 - 600^4)/(310^4 - 300^4) = 7{,}8\) times as much as between \(300\) K and \(310\) K, while conduction at the same difference and the same \(k\) carries the same in both cases. A boundary condition with radiation therefore makes the problem nonlinear (COOK, PDF p. 469).
  3. The material is isotropic (definition 3.10) and the conductivity \(k\) is constant. BARBER (§14.2, PDF p. 235, read) says that for real materials it depends on temperature, but the nonlinearity matters only over a large range of temperatures, and COOK (§12.1, PDF p. 469, read) says that dependence on temperature makes the conduction problem itself nonlinear. A measure: let \(k\) lie over the range of temperatures between \(0{,}95\,\bar k\) and \(1{,}05\,\bar k\), where \(\bar k\) is the middle value (an example, not a datum from a source). With the middle value we then misjudge the flux at a given gradient by at most \(5{,}3\) %. The error is largest at \(k = 0{,}95\,\bar k\), where \((\bar k - k)/k = 0{,}05/0{,}95 = 5{,}3\) %. This bound holds for the flux at a given gradient, not for the error of the computed temperature.
  4. The density \(\rho\) and the specific heat \(c\) are constant (definition 14.6), as in KREYSZIG §12.5 (PDF p. 583, read, assumption 1). When this fails: like the conductivity, both may depend on temperature (COOK §12.1, PDF p. 469, read). The density changes at \(\Delta T = 30\) K by only \(0{,}14\) %, from idealization 1. A measure for \(c\): let \(c\) lie over the range of temperatures between \(0{,}95\,\bar c\) and \(1{,}05\,\bar c\) (an example, not a datum from a source). With the middle value \(\bar c\) we then misjudge the stored power \(\rho c\,T_{\mathrm{t}}\) at a given rate of warming \(T_{\mathrm{t}}\) by at most \(0{,}05/0{,}95 = 5{,}3\) %, by the same calculation as in idealization 3. For a \(c\) that depends on \(T\) the term \(\rho c(T)\,T_{\mathrm{t}}\) would be nonlinear in \(T\).
  5. The source \(Q\) is a given continuous function of place and time. It does not depend on the solution. The model takes the power of the source as known before we compute the temperature, as for the sources that COOK lists (§12.1, PDF p. 468, read): resistance to electric current, absorption of microwave radiation and radioactive decay. When this fails: if the power of the source depends on the temperature, \(Q = Q(T)\), the source inside is unknown until we know \(T\). What this changes: for a \(Q(T)\) that is not linear in \(T\) (for example with a term \(T^2\)) the equation is no longer linear, and the rule that a sum of solutions of the equation without a source is again a solution, which section 14.5 uses, no longer holds.
  6. The temperature is measured in kelvins or in degrees Celsius: only the derivatives \(T_{\mathrm{t}}\) and \(\nabla T\) occur in the equations, and they are the same for both units, because the two differ only by a shift of the zero, which moves both profile curves up or down but changes neither their slope nor their rise (COOK §12.1, PDF p. 469). For radiation this would not hold.

In the derivation we count the energy of the part \(V\) over small cubes, as figure 14.14 shows.

Derivation 14.7 Heat balance of a part of the body (toplotna bilanca dela telesa)

Goal: for a part \(V\) of a body made of boxes and for time \(t\) write how the heat in \(V\) changes, with integrals over \(V\) and its surface \(S\). The assumptions are idealizations 1 to 5. We also need the temperature \(T(\vek x, t)\) to have a continuous derivative \(T_{\mathrm{t}}\) and continuous second partial derivatives in the coordinates on an open set \(D\) that contains \(V\), at all times in an open interval \(I\).

1
\[U(t) = \int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,e\big(T(\vek x, t)\big)\,\mathrm{d}V .\]
Hint

We cut the part \(V\) into small cubes. One cube with volume \(\Delta V\) has the mass \(\rho\,\Delta V\). Since \(e\) is the internal energy per unit mass (definition 14.6), the cube has the internal energy \(\rho\,e(T)\,\Delta V\). The energy of the part is the sum over all cubes, and for smaller and smaller cubes the integral over \(V\).

2
\[\frac{\mathrm{d}U}{\mathrm{d}t} = \int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho c\,T_{\mathrm{t}}\,\mathrm{d}V .\]
Hint

In a short time \(\Delta t\) the temperature of the cube rises by \(T_{\mathrm{t}}\,\Delta t\). Since \(c\) is the slope of the line \(e(T)\), its energy per unit mass grows by \(c\,T_{\mathrm{t}}\,\Delta t\), the energy of the cube by \(\rho c\,T_{\mathrm{t}}\,\Delta t\,\Delta V\), so per unit time by \(\rho c\,T_{\mathrm{t}}\,\Delta V\). The sum of this over the cubes is the integral on the right. More precisely: by definition 4.11 the integral over \(V\) is the sum of the integrals over the cells, each cell is a box. For a cell we use lemma 8.32 (the parameter is the time): the function \(\rho\,e(T)\) is continuous, its derivative with respect to time \(\rho\,e'(T)\,T_{\mathrm{t}} = \rho c\,T_{\mathrm{t}}\) is continuous, since \(e' = c\). The derivative of a finite sum is the sum of the derivatives.

3
\[\text{heat entering through } S \text{ per unit time:} \quad -\int_{\mathrm{S}} \vek q\cdot\vek n\,\mathrm{d}A .\]
Hint

Through the surface \(S\) flows outward \(\vek q\cdot\vek n\) per unit area, where \(\vek n\) is the outward normal (definition 14.2), and what enters is the opposite value. On the left, where heat enters, \(\vek q\cdot\vek n < 0\), so there \(-\vek q\cdot\vek n > 0\). The expression has the unit \(\text{W/m}^2\cdot\text{m}^2 = \text{W}\).

4
\[\text{heat generated in } V \text{ per unit time:} \quad \int_{\mathrm{V}} Q\,\mathrm{d}V .\]
Hint

\(Q\) is the power of the sources inside per unit volume, in W/m\(^3\) (idealization 5).

5
\[\int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho c\,T_{\mathrm{t}}\,\mathrm{d}V = -\int_{\mathrm{S}} \vek q\cdot\vek n\,\mathrm{d}A + \int_{\mathrm{V}} Q\,\mathrm{d}V . \tag{14.5}\]
Hint

The first law: the rate of change of the total energy equals the power of the external forces plus the heat supplied per unit time (CM §5.4.2, PDF p. 236). The total energy is kinetic and internal. For a body at rest (idealization 1) the kinetic energy and the power of the external forces are zero, since nothing moves, what remains is \(\mathrm{d}U/\mathrm{d}t\) from step 2 on the left and the heat from steps 3 and 4 on the right: what flows through the surface and what is generated inside.

6
\[\int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho c\,T_{\mathrm{t}}\,\mathrm{d}V = k\int_{\mathrm{S}} \nabla T\cdot\vek n\,\mathrm{d}A + \int_{\mathrm{V}} Q\,\mathrm{d}V . \tag{14.6}\]
Hint

By Fourier's law (definition 14.3) \(\vek q = -k\,\nabla T\) with constant \(k\) (idealization 3), so \(-\vek q\cdot\vek n = k\,\nabla T\cdot\vek n\).

Source: CM §5.4.2 (PDF pp. 236 to 238, read), KREYSZIG §12.5 (PDF pp. 583 to 584, read), BARBER §14.2 (PDF p. 235, read). CM takes a volume element \(\mathrm{d}x\) and KREYSZIG and BARBER a small cube, we take a part \(V\) of finite size, as in theorem 13.3. The derivation step by step is not from the sources.

Equation (14.6) holds for every part \(V\). Does the equation at every point follow from it? Yes, because \(V\) is arbitrary: a continuous function that has integral zero over every box is zero everywhere. Figure 14.15 shows why.

First on the bar, whose position runs along the axis \(x\) from \(0\) to \(L\). Suppose the function \(f\) were positive at the point \(x_0\). A continuous function does not jump, so it stays above half its value, \(f(x_0)/2\), also on some piece \([a, b]\) around \(x_0\). The integral over this piece is the area under the curve and is at least the area of the rectangle of height \(f(x_0)/2\) and width \(b - a\), so it is positive, not zero. In the body the piece is replaced by a cube \(K\) around the point \(P\), which lies in the ball where \(f > f(P)/2\), and the ball lies in the region \(D\) where we know the temperature. Why must this be shown and not just stated? Because the argument uses the continuity of the function under the integral, and that needs continuous second derivatives of the temperature. Where a second derivative does not exist, the equation at a point has no meaning, while the balance still holds (example 14.12 below).

Theorem 14.8 Heat equation (enačba prevajanja toplote)

Let \(D\) be an open set of points of the body, \(I\) an open time interval and \(T(\vek x, t)\) a temperature with continuous \(T_{\mathrm{t}}\) and continuous second partial derivatives in the coordinates on \(D\times I\). Let the material be as in idealizations 1 to 5, with constant \(\rho > 0\), \(c > 0\) and \(k > 0\), and let \(Q\) be continuous on \(D\times I\). Then equation (14.6) holds for every body \(V\) made of boxes in \(D\) and every time \(t \in I\) if and only if at every point \(\vek x\) of \(D\) and at every time \(t\) of \(I\)

\[\rho c\,T_{\mathrm{t}} = k\,\nabla^2 T + Q , \tag{14.7}\]
\[\rho c\,\dot T = k\,T_{,\mathrm{ii}} + Q \tag{14.7}\]

where \(\nabla^2\) is the Laplace operator (definition 11.5).

Proof

We must show that equation (14.6) for all parts \(V\) is one and the same equation as (14.7) at all points. We write the difference of the two sides as an integral of one continuous function over \(V\). Then we use that a continuous function with integral zero over every box cannot differ from zero at any point (figure 14.15, right).

  1. Let \(f = \rho c\,T_{\mathrm{t}} - k\,\nabla^2 T - Q\). The function \(f\) is continuous on \(D\times I\), because \(T_{\mathrm{t}}\), the second derivatives of \(T\) and \(Q\) are continuous.
  2. The field \(\vek v = k\,\nabla T = k\,(T_{\mathrm{x}}, T_{\mathrm{y}}, T_{\mathrm{z}})\) is continuously differentiable on \(D\), because \(T\) has continuous second derivatives. Its divergence (definition 4.13) is \(k\,(T_{\mathrm{xx}} + T_{\mathrm{yy}} + T_{\mathrm{zz}}) = k\,\nabla^2 T\). By the divergence theorem 4.14, item 2, \(\int_{\mathrm{V}} k\,\nabla^2 T\,\mathrm{d}V = \int_{\mathrm{S}} k\,\nabla T\cdot\vek n\,\mathrm{d}A\), an integral over the surface \(S\) with the outward normal \(\vek n\).
  3. By step 2 and linearity of the integral (lemma 4.10, item 1) the difference of the left and right sides of equation (14.6) equals \(\int_{\mathrm{V}} f\,\mathrm{d}V\). For \(V\) the equation is thus \(\int_{\mathrm{V}} f\,\mathrm{d}V = 0\).
  4. If (14.7) holds, then \(f = 0\) everywhere, so \(\int_{\mathrm{V}} f\,\mathrm{d}V = 0\) for every \(V\), and by step 3 (14.6) holds.
  5. Conversely, let (14.6) hold for every \(V\) and time \(t\). With \(t\) fixed assume that \(f(P, t) \neq 0\) at some point \(P\) of \(D\), say \(f(P, t) > 0\) (for \(< 0\) the calculation is the same with the signs exchanged). By continuity, and since \(D\) is open, there is a ball with centre \(P\) that lies in \(D\) and in which \(f(\cdot, t) > f(P, t)/2\). In it lies a box \(K\) that contains \(P\) (a cube with centre \(P\) and a side for which half the space diagonal is smaller than the radius of the ball). By monotonicity and the property of a constant function (lemma 4.10, items 2 and 4) \(\int_{\mathrm{K}} f\,\mathrm{d}V \ge \tfrac12 f(P, t)\,V_{\mathrm{K}} > 0\), where \(V_{\mathrm{K}}\) is the volume of the box, just as for the bar the integral is at least the area of the rectangle. This contradicts \(\int_{\mathrm{K}} f\,\mathrm{d}V = 0\) from step 3 for \(V = K\). So \(f = 0\) everywhere and (14.7) holds.

Source: KREYSZIG §12.5 (PDF p. 584, read): "Since this holds for any region in the body, the integrand (if continuous) must be zero everywhere." BARBER (14.15) (PDF p. 235), CM (8.3.3) (PDF p. 402), SADD (12.1.8) (PDF p. 379, without the coupling term). The proof with a box and continuity is not from the sources.

The theorem holds for bodies made of boxes. A round bar or a plate is not one, but by corollary 4.40 on a body cut into pieces equation (14.6) holds also for every part that we can cut into pieces between graphs, and we use this for the bar. If the conductivity \(k\) is not constant but a function with continuous first derivatives, the same proof with \(\vek v = k\,\nabla T\) gives \(\rho c\,T_{\mathrm{t}} = \nabla\cdot(k\,\nabla T) + Q\). A layered body, however, has a jump of \(k\), and there the equation at a point does not hold, as example 14.12 below shows.

The units are in order: \(\rho c\,T_{\mathrm{t}}\) has the unit \(\text{J/(m}^3\text{K)}\cdot\text{K/s} = \text{W/m}^3\), and \(k\,\nabla^2 T\) the unit \(\text{W/(m K)}\cdot\text{K/m}^2 = \text{W/m}^3\). Both sides are power per unit volume. Dividing by \(\rho c\) leaves one number of the material.

Definition 14.9 Thermal diffusivity (temperaturna prevodnost)

The thermal diffusivity of a material with conductivity \(k\), density \(\rho\) and specific heat \(c\) is the number, which says how fast an initial disturbance spreads,

\[\kappa = \frac{k}{\rho c} , \tag{14.8}\]

in m\(^2\)/s. The heat equation (theorem 14.8) is then \(T_{\mathrm{t}} = \kappa\,\nabla^2 T + Q/(\rho c)\).

Explanation

The name has its reason in \(\kappa\) combining two opposite properties: \(k\) says how well the material conducts heat, \(\rho c\) how much heat is needed to raise the temperature. A larger \(k\) means a faster flow, a larger \(\rho c\) slower warming, so \(\kappa\) is a measure of how fast a temperature disturbance spreads. The unit m\(^2\)/s is area per time (BARBER §14.2, PDF p. 236). "Diffusivity" is the same word as in diffusion, because the same equation holds for the mixing of one substance with another (KREYSZIG §12.5, PDF p. 584, read).

Source: BARBER (14.17) (PDF pp. 235 to 236, read), KREYSZIG §12.5 (PDF p. 584, read).

The letters \(c\) and \(\kappa\) already have other meanings in the book: \(c\) is the wave speed in chapter 13, \(\kappa\) the curvature in chapter 5. In this chapter they never meet their other meaning, the curvature of a beam will be written \(v''\).

For the bar of figure 14.12 we get the equation directly from the balance (14.4), with a finite piece and the fundamental theorem of calculus, as for the string (theorem 13.3). Figure 14.17 shows how the flux through the two cross-sections depends on the slope of the profile and what the fundamental theorem of calculus does here.

Above the bar we draw the temperature profile, which falls from the hot side on the left, and on the bar we choose the piece between \(a\) and \(b\). At the cross-section \(a\) the profile has the slope \(T_{\mathrm{x}}(a, t)\), the steepness of the tangent that touches it there. Fourier's law \(q = -k\,T_{\mathrm{x}}\) (definition 14.3) says that heat flows down the profile, and the more strongly the steeper the profile is. So at \(a\) the power \(A\,q(a, t) = -kA\,T_{\mathrm{x}}(a, t)\) enters the piece. At \(b\) the profile is flatter, the slope \(T_{\mathrm{x}}(b, t)\) is smaller in size, and the power that leaves, \(-kA\,T_{\mathrm{x}}(b, t)\), is smaller. The difference between what enters and what leaves is \(kA\,\big(T_{\mathrm{x}}(b, t) - T_{\mathrm{x}}(a, t)\big)\), the difference of the fluxes in the balance (14.4) written with the slopes. By how much does the slope change from \(a\) to \(b\)? Below we draw the curve \(T_{\mathrm{xx}}\), which says at each point how fast the slope changes there. On a short stretch of length \(\Delta x\) the slope changes by about \(T_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\Delta x\), the area of a thin column under the curve. From \(a\) to \(b\) these contributions add up to the area under the curve \(T_{\mathrm{xx}}\) between \(a\) and \(b\), so \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b T_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}x = T_{\mathrm{x}}(b, t) - T_{\mathrm{x}}(a, t)\). This is the fundamental theorem of calculus (theorem 3.55), applied to the derivative \(T_{\mathrm{x}}\). With it the difference of the fluxes is also written as an integral over the piece.

Corollary 14.10 Heat conduction in a bar (prevajanje toplote v palici)

Let a bar of constant cross-section of area \(A\) lie along \([0, L]\), insulated on its whole mantle. Let the temperature \(T(x, t)\) and the source \(Q(x, t)\) be constant over every cross-section. Let \(I\) be an open time interval and let \(T_{\mathrm{t}}\), \(T_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and \(Q\) be continuous on \([0, L]\times I\) (\(T_{\mathrm{x}}\) continuously differentiable in \(x\)). Let \(\rho\), \(c\), \(k\) be constant. Then the balance (14.4) with \(q = -k\,T_{\mathrm{x}}\), that is

\[\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \rho c A\,T_{\mathrm{t}}\,\mathrm{d}x = k A\,\big(T_{\mathrm{x}}(b, t) - T_{\mathrm{x}}(a, t)\big) + \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b A\,Q\,\mathrm{d}x , \tag{14.9}\]

holds for all \(0 \le a < b \le L\) and every \(t \in I\) if and only if for every \(x \in [0, L]\) and every \(t \in I\)

\[\rho c\,T_{\mathrm{t}} = k\,T_{\mathrm{xx}} + Q . \tag{14.10}\]
Proof

We must show that (14.9) for all pieces is the same equation as (14.10) at all points. We write the difference as an integral of a continuous function over a piece, as in the proof of theorem 13.3. Then we use the argument of figure 14.15, left.

  1. Let \(f = \rho c\,T_{\mathrm{t}} - k\,T_{\mathrm{xx}} - Q\). Fix \(t\). The function \(f(\cdot, t)\) is continuous on \([0, L]\), because \(T_{\mathrm{t}}\), \(T_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and \(Q\) are continuous.
  2. The function \(T_{\mathrm{x}}(\cdot, t)\) is differentiable with continuous derivative \(T_{\mathrm{xx}}\), so by the fundamental theorem of calculus (theorem 3.55, item 1) \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b T_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}x = T_{\mathrm{x}}(b, t) - T_{\mathrm{x}}(a, t)\): the area under the curve \(T_{\mathrm{xx}}\) is the difference of the slopes at the two ends of the piece.
  3. By step 2 and linearity (lemma 3.54) the difference of the left and right sides of equation (14.9), divided by \(A\), equals \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b f\,\mathrm{d}x\). Equation (14.9) for the piece \([a, b]\) is thus \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b f\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\).
  4. If (14.10) holds, then \(f = 0\), so \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b f\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\) for every piece.
  5. Conversely, let (14.9) hold for every piece. By step 3 \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b f\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\) for all \(0 \le a < b \le L\). Assume that \(f(x_0, t) > 0\) at some point \(x_0 \in [0, L]\) (for \(f(x_0, t) < 0\) the calculation is the same with the signs exchanged). Since \(f(\cdot, t)\) is continuous at \(x_0\) (definition 1.59 with \(\eta = f(x_0, t)/2\)), there is a piece \([a, b] \subset [0, L]\) with \(a < b\) that contains \(x_0\), on which \(f(\cdot, t) > f(x_0, t)/2\), above the dashed height. By lemma 3.54, item 3, then \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b f\,\mathrm{d}x \ge \tfrac12 f(x_0, t)\,(b - a) > 0\), the area of the rectangle. This contradicts \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b f\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\). So \(f = 0\) everywhere and (14.10) holds.

Source: KREYSZIG §12.6 (PDF p. 585, read) takes the bar equation \(u_{\mathrm{t}} = c^2 u_{\mathrm{xx}}\) from the equation of the body, CM (8.3.7) (PDF p. 403) for the fin. The proof with a finite piece is not from the sources.

The form is the same as for the string, with one difference: for the string the second derivative in time occurs and so two initial conditions are needed, here the first derivative occurs, so one initial condition suffices (section 14.3). The explanation lies in what the flux determines: the string accelerates, because the force determines the acceleration, in heat the flux determines only the rate of change of the temperature.

What does the equation say about one point? Without a source \(T_{\mathrm{t}} = \kappa\,T_{\mathrm{xx}}\): the temperature at a point changes the faster, the more the temperature profile is bent there. Figure 14.19 shows three shapes of the profile.

Under each profile is the bar with a piece around the middle, above it the temperature along the bar. On the left the profile has a peak in the middle: the middle is warmer than both neighbours. Heat flows from warmer to colder (\(q = -k\,T_{\mathrm{x}}\), definition 14.3), so out of the piece it flows through both cross-sections. The piece only gives heat away, so it cools: a moment later the peak is lower, by \(\Delta T\). At the peak the slope falls from positive on the left to negative on the right, so \(T_{\mathrm{xx}} < 0\), and the equation gives \(T_{\mathrm{t}} < 0\). In the middle is a trough: the middle is colder than its neighbours, heat flows into the piece from both sides and the bottom rises, \(T_{\mathrm{xx}} > 0\) and \(T_{\mathrm{t}} > 0\). On the right is a straight line: the slope is the same everywhere, so the flux through both cross-sections is the same, as much heat as enters on the warmer side leaves on the colder side. The piece gains and loses nothing, the temperature stays the same, \(T_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0\) and \(T_{\mathrm{t}} = 0\). So what matters is not how warm a point is, but how much it differs from its neighbours on both sides.

Now we can answer the question from the start of the section. At the place \(x_2\) of the bar in hot water the profile is bent upward like a trough, so it warms there, the faster, the more it is bent. At time \(t_1\) the profile at \(x_2\) was still almost flat, so the place then hardly warmed. The places warm one after another because the bent part of the profile travels away from the water.

Let us test the theorem on numbers. We take the copper bar for which KREYSZIG (§12.6, example 1, PDF p. 587) gives all the data, in the units of the source (cm, s, cal, degrees Celsius).

Example 14.11 Copper bar: balance of a piece (bakrena palica: bilanca kosa)

The bar has length \(L = 80\) cm, conductivity \(k = 0{,}95\) cal/(cm s °C), specific heat \(c = 0{,}092\) cal/(g °C) and density \(\rho = 8{,}92\) g/cm\(^3\) (KREYSZIG, PDF p. 587). So \(\rho c = 0{,}82064\) cal/(cm\(^3\) °C), and the thermal diffusivity is \(\kappa = k/(\rho c) = 1{,}1576\) cm\(^2\)/s. The ends are held at \(0\) °C. Let the temperature be \(T = 100\,\sin(\pi x/L)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma t}\) with a decay rate \(\gamma\), at time \(0\) thus \(100\,\sin(\pi x/L)\). We ask which \(\gamma\) satisfies equation (14.10) and how we test the balance of a piece. We compute powers per unit cross-section area (SymPy, num-02.py).

Figure 14.20 shows on the left the temperature profile at time \(t = 0\), with the position \(x\) on the horizontal axis and the temperature \(T\) on the vertical one. The curve is half a sine, zero at both ends. In blue is the piece between \(a = 20\) cm and \(b = 40\) cm. The two orange segments are tangents to the profile at \(a\) and \(b\): at \(a\) the slope is \(T_{\mathrm{x}}(a) = 100\,(\pi/L)\cos(\pi/4) = 2{,}777\) °C/cm, at \(b\) it is zero, because the peak is there.

  1. The right \(\gamma\): from equation (14.10) without a source \(\rho c\,(-\gamma) = k\,(-(\pi/L)^2)\), so \(\gamma = \kappa\pi^2/L^2 = 1{,}7852\cdot 10^{-3}\) 1/s. The decay rate is not arbitrary, it is determined by the material and the length of the bar. At the peak at \(x = 40\) cm \(T_{\mathrm{xx}} = -100\,(\pi/L)^2 = -0{,}1542\) °C/cm\(^2\), so there \(T_{\mathrm{t}} = \kappa\,T_{\mathrm{xx}} = -100\,\gamma = -0{,}1785\) °C/s: the peak cools by just under a fifth of a degree per second.
  2. The piece \([20, 40]\) at time \(0\): the left side of the balance, the stored power per area, is \(\int_{20}^{40}\rho c\,T_{\mathrm{t}}\,\mathrm{d}x = -2{,}638\) cal/(cm\(^2\) s). The right side, from the slopes of the orange tangents, is \(k\,(T_{\mathrm{x}}(40) - T_{\mathrm{x}}(20)) = 0{,}95\,(0 - 2{,}777) = -2{,}638\) cal/(cm\(^2\) s). The equality is exact (SymPy), negative because the piece is cooling. The flux at \(a\) points in the direction of decreasing \(x\) (\(q(a) = -k\,T_{\mathrm{x}}(a) < 0\)): heat flows out of the piece toward the cold end.
  3. The whole bar \([0, 80]\), under the whole profile: \(-7{,}461\) on both sides. The bar as a whole loses heat through both ends.

So the balance holds for every piece and for the right \(\gamma\). The test also shows when it does not hold.

Near miss 1: a wrong decay rate. Let \(\gamma' = 1{,}2\,\gamma\), with the same shape \(\sin(\pi x/L)\). The left side for the piece \([20, 40]\) is then \(-3{,}166\), the right stays \(-2{,}638\) (the slopes of the tangents do not change), the difference is \(-0{,}528\) cal/(cm\(^2\) s). For the whole bar \(-8{,}954\) against \(-7{,}461\). The residual \(f = \rho c\,T_{\mathrm{t}} - k\,T_{\mathrm{xx}}\) at time \(0\) is \(f = -0{,}0293\,\sin(\pi x/L)\) cal/(cm\(^3\) s), so at \(x = 40\) cm it is \(-0{,}0293\), which is \(1/6\) of the term \(\rho c\,T_{\mathrm{t}}\). The residual differs from zero at every interior point \(0 < x < L\), so the equation fails at every interior point. A bar that decayed faster than conduction demands would lose more heat than can flow out through its ends.

Near miss 2: the whole bar holds, a piece does not. Let \(T = 100\,\sin(2\pi x/L)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma t}\) with the same \(\gamma\) as before (the right one for \(\sin(2\pi x/L)\) would be four times larger, \(4\gamma\)). This is the right profile. Its axis \(T\) reaches below zero too, because the temperature on the right half of the bar is negative. For the whole bar both sides are zero: the stored power is \(0\), because the positive and the negative part of the sine cancel, and \(k\,(T_{\mathrm{x}}(80) - T_{\mathrm{x}}(0)) = 0\), because \(T_{\mathrm{x}}\) is the same at both ends. For the piece \([0, 20]\), however, the stored power is \(-1{,}865\). The right side takes the slopes of the orange tangents at \(0\) and \(20\) cm and is \(k\,(T_{\mathrm{x}}(20) - T_{\mathrm{x}}(0)) = 0{,}95\,(0 - 7{,}854) = -7{,}461\) cal/(cm\(^2\) s). The residual at the point \(x = 20\) cm is \(0{,}4395\) cal/(cm\(^3\) s), with the terms \(\rho c\,T_{\mathrm{t}} = -0{,}1465\) and \(k\,T_{\mathrm{xx}} = -0{,}5860\), a ratio of \(4{,}0\), which is the ratio of the right and the used decay rate. For the whole bar the residuals of the two halves cancel, in a piece they do not. That is why theorem 14.8 demands the balance for every piece, not only for the whole bar.

The theorem also has the hypothesis that the second derivatives are continuous. What happens without it is shown by the junction of two materials.

Example 14.12 A bar of two layers: the balance holds, the equation at a point does not (palica iz dveh plasti)

A bar of length \(L\) has conductivity \(k\) on the left half and \(2k\) on the right half (figure 14.21, top). Its ends are held at \(100\) and \(0\) °C, the temperature does not change with time and there is no source. Then the balance (14.4) for every piece, divided by \(A\), is \(0 = q(a) - q(b) = k_{\text{at } \mathrm{b}}\,T_{\mathrm{x}}(b) - k_{\text{at } \mathrm{a}}\,T_{\mathrm{x}}(a)\), where \(q = -k\,T_{\mathrm{x}}\) with the conductivity at the place of the cross-section. So the flux \(q = -k\,T_{\mathrm{x}}\) must be constant along the bar. The left half therefore has a slope twice as large as the right.

  1. In each half \(k\) is constant, the temperature does not change with time and there is no source, so there \(T_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0\) by corollary 14.10 and the profile is a straight line. Let \(T_{\mathrm{m}}\) be the temperature at the junction. The flux through the left half is \(k\,(100 - T_{\mathrm{m}})/(L/2)\), through the right \(2k\,T_{\mathrm{m}}/(L/2)\). Equality gives \(100 - T_{\mathrm{m}} = 2\,T_{\mathrm{m}}\), so \(T_{\mathrm{m}} = 33{,}33\) °C.
  2. The slopes are \(T_{\mathrm{x}} = -133{,}33\) °C per \(L\) on the left and \(-66{,}67\) °C per \(L\) on the right (SymPy). The two orange segments continue the two straight lines across the junction and do not match the profile on the other side. The profile therefore has a kink at the junction, so the second derivative \(T_{\mathrm{xx}}\) does not exist at the point of the junction.
  3. The balance holds for every piece, also for one that contains the junction: the flux \(q = -k\,T_{\mathrm{x}}\) is \(k\cdot 133{,}33\) °C\(/L\) on the left and \(2k\cdot 66{,}67\) °C\(/L\) on the right, so it is the same \(133{,}33\,k\) °C\(/L\) on both sides, and the left side of the balance is zero. The equation at a point, however, is not satisfied at the junction, because \(T_{\mathrm{xx}}\) does not exist.

This is not a failure of the theorem, since the example does not satisfy its hypothesis: the theorem demands continuous second derivatives. Without it the balance is still valid, but the equation at a point is no longer described by \(k\,\nabla^2 T\). The balance holds also across the junction, which means that the flux \(q = -k\,T_{\mathrm{x}}\) is continuous at the junction, not the slope \(T_{\mathrm{x}}\). Such a condition at the junction of layers is continued in section 14.3.

The summary of the section is short. We showed that the same law of conservation of energy, written for a part of a body, gives an integral equation, and that from it, because the part is arbitrary, the equation at a point follows, \(\rho c\,T_{\mathrm{t}} = k\,\nabla^2 T + Q\), under assumptions that we named and gave a measure. The equation says that a point warms or cools the faster, the more the temperature profile is bent there. The test on the copper bar showed that the equation selects the decay rate and that the balance must hold for every piece. In the next section we write what determines the solution: the initial condition and the conditions on the surface of the body (section 14.3).
