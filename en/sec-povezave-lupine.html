Chapter 22 Shells

22.10 Connections

The concepts of this chapter return in several courses. The course abbreviations are the same as in section 1.19. For each concept we say in which chapters it returns.

  • Middle surface, the two fundamental forms and principal curvatures: a surface described by a parametrization as in definition 4.61 has, by section 22.1, two tangent vectors and a normal. The principal curvatures of theorem 22.15 are the eigenvalues of a symmetric matrix, the same computation as the principal stresses of theorem 2.61. A flat plate is a shell with curvatures \(1/R_1 = 1/R_2 = 0\), so the plate (MK, AK, chapter 18) is the limiting case of this chapter. The promise of chapter 11 about non-orthogonal tangent vectors was kept only in part. We met a non-orthogonal grid in example 22.8, where the first fundamental form shows that the two tangent vectors are not perpendicular, but general tensor calculus in curvilinear coordinates was not developed. From section 22.3 on we use coordinates along lines of curvature, which are orthogonal in the sense of definition 11.13.
  • Kirchhoff-Love assumptions, resultants and Hooke's law: theorem 22.28 gives \(N_{\mathrm{i}} = C(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{i}}^0 + \nu\varepsilon_{\mathrm{j}}^0)\) and \(M_{\mathrm{i}} = D(\kappa_{\mathrm{i}} + \nu\kappa_{\mathrm{j}})\) with \(C = Eh/(1 - \nu^2)\) and \(D = Eh^3/(12(1 - \nu^2))\). This \(D\) is the bending stiffness of the plate, which chapter 18 derives for a flat plate (MK, AK). The derivation in this chapter holds for a curved shell too, so the plate is, at least at this point, only a special case. The letter \(D\) has other meanings in the book: the dynamic factor of definition 9.53 and the flexibility matrix of definition 4.138. The letter \(K\) is, besides the Gaussian curvature, the bulk modulus of theorem 11.28. Composite shells, promised by chapter 3, are not treated here: theorem 22.28 holds for an isotropic material, and the anisotropic case is stated in the sources (VK section 18.3, PDF p. 536, orthotropic shells, and ONATE2 §9.3.3.3, PDF p. 570, layered axisymmetric shells) and not derived.
  • Energy: theorem 22.29 splits the strain energy into a membrane part, proportional to \(h\), and a bending part, proportional to \(h^3\). This keeps the promise of chapter 13 that the energy of a shell is the sum of these two parts, and Hamilton's principle of theorem 13.28 carries over to the shell with the same idea. The difference between the two parts shows on the ring of section 22.5 (\(R/h = 25\), lemma 22.41). Under pressure \(p\) every point of the ring moves outward by \(pR^2/(Eh)\). Under two opposite forces \(F = pR\) each point of application moves \(0{,}8927\,(R/h)^2 = 558\) times as far, because in ovalizing the middle surface hardly stretches and the ring bends. Point 3 of the lemma gives \(1116\), because there it compares with the displacement under pressure the decrease of the distance between the two points of application, which is the sum of the displacements of both points. Whoever models a shell with finite elements (AK, chapters 15, 18 and 17) must therefore know which part of the energy the element can represent.
  • Membrane theory: theorem 22.32 is the equilibrium of a cap and a band, written locally by differentiation with the fundamental theorem of calculus (theorem 3.40). The result holds for an axisymmetric load. For a load that is not axisymmetric a shear force \(N_{\varphi\theta}\) enters. The equilibrium of a cap then no longer determines the forces, and partial differential equations in \(\varphi\) and \(\theta\) are needed, which VK solves with a Fourier series in \(\theta\) (VK, PDF pp. 358 to 361). Here we only name them. Theorem 22.37 proves that the Lamé hoop stress in the wall of a tube differs from the membrane value \(pR/h\) by at most \(p\). For the tube of the chapter (\(R = 50\) mm, \(h = 2\) mm, \(p = 3\) MPa) this is at most \(3\) MPa out of \(75\) MPa, that is \(h/R = 4\) %. For the thick tube of chapter 11 (inner radius \(40\) mm, outer \(60\) mm, \(h/R = 0{,}4\)) the bound \(p\) allows an error of \(40\) %. The actual error at the outer radius is, by point 1 of the theorem, \(p\,(1 - h/(4R)) = 0{,}9\,p\), that is \(36\) % of \(pR/h = 2{,}5\,p\). So the membrane stress does not suffice there. Membrane theory also does not say when a shell under compression loses stability. That is buckling, which chapter 17 (MK, AK) treats for a column with imperfections and shows for shells only from the sources. Here we only name it.
  • Edge effect and the beam on an elastic foundation: the equation \(D w'''' + (Eh/R^2)\,w = p - \nu N_{\mathrm{x}}/R\) of theorem 22.42 is the beam equation \(EI\,v'''' = q\) of equation (5.33) with a foundation term \(k\,w\) added. With \(EI \to D\) and \(k \to Eh/R^2\) the cylinder is therefore the first example of a beam on an elastic foundation, which chapter 25 treats (DSK). For the tube of the chapter (\(R = 50\) mm, \(h = 2\) mm, \(E = 196\) GPa, \(\nu = 0{,}3\)) \(k = Eh/R^2 = 156{,}80\) N/mm\(^3\) and \(\beta = (k/(4D))^{1/4} = 0{,}12854\) mm\(^{-1}\), so \(1/\beta = 7{,}78\) mm and \(\pi/\beta = 24{,}44\) mm. The deviation \(w - w_{\mathrm{p}}\) of theorem 22.43 is \(\mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}\) times a combination of \(\cos\beta x\) and \(\sin\beta x\). When \(x\) grows by \(\pi/\beta\), the deviation changes sign and drops to \(\mathrm{e}^{-\pi} = 0{,}043\) of its previous value. The chapter on the foundation can refer to it and need not derive it again.
  • Shell junctions: theorem 22.48 shows, under Geckeler's approximation, that for a hemisphere of the same thickness as the tube \(M_0 = 0\) and \(Q_0 = p/(8\beta)\), for our tube \(2{,}917\) N/mm. For a rigid flat end or a conical head the edge effect grows (section 22.7). Geckeler's approximation (definition 22.47), in which a sphere near its edge is treated as a cylinder, is stated from the sources (VK, section 16.4) and not proved.
  • Finite elements of shells: the axisymmetric cylinder element is the beam element of lemma 12.4 with \(EI \to D\) and a hoop term added (section 22.8). Because of the hoop term the nodal values are no longer exact, as they were in theorem 12.14 (lemma 22.54). For the tube with a clamped end, with two elements the largest error of \(w\) at the nodes is \(0{,}136\,w_{\mathrm{p}}\), with 64 elements \(1{,}54 \cdot 10^{-6}\,w_{\mathrm{p}}\). Elements longer than \(1/\beta\) do not capture the edge moment: at \(\beta h_{\mathrm{e}} \approx 1\) (\(h_{\mathrm{e}}\) is the element length) it is 16 % too small, at \(\beta h_{\mathrm{e}} > 4{,}3\) less than a quarter of the true one. Axisymmetric elements for solid bodies are in chapter 15 (AK), where the Lamé tube of chapter 11 is the test. The flat element of section 22.9 is a membrane element plus a plate bending element, supplied by chapters 15 and 18 (AK). Six unknowns at a shell node (three displacements and three rotations) against three at a solid node mean that joining shell and solid is a question of constraints between nodes, of the kind chapter 19 (AK) treats for bars and a beam. Theorem 22.59 says that at a coplanar node, where all elements lie in one plane, the stiffness matrix is singular unless a support prevents the rotation about the normal. So at such a node the rotation about the normal is removed or given an artificial stiffness \(k_\theta > 0\), which by point 3 of the theorem does not change the solution.
  • Rotating shells and disks: rotation of a shell of revolution with constant angular velocity about its axis gives an axisymmetric load of inertia forces, so the band equilibrium of theorem 22.32 would be the first step for a rotating shell too. Rotating shells are not treated in the book. Chapter 24 (DSK) treats a thin rotating disk with inertia forces as the plane stress state of theorem 11.33.
  • Vibration of shells: the split of the energy of theorem 22.29 into a stretching and a bending part is the idea Rayleigh used for the vibration of shells (history in section 22.5). We do not compute the vibration of shells here. For the vibration of beams and bars see chapter 13.
  • Limits of the chapter: for the cylinder and the shell with axial symmetry what the theorems of the chapter state is proved. Stated without proof are the existence of coordinates along lines of curvature (VK, section 11.2), the Gauss and Codazzi conditions, the general displacement-strain relations for arbitrary lines of curvature, Geckeler's approximation and its error, membrane theory for loads without axial symmetry, existence and uniqueness of the solution of the shell equations, the matrices of the conical element, the treatment of warped flat elements and membrane locking, and the buckling of shells. Shallow shells (Donnell, Mushtari, Vlasov, VK PDF p. 302), curved degenerated elements (COOK PDF p. 577) and composite shells are only named here.
