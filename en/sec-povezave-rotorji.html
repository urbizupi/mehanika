Chapter 24 Rotordynamics

24.10 Connections (povezave)

The course abbreviations are the same as in section 1.19. The chapter built on seven chapters before it, and here we close the circle: for each we say what we took from it and what the chapter on rotors added. Blocks of chapters that are written at the same time as this one are not named, only the chapter.

  • Analytical dynamics (analitična dinamika, VD, DSK, chapter 8): the disk is a rigid body, and we took its angular velocity from lemma 8.76 on the antisymmetric matrix \(\mat R^\T \dot{\mat R}\) and its kinetic energy from theorem 8.82. To second order in the slopes this is \(T = \tfrac12 m(\dot v^2 + \dot w^2) + \tfrac12 J_{\mathrm{t}}(\dot\alpha^2 + \dot\beta^2) + \tfrac12 J_{\mathrm{p}}\Omega^2 - J_{\mathrm{p}}\Omega\,\alpha\dot\beta\) (section 24.3). The last term, linear in the velocities, is \(T_1\) of theorem 8.86 on the split \(T_2 + T_1 + T_0\), which chapter 8 only announced as gyroscopic and transport forces. Now it gives, by theorem 24.17, the skew-symmetric matrix \(\Omega\,\mat G\) in the equations of motion. Its power is \(\dot{\vek x}^\T\,\Omega\mat G\,\dot{\vek x} = 0\), so the energy \(\tfrac12\dot{\vek x}^\T\mat M\dot{\vek x} + \tfrac12\vek x^\T\mat K\vek x\) is constant (lemma 24.19). Theorem 8.106 on conservation of energy does not apply directly here, because the constraint \(\Omega t\) is rheonomic and \(T\) has the term \(T_1\), so the lemma proves conservation from the equation of motion. In coordinates that turn with the rotor (lemma 24.39), the split \(T_2 + T_1 + T_0\) of theorem 8.86 gives a transport term (centrifugal) and a Coriolis term (gyroscopic). The centrifugal body force \(\rho\Omega^2 r\) in the rotating disk (section 24.9) is the inertial force \(-\rho\vek a\) of d'Alembert's theorem 8.71, with which we write the motion of the disk as equilibrium.
  • Vibration with one degree of freedom (nihanje z eno prostostno stopnjo, VD, DSK, chapter 9): the machine of example 9.64 with the forcing \(m_{\mathrm{u}} a_{\mathrm{u}} \Omega^2 \cos\Omega t\) became the Jeffcott rotor in section 24.2, where \(m_{\mathrm{u}} = m\) is the whole disk. By theorem 24.7 the orbit is a circle of radius \(a_{\mathrm{u}} r^2/|1 - r^2 + 2\mathrm i\zeta r| = a_{\mathrm{u}} r^2 D\), where \(D\) is the dynamic factor of definition 9.53. The factor \(r^2\) comes from the force \(m a_{\mathrm{u}}\Omega^2\), which grows with the speed, so for small damping the peak is at \(r > 1\), while the peak of \(D\) alone is at \(r < 1\) (section 24.2). At \(r = 1\) and \(\zeta = 0{,}02\) this is \(a_{\mathrm{u}}/(2\zeta) = 25\,a_{\mathrm{u}}\), so \(1{,}25\) mm for \(a_{\mathrm{u}} = 0{,}05\) mm. We repeated the run through the critical speed of section 9.8.3 on this rotor and found peaks of \(0{,}961\), \(0{,}836\) and \(0{,}590\) of the steady peak at accelerations \(5\), \(20\) and \(100\) rad/s\(^2\), a numerical observation and not a proof. Theorem 24.43 with the stability limit \(\Omega_{\mathrm{s}} = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}(1 + c_{\mathrm{e}}/c_{\mathrm{i}})\) generalizes theorem 9.48 on the stability of a linear equation with real constant coefficients to an equation with the complex coefficient \(k - \mathrm i\Omega c_{\mathrm{i}}\), where the real parts of the roots of a complex quadratic equation decide stability. For \(c_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\) the equation is real again and theorem 9.48 itself holds. For \(\zeta_{\mathrm{e}} = 0{,}02\) and \(\zeta_{\mathrm{i}} = 0{,}01\) the limit is \(3{,}000\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = 4449\) rev/min, and without external damping already \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) itself (\(1483\) rev/min). A rotor with external damping alone is stable at every speed (largest \(\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda = -3{,}106\) 1/s).
  • Complex numbers (kompleksna števila, VD, DSK, chapter 9): the complex coordinate \(p = v + \mathrm i w\) of definitions 9.19 and 9.24 turns two real equations into one. The form \(p = P\,\mathrm e^{\mathrm i\omega t}\) of theorem 9.29 gives a circular orbit, and the sign of \(\omega\) separates forward whirl (\(\omega > 0\), in the sense of the spin) from backward whirl (\(\omega < 0\)) of definition 24.3. Each complex equation was first written as two real ones and only then combined, because the complex form holds only for a rotor and bearings that are symmetric about the axis (section 24.1). With bearings of different stiffness (\(2\cdot 10^6\) and \(6\cdot 10^6\) N/m per bearing) the orbit is no longer circular and there are two critical speeds (\(1303\) and \(1415\) rev/min).
  • Several degrees of freedom (več prostostnih stopenj, VD, DSK, chapter 10): the real natural frequencies of theorem 10.13 follow from symmetric positive definite \(\mat M\) and \(\mat K\). The gyroscopic matrix \(\mat G\) is skew-symmetric and the eigenproblem \((\lambda^2 \mat M + \lambda\,\Omega\mat G + \mat K)\,\vek\varphi = \vek 0\) is no longer of that kind, so theorem 24.21 proves the reality of the frequencies separately, by a quadratic equation for \(\lambda\) after multiplying by \(\bar{\vek\varphi}^\T\), whose discriminant \(\Omega^2 g^2 + 4m_\varphi k_\varphi\) is positive. A numerical computation for the rotor \(R_2\) confirms it: at \(0\), \(3000\) and \(30\,000\) rev/min the largest \(|\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda|\) is below \(10^{-13}\) 1/s, which is rounding error. The unbalance response of section 24.6 has the form of the modal superposition of theorem 10.49, only with the matrix \(\mat D = \mat M - \mat G_{\mathrm{p}}\) of no fixed sign in place of the positive definite mass matrix. The modes are real because in synchronous forward whirl \(\mat D\) and \(\mat K\) are real symmetric matrices (theorem 24.33). The Rayleigh quotient (definition 10.25, theorem 10.26) has its continuous counterpart in theorem 13.54, which in section 24.5 gives the direction of the error of the estimate with the mass of the shaft: the estimate \(1415{,}98\) rev/min is an upper bound, and the finite element mesh gives \(1415{,}83\) rev/min at rest.
  • Beams and finite elements (nosilci in končni elementi, MK, AK, chapters 5 and 12): the deflection curve of a simply supported beam (theorem 5.57) gives the stiffness \(k = 48EI/L^3 = 1{,}1822\cdot 10^6\) N/m of the steel shaft \(d = 40\) mm, \(L = 1\) m with the disk at midspan (\(I\) from definition 5.26). The largest bending stress at the critical speed and \(\zeta = 0{,}02\), \(58{,}8\) MPa against \(19{,}13\) MPa from the weight of the disk alone, is by theorem 5.35 computed from the force \(k|p| = 1478\) N at midspan. The two shaft elements of section 24.5 are the matrices of lemmas 12.4 and 12.43 in each of the two planes, and the assembly is theorem 12.9 in complex form (theorem 24.29). Meshes of \(n = 2\), \(4\), \(8\), \(16\) elements give the first critical speed of the bare shaft \(4745{,}4\), \(4728{,}0\), \(4726{,}9\) and \(4726{,}8\) rev/min, which agrees with the closed form \(4726{,}8\) rev/min.
  • Vibration of continuous systems (nihanje zveznih sistemov, VD, DSK, chapter 13): the shaft with a disk of theorem 13.21 is a torsional model there, a disk at the end of a clamped shaft that twists. Here the disk sits at the middle of a simply supported shaft that bends. The models differ, and they share only the idea that the shaft gives the stiffness and the disk the mass. The mass of the shaft, which the Jeffcott rotor neglects, is \(20{,}0\) % of the mass of the disk and by the estimate with theorem 13.54 (Rayleigh factor \(17/35\)) it lowers the first frequency by \(4{,}53\) %, from \(1483\) to \(1416\) rev/min. The finite element mesh with the mass of the shaft confirms the value (\(1415{,}8\) rev/min). Rotary inertia from theorem 13.62 lowers the frequency of a shaft at rest by the factor \(1/\sqrt{1 + \varepsilon}\). In synchronous forward whirl the polar term changes sign, and the critical speed lies above the Euler-Bernoulli value \(4724{,}5\) rev/min by the factor \(1/\sqrt{1 - \varepsilon}\), that is by \(0{,}049\) % (section 24.5). The critical speed \(4726{,}8\) rev/min is thus \(0{,}099\) % above the frequency at rest \(4722{,}1\) rev/min, which does not contradict chapter 13, because there the shaft is at rest.
  • Boundary-value problem of elasticity (robni problem elastičnosti, VT, MK, chapter 11): the rotating disk of section 24.9 follows the same route as theorem 11.27, with the displacement \(u(r)\) and the body force \(\rho\Omega^2 r\) in the equilibrium equation of corollary 11.24. The homogeneous part \(C_1 r + C_2/r\) is the axisymmetric Airy solution with \(B = 0\) (theorem 11.48). A solid disk of radius \(b = 200\) mm at \(3000\) rev/min has \(12{,}70\) MPa at the centre, and with a bore of diameter \(40\) mm (\(a = 20\) mm) the hoop stress at the bore is \(25{,}46\) MPa. A bore of diameter \(2\) mm gives \(25{,}40\) MPa, which is \(2{,}0000\) times the stress of the solid disk. As in Kirsch's problem (section 11.13, factor \(3\)), a small hole does not raise the stress by a small fraction but by a finite factor.
  • Unbalance of a rigid rotor (neuravnoteženost togega rotorja, DSK, chapter 23): static and dynamic balancing of a rigid rotor in two planes and the torsional vibration of several disks on a shaft are there. This chapter takes as known that two planes remove force and moment for a rigid rotor. Section 24.8 shows that for a flexible rotor this is not enough: two correction masses at \(0{,}1\,L\) and \(0{,}9\,L\) leave \(51{,}5\) % of the unbalance of the first mode.
  • Bearings (ležaji, DSK, chapter 25): we gave the stiffness of the bearings in this chapter as a number (from \(10^5\) to \(10^9\) N/m in section 24.6), and the chapter on contact derives it from the contact stiffness of rolling bearings. A precise model of a bearing with an oil film and oil whirl are only named.
  • Damping (dušenje, DSK, chapter 20): we treated internal damping of a rotating rotor as a force on the velocity relative to the rotating frame (section 24.7) and proved its effect by the roots of a complex quadratic equation. The state-space form, complex modes and all material damping models are in chapter 20. The quadratic eigenproblem of section 24.4 we solved numerically for the numbers, with a matrix of twice the size, without deriving this form.
  • Reduced models (zmanjšani modeli, DSK, chapter 21): every mesh of section 24.5 has at each node the complex pair \((p_{\mathrm{j}}, p_{\mathrm{j}}')\), which are four real degrees of freedom. A mesh of \(n\) elements has \(n + 1\) nodes, and the two rigid bearings remove the displacements of both ends, so \(4n\) real degrees of freedom remain, \(64\) for \(n = 16\) and \(32\) for \(n = 8\). For a rotor with several disks and bearings we would keep only the first modes, which is the subject of that chapter. The torsional stiffness \(G I_{\mathrm{p}}\) of a round shaft was taken as given in chapter 13, and its derivation and the stiffness of non-circular sections are in chapter 16 (MK, VD).
  • Notation (oznake): \(\Omega\) is the spin speed and \(\omega\) the whirl frequency (the bold \(\boldsymbol\omega\) of chapter 8 is the angular velocity vector), \(p = v + \mathrm i w\) the complex displacement (not the pressure), \(J_{\mathrm{p}}\) and \(J_{\mathrm{t}}\) the polar and transverse mass moments of inertia of the disk (not the area moments \(I\) and \(I_{\mathrm{p}}\) of the section), bold \(\mat G\) the gyroscopic matrix (italic \(G\) is the shear modulus), the frequency ratio \(r = \Omega/\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) of chapter 9 (in section 24.9 \(r\) is the radius).
  • Only named: hydrodynamic bearings and oil whirl, asymmetric rotors and parametric instability, bent rotors, shear in a shaft element (Timoshenko element) and balancing without a phase reading. For all of these the first step, the equation from the energies of the rigid body and the elastic shaft, does not change, but the bearing matrix or the forcing term does.
  • Limits of the chapter: stated without proof are only the numerical run through the critical speed (computed with the fourth-order Runge-Kutta method), the solution of the quadratic eigenproblem with a matrix of twice the size, least squares for balancing in several planes and at several speeds, the Routh-Hurwitz theorem and the values of the coefficients of hydrodynamic bearings. The model is limited by named simplifications: the mass of the shaft is neglected (\(4{,}53\) % error of the first frequency), slopes are small (kinetic energy to second order), the spin speed is constant, and the bearings are rigid and short unless stated otherwise. Rotary inertia of a shaft \(d = 40\) mm, \(L = 1\) m moves its first critical speed by only \(0{,}049\) % from the Euler-Bernoulli value, which is why the Jeffcott rotor does not contain it.
