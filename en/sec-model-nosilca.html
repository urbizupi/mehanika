Chapter 5 Beam theory (teorija nosilcev)

5.1 Beams and their model (nosilec in njegov model)

In this section we answer the question how to describe a slender body that carries a load across it, what holds it and what loads it, and which numbers are enough to describe it. With the answer we set up the model that the chapter will refine, and we choose two bodies on which we shall try every new idea with numbers. The axis \(x\) points along the body and we measure \(x\) from its left end, the axis \(y\) points vertically up, the axis \(z\) toward the reader, so that the triple \(x\), \(y\), \(z\) is right-handed, as in chapters 1 and 2.

5.1.1 Line structures (linijske konstrukcije)

Structures are divided by shape (figure 5.2). A line structure (linijska konstrukcija) is made of bodies that are much longer in one direction than in the other two. We already know the bar: forces act only at its ends or along its axis, so it carries only axial force (definition 4.4). A cable (vrv, kabel) is a very flexible bar that carries only tension. A slender body that also carries load across it, as we shall see in the definition below, is the subject of this chapter. Several such bodies that are rigidly joined to each other make a structure of rigidly joined bodies, which one of the last sections of this chapter treats. A surface structure (ploskovna konstrukcija) has one dimension, the thickness, much smaller than the other two. If it is flat we call it a plate (plošča), if it is curved a shell (lupina). Both have their own chapters (18, 22). When all three dimensions are comparable we have a massive body (masivno telo), such as a cube or the base of a machine. For such a body we must solve the equations of elasticity in all three directions at once. The division follows EP §2.5.1 (pp. 71-72).

For us the slender body that carries a load across it is the most important. In the model we shall replace the cross section by a point on the axis, so we must first say what the axis is. A cross section (prečni prerez) is here the plane surface that we get by cutting the body perpendicular to the axis \(x\), along which the body is long, as in chapter 2 (figure 2.11). For a symmetric cross section, such as a rectangle or a double T, the centre of the cross section lies where its axes of symmetry meet. Why exactly there? The parts of the cross section on the two sides of the vertical axis of symmetry are mirror images of each other, so the centre cannot be closer to one of them and lies on this axis. The same holds for the horizontal axis, so the centre lies where the two meet. The general rule, valid for every cross section, we shall state later in this chapter, when we can compute the mean position of the points of the cross section. For now we take bodies with a symmetric cross section and the axis through the centres of the cross sections.

Definition 5.1 Beam (nosilec)

A beam (nosilec) is a body occupying the set of points \((x, y, z)\) with \(0 \le x \le L\) and \((y, z) \in P\), where \(P\) is a plane figure with its centre at the point \(y = z = 0\), whose dimensions are much smaller than \(L\). The cross section (prečni prerez) at \(x\) is the figure \(P\) in the plane perpendicular to the axis \(x\), the axis (os) of the beam is the axis \(x\), the line through the centres of the cross sections. For a symmetric cross section the centre is the intersection of the axes of symmetry, in general it is the mean position of the points of the figure, which we define precisely in section 5.4.2. A beam whose cross section varies along the axis has its own figure \(P(x)\) at each \(x\) instead of one figure \(P\) (EP p. 72), in this chapter the cross section is the same everywhere. The beam is loaded by forces and couples that also have a component perpendicular to the axis, and supported at its ends or at several places.

Source: EP §2.5.1 (p. 71), ONATE2 §1.2 (PDF p. 33), SADD §D.3 (PDF p. 574).

How long must a beam be compared with its cross section? EP says that a beam has a very large ratio of length to the dimensions of the cross section, "say, 10 to 100 or more" (EP §2.5.1, p. 71). The ratio \(L/h\), where \(h\) is the height of the cross section in the plane in which the beam bends, we call slenderness (vitkost). In figure 5.4 there is a long body with \(L/h = 25\), a middle one with \(L/h = 5\) and a short one with \(L/h = 1\). The first beam of the chapter will be as slender as the long body, \(L/h = 25\), and the second, with the same cross section, twice as long, \(L/h = 50\).

Why does slenderness allow a simpler model? The forces across a cross section gave three numbers in chapter 2: the force along the axis, the force across it and the moment (figure 2.11). The model will replace the whole cross section by them and, about how the cross section deforms, will only assume that it stays plane and perpendicular to the axis (we study this in the next section), instead of solving the equations of elasticity in all three directions. How close this is to the truth depends on slenderness. The model neglects the shear deformation, the sliding of neighbouring cross sections against each other. Later in the chapter, for a steel beam clamped in a wall and loaded by a force at its free end, we shall compute with a model that does allow for shear deformation (the Timoshenko beam) that the displacement of the free end is larger than the one this model gives, by 0,125 % at \(L/h = 25\) and by 19,5 % at \(L/h = 2\). The Timoshenko beam is a simplification too, so the two numbers tell how much the neglected sliding changes the result, not the difference from the real body. The share is proportional to the square of the ratio \(h/L\): the ratio \(h/L\) is 12,5 times larger at \(L/h = 2\) than at \(L/h = 25\), and the share \(12{,}5^2 = 156{,}25\) times larger, \(0{,}1248\ \% \cdot 156{,}25 = 19{,}5\ \%\). EP summarizes that such theories give results "accurate enough for engineering analysis and design" (EP §2.5.1, p. 72). One cost is already visible. The cross section is a point on the axis in the model, so the model does not say how the forces are distributed over the cross section near a support or near the place where a load acts. Likewise, the solution of plane elasticity for pure bending, which satisfies the conditions at the ends only through a force and a moment, is not necessarily valid near the ends (SADD example 8.2, PDF p. 171). How far from an end this reaches we shall compute for a long straight strip in chapter 11 (section 11.11): two distributions of forces on the end with the same resultant and the same moment give stresses whose difference falls to 1,5 % or less within one height of the cross section, under an assumption stated there. In the cantilever with \(L/h = 25\) one height of the cross section at the wall is only 4 % of the length, so for it this cost concerns only two short bands at the two ends.

Besides slenderness the model makes one more restriction. All loads and supports are to lie in the plane \(xy\) and the cross section is to be symmetric about the axis \(y\), like the rectangle and the double T. Then the beam only bends in this plane, without twisting about the axis and without bending sideways (EP §2.5.1, p. 72, ONATE2 §1.2.1, PDF p. 33). Why? The plane \(xy\) cuts such a beam into two mirror halves. Let us look at the cantilever from above (figure 5.5, left). It is clamped in a wall, the plane \(xy\) cuts it along the axis, and in this plane acts also the force \(F\), pointing down, away from us. So the beam and the load are the same in the mirror of this plane. Suppose the beam bent toward the reader, so that the free end moved by \(s\). The mirror image of this state is bending away from the reader under the same load. If the material too is the same in the mirror of the plane \(xy\) (isotropic, like the steel of the book), this is another solution of the same problem. But the problem has a single field of strains for given loads (lemma 4.134, point 1), as long as the deformations are small and the loads do not change with the deformation, as the lemma assumes. Bending toward the reader and bending away from the reader, however, have different strains: at the clamp the fibre on the far side gets longer in the first and shorter in the second. So the beam does not bend sideways, and for the same reason it does not twist. For a symmetric cross section the restriction therefore costs nothing while the deformations are small. For a cross section that is not symmetric about the axis \(y\), such as an L-shaped cross section, the mirror argument is not available, because its mirror image is another body. Such a beam may also bend sideways or twist under a vertical load. What happens with unsymmetric cross sections and with beams loaded in two planes we state as a limit of the model in the last section of the chapter.

Even for a symmetric cross section the restriction has a limit. Take a cantilever with a narrow deep cross section, whose height \(h\) is several times its width \(b\), clamped in a wall, and load it at the free end by the force \(F\) downward, so that we bend it in the plane of its larger stiffness. Under a small force it only bends down. Under a large load, however, it can suddenly tip sideways: the free end moves sideways and the cross section turns about the axis. This is called lateral buckling (bočna zvrnitev). There the lemma no longer applies, since it writes equilibrium on the shape of the body before loading (definition 4.133), while the tipping arises because the loads act on a bent and slightly twisted beam differently than on a straight one. Prandtl computed the lateral buckling of narrow rectangular cross sections and checked the results by experiments (TIMO PDF p. 332).

5.1.2 Supports and loads (podpore in obremenitve)

Loads first, then supports. Three kinds of loads act on a beam (figure 5.10). A point force (točkovna sila) acts at one point of the axis. For it we already have the force vector (definition 2.1), here with components \(F_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\). But how do we describe a load that acts over the whole length or over part of it, like weight or snow on a roof? Let \(q(x)\) be the force per unit length of the beam in the direction \(y\) at the place \(x\). With it we write how much force a small piece of the beam takes, and write the sum as an integral. We did similarly for a bar with the axial load (definition 4.74).

Definition 5.2 Distributed transverse load (porazdeljena prečna obtežba)

Let the beam lie along the axis \(x\) from \(0\) to \(L\). A distributed transverse load (porazdeljena prečna obtežba) is a piecewise continuous function \(q\) on the interval \([0, L]\) with one-sided limits at the ends of the pieces, whose input is the place \(x\) on the axis and whose output is the force per unit length of the beam in the direction of the axis \(y\) at that place, in newtons per metre. The load is positive when it points up. A load on a part of the length jumps to zero at the ends of that part, so we integrate piece by piece. On the part of the beam between the places \(x_1 < x_2\) acts, in the direction of the axis \(y\), the force

\[\int_{\mathrm{x}_1}^{x_2} q(t)\,\mathrm{d}t , \tag{5.1}\]

and its moment about the point \((0, 0, 0)\) has the component \(z\)

\[\int_{\mathrm{x}_1}^{x_2} t\,q(t)\,\mathrm{d}t . \tag{5.2}\]

Source: EP §2.5.1 (p. 72, figure 2.5.2), where the symbol is \(q\). For the analogy with the axial load of a bar see ONATE1 §2.2 (PDF pp. 66-67). The moment follows from definition 2.37 applied to the forces \(q(t_{\mathrm{i}})\,\Delta t_{\mathrm{i}}\) at the points \((t_{\mathrm{i}}, 0, 0)\), and from the limit of Riemann sums.

The moment of the load is the limit of the sums of the moments of the forces \(q(t_{\mathrm{i}})\,\Delta t_{\mathrm{i}}\) on pieces of length \(\Delta t_{\mathrm{i}}\), each at the point \((t_{\mathrm{i}}, 0, 0)\). For \(\vek r = (t_{\mathrm{i}}, 0, 0)\) and \(\vek F = (0, q(t_{\mathrm{i}})\,\Delta t_{\mathrm{i}}, 0)\) the vector product is \(\vek r\times\vek F = (0, 0, t_{\mathrm{i}}\,q(t_{\mathrm{i}})\,\Delta t_{\mathrm{i}})\), the other two components are zero. The moment of a piece is therefore its force multiplied by the arm \(t_{\mathrm{i}}\) from the point \(O\). Since \(t\,q(t)\) is a continuous function on each piece, the sums are Riemann sums and their limit is (5.2). The weight of the beam is an example. Steel has density \(\rho\), the beam has cross-section area \(A\), so the mass of a unit of length is \(\rho A\) and the weight of a unit of length is \(\rho A g\). The weight points down, so \(q = -\rho g A\) (a constant along the whole length for a beam with the same cross section). The uniform load \(q(x) = -q_0\) with \(q_0 > 0\) has by (5.1) the resultant \(-q_0 L\) and by (5.2) the moment \(-q_0 L^2/2\), equal to the moment of the force \(-q_0 L\) at the point \(x = L/2\). Why exactly at the middle? The load is uniform, so about the middle the left half has the same amount of force on the same arms as the right half, only on the other side, and their moments cancel. The replacing force must also have zero moment about the middle, so it acts at the middle. For the equilibrium of the whole beam we may therefore replace the uniform load by one force at the middle, but not for the internal forces, as we shall see in the section on internal forces.

The third kind of load is a pair of opposite forces. The forces \(\vek F\) and \(-\vek F\) at the ends of a ruler, across it, have zero resultant, yet they turn the ruler (figure 2.7). A lever welded across a beam loads it in the same way if two opposite forces push it. Such a pair is named in the definition.

Definition 5.3 Couple (dvojica sil)

A couple (dvojica sil) is a pair of forces \(\vek F\) and \(-\vek F\) that act at different points \(A_1\) and \(A_2\) and do not lie on the same line.

Source: ONATE2 §1.2.1 (PDF p. 33), where point forces and moments act on a plane beam. The definition of a couple is ours.

Since the resultant is zero, by lemma 2.39 on the independence of the moment of the point, the moment of such a pair of forces does not depend on the point about which we compute it. Why? If we move the point, the moment of the force \(\vek F\) changes by some vector and the moment of the opposite force \(-\vek F\) by the opposite vector, and the two changes cancel. In the plane we see this on the lever arms (figure 5.9).

Let us draw a ruler of length \(d = 0{,}1\) m with a force of \(500\) N up at the right end and an equal force down at the left one, as in the example of figure 5.8. Let the point \(O\) lie \(0{,}05\) m to the left of the left end. The moment of a force about \(O\) is the magnitude of the force times the lever arm, the distance from \(O\) to the line of the force (lemma 2.38). The upward force has the arm \(0{,}15\) m and turns counterclockwise with \(75\) N m, the downward force has the arm \(0{,}05\) m and turns clockwise with \(25\) N m. The total moment is \(75 - 25 = 50\) N m. The arms differ by exactly the length of the ruler \(d\), so the total moment is \(500\ \text{N}\cdot 0{,}1\ \text{m}\) wherever to the left of the ruler we put \(O\). The arm is the horizontal distance to the vertical line of the force, so it also does not change if we move \(O\) up or down. If on the same ruler with the same forces we put the point \(O\) between the forces, \(0{,}03\) m from the left end, both forces turn counterclockwise, their arms \(0{,}07\) m and \(0{,}03\) m add up to \(d\) and the total moment is again \(35 + 15 = 50\) N m. Let us state the moment with one vector.

Lemma 5.4 Moment of a couple (moment dvojice)

Let the forces \(\vek F\) and \(-\vek F\) act at the points \(A_1\) and \(A_2\). Then their total moment about every point \(O\) equals

\[\vek M_0 = \overrightarrow{A_2 A_1}\times\vek F , \tag{5.3}\]

the vector from the point where \(-\vek F\) acts to the point where \(\vek F\) acts, vector-multiplied by the force \(\vek F\).

Proof

We shall show that we may choose the point \(O\) to be \(A_2\), and then a single term remains.

  1. The resultant of the forces is \(\vek F + (-\vek F) = \vek 0\), so by lemma 2.39 the total moment does not depend on the point about which we compute it. We therefore move the point \(O\) to \(A_2\), \(O = A_2\).
  2. The force \(-\vek F\) acts at the point \(A_2\), the vector from \(A_2\) to \(A_2\) is zero, and its moment is \(\vek 0\times(-\vek F) = \vek 0\) (item 3 of lemma 2.34, used with the number zero).
  3. The force \(\vek F\) acts at the point \(A_1\), its moment about \(A_2\) is by definition 2.37 equal to \(\overrightarrow{A_2 A_1}\times\vek F\). This is the total moment.

Source: The proof is not from a source.

Equation (5.3) also tells why the definition requires that the forces do not lie on the same line. Then the vector from \(A_2\) to \(A_1\) would be parallel to the force \(\vek F\), the vector product would be zero and the forces would only cancel, they would not turn the body. On the ruler this means that the forces pull along the ruler. About the point \(O\) above the ruler they have the same arm \(0{,}05\) m and turn in opposite directions, so the total moment is \(25 - 25 = 0\) and the ruler is only stretched. For the equilibrium of the whole beam and for the reactions only the vector \(\vek M_0\) counts, not where exactly the forces are. For the internal forces it matters where the couple acts, as we shall see in the section on internal forces. Take the axis \(x\) with the origin \(O\) and on it the force \(\vek F = (0, 500, 0)\) N at the point \(A_1 = (50, 0, 0)\) mm, \(0{,}05\) m to the right of \(O\), and \(-\vek F\) at \(A_2 = (-50, 0, 0)\) mm, \(0{,}05\) m to the left of \(O\). The vector from \(A_2\) to \(A_1\) is \((100, 0, 0)\) mm, of length \(0{,}1\) m, so by (5.3)

\[\vek M_0 = (100, 0, 0)\ \text{mm}\times(0, 500, 0)\ \text{N} = (0, 0, 50\,000)\ \text{N mm} .\]

The couple has only the component \(z\), \(50\,000\) N mm, that is \(50\) N m, because both forces lie in the plane \(xy\). We get the same vector if we double the force and halve the distance, since the product does not change. The vector \(\vek M_0\) points in the positive direction of the axis \(z\), toward the reader, and the couple turns the beam counterclockwise as seen by a reader looking toward the negative axis \(z\). A point couple (točkovna dvojica) \(M_0\) on a beam is the limit of such a pair of forces when the distance between the forces shrinks to zero and the forces grow so that \(M_0\) stays the same. Then it loads the beam only with the moment \(M_0 = (\vek M_0)_{\mathrm{z}}\) and with zero resultant (ONATE2 §1.2.1, PDF p. 33).

How is a beam supported? A beam lies on a wall, lies on columns, or is clamped in a wall. A support prevents some displacements and therefore acts on the beam with forces or couples that we call reactions (reakcije). They are external forces, since they act from outside, and they are not known in advance, because the support produces just as much of them as it takes to prevent the displacement. For a plane beam we know three typical supports (figure 5.11).

Definition 5.5 Beam supports (podpore nosilca)

The supports of a plane beam in the plane \(xy\) are three idealizations.

  1. A clamp (vpetje, fixed support) prevents displacement in the directions \(x\) and \(y\) and rotation in the plane. The reactions are a force \((R_{\mathrm{x}}, R_{\mathrm{y}})\) and a couple \(M_{\mathrm{A}}\) (component \(z\)).
  2. A fixed pin support (nepomična členkasta podpora, pin) prevents displacement in the directions \(x\) and \(y\) and allows rotation. The reactions are the components of a force \((R_{\mathrm{x}}, R_{\mathrm{y}})\).
  3. A roller support (pomična členkasta podpora, roller) prevents displacement only in the direction perpendicular to the base along which the support rolls, here the direction \(y\). It allows displacement along the base and rotation. The support holds in both directions (for example rollers between two guides), so the reaction is one force \(R_{\mathrm{y}}\), which may be positive or negative.

Source: SADD §D.3, figure D.5 (PDF p. 575). The symbols of the reactions are ours.

Each of the three is an idealization and each has a real counterpart. A clamp is the end of a beam welded to a thick plate or built into a wall. A real beam turns a little in the clamp, because the base bends a little, and here we set this rotation to zero. This is sensible when the base is much stiffer than the beam, like a thick plate or a wall next to a thin bar, but not for a beam bolted to thin sheet metal. What this costs us, geometry tells. Take as an example that a beam of length \(1\) m turns in the clamp by \(0{,}001\) radian. The angle is not measured, only chosen to see the size of the effect. The beam tilts as a whole, so its free end drops by a further \(1\) mm, the length times the angle. More precisely it drops by \(1000\ \text{mm}\cdot\sin 0{,}001\), which differs from \(1\) mm by less than a millionth of a millimetre. This \(1\) mm is 12,5 % of the deflection \(7{,}97\) mm that the cantilever of the next section has under the force \(500\) N (we compute it in section 5.9.2). So such a small rotation is not negligible. A pin support is a bolt in a hole that allows rotation, but has play and friction. A roller support is a beam lying on rollers or on a greased plate, and the rollers give along the beam only a small friction force, not exactly zero. A beam that only lies on rollers can only push, so it would lift off if the reaction had to pull. Which idealization is more suitable depends on the structure. We estimate the cost of a rigid clamp in section 5.15.1, once we can compute the deflection.

The reactions are unknown. When can we compute them from equilibrium? The whole beam is in equilibrium with the external forces and the reactions (definition 2.3). For a beam in the plane \(xy\) the force and position vectors lie in the plane, so the moment \(\vek r\times\vek F\) has only the component \(z\), like the moment of the couple above, and the two equations, (2.2) and (2.45), give together three equations: the components \(x\) and \(y\) of the forces and the component \(z\) of the moments about a chosen point. The choice of the point for the moments does not change the number of equations, since by the first equation the resultant is zero and by lemma 2.39 the moment does not depend on the point.

Definition 5.6 Statically determinate beam (statično določen nosilec)

A plane beam is statically determinate (statično določen) when the three equations of equilibrium of the whole beam determine all the reactions of the supports uniquely, as for the cantilever. It is statically indeterminate (statično nedoločen) when it has more unknown reactions than there are independent equations of equilibrium, and for given loads the equations have infinitely many solutions, as for the propped cantilever. It is a mechanism (mehanizem) when the supports allow a rigid displacement, even a small one, so equilibrium for general loads is not possible, as for the beam on two roller supports.

Source: EP example 3.2.6 (p. 122) says of a frame "this is a determinate structure", because all the external reactions follow from the equilibrium of the whole structure, and EP example 3.2.3 (pp. 115-116) says of a shaft loaded in torsion and clamped between two rigid walls "this is an indeterminate structure", because there is one equation of equilibrium and two unknown torques. The two examples use the words only in a worked example, the definition for a beam in the plane is ours, of the same kind as for the truss (chapter 4, section 4.7.5).

Counting the reactions says where the beam belongs, if the supports prevent all rigid displacements. A rigid displacement (definition 1.11) of a plane body has three free parts: displacement in the direction \(x\), displacement in the direction \(y\) and rotation. If the supports prevent all three, also the small displacements to first order (for forces alone, the lines of the reactions are not all parallel and do not all pass through one point, while the couple of a clamp prevents the rotation by itself), and the beam has exactly three unknown reactions, it is statically determinate. If it has more than three, it is statically indeterminate. We saw the same idea for the truss in section 4.7.5: three unknown forces in the bars, but only two equations of equilibrium of the joint, so equilibrium itself could not tell the true forces. If the supports do not prevent some rigid displacement, it is a mechanism, even if there are not few reactions. Such is the beam on two roller supports. The figure also shows a cantilever with three and a propped cantilever with four reactions, the number is under each beam. The beam is a thick line, the clamp is shown by a hatched wall and the roller support by rollers. Both roller supports give only a force in the direction \(y\), so no reaction balances the horizontal force \(H\) and the beam slides along its axis (figure 5.12). The same holds if the supports are geometrically unstable. A pin at \(A\) and a roller support at \(B\) against a vertical wall, which gives a force along the axis, have three reactions, which all pass through \(A\). The force \(F\) at the middle has the arm \(L/2\) about \(A\) and the reactions have the arm zero, so the equilibrium of moments about \(A\) would require \(-F L/2 = 0\), which is false. The beam turns about \(A\), although it has three reactions.

5.1.3 Two beams that will stay with us (dva nosilca, ki nas bosta spremljala)

Now let us choose concrete beams that we shall use throughout the chapter. They have the same cross section and the same material, they differ in the supports. The cross section is a steel strip of width \(b = 20\) mm in the direction \(z\) and height \(h = 40\) mm in the direction \(y\), standing on its edge (figure 5.13). The centre of the cross section is where the symmetry axes meet, and the axis \(x\) points out of the drawing toward the reader. The area is \(A = b\,h = 800\ \text{mm}^2 = 8\cdot 10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2\). We choose the rectangle because we know how to integrate it. The material is the steel of the book: modulus of elasticity \(E = 196\) GPa (section 3.1.2), yield strength \(\sigma_{\mathrm{Y}} = 220\) MPa (definition 2.7) and density \(\rho = 7800\ \text{kg/m}^3\). The weight per unit length is \(\rho g A = 7800 \cdot 9{,}81 \cdot 8\cdot 10^{-4} = 61{,}2\) N/m (section 5.1.2).

The first beam is a cantilever (konzola, cantilever). Its length is \(L = 1\) m, so \(L/h = 25\). At \(x = 0\) it is clamped, and at the free end \(x = L\) a force \(F = 500\) N pushes it down (figure 5.14). The axes are \(x\) along the beam, \(y\) up and \(z\) toward the reader. The weight of the beam is \(61{,}2\) N, which is 12 % of the force. At the clamp the weight would add the moment \(61{,}2\ \text{N/m}\cdot(1\ \text{m})^2/2 = 30{,}6\) N m (by (5.2)), which is 6 % of the moment of the force that we compute below. So we treat the weight separately, at the deflection, which we shall compute later in the chapter. In a long beam such as a bridge the weight can be larger than the load and must not be left out. The reactions of the clamp we compute from the equilibrium of the whole beam.

The unknowns are \(R_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(R_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(M_{\mathrm{A}}\). The force \(\vek F = (0, -500, 0)\) N acts at the free end, at the point \(B = (1, 0, 0)\) m, the clamp is at the point \(A = (0, 0, 0)\).

  1. Equilibrium of forces ((2.2)): \((R_{\mathrm{x}},\ R_{\mathrm{y}} - 500,\ 0) = \vek 0\) N. So \(R_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\) and \(R_{\mathrm{y}} = 500\) N.
  2. Equilibrium of moments about \(A\) ((2.45)). The vector from \(A\) to \(B\) is the whole length of the beam, \((1, 0, 0)\) m, so the moment of the force \(\vek F\) is by definition 2.37 \((1, 0, 0)\times(0, -500, 0) = (0, 0, -500)\) N m. The moment of the reactions is \((0, 0, M_{\mathrm{A}})\), since the force \(R_{\mathrm{y}}\) acts at \(A\), the vector from \(A\) to \(A\) is zero, and the force \(R_{\mathrm{x}}\) is zero.
  3. Component \(z\): \(M_{\mathrm{A}} - 500 = 0\), so \(M_{\mathrm{A}} = 500\) N m.

So the clamp holds the beam with a force of 500 N up and a couple of 500 N m counterclockwise. The direction says what the clamp does: the load pushes the free end down and tries to turn the beam clockwise, and the clamp prevents the rotation with an opposite couple. We got the reactions from equilibrium alone, without any datum about the material, so the cantilever is statically determinate (definition 5.6).

The second beam is the simply supported beam (prostoležeči nosilec, simply supported beam). Its length is \(L = 2\) m, so \(L/h = 50\). At \(x = 0\) it lies on a fixed pin support, at \(x = L\) on a roller support (figure 5.15). We load it in two ways. In the first there is a uniform load \(q = -q_0\) with \(q_0 = 1\) kN/m, which is \(1000\) N/m down along the whole length. In the second there is a force \(F = 1\) kN down at the middle.

The unknowns are \(R_{\mathrm{Ax}}\), \(R_{\mathrm{Ay}}\) at the pin support and \(R_{\mathrm{By}}\) at the roller support. Let us compute the reactions for the uniform load. For equilibrium we may replace the load by a single force, as figure 5.16 shows.

  1. Forces, component \(x\): \(R_{\mathrm{Ax}} = 0\), since there is no other force on the beam in the direction \(x\). Component \(y\): \(R_{\mathrm{Ay}} + R_{\mathrm{By}} - q_0 L = 0\) by (5.1), since the resultant of the load is \(-q_0 L = -2000\) N.
  2. Moments about \(A\), component \(z\). The force \(R_{\mathrm{By}}\) acts at \(B\), \((L, 0, 0)\), its moment is \((L, 0, 0)\times(0, R_{\mathrm{By}}, 0) = (0, 0, L\,R_{\mathrm{By}})\). The moment of the load is by (5.2) \(-q_0 L^2/2 = -2000\) N m. It equals the moment of the resultant \(2000\) N on the lever arm \(L/2 = 1\) m, since there is as much of the uniform load to the left of the middle as to the right. The force \(R_{\mathrm{Ay}}\) acts at \(A\) and has no moment. So \(L\,R_{\mathrm{By}} - q_0 L^2/2 = 0\).
  3. From step 2, \(R_{\mathrm{By}} = q_0 L/2 = 1000\) N, and from step 1, \(R_{\mathrm{Ay}} = q_0 L - R_{\mathrm{By}} = 1000\) N.

Each support carries half of the load, because the resultant lies at the middle, equally far from both supports. For the force \(F = 1\) kN at the middle the moment about \(A\) is \(L\,R_{\mathrm{By}} - F L/2 = 0\), so \(R_{\mathrm{By}} = F/2 = 500\) N and \(R_{\mathrm{Ay}} = 500\) N. Here too the reason is symmetry. The beam is statically determinate.

For comparison take a propped cantilever (podprta konzola, propped cantilever), the same beam with \(L = 2\) m, clamped at \(x = 0\) and supported on a roller support at \(x = L\), loaded by the uniform load \(q_0 = 1\) kN/m down. The reactions are \(R_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(R_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(M_{\mathrm{A}}\) and \(R_{\mathrm{B}}\), four unknowns. Equilibrium gives \(R_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\), \(R_{\mathrm{y}} = q_0 L - R_{\mathrm{B}}\) and \(M_{\mathrm{A}} = q_0 L^2/2 - L\,R_{\mathrm{B}}\), so three equations for four unknowns. For every choice of the number \(R_{\mathrm{B}}\) the remaining reactions are such that the beam is in equilibrium. Figure 5.17 shows two choices:

\[\begin{aligned} R_{\mathrm{B}} &= 0 \text{ N}: & R_{\mathrm{y}} &= 2000\ \text{N} , & M_{\mathrm{A}} &= 2000\ \text{N m} , \\ R_{\mathrm{B}} &= 1000\ \text{N}: & R_{\mathrm{y}} &= 1000\ \text{N} , & M_{\mathrm{A}} &= 0 . \end{aligned}\]

Both beams carry the same load. At the top \(R_{\mathrm{B}} = 0\): the support does not push, and the clamp holds the beam with a force of \(2000\) N and a couple of \(2000\) N m. This is an ordinary cantilever with the load along the whole length. At the bottom the support pushes with \(R_{\mathrm{B}} = 1000\) N, and the clamp holds only with a force of \(1000\) N and without a couple. This is a simply supported beam with a clamp that does not turn it.

Let us draw all choices at once (figure 5.18). On the horizontal axis we put the chosen number \(R_{\mathrm{B}}\), on the vertical one the reactions of the clamp. The force of the clamp \(R_{\mathrm{y}} = 2000\ \text{N} - R_{\mathrm{B}}\) is a line that falls by \(1\) N for every newton of \(R_{\mathrm{B}}\). The couple of the clamp \(M_{\mathrm{A}} = 2000\ \text{N m} - 2\ \text{m}\cdot R_{\mathrm{B}}\) falls by \(2\) N m for every newton, because \(R_{\mathrm{B}}\) has the arm \(L = 2\) m about the clamp. Every number \(R_{\mathrm{B}}\) with the values of both lines above it is one beam in equilibrium. The choices of figure 5.17 are only two of infinitely many.

Equilibrium does not know which number \(R_{\mathrm{B}}\) is the true one. The deformation of the beam tells. With \(R_{\mathrm{B}} = 0\) the beam would bend like a cantilever, its axis would go down from the straight position and the end would sink below the support. So the support must push up, \(R_{\mathrm{B}} > 0\). With \(R_{\mathrm{B}} = 1000\) N the beam would bend like a simply supported one, its axis would turn at the wall against the straight position, clockwise. The clamp does not allow this rotation, so it must act on the beam with a couple counterclockwise, which is our positive direction, so \(M_{\mathrm{A}} > 0\), which by \(M_{\mathrm{A}} = q_0 L^2/2 - L\,R_{\mathrm{B}}\) is the same as \(R_{\mathrm{B}} < 1000\) N, where the line \(M_{\mathrm{A}}\) crosses the axis. The true value therefore lies somewhere in between. We shall compute it exactly in three ways: with the equation for the deflection, with virtual forces and with energy, all later in this chapter. We shall then check the result: \(R_{\mathrm{B}} = 750\) N, \(R_{\mathrm{y}} = 1250\) N, \(M_{\mathrm{A}} = 500\) N m.

The question of the section was what a beam is, what holds it and what loads it. The answer is a model: a straight axis, a slender beam, a plane problem, three idealized supports and three kinds of loads. The equilibrium of the whole beam gives the reactions for the cantilever and the simply supported beam, but not for the propped cantilever. And what happens inside the beam? Equilibrium of the whole does not say this, and the next section answers it.
