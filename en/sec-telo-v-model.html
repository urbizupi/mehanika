Chapter 17 FEM modeling in practice

17.2 The body in the model: geometry, supports and loads (telo v modelu)

A cantilever bracket made of thin sheet, fixed to a column with three bolts, has rounded edges where the column meets the arm, holes in the arms, a pin at the end from which a weight hangs, and washers under the bolts. The computer does not see any of this. It sees only what we enter into the model: the shape, where the body is held and where it is loaded. In section 17.1 we separated three errors and the model that is their beginning. This section treats the first of them, the modelling error (napaka modela): what of the body, the supports and the loads we take into the model and what we leave out. A finer mesh does not remove this error, because a mesh approximates the solution of the model, not of the body. Conversely, no exact solution removes the modelling error either. The answer to the question we ask decides what is a detail and what is not.

BATHE shows this on a cantilever bracket fixed with two bolts, with rounded edges and a hole, that carries a load (PDF p. 22-26). With a beam model he obtains the bending moment in the section AA that differs from the plane stress model by less than one percent, and the displacement of the load \(17\) % smaller, \(0{,}053\) cm instead of \(0{,}064\) cm (BATHE, PDF p. 22 and 26). BATHE therefore considers the beam model reliable for the displacement only to within twenty percent. If we ask for the largest stress in the bracket, the beam model is not enough, because it "totally neglects the stress increase due to the fillets" (BATHE, PDF p. 26). The same source writes that the choice of the model must follow the question, and that finite elements solve only the chosen model and cannot predict more than it contains (BATHE, PDF p. 26, points 1 and 3). This section gives these claims for three decisions: how much geometry to keep, when to cut a part of the body out and compute it in more detail, and how to write supports and forces.

17.2.1 What of the body we take into the model (kaj od telesa vzamemo v model)

The first question is which detail we may leave out. The answer is hidden in chapter 11: the disturbance due to a hole falls with the distance from it, and where it is small enough we do not need the hole. The plate of chapter 11 has a hole of radius \(a = 10\) mm and a uniform tension \(\sigma_0 = 50\) MPa in the direction \(x\). As there, we take it as infinite and compute with Kirsch's solution. For a plate of \(200 \times 200\) mm this gives an error of at most \(2{,}5\) % of \(\sigma_0\) at its edge (section 11.13.6). Over it we ask how far from the hole its influence on the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) is smaller than a chosen limit. Figure 17.5 shows the deviation of \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) from \(\sigma_0\) by equations (11.69) and (11.70).

In the figure there are the axes: along the horizontal one runs the distance \(r/a\) from the centre of the hole in hole radii, along the vertical one the deviation of \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) from \(\sigma_0\) in percent. The deviation on the axis \(y\) (blue) is positive and on the axis \(x\) (orange) negative, and the figure draws its absolute value. The scale is logarithmic because both curves fall approximately by the power \((a/r)^2\) and are therefore close to straight lines on such a graph. Why exactly this power? By equations (11.69) and (11.70) the leading term of the deviation is \(+\tfrac12(a/r)^2\) on the axis \(y\) and \(-\tfrac52(a/r)^2\) on the axis \(x\), so on the axis \(x\) the leading term is five times larger at the same distance. If only the leading term held, the distance on the axis \(x\) for the same limit would be \(\sqrt5 \approx 2{,}24\) times larger. The next term, \(+\tfrac32(a/r)^4\) on both axes, increases the deviation on the axis \(y\) and decreases it on the axis \(x\), so the ratio of the distances is smaller: \(2{,}17\) for the limit \(1\) %, only \(2{,}00\) for the limit \(5\) %, which lies closer to the hole. The dashed limits \(5\) % and \(1\) % cut the curves at the distances shown by the dots with numbers: on the axis \(y\) at \(3{,}52\,a\) and \(7{,}27\,a\), on the axis \(x\) at \(7{,}03\,a\) and \(15{,}79\,a\). Chapter 11 has already computed these numbers, here we use them as a measure for a decision. The distances on the horizontal axis are expressed in hole radii, so the same measure holds for every circular hole in a large plate under uniaxial tension, for \(a = 10\) mm the distance \(7{,}03\,a\) is \(70{,}3\) mm.

Example 17.4 Dropping the hole when the question is far from it (luknjo izpustimo, ko je vprašanje daleč od nje)

The plate with a hole has, by figure 17.5 and chapter 11, two questions with different answers.

  1. Question A: what is the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) at the point on the axis \(x\) at \(r = 10\,a = 100\) mm from the centre of the hole? If we leave out the hole, then \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_0 = 50\) MPa in the whole plate. With Kirsch's solution (theorem 11.51) it is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0{,}97515\,\sigma_0 = 48{,}758\) MPa. The error of the model without the hole is \(1{,}242\) MPa or \(2{,}5\) % of the stress \(\sigma_0\), as figure 17.5 shows at \(r/a = 10\) on the axis \(x\).
  2. Question B: what is the largest stress in the plate? Without the hole it is \(50\) MPa. With Kirsch's solution it is \(3\,\sigma_0 = 150\) MPa at the hole (chapter 11, section 11.13.4). The error of the model without the hole is \(100\) MPa or two thirds of the true value, and the model predicts three times too little, in the direction that is dangerous for strength.

For question A leaving out the detail is justified, for question B it is not. For both we computed from the same solution, and the difference between them is not in the model but in the point we ask about: the distance \(10\,a\) is far, the distance \(a\) is at the hole. For question B even a finer mesh of the model without the hole does not help, because its solution approaches \(50\) MPa, not \(150\) MPa.

The last claim of the example is general: a finer mesh reduces the discretization error (diskretizacijska napaka), not the modelling error. COOK writes this for rounded edges: if the initial mesh comes from a simplified geometry, for example without the fillets, adaptive mesh refinement can lead to a solution that converges to an incorrect result (COOK, PDF p. 345). If we cannot estimate this error by a number that the computer calculates, we must look it up in the sources: tables of stress concentration factors for a fillet or a hole (COOK, PDF p. 370), or a computation with a model that contains the detail. We do not compute the rounded edge here, because the book has no source of a number for it yet, and so we only name it as an example (BATHE, PDF p. 26).

The geometry in real work comes from a design program that is made for manufacturing and therefore contains more than the analysis needs: ONATE1 writes that simplifications are usually introduced before the mesh is created, and that the data must be cleaned of errors and of superfluous data (ONATE1, PDF p. 382). This too is a decision about the modelling error, which we make before the mesh begins.

17.2.2 Submodel (podmodel)

The second question: what if the detail that the question needs lies in a small part of the body, and the whole body is too large to be divided everywhere equally into small elements? We compute the whole body with a coarse mesh, then cut out of it a part around the detail and compute it once more with a fine mesh, prescribing on the cut the displacements that the coarse computation gave. This is called a submodel (podmodel, COOK, PDF p. 370-371). For it holds the question that COOK poses with the condition that the cut must be "far enough" from the disturbance, and that we split up: what exactly does the submodel solve and how large is its error.

Take the tube of chapter 11 (theorem 11.27) in plane strain (definition 11.30), of steel with \(E = 196\) GPa and \(\nu = 0{,}3\), with inner radius \(a = 40\) mm, outer \(b = 60\) mm and oil of pressure \(p = 30\) MPa in the bore. Figure 17.6 shows its cross-section.

First we draw the wall of the tube: two coaxial circles with radii \(a\) and \(b\), with material between them. The oil pressure acts on the inner edge and pushes it outwards, so the arrows on the inner circle point into the wall and end on it. The outer edge is free. The radii \(a\), \(b\) and \(c\) are measured from the centre and have stops at both ends. Then we cut out the submodel, the ring between \(a\) and \(c\), which has the free inner edge with pressure and the cut at \(c\). The cut is dashed because it does not exist in the real body: on it we must replace what is outside, and that is the displacement \(u(c)\) that we prescribe at every point of the cut. We drew the displacements strongly enlarged so that we can see them. On the right is the error in the ring, which we explain by the lemma.

First we say what the submodel solves, and this is the uniqueness theorem of chapter 11 in another dress. The body \(\Omega\) has a boundary value problem (definition 11.1) with data \((\vek b, \bar{\vek t}, \bar{\vek u})\). The cut is a surface \(\Gamma\) in the interior of the body that separates a part \(\Omega'\) from the rest of the body. The boundary of the part \(\Omega'\) is the cut \(\Gamma\) and a part of the boundary of the body \(\Omega\), on which the same data hold as before.

Lemma 17.5 Submodel (podmodel)

Let \(\vek u\) be the solution of the boundary value problem of the body \(\Omega\) for data \((\vek b, \bar{\vek t}, \bar{\vek u})\) of an isotropic, linearly elastic material with \(E > 0\) and \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\). Let \(\Omega'\) be a part of the body bounded by the cut surface \(\Gamma\) and by a part of the boundary of the body \(\Omega\), and let the only small rigid motion that is zero on the part of the boundary with the given displacement and on \(\Gamma\) be the field \(\vek 0\). Let \(\vek g\) be a continuous displacement field on \(\Gamma\). The submodel (podmodel) is the boundary value problem for \(\Omega'\) with the body force \(\vek b\), with the same data \(\bar{\vek t}\) and \(\bar{\vek u}\) on the part of the boundary of the body \(\Omega\) and with the prescribed displacement \(\vek g\) on \(\Gamma\). Then:

  1. for \(\vek g = \vek u|_\Gamma\) the restriction \(\vek u|_{\Omega'}\) is a solution of the submodel and the only one,
  2. if the submodel for \(\vek g = \vek u|_\Gamma + \vek e\) and for a field \(\vek e\) on \(\Gamma\) has a solution \(\tilde{\vek u}\), then \(\tilde{\vek u} = \vek u|_{\Omega'} + \vek z\), where \(\vek z\) is the solution of the boundary value problem for \(\Omega'\) without body force, with zero forces and displacements on the part of the boundary of the body \(\Omega\) and with the displacement \(\vek e\) on \(\Gamma\). The error of the submodel is therefore the solution of a problem in which the error of the data on the cut is the only cause of the load.
Proof

We must show that the restriction solves the submodel, then use uniqueness, and finally addition.

  1. The requirements of the boundary value problem (definition 11.1) are local: the equilibrium equations and Hooke's law hold at every interior point, the condition of force on \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) and of displacement on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) at every point of the boundary. The field \(\vek u\) fulfils them at all points of the body \(\Omega\), hence also at the points of the part \(\Omega'\) and on the part of its boundary that lies on the boundary of the body \(\Omega\), since the normal is the same there.
  2. On \(\Gamma\) we have \(\vek u = \vek g\) by the choice \(\vek g = \vek u|_\Gamma\). The restriction \(\vek u|_{\Omega'}\) is therefore a solution of the submodel.
  3. By theorem 11.4, point 3, the solution of the submodel is unique, because the only small rigid motion with zero on the part of the boundary with the given displacement is the field \(\vek 0\) by assumption. This is point 1.
  4. Let \(\tilde{\vek u}\) be the solution of the submodel with the datum \(\vek g = \vek u|_\Gamma + \vek e\) and \(\vek z = \tilde{\vek u} - \vek u|_{\Omega'}\). By corollary 11.3 with \(\alpha = 1\) and \(\beta = -1\) the field \(\vek z\) is the solution for the data \((\vek 0, \vek 0, \vek 0)\) on the part of the boundary of the body \(\Omega\) and \(\vek g - \vek u|_\Gamma = \vek e\) on \(\Gamma\).
  5. By step 4, \(\tilde{\vek u} = \vek u|_{\Omega'} + \vek z\), which is point 2.

Source: The submodel after COOK section 10.10 (PDF p. 370-372), where the procedure is described. The lemma and the proof are ours, not from the source. The existence of the solution \(\tilde{\vek u}\) is not proved in the book (chapter 11, section 11.1).

The lemma says two things. If the displacements on the cut are exact, the submodel is exact in the whole ring, however fine a mesh we use, and the error of the submodel is only the discretization error of the submodel. If they are not exact, the error is all that the error of the cut causes. Whether it falls with the distance from the cut, the lemma does not say. We test it on the tube.

Example 17.6 Submodel of the tube with coarse displacements on the cut (podmodel cevi z grobimi pomiki na rezu)

For the tube of figure 17.6 the exact displacement on the inner edge is \(u(a) = 16{,}8735\) \(\mu\)m and on the cut at \(c = 50\) mm it is \(u(c) = 14{,}6449\) \(\mu\)m (chapter 11, theorem 11.27). The coarse model of the tube has \(n\) linear elements along the radius: the displacement is piecewise linear in \(r\) and Ritz's method (definition 6.32) chooses the nodal displacements with the smallest total potential energy. For \(n = 2\) it has nodes at \(40\), \(50\) and \(60\) mm, so the displacement on the cut is simply a nodal value.

The submodel is the ring \(40 \le r \le 50\) mm with the pressure \(p\) at \(r = a\) and the prescribed displacement \(g = u_{\mathrm{n}}(c)\) at \(r = c\). A closed-form solution exists for it, because it is axisymmetric: the field \(u = \alpha r + \beta/r\) has, by \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} = u'\) and \(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} = u/r\) (corollary 11.21) and Hooke's law with the Lamé constants \(\lambda\) and \(\mu\), the radial stress

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = 2(\lambda + \mu)\,\alpha - \frac{2\mu\,\beta}{r^2} . \tag{17.2}\]

The ring therefore has two conditions: \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(a) = -p\) and \(u(c) = g\), and from them \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) follow. For the error \(z\) by lemma 17.5 the same holds with \(p = 0\) and \(g = e\): from \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(a) = 0\) we get \(\beta = (\lambda + \mu)\,\alpha\,a^2/\mu\) and \(z(r) = \alpha\,\big(r + k\,a^2/r\big)\) with \(k = (\lambda + \mu)/\mu = 1/(1 - 2\nu) = 2{,}5\) at \(\nu = 0{,}3\). From \(z(c) = e\) we get

\[\frac{z(a)}{z(c)} = \frac{a + k\,a}{c + k\,a^2/c} = \frac{40 \cdot 3{,}5}{50 + 2{,}5 \cdot 1600/50} = \frac{140}{130} = 1{,}0769 . \tag{17.3}\]

\(n = 2\) \(14{,}5030\) \(-0{,}1419\) \(16{,}6922\) \(-0{,}1812\) \(16{,}7206\) \(-0{,}1528\) \(1{,}0769\)

\(n = 8\) \(14{,}6357\) \(-0{,}0092\) \(16{,}8617\) \(-0{,}0118\) \(16{,}8636\) \(-0{,}0099\) \(1{,}0769\)

If the cut receives the exact displacement \(14{,}6449\) \(\mu\)m, the submodel gives the exact \(16{,}8735\) \(\mu\)m, as point 1 of the lemma says. The ratio of errors in the last column is in all rows equal to (17.3), as point 2 says: the error on the inner edge is \(1{,}077\) times the error on the cut.

The example answers COOK's question of when the cut is "far enough". The submodel does not reduce the error to the end, because it has the error of the cut in full, even enlarged. For \(n = 2\) the error of the coarse \(u_{\mathrm{n}}(a)\) was \(-0{,}1812\) \(\mu\)m, of the submodel \(-0{,}1528\) \(\mu\)m: the improvement is only \(16\) %, because the submodel corrects the computation of the ring, not the displacement on the cut. Why does the error not fall towards the inner edge? The right part of the figure shows \(z(r)/z(c)\): it equals \(1\) at \(r = c\) and \(1{,}077\) at \(r = a\). The error of the datum on the cut is a uniform radial displacement that compresses or stretches the ring as a whole, and no distance separates it from the inner edge. Saint-Venant's principle does not help here, although a uniform radial load on the cut is self-equilibrated: the principle speaks of points far from the loaded part of the boundary (section 11.11), but the error acts along the whole cut, a circle of diameter \(100\) mm, and every point of the ring is at most \(10\) mm from the cut. So the meaning of "far enough" is not only geometric: the cut must lie where the coarse mesh computation is accurate, and COOK suggests checking that the stresses on the cut in the coarse model and in the submodel do not differ significantly (COOK, PDF p. 372). COOK also writes that the submodel may underestimate the largest stress, because the coarse model is usually too stiff and so underestimates the displacements on the cut (COOK, PDF p. 372). The error of the coarse \(u_{\mathrm{n}}(a)\) is negative, as theorem 6.34, point 2, says: the work of the oil on the displacement \(\ell(u_{\mathrm{n}}) = 2\pi a\,p\,u_{\mathrm{n}}(a)\) for a Ritz field is not larger than for the true one, hence \(u_{\mathrm{n}}(a) \le u(a)\). The theorem does not say that the error on the cut is negative too, since it speaks only of the work of the oil on the inner edge. The table shows it.

The modelling error at the cut is therefore not removed by a finer mesh of the submodel. It is removed by a better datum on the cut: by a finer mesh of the coarse model, or by a cut where the coarse model knows the displacements better. This too is a decision about what of the body we take into the model: which displacements on the cut we take over from outside.

17.2.3 Supports and forces in the model (podpore in sile v modelu)

When the geometry is fixed, the body must be held and loaded. For supports COOK warns that boundary conditions are often wrongly represented in the mathematical model or wrongly entered, and that changes that seem small can change the result substantially (COOK, PDF p. 366). The easiest example is a straight beam held at both ends by a piece of wall of unknown stiffness: we do not know whether the support is pinned or clamped, we know only that it is somewhere in between.

We check this on the simply supported beam of chapter 5. A beam of length \(L\) and bending stiffness \(EI\) carries a uniform load \(q_0\) and is at each end rotationally held by a spring, so that the spring at a rotation of the end creates a moment \(M_{\mathrm{e}}\) against the rotation. For \(k = 0\) the support is pinned (simply supported beam), for \(k \to \infty\) clamped. We denote \(s = kL/(2EI)\).

Lemma 17.7 A support between two bounds (podpora med dvema mejama)

Let a beam of length \(L\) with constant bending stiffness \(EI\) be under a uniform load \(q_0 > 0\) downwards, with small displacements, supported at both ends by a transverse support and a rotational spring of stiffness \(k \ge 0\) that acts with the moment \(M_{\mathrm{e}}\) against the rotation of the end, \(M_{\mathrm{e}} = -k\,v'(0)\) at the left end and \(M_{\mathrm{e}} = k\,v'(L)\) at the right (\(v\) is the transverse displacement, positive upwards, as in chapter 5). For \(s = kL/(2EI)\) the moment at the end and at mid-span is

\[M_{\mathrm{e}} = \frac{q_0 L^2}{12}\,\frac{s}{1 + s} , \qquad M\!\left(\tfrac L2\right) = \frac{q_0 L^2}{8} - M_{\mathrm{e}} . \tag{17.4}\]

The moment at mid-span falls with growing \(s\) and for every \(k\) lies between \(q_0 L^2/24\) (clamped) and \(q_0 L^2/8\) (pinned support). The upper bound is reached at \(s = 0\), the moment approaches the lower one as \(s \to \infty\).

Proof

We must show that equilibrium and the support determine the moment \(M_{\mathrm{e}}\) with one equation, and then study the dependence on \(s\).

  1. The beam and the supports are symmetric about the middle, so the spring moments at both ends are equal, \(M_{\mathrm{e}}\), and let \(R = q_0 L/2\) be the reaction at each end (equilibrium of the whole beam, symmetry). The moment in the section \(x\) we get from the equilibrium of the left piece, which has the reaction \(R\) and the support moment \(M_{\mathrm{e}}\) against the rotation: \(M(x) = R x - \tfrac12 q_0 x^2 - M_{\mathrm{e}} = \tfrac12 q_0 x (L - x) - M_{\mathrm{e}}\).
  2. The deflection equation \(EI\,v'' = M\) (as in theorem 5.57) gives by integration \(EI\,v' = \tfrac14 q_0 L x^2 - \tfrac16 q_0 x^3 - M_{\mathrm{e}} x + C_1\) and \(EI\,v = \tfrac1{12} q_0 L x^3 - \tfrac1{24} q_0 x^4 - \tfrac12 M_{\mathrm{e}} x^2 + C_1 x\), since \(v(0) = 0\).
  3. The condition \(v(L) = 0\) gives \(0 = \tfrac1{12} q_0 L^4 - \tfrac1{24} q_0 L^4 - \tfrac12 M_{\mathrm{e}} L^2 + C_1 L\), so \(C_1 = -\tfrac1{24} q_0 L^3 + \tfrac12 M_{\mathrm{e}} L\) and \(v'(0) = C_1/(EI)\).
  4. The support gives \(M_{\mathrm{e}} = -k\,v'(0) = k\,\big(\tfrac1{24} q_0 L^3 - \tfrac12 M_{\mathrm{e}} L\big)/(EI)\), so \(M_{\mathrm{e}}\,(1 + s) = \tfrac{1}{12} q_0 L^2\,s\) with \(s = kL/(2EI)\), and this is the first equation (17.4). The second is \(M(L/2) = \tfrac18 q_0 L^2 - M_{\mathrm{e}}\) from step 1.
  5. The function \(s/(1 + s) = 1 - 1/(1 + s)\) grows from \(0\) at \(s = 0\) towards \(1\), because \(1/(1 + s)\) falls. Therefore \(M_{\mathrm{e}}\) grows from \(0\) towards \(q_0 L^2/12\), and the moment at mid-span falls from \(q_0 L^2/8\) towards \(q_0 L^2/8 - q_0 L^2/12 = q_0 L^2/24\).

Source: COOK section 10.8 (PDF p. 366): an analysis with pinned and with clamped supports bounds the true moment at mid-span. The lemma with the spring and the proof are not from the source. Theorem 5.57 is chapter 5.

For \(L = 2\) m and \(q_0 = 1\) kN/m, \(q_0 L^2/8 = 0{,}5000\) kNm and \(q_0 L^2/24 = 0{,}1667\) kNm. Between them lie the values \(M(L/2)\) for intermediate \(s\): \(0{,}4697\) (\(s = 0{,}1\)), \(0{,}3889\) (\(0{,}5\)), \(0{,}3333\) (\(1\)), \(0{,}2778\) (\(2\)), \(0{,}2222\) (\(5\)), \(0{,}1970\) (\(10\)), \(0{,}1700\) (\(100\)) kNm. Figure 17.7 shows the moments along the beam.

The beam is the grey horizontal line, supported at its ends. The first curve, \(s = 0\), is the pinned support: the moment is a parabola with the peak \(q_0 L^2/8\) and zeros at the ends. The second, \(s = 1\), is a spring of stiffness \(k = 2EI/L\): the moment has at the ends the negative value \(-q_0 L^2/24\) and at mid-span \(q_0 L^2/12\). The third, \(s \to \infty\), is the clamp: at the ends the moment is \(-q_0 L^2/12\) and at mid-span \(q_0 L^2/24\). The mid-span moments of the two bounding supports differ by a factor of \(3\), \(\tfrac18 : \tfrac1{24} = 3\). The true moment lies between them, so with the pinned support alone we overestimate it up to three times, and with the clamp alone we underestimate it up to three times.

This is the near miss of the section: one analysis with a chosen support that will never exist can be wrong by up to a factor \(3\) in the mid-span moment, however fine the mesh. Two analyses with the bounding supports, however, capture the true moment, as the lemma says, and COOK recommends them for this (COOK, PDF p. 366). COOK warns of two more cases: a support that prevents the stretching of the lower flange of a beam creates horizontal forces along the whole beam and must not be replaced by a free pin (COOK, PDF p. 366), and supports must be at nodes, because restricting the displacement between nodes requires additional conditions (COOK, PDF p. 367). Units and material data we check as we learned for the truss (section 7.10.2, check (g)), since a wrong system of units has the same weight as a wrong support.

17.2.4 A point force in the plane (točkovna sila v ravnini)

The last decision is how we write the load. For a force that acts on a small region it is natural to take it at a point: in the bar model we did this without trouble, the displacement under the force was finite (chapter 7). COOK, however, writes three claims about a force \(P\) at a point: in a beam the displacement and the stress are finite, in a flat plate under a transverse force the displacement is finite and the stress infinite, in a plane or solid body the displacement and the stress are infinite (COOK, PDF p. 361). If we repeat the mesh refinement around the force, the displacement in the body grows (COOK, PDF p. 361). We shall prove the last claim for the displacement in the plane. The question is why the plane and not the bar.

The idea comes from energy. If we find a field with displacement \(1\) at the point of the force and with small energy, the force does work on it, the strain energy is small and the balance is favourable. In a bar this is not possible: the displacement falls with the distance, and the slope that this means is expensive in energy. In the plane, however, the displacement falls in all directions at once, the radius of the circles grows, and the energy stays small. Figure 17.8 shows the field that we shall use.

On the left is the profile of the field \(w_\rho\) along the radius \(r\) from the point of the force, which lies at \(r = 0\). The field has the value \(1\) in the circle of radius \(\rho\) around the point, then falls with the logarithm of the radius and reaches \(0\) at radius \(R\), and outside it is zero. We drew two values of \(\rho\): for the smaller one the peak is thinner and the fall faster at the start, but with a long tail to \(R\). The dashed straight line is the field in a bar that falls linearly. On the right is the energy of the field, \(\int |\nabla w_\rho|^2\) over the disc, as a function of \(\rho\): it falls as we decrease \(\rho\). We shall prove this in the next lemma, in which we compute with circular rings.

Lemma 17.8 Energy of the field around a point (energija polja okoli točke)

Let \(0 < \rho < R\) and \(w_\rho(r) = 1\) for \(r \le \rho\), \(w_\rho(r) = \ln(R/r)/\ln(R/\rho)\) for \(\rho \le r \le R\) and \(w_\rho(r) = 0\) for \(r \ge R\), where \(r\) is the distance from a chosen point of the plane. The field is continuous, piecewise continuously differentiable, has the value \(1\) at the point and is zero outside the circle of radius \(R\). For the gradient \(\nabla w_\rho\) it holds

\[\int_{\mathrm{r} \le \mathrm{R}} |\nabla w_\rho|^2\,\mathrm{d}A = \frac{2\pi}{\ln(R/\rho)} . \tag{17.5}\]

This number falls towards zero as \(\rho \to 0\) at constant \(R\).

Proof

We must show that the gradient on the ring is known and that the integral over circular rings follows.

  1. On \(\rho < r < R\), \(w_\rho = \ln(R/r)/\ln(R/\rho)\), so by the rule for the derivative of the logarithm \(w_\rho'(r) = -1/(r\,\ln(R/\rho))\) (lemma 1.81). The gradient of a field that depends only on \(r\) points along the radius and has magnitude \(|w_\rho'(r)|\), so \(|\nabla w_\rho|^2 = 1/(r^2\,\ln^2(R/\rho))\). For \(r < \rho\) and \(r > R\), \(w_\rho\) is constant and \(\nabla w_\rho = \vek 0\).
  2. The function \(|\nabla w_\rho|^2\) depends only on \(r\), so the integral over the circle of radius \(R\) equals the integral over \(r\) with the weight of the area of a circular ring \(2\pi r\) per unit radius, \(\int_\rho^R |w_\rho'(r)|^2\,2\pi r\,\mathrm{d}r\) (the integral over a circular ring as the limit of sums of areas of thin rings, not as a product of numbers).
  3. We insert step 1: \(\int_\rho^R \dfrac{2\pi r}{r^2\,\ln^2(R/\rho)}\,\mathrm{d}r = \dfrac{2\pi}{\ln^2(R/\rho)}\int_\rho^R \dfrac{1}{r}\,\mathrm{d}r = \dfrac{2\pi}{\ln^2(R/\rho)}\,\big(\ln R - \ln\rho\big) = \dfrac{2\pi}{\ln(R/\rho)}\), by the definition of the natural logarithm (definition 9.10) and the rule for the logarithm of a quotient.
  4. As \(\rho \to 0\), \(R/\rho \to \infty\) and \(\ln(R/\rho) \to \infty\), so \(2\pi/\ln(R/\rho) \to 0\).

Source: The function \(w_\rho\) is a so-called logarithmic test function. The lemma and the proof are not from the source.

Numbers: \(2\pi/\ln(R/\rho)\) is \(1{,}606\), \(1{,}011\), \(0{,}738\), \(0{,}581\) and \(0{,}354\) for \(\rho = 1\), \(0{,}1\), \(0{,}01\), \(0{,}001\) and \(10^{-6}\) mm at \(R = 50\) mm (num-02.py). They fall slowly: for it to fall below \(0{,}354\) we must decrease \(\rho\) a millionfold. Now we assemble the theorem.

Take a plane strain state (definition 11.30) of thickness \(b\) of an isotropic material with bulk modulus \(K\) and shear modulus \(G\) (theorem 3.48, \(K, G > 0\)). The body contains a circle of radius \(R\) around a point \(P_0\), and there acts a force \(F\) in the direction of the axis \(x\). The supports stand outside the circle. For a point force the work of the force on a field \(\vek v\) is \(F\,v_{\mathrm{x}}(P_0)\).

Theorem 17.9 Point force in the plane (točkovna sila v ravnini)

In the described body (plane strain with thickness \(b\), \(K, G > 0\), a circle of radius \(R\) around \(P_0\) in the body, supports outside the circle) let \(\Pi(\vek v) = U(\vek v) - F\,v_{\mathrm{x}}(P_0)\) be the total potential energy (definition 4.89) for a continuous, piecewise continuously differentiable displacement field \(\vek v\) and \(M' = (K + G)\,b\). Then:

  1. for every \(\rho \in (0, R)\) and the field \(\vek v_{\mathrm{t}} = t\,w_\rho\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) it holds \(U(\vek v_{\mathrm{t}}) \le M'\,t^2\,\dfrac{2\pi}{\ln(R/\rho)}\),
  2. for the same fields \(\min_{\mathrm{t}} \Pi(\vek v_{\mathrm{t}}) \le -\dfrac{F^2\,\ln(R/\rho)}{8\pi\,M'}\),
  3. the total potential energy on these fields is not bounded below, so there is no field with finite energy that would solve the weak form \(B(\vek u, \vek v) = F\,v_{\mathrm{x}}(P_0)\) for every \(\vek v\).
Proof

We must show that the energy of the field \(\vek v_{\mathrm{t}}\) is bounded above by the energy of the gradient, which is small by the lemma, and then that the potential energy is not bounded below.

  1. The energy per unit volume is by equation (3.43) \(w = \tfrac12 K\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}^2 + G \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} e_{\mathrm{ij}}^2\), where \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\) for plane strain (\(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\)) and \(e_{\mathrm{ij}}\) are the components of the strain deviator. By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality for two numbers \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}^2 \le 2(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}^2 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}^2) \le 2\sum\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}^2\). Since \(\sum\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}^2 = \sum e_{\mathrm{ij}}^2 + \tfrac13\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}^2\), \(\sum e_{\mathrm{ij}}^2 \le \sum\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}^2\). Therefore \(w \le (K + G)\sum\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}^2\).
  2. The strain is the symmetric part of the gradient \(\nabla\vek v\) and its sum of squares of components is not larger than the sum of squares of all components of the gradient, because the sum of squares of components of a matrix equals the sum of squares of the symmetric and of the antisymmetric part. For \(\vek v_{\mathrm{t}}\) the gradient has only the components \(t\,\partial w_\rho/\partial x\) and \(t\,\partial w_\rho/\partial y\), so \(\sum\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}^2 \le t^2\,|\nabla w_\rho|^2\).
  3. We integrate over the body of thickness \(b\): \(U(\vek v_{\mathrm{t}}) = b\int w\,\mathrm{d}A \le (K + G)\,b\,t^2 \int |\nabla w_\rho|^2\,\mathrm{d}A = M'\,t^2\,2\pi/\ln(R/\rho)\) by lemma 17.8. This is point 1.
  4. The field \(\vek v_{\mathrm{t}}\) has at the point \(P_0\) the value \(v_{\mathrm{x}} = t\), so \(\Pi(\vek v_{\mathrm{t}}) \le M'\,I\,t^2 - F t\) with \(I = 2\pi/\ln(R/\rho)\). The function \(q(t) = M' I\,t^2 - F t\) has, by completing the square, the smallest value \(-F^2/(4 M' I)\) at \(t = F/(2 M' I)\). This is point 2.
  5. As \(\rho \to 0\), \(I \to 0\) (lemma 17.8), so \(-F^2/(4M'I) \to -\infty\). The potential energy on admissible fields is therefore not bounded below.
  6. Suppose \(\vek u\) is a field with finite energy that for every field \(\vek v\) solves \(B(\vek u, \vek v) = F\,v_{\mathrm{x}}(P_0)\). By theorem 6.34, point 1, then \(\Pi(\vek v) \ge \Pi(\vek u)\) for every \(\vek v\), and \(\Pi(\vek u)\) is a finite number. This contradicts step 5, so such a \(\vek u\) does not exist. This is point 3.

Source: COOK section 10.6 (PDF p. 361) states the claim for a plane or solid body without proof. The theorem and the proof are not from the source. The bound \(K + G\) from the energy (3.43) is ours.

Now the mesh. Let \(\mathcal V_1 \subset \mathcal V_2 \subset \dots\) be a sequence of trial spaces (definition 6.32) of fields from the theorem that have a node at \(P_0\), and let one assumption (H) hold: for every \(\rho\) and \(t\) there exist fields \(\vek v_{\mathrm{n}} \in \mathcal V_{\mathrm{n}}\) for which \(\|\nabla(\vek v_{\mathrm{n}} - \vek v_{\mathrm{t}})\|^2 = \int \sum_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j}} \big(\partial(v_{\mathrm{n},\mathrm{i}} - v_{\mathrm{t},\mathrm{i}})/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}\big)^2\,\mathrm{d}A\) goes to zero and \(v_{\mathrm{n},\mathrm{x}}(P_0)\) goes to \(t\), where \(\vek v_{\mathrm{t}} = t\,w_\rho\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). For piecewise linear elements with a node at the point of the force, whose size goes to zero, this holds by interpolating the field \(w_\rho\) at the nodes. We do not write the proof, because plane elements are not part of this section (chapter 15).

Corollary 17.10 Displacement under a point force on nested meshes (pomik pod točkovno silo na gnezdenih mrežah)

Let \(\vek u_{\mathrm{n}}\) be the Ritz solution (definition 6.32) in \(\mathcal V_{\mathrm{n}}\) for the force \(F > 0\) at the point \(P_0\), under the assumption (H). Then \(u_{\mathrm{n},\mathrm{x}}(P_0)\) does not fall with \(n\) and is unbounded above: for every \(\rho\), \(\liminf_{\mathrm{n}} u_{\mathrm{n},\mathrm{x}}(P_0) \ge F\,\ln(R/\rho)/(4\pi\,M')\), and so it grows beyond every bound as \(\rho\) decreases.

Proof

We must show that the energy falls and with it the displacement grows, and then that it falls below the bound of the theorem.

  1. The Ritz solution satisfies the Ritz equations \(B(\vek u_{\mathrm{n}}, \vek w) = F\,w_{\mathrm{x}}(P_0)\) for every \(\vek w \in \mathcal V_{\mathrm{n}}\) (definition 6.32, zero essential conditions). With \(\vek w = \vek u_{\mathrm{n}}\) we get \(B(\vek u_{\mathrm{n}}, \vek u_{\mathrm{n}}) = F\,u_{\mathrm{n},\mathrm{x}}(P_0)\), so \(\Pi(\vek u_{\mathrm{n}}) = \tfrac12 B(\vek u_{\mathrm{n}}, \vek u_{\mathrm{n}}) - F\,u_{\mathrm{n},\mathrm{x}}(P_0) = -\tfrac12 F\,u_{\mathrm{n},\mathrm{x}}(P_0)\). This is the computation of theorem 6.34, point 2, which does not need the true solution.
  2. Since \(\mathcal V_{\mathrm{n}} \subset \mathcal V_{\mathrm{n}+1}\) and \(\vek u_{\mathrm{n}+1}\) has the smallest energy in the larger space, \(\Pi(\vek u_{\mathrm{n}+1}) \le \Pi(\vek u_{\mathrm{n}})\), as in step 1 of the proof of theorem 6.35. With step 1 and \(F > 0\), \(u_{\mathrm{n},\mathrm{x}}(P_0)\) therefore does not fall with \(n\).
  3. Choose \(\rho\) and \(t = F/(2M'I)\) as in theorem 17.9, point 2. By (H) there exist \(\vek v_{\mathrm{n}} \in \mathcal V_{\mathrm{n}}\) that approach \(\vek v_{\mathrm{t}}\). The function \(\sqrt{U}\) is a seminorm, because \(U\) is a nonnegative quadratic form, so by the triangle inequality \(\sqrt{U(\vek v_{\mathrm{n}})} \le \sqrt{U(\vek v_{\mathrm{t}})} + \sqrt{U(\vek v_{\mathrm{n}} - \vek v_{\mathrm{t}})}\), and the last term goes to zero by (H), because steps 1 and 2 of the proof of theorem 17.9 give \(U(\vek w) \le M'\,\|\nabla \vek w\|^2\) for every field \(\vek w\).
  4. Therefore \(\limsup_{\mathrm{n}} U(\vek v_{\mathrm{n}}) \le U(\vek v_{\mathrm{t}})\) and \(\limsup_{\mathrm{n}} \Pi(\vek v_{\mathrm{n}}) \le \Pi(\vek v_{\mathrm{t}}) \le -F^2/(4M'I)\) (theorem 17.9, point 2).
  5. The Ritz solution has the smallest energy in \(\mathcal V_{\mathrm{n}}\) (definition 6.32), so \(\Pi(\vek u_{\mathrm{n}}) \le \Pi(\vek v_{\mathrm{n}})\), and \(\limsup_{\mathrm{n}} \Pi(\vek u_{\mathrm{n}}) \le -F^2/(4M'I)\). With step 1, \(\liminf_{\mathrm{n}} u_{\mathrm{n},\mathrm{x}}(P_0) \ge F/(2M'I) = F\,\ln(R/\rho)/(4\pi M')\).

Source: COOK section 10.6 (PDF p. 361) writes that the displacement in the body grows when the mesh refinement at the force is repeated. The proof is not from the source.

The corollary says that the displacement under a point force on nested meshes does not converge but grows. How fast? Take steel with \(E = 196\) GPa and \(\nu = 0{,}3\) (plane strain), so \(K = 163{,}3\) GPa and \(G = 75{,}38\) GPa, thickness \(b = 10\) mm and \(F = 1\) kN. Then \(M' = 2{,}387 \cdot 10^6\) N/mm. The lower bounds of the displacement by the corollary are \(0{,}130\), \(0{,}207\), \(0{,}284\), \(0{,}361\) and \(0{,}591\) \(\mu\)m for \(\rho = 1\), \(0{,}1\), \(0{,}01\), \(0{,}001\) and \(10^{-6}\) mm (num-02.py). These are small values, but they grow for every tenfold smaller \(\rho\) by the same step \(0{,}077\) \(\mu\)m, as the logarithm in the corollary says.

We test it on a real mesh. A square \(100 \times 100\) mm (\(R = 50\) mm) of this steel, clamped along its edge, has at its centre the force \(F\) in the direction \(x\). We divide it into \(n \times n\) squares, each with a diagonal into two triangles, and take a piecewise linear field on the triangles. For \(n = 4\), \(8\), \(16\), \(32\) (up to \(1922\) unknowns) the displacement at the point of the force is \(0{,}2534\), \(0{,}3517\), \(0{,}4472\) and \(0{,}5417\) \(\mu\)m. The increase at each halving of the element size is \(0{,}0983\), \(0{,}0955\) and \(0{,}0945\) \(\mu\)m: almost constant, which is the sign of logarithmic growth. The cause is in the corollary: halving the element size halves \(\rho\), and \(\ln(R/\rho)\) grows by \(\ln 2\). The measured increase is about four times larger than the lower bound \(F\,\ln 2/(4\pi M') = 0{,}023\) \(\mu\)m per halving, because \(M'\) is only a rough upper bound of the energy and the field \(w_\rho\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) is not the field of smallest energy. The displacement under the force is therefore not settled by any mesh density.

Lemma 17.8 has an important near case. In a bar (chapter 7) the displacement under the force is finite. Imagine a field that has the value \(1\) at the point of the force and is zero at distance \(R\) on one side. By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality for the integral, \(1 = -\int_0^R w'\,\mathrm{d}x \le \sqrt{R}\,\sqrt{\int_0^R w'^2\,\mathrm{d}x}\), so \(\int_0^R w'^2\,\mathrm{d}x \ge 1/R\). The energy of every such field is therefore bounded below by \(1/R\), a bound that does not depend on how narrow the peak is. In a bar there are therefore no fields with the value \(1\) at the point of the force whose energy would go to zero, and the argument of theorem 17.9 does not work in a bar. The smallest energy \(1/R\) belongs exactly to the linear drop, the dashed straight line in the figure, and the displacement under the force in a bar is finite, as we computed it in chapter 7. In the plane there is such a field with energy that goes to zero: this is the difference between the dimensions.

Another near case is a load on a small region. If we distribute the force \(F\) uniformly among the nodes of the square \(|x|, |y| \le 12{,}5\) mm around the force, the displacement at the centre for \(n = 8\), \(16\), \(32\) is \(0{,}1596\), \(0{,}1818\) and \(0{,}1952\) \(\mu\)m, and the changes are \(0{,}0222\) and \(0{,}0134\) \(\mu\)m: they get smaller, the displacement settles. The work of such a force on the field \(w_\rho\) is bounded by its average and the energy stays finite (here the mesh counterpart of the region is a square of fixed size, not a point). By Saint-Venant's principle (section 11.11) the displacements and stresses outside a small region differ only little from those under a point force, and COOK writes that two statically equivalent load distributions differ only near the loaded region (COOK, PDF p. 128). COOK remarks that a point force is a convenient idea, since all real forces are distributed over an area greater than zero (COOK, PDF p. 361). The size of this region is a true length that replaces \(\rho\). How large it is, is decided by the contact of bodies, which chapter 25 treats.

A consequence for modelling: if the displacement under the force grows by a constant step at every mesh refinement, this is not a mesh error but a sign that the model has no finite displacement under the force. The answer is in the model, not in the mesh: we distribute the force over a region of a size that the physical problem knows, or we take it as a statically equivalent load (definition 11.44) where the result near the force does not interest us.

17.2.5 What we showed (kaj smo pokazali)

This section treated the modelling error for the body, the supports and the forces. For geometry we showed on the hole in a plate that leaving out a detail is justified only for questions that lie far from it: at \(r = 10\,a\) the error is \(2{,}5\) %, while for the largest stress the error is \(67\) % (example 17.4, figure 17.5). For the submodel we proved with lemma 17.5 that the error of the submodel is the solution of a problem with the error of the cut as datum, and on the tube we computed that the inner edge enlarges it by a factor \(1{,}077\) (figure 17.6). For supports we proved that the true mid-span moment lies between the moments of the pinned and the clamped support, which differ by a factor of three (lemma 17.7, figure 17.7). For the point force we proved that in a plane body the displacement under it on nested meshes grows beyond every bound (theorem 17.9, corollary 17.10, figure 17.8), and confirmed it with numbers. All these errors are modelling errors: a finer mesh does not remove them, in the last case it even reveals them.

For what follows: the discretization error, that is the difference between the solution on a mesh and the true solution of the model, is treated by section 17.3, where we estimate the order of convergence from a sequence of meshes. Plane and solid elements are in chapter 15, symmetry and constraints in chapter 19, contact of bodies in chapter 25.
