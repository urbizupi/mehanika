Chapter 1 Strain

1.9 Mathematics: sequences and their limits

At the end of the previous section we announced endless strings of numbers that approach some number. We have met them already. In section 1.2 we computed, for the hanging bar, the strain of ever shorter pieces at the top, and the strains approached the number \(3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-6}\). In this section such a string of approximations, which follow one another without end, gets a name and a precise definition. Then we say when such a string approaches a number, and we show that it cannot approach two numbers at once and that its numbers are then bounded. We will need such strings for the proofs in the next sections, because with them we can catch a point as the place that a string of points approaches.

1.9.1 Sequence

Let us return to the hanging bar of section 1.7.1: a steel bar of length \(L = 10\) m stretches under its own weight. It is fixed only at the centre \(O\) of its top end, and its weight is carried by a force that pulls the top face up evenly, which is an idealization (figure 1.40). A point on the axis of the bar at the depth \(x\) moves down by \(u(x) = a\,(2Lx - x^2)\), where \(a = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1}\). Above the point at the top there is no material, so we take pieces below it. At the top let us take a piece of length \(1\) m, then \(\tfrac12\) m, \(\tfrac13\) m, \(\tfrac14\) m and so on, each time a piece of length \(1/n\) m for the next natural number \(n\) (figure 1.56, left). By (1.6), written for \(x = 0\) and \(\Delta x = 1/n\) m, the strain of the \(n\)-th piece is

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{n}} = \frac{u(1/n) - u(0)}{1/n} = a\,(2L - 1/n) = 2aL - \frac{a}{n} ,\]

where \(1/n\) means the length of the piece in metres. The first few values are \(\varepsilon_1 = 3{,}7088 \cdot 10^{-6}\), \(\varepsilon_2 = 3{,}8064 \cdot 10^{-6}\), \(\varepsilon_3 = 3{,}8389 \cdot 10^{-6}\), \(\varepsilon_{10} = 3{,}8845 \cdot 10^{-6}\) and \(\varepsilon_{1000} = 3{,}9038 \cdot 10^{-6}\) (figure 1.56, right). To every natural number \(n\) belongs one strain \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{n}}\), and it matters which strain belongs to which \(n\): the strain at \(n = 2\) is the strain of the piece of length \(\tfrac12\) m.

Definition 1.37 Sequence (zaporedje)

A sequence (zaporedje) of real numbers is a function whose input is a natural number \(n\) and whose output is a real number, which we write \(x_{\mathrm{n}}\) and call the \(n\)-th term (člen) of the sequence. We write the whole sequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) or \(x_1, x_2, x_3, \dots\) A sequence is bounded (omejeno) if the set of its terms \(\{x_{\mathrm{n}} : n \in \mathbb N\}\) is a bounded set (definition 1.29).

Source: LEBL1 Def. 2.1.1 (PDF p. 51).

So a sequence is a function as in definition 1.4, whose domain is the set of natural numbers. The number \(n\) is the place of the term in the sequence, and \(x_{\mathrm{n}}\) is the term at this place. The strains of the pieces are a sequence \((\varepsilon_{\mathrm{n}})\), and its third term is \(\varepsilon_3 = 3{,}8389 \cdot 10^{-6}\). Another example is the sequence \((1/n)\) with the terms \(1, \tfrac12, \tfrac13, \dots\), which we can draw in two ways (figure 1.57). As the graph of a function these are dots above the numbers \(1, 2, 3, \dots\), which we do not join with a line, because the sequence has no values in between. On the number line they are the points \(1, \tfrac12, \tfrac13, \dots\), which crowd together toward \(0\).

A sequence is not the set of its terms. The sequence \(((-1)^n)\), that is, \(-1, 1, -1, 1, \dots\), has infinitely many terms, but the set of its terms is \(\{-1, 1\}\). In a sequence what counts is the place of each term and the fact that a term repeats.

1.9.2 Limit of a sequence

What exactly does it mean that the strains \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{n}}\) approach the number \(2aL\)? It is not enough that the distance to \(2aL\) decreases, since the distance to the number \(4 \cdot 10^{-6}\) decreases too, and the strains do not reach this number and do not come arbitrarily close to it. We require that the distance eventually becomes smaller than every allowed error and stays so.

From history

A distance that decreases at every step, while the steps never end, is a difficulty older than our strains. In the fifth century BC Zeno of Elea argued that Achilles cannot overtake a tortoise. He must first reach the place from which the tortoise started, and by then the tortoise has moved on a little way. Achilles must traverse that too, and the tortoise is ahead again, he is always nearer but never makes up to it (CAJORI, PDF p. 39). None of Zeno's writings have come down to us, and we know him through his critics (CAJORI, PDF p. 39).

Back to the strains \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{n}}\) that approach the number \(2aL\). Let us see why Achilles does overtake the tortoise after all: his total distance too approaches a bound. Let Achilles be ten times faster than the tortoise and let the tortoise have a head start of 100 m. When Achilles runs these 100 m, the tortoise has gone 10 m. When Achilles runs these 10 m as well, the tortoise has gone 1 m, then 0.1 m and so on. Achilles' distances are therefore 100 m, 10 m, 1 m, 0.1 m and so on, each a tenth of the previous one (figure 1.58). After the first step 100 m have been run, after the second 110 m, after the third 111 m, after the fourth 111.1 m. The total distance approaches \(1000/9 \approx 111{,}11\) m. There Achilles and the tortoise are at the same place at the same time, since in the time in which Achilles runs 111.11 m the tortoise has covered a tenth of this distance and is \(100 + 11{,}11 = 111{,}11\) m from the start. The total distance never reaches this place at any step, since after the fourth step about 0.011 m is still missing, after the fifth about 0.0011 m and so on, always something.

From history

Gregory St. Vincent (1584 to 1667), whose large book Opus geometricum appeared in Antwerp in 1647 (CAJORI, PDF p. 197-198), was the first to treat the problem of Achilles as the summation of infinitely many ever smaller distances, and the first to state the exact time and place at which Achilles overtakes the tortoise. He thought of the number that the sum approaches as an obstacle against further advance, like a rigid wall. It did not bother him that the sum never reaches it (CAJORI, PDF p. 198).

The abbreviation lim, which we write from section 1.3.3 on, as far as is known, did not exist before Simon Lhuilier (CAJNOT2, PDF p. 278). In the same textbook Cauchy also wrote: when the successive values attributed to a variable approach a fixed value indefinitely, so as to end by differing from it as little as is wished, this fixed value is called the limit of all the others (CAJORI, PDF p. 412).

Let us return to numbers that approach a bound, as the strains \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{n}}\) approach the number \(2aL\). The definition that follows says the same as Cauchy's description, but with a permitted error \(\eta\) and a place \(N\) in the sequence from which on the distance is smaller than \(\eta\). This is the same idea as for the limit of a function in section 1.3.3, except that instead of "close enough to zero" we say "far enough along the sequence".

Definition 1.38 Limit of a sequence (limita zaporedja)

A sequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) converges (konvergira) to a number \(d\) if for every \(\eta > 0\) there is a natural number \(N\) such that \(|x_{\mathrm{n}} - d| < \eta\) for every \(n \ge N\). The number \(d\) is then the limit (limita) of the sequence, and we write

\[\lim_{\mathrm{n} \to \infty} x_{\mathrm{n}} = d . \tag{1.25}\]

A sequence that converges to some number is convergent (konvergentno), otherwise it is divergent (divergentno).

Source: LEBL1 Def. 2.1.2 (PDF p. 52).

The definition is a game of two players. The first chooses an allowed error \(\eta\), however small. The second must find a place \(N\) from which on all terms are closer to the number \(d\) than \(\eta\). The sequence converges if the second player always wins. In figure 1.59 this is the band between \(d - \eta\) and \(d + \eta\): from the place \(N\) on all dots are in the band, while before it they may be outside. The place \(N\) depends on \(\eta\). A narrower band usually requires a later place (figure 1.60). We read \(n \to \infty\) as "when \(n\) goes past every bound". The sign \(\infty\) (infinity) is not a number, and the notation only says that we look at ever later terms.

The limit is a property of the tail of the sequence, that is, of the terms from some place on. The first few terms do not affect it, because the definition speaks only of \(n \ge N\). Nor does any term have to reach the limit.

The simplest example is the constant sequence \(c, c, c, \dots\) It converges to \(c\), because \(|c - c| = 0 < \eta\) for every \(n\), so for every \(\eta\) the place \(N = 1\) will do. We need the next example in almost every computation that follows.

Lemma 1.39 Limit of the sequence 1/n (limita zaporedja 1/n)

The sequence \((1/n)\) converges to \(0\): \(\lim_{\mathrm{n} \to \infty} 1/n = 0\).

Proof

We must show that for every \(\eta > 0\) there is a place \(N\) from which on \(|1/n - 0| < \eta\).

  1. We pick any error \(\eta > 0\).
  2. By the Archimedean property 1.35 there is a natural number \(N\) such that \(1/n < \eta\) for every \(n \ge N\).
  3. For every \(n\) we have \(1/n - 0 = 1/n > 0\), so \(|1/n - 0| = 1/n\).
  4. By steps 2 and 3, \(|1/n - 0| < \eta\) for every \(n \ge N\). We picked the error freely, so the limit of the sequence is \(0\).

Source: LEBL1 Example 2.1.4 (PDF p. 53).

For the error \(\eta = 0{,}01\), for example, \(N = 101\): for \(n \ge 101\) we have \(1/n \le \tfrac{1}{101} < 0{,}01\), while at \(n = 100\) we have \(1/n = 0{,}01\), which is not less than \(0{,}01\).

Now we can say exactly what the strains of the pieces approach. Let us show that \(\lim_{\mathrm{n} \to \infty} \varepsilon_{\mathrm{n}} = 2aL\).

  1. We pick any error \(\eta > 0\).
  2. The distance of a term from the number \(2aL\) is \(|\varepsilon_{\mathrm{n}} - 2aL| = |{-a/n}| = a/n\), because \(a > 0\).
  3. The condition \(a/n < \eta\) holds exactly when \(1/n < \eta/a\). The number \(\eta/a\) is positive, so by the Archimedean property 1.35 there is an \(N\) such that \(1/n < \eta/a\) for every \(n \ge N\).
  4. So for every \(n \ge N\) we have \(|\varepsilon_{\mathrm{n}} - 2aL| = a/n < \eta\). We picked the error freely, so \(\lim_{\mathrm{n} \to \infty} \varepsilon_{\mathrm{n}} = 2aL = 3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-6}\).

This is the strain at the point at the top of the bar, which in section 1.4 we got as a derivative. There we required that the strain of a piece approach the derivative for all short enough pieces, not only for chosen lengths. Here we took only the lengths \(1, \tfrac12, \tfrac13, \dots\) m and got the same number. A single chosen sequence of lengths does not prove that the derivative exists, since the quotient could behave differently for other lengths. That every such sequence approaches the derivative, when the derivative exists, we will show in one of the next sections. At the very top this limit is one-sided. There is no material above the top, so the length \(\Delta x\) of the piece approaches zero only from the positive side, and the strain at the top is the limit of the quotient from the right (definition 1.8), that is, the one-sided derivative of definition 1.9. The number \(3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-6}\) at the very top holds for the idealized way of holding the bar. For a bar welded to the ceiling it would be different near the ceiling.

1.9.3 A sequence without a limit

Does every sequence have a limit? No. The sequence \(((-1)^n)\), that is, \(-1, 1, -1, 1, \dots\), jumps between \(-1\) and \(1\) and does not settle at any number. Why can it not converge to any number \(d\)? Neighbouring terms are \(2\) apart. If both were closer to the number \(d\) than \(\tfrac12\), they would be closer to each other than \(1\) (figure 1.61).

Lemma 1.40 A sequence without a limit (zaporedje brez limite)

The sequence \(((-1)^n)\) is bounded and divergent.

Proof

We must show that the terms are bounded and that the sequence does not converge to any number.

  1. The term \((-1)^n\) is \(1\) if \(n\) is even, and \(-1\) if \(n\) is odd. So \(-1 \le (-1)^n \le 1\) for every \(n\), and the sequence is bounded.
  2. We prove the divergence by contradiction. Suppose that the sequence converges to some number \(d\).
  3. We use the definition of the limit of a sequence 1.38 with the error \(\eta = \tfrac12\): there is an \(N\) such that \(|(-1)^n - d| < \tfrac12\) for every \(n \ge N\). We choose the error \(\tfrac12\) because the sum of two such errors is \(1\), which is less than the distance \(2\) between the terms \(1\) and \(-1\).
  4. Let \(n = 2N\). This number is even and at least \(N\), and the number \(n + 1\) is odd and also at least \(N\). So \((-1)^n = 1\) and \((-1)^{n+1} = -1\), and by step 3, \(|1 - d| < \tfrac12\) and \(|-1 - d| < \tfrac12\).
  5. We estimate the distance between \(1\) and \(-1\) through the number \(d\). We write \(1 - (-1) = (1 - d) + (d - (-1))\). By the triangle inequality for numbers \(|a + b| \le |a| + |b|\) (lemma 1.6, item 1),
    \[2 = |1 - (-1)| \le |1 - d| + |d - (-1)| .\]
  6. The numbers \(d - (-1)\) and \(-1 - d\) are opposite, so they have the same absolute value, \(|d - (-1)| = |-1 - d|\). So by step 4 the right side of the inequality of step 5 is smaller than \(\tfrac12 + \tfrac12 = 1\), and it follows that \(2 < 1\).
  7. This is not true. The assumption of step 2 is false, so the sequence does not converge to any number.

Source: LEBL1 Example 2.1.5 (PDF pp. 53-54).

So the sequence \(((-1)^n)\) is bounded, and still it has no limit. Being bounded is not enough for convergence.

1.9.4 Uniqueness of the limit

The notation \(\lim_{\mathrm{n} \to \infty} x_{\mathrm{n}} = d\) claims that there is only one limit. Let us check that a sequence cannot converge to two different numbers. The idea is the same as for the limit of a function in lemma 1.7 on the uniqueness of the limit. If the terms approached two numbers, late terms would be close to both, so the two numbers would be close to each other, closer than every error (figure 1.62).

Lemma 1.41 Uniqueness of the limit of a sequence (enoličnost limite zaporedja)

A convergent sequence has only one limit.

Proof

We must show that two numbers \(d_1\) and \(d_2\) to which the same sequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) converges are equal.

  1. We pick any error \(\eta > 0\). We will show that \(|d_1 - d_2| < \eta\).
  2. Since \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) converges to \(d_1\), the definition of the limit of a sequence 1.38 gives for the error \(\eta/2\) a place \(N_1\) such that \(|x_{\mathrm{n}} - d_1| < \eta/2\) for every \(n \ge N_1\). We take half the error because in step 5 we will add two such errors.
  3. Since \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) converges to \(d_2\), for the same error \(\eta/2\) there is a place \(N_2\) such that \(|x_{\mathrm{n}} - d_2| < \eta/2\) for every \(n \ge N_2\).
  4. Let \(n\) be the larger of the numbers \(N_1\) and \(N_2\). Since \(n \ge N_1\) and \(n \ge N_2\), both inequalities of steps 2 and 3 hold for this \(n\).
  5. We write the difference through the term \(x_{\mathrm{n}}\), \(d_1 - d_2 = (d_1 - x_{\mathrm{n}}) + (x_{\mathrm{n}} - d_2)\). By the triangle inequality for numbers (lemma 1.6, item 1) and since \(|d_1 - x_{\mathrm{n}}| = |x_{\mathrm{n}} - d_1|\), we have
    \[|d_1 - d_2| \le |x_{\mathrm{n}} - d_1| + |x_{\mathrm{n}} - d_2| < \tfrac{\eta}{2} + \tfrac{\eta}{2} = \eta .\]
  6. We picked the error freely, so \(|d_1 - d_2| \le \eta\) for every \(\eta > 0\). Since \(|d_1 - d_2| \ge 0\), by lemma 1.34 on an inequality tested with all positive numbers \(|d_1 - d_2| = 0\), and so \(d_1 = d_2\).

Source: LEBL1 Prop. 2.1.6 (PDF p. 53).

The proof repeated the pattern of the proof of the uniqueness of the limit of a function: when the sum of two errors must be small, we allow each of them half. The only new thing is that the two requirements each held from their own place on, and both hold at once from the larger of the two places on.

1.9.5 Boundedness of a convergent sequence

The sequence \(((-1)^n)\) showed that a bounded sequence need not be convergent. The converse, however, does hold: a convergent sequence is always bounded. Why? From some place \(N\) on the terms are close to the limit, say closer than \(1\), and so they are bounded. Before this place there are only finitely many terms, \(x_1, \dots, x_{\mathrm{N}-1}\), and finitely many numbers can be caught by one bound (figure 1.63).

Lemma 1.42 A convergent sequence is bounded (konvergentno zaporedje je omejeno)

If a sequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) converges, there is a number \(B\) such that \(|x_{\mathrm{n}}| \le B\) for every \(n\). So the sequence is bounded, with the lower bound \(-B\) and the upper bound \(B\).

Proof

We must show that one bound \(B\) bounds all the terms.

  1. Let the sequence converge to \(d\). We use the definition of the limit of a sequence 1.38 with the error \(\eta = 1\): there is an \(N\) such that \(|x_{\mathrm{n}} - d| < 1\) for every \(n \ge N\). The size of the error does not matter. We only need some bound for the terms from \(N\) on.
  2. For \(n \ge N\) we write \(x_{\mathrm{n}} = (x_{\mathrm{n}} - d) + d\). By the triangle inequality for numbers (lemma 1.6, item 1), \(|x_{\mathrm{n}}| \le |x_{\mathrm{n}} - d| + |d| < 1 + |d|\).
  3. As the bound we take the sum of the absolute values of the terms before \(N\) and of the number \(1 + |d|\):
    \[B = |x_1| + |x_2| + \dots + |x_{\mathrm{N}-1}| + 1 + |d| .\]
    For \(N = 1\) there are no terms before \(N\), and \(B = 1 + |d|\).
  4. Let \(n < N\). Then \(|x_{\mathrm{n}}|\) is one of the terms of the sum \(B\), and all the other terms of the sum are not negative. So \(|x_{\mathrm{n}}| \le B\).
  5. Let \(n \ge N\). By step 2, \(|x_{\mathrm{n}}| < 1 + |d|\), and \(1 + |d| \le B\), because the other terms of the sum \(B\) are not negative. So \(|x_{\mathrm{n}}| < B\).
  6. Every \(n\) is smaller than \(N\) or at least \(N\), so by steps 4 and 5, \(|x_{\mathrm{n}}| \le B\) for every \(n\). The inequality \(|x_{\mathrm{n}}| \le B\) means \(-B \le x_{\mathrm{n}} \le B\), so \(B\) is an upper bound and \(-B\) a lower bound of the set of terms.

Source: LEBL1 Prop. 2.1.7 (PDF p. 54). The source takes as the bound the largest of the numbers \(|x_1|, \dots, |x_{\mathrm{N}-1}|, 1 + |d|\). The sum that we take is larger, but just as good as a bound, and it does not need a proof that finitely many numbers have a largest one.

For the strains of the pieces of the hanging bar the bound is simple. All terms are smaller than \(2aL\), because \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{n}} = 2aL - a/n\), and positive, because \(2L = 20\) m is more than \(1/n \le 1\) m. So \(|\varepsilon_{\mathrm{n}}| \le 2aL\) for every \(n\).
