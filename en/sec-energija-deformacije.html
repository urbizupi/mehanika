Chapter 3 Constitutive equations

3.6 Work and strain energy

In section 3.5 we computed how much work a machine does when it stretches a steel bar slowly. The force at the end of the bar grows from zero to 10 kN, the end of the bar moves by 6,496 mm, and the work is 32,48 J. Now we ask where this work goes. When we unload the bar slowly, it shortens back to its original length and pulls the machine along as it does so. Does it give back all the work it received? And what about a small piece of material inside a body, where not only the stretch in one direction changes, but all the strain components at once?

The section sets out to answer three questions. First, how much work it takes to deform a small piece of material, and where that work goes. Second, what it means that a material stores the work and gives all of it back on unloading, and what follows from this for the elastic constants. We will show that the material stiffness matrix of such a material is symmetric, which keeps the promise of section 3.4 that there are at most 21 different constants, and that the bulk modulus and the shear modulus of an isotropic material must be positive if the material is to be stable, that is, if it is not to deform by itself, without a force. Third, how the stored energy splits into a part that changes the volume of the piece and a part that changes its shape. At the end we meet an energy in which stress and strain swap roles, which we will need in chapter 4.

The physical model is this. We load the body slowly. This means two things: the body is in equilibrium at every instant, and all its parts move so slowly that their kinetic energy is negligible compared with the work of the forces. We call such loading quasi-static loading (kvazistatično obremenjevanje) (the assumption SADD §6.1, PDF p. 128, the term BOWER §1.1.4, PDF p. 34). In the first subsection we show with numbers what slowly means. The strains are small, all components at most \(0{,}001\) in absolute value as before, so we compute with the small strain tensor (definition 1.115) and with areas and volumes before loading. The temperature does not change, so there are no strains caused by temperature as in section 3.3. At first we assume nothing about the material. Then, step by step, we assume that it gives the work back, that it is linear elastic (definition 3.3) and that it is isotropic (definition 3.4). For every claim we say which of these assumptions it uses.

The route is this. First we look at where the work goes in the bar, and we convert it to a unit of volume. Then we compute the work that the surrounding material does on a small box when all its strain components change. From the requirement that the material gives back all the work along any loading path, we obtain an energy per unit volume that depends only on the strain, and we show that the stresses are its partial derivatives. From this it follows that an elastic constant does not change when the first and the second pair of indices are swapped, and which bounds the constants must satisfy.

3.6.1 Where the work goes

Recall the setting of section 3.5. The steel bar of chapter 2 has length \(L = 10\) m and a round cross section of diameter 10 mm, with area \(A = 78{,}54\ \text{mm}^2\). The left end is held by the fixed jaw of a tension testing machine, and the machine pulls the right end along the axis of the bar with a force that it raises slowly from zero to \(F_1 = 10\) kN. We neglect the weight of the bar, because it is only 0,6 % of the final force (section 3.5). The elongation by which the right end has moved up to a given instant is called \(e\). The stress stays below the yield stress of 220 MPa throughout, so the force is proportional to the elongation, \(F(e) = k\,e\). The number \(k = EA/L = 1{,}539 \cdot 10^6\) N/m says how much force each metre of elongation takes, and \(E = 196\) GPa is Young's modulus of steel (definition 3.1). At the final force the elongation is \(\Delta L = F_1/k = 6{,}496\) mm. The function \(\tfrac12 k\,e^2\) has the derivative \(k\,e\), so by the fundamental theorem of calculus 3.40, item 1, the work the force does on the way from \(e = 0\) to \(e = \Delta L\) is

\[\begin{aligned} \int_0^{\Delta L} k\,e\,\mathrm{d}e &= \tfrac12\,k\,\Delta L^2 = \tfrac12\,F_1\,\Delta L \\ &= \tfrac12 \cdot 10\,000\ \text{N} \cdot 6{,}496 \cdot 10^{-3}\ \text{m} = 32{,}48\ \text{J} . \end{aligned} \tag{3.34}\]

In the second step we used \(k\,\Delta L = F_1\). On the graph of force against elongation this work is the area of the triangle under the line (figure 3.52, left).

Why did we pull the bar slowly? When the end of the bar moves, the material of the bar moves too, and part of the work could go into motion. Let us estimate how much. Pulled slowly, the bar stretches evenly, each of its parts by the same fraction of its length. The point that was at distance \(x\) from the jaw before loading therefore moves by \(e\,x/L\) when the end moves by \(e\): by nothing at \(x = 0\), by the whole \(e\) at \(x = L\). Suppose the machine moves the end at a constant speed \(v\), so that the elongation grows by \(v\,\Delta t\) in the time \(\Delta t\). The displacement of the point at \(x\) then grows by \(v\,\Delta t\,x/L\), so its speed is \(v\,x/L\) (figure 3.51). The piece of the bar between \(x\) and \(x + \Delta x\) has the mass \(\rho A\,\Delta x\), where \(\rho = 7800\) kg/m³ is the density of steel, and the kinetic energy \(\tfrac12\,\rho A\,\Delta x\,(v x/L)^2\), half the mass times the square of the speed (EP §3.1.2, p. 102). We get the kinetic energy of the whole bar by adding up the contributions of the pieces and making the pieces shorter, that is, with an integral. The function \(x^3/3\) has the derivative \(x^2\), so by the fundamental theorem of calculus the kinetic energy of the bar is

\[\int_0^L \tfrac12\,\rho A\,\frac{v^2 x^2}{L^2}\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{\rho A\,v^2}{2L^2} \cdot \frac{L^3}{3} = \frac{m\,v^2}{6} ,\]

where \(m = \rho A L = 6{,}126\) kg is the mass of the bar. Where does the six come from? The half comes from the formula for kinetic energy. The third says that the points of the bar do not all move with the speed \(v\). The speed grows from zero at the jaw to \(v\) at the end, its square grows along a parabola from zero to \(v^2\), and the average of this parabola over the length of the bar is a third of its largest value, as the integral \(\int_0^L x^2\,\mathrm{d}x = L^3/3\) says.

If the machine stretched the bar in a single second, the speed of the end would be \(v = 6{,}496\) mm/s, and the kinetic energy of the bar at the end of the stretching would be

\[\frac{m\,v^2}{6} = \frac{6{,}126\ \text{kg} \cdot (6{,}496 \cdot 10^{-3}\ \text{m/s})^2}{6} = 4{,}3 \cdot 10^{-5}\ \text{J} ,\]

which is \(1{,}3 \cdot 10^{-6}\) of the work of 32,48 J, about a millionth. With slower stretching it is smaller still, because it grows with the square of the speed. Almost all the work of the machine therefore goes into the material of the bar. This is what slowly means: the kinetic energy is negligible compared with the work. The sources, too, assume in their derivations of energy that the stress grows so slowly that inertia does not count (SADD §6.1, PDF p. 128, EP §3.1.2, p. 100). BOWER also gives a criterion for when loading is slow enough (BOWER §1.1.4, PDF p. 34). For the estimate he takes the speed of a shear wave (strižno valovanje), that is, of a shear disturbance travelling through the solid. In steel this speed is

\[c = \sqrt{\frac{G}{\rho}} = \sqrt{\frac{75{,}38 \cdot 10^9\ \text{Pa}}{7800\ \text{kg/m}^3}} = 3109\ \text{m/s} ,\]

where \(G\) is the shear modulus of steel (definition 3.9). The stresses settle to the values they would have at rest in about ten times the time the disturbance needs to cross the body. If the load does not change noticeably in that time, a computation as if the body were in equilibrium is enough. For our bar this time is \(10\,L/c = 100\ \text{m}/(3109\ \text{m/s}) = 0{,}032\) s, more than thirty times shorter than one second. A disturbance that stretches or shortens the bar along its axis travels faster, at the speed \(\sqrt{E/\rho} = 5013\) m/s (GR §7.5.3, PDF p. 580), and crosses the bar ten times in only \(0{,}020\) s. The estimate with the speed of the shear wave is therefore on the safe side.

Where does the work go? We now unload the bar slowly. Below the yield stress it returns along the same line along which it was loaded (figure 2.11, grey arrow). During unloading its right end moves back towards the jaw, while the bar still pulls the machine's grip at that end towards itself with the force \(k\,e\). The force of the bar and the motion of the grip point the same way, so now the bar does work on the machine. Since the force at every elongation is the same as during loading, this work is again the area of the same triangle, 32,48 J. The bar has stored the work and given all of it back, like a spring. The energy that a body stores because it is deformed is called strain energy (deformacijska energija) (SADD §6.1, PDF p. 128, BOWER §3.2.7, PDF p. 102). We write it \(U\). For our bar \(U = 32{,}48\) J.

Above the yield stress it is different. If we stretch the bar up to the point \(B\) in figure 2.11 and then unload it, it returns along a line parallel to the initial one and stays permanently longer at zero force. The area under the loading curve is the work the machine did, the area under the unloading line the work the bar gave back (figure 3.52, right). The difference, the grey area between the curve and the line, is not given back. The first law of thermodynamics says where it goes: the work done by the stresses is either stored in the material as energy that the material can give back, or turned into heat, or both (BOWER §3.1.1, PDF p. 93). Some materials turn part of the work into heat even below the yield stress, and under repeated loading their loading and unloading curves form a loop. BOWER describes such materials with the viscoelastic model, which we mentioned in section 3.1.4 and in which the stress also depends on how fast the strain changes (BOWER §1.1.5, PDF p. 35). For metals below the yield stress we neglect this part, as is usual with the linear elastic model (BOWER §1.1.5, PDF p. 34).

How much energy does one cubic metre of steel in our bar store? All parts of the bar are stretched equally and equally stressed, so every cubic centimetre of the bar stores an equal share of the energy. The volume of the bar is \(V = A L = 78{,}540 \cdot 10^{-6}\ \text{m}^2 \cdot 10\ \text{m} = 7{,}8540 \cdot 10^{-4}\ \text{m}^3\), and the energy per unit volume is \(32{,}481\ \text{J}/(7{,}8540 \cdot 10^{-4}\ \text{m}^3) = 41\,356\ \text{J/m}^3\). Let us write it with the stress and the strain of the bar:

\[\frac{U}{A L} = \frac{\tfrac12\,F_1\,\Delta L}{A L} = \frac12 \cdot \frac{F_1}{A} \cdot \frac{\Delta L}{L} = \tfrac12\,\sigma\,\varepsilon ,\]

where \(\sigma = F_1/A = 127{,}324\) MPa is the stress in the bar (definition 2.6) and \(\varepsilon = \Delta L/L = \sigma/E\) its strain (definition 1.2). Indeed \(\tfrac12\,\sigma\,\varepsilon = \sigma^2/(2E) = (127{,}324 \cdot 10^6\ \text{Pa})^2/(2 \cdot 196 \cdot 10^9\ \text{Pa}) = 41\,356\ \text{J/m}^3\). The units agree, because \(1\ \text{Pa} = 1\ \text{N/m}^2 = 1\ \text{N}\,\text{m}/\text{m}^3 = 1\ \text{J/m}^3\). The half has the same origin as for the work: during loading the stress grew evenly from zero to \(\sigma\), so on average it was \(\sigma/2\).

The expression \(\tfrac12\,\sigma\,\varepsilon\) no longer contains the length and the cross-section area of the bar, only the stress and the strain of the material. The energy per unit volume is therefore a property of the state of the material, not of the bar. In the bar, however, only the stress along the axis was nonzero, and the relation between stress and strain was a straight line. What about a small piece of material in a body, where all the stress and strain components change, and a material for which the relation is not a straight line? To answer, we must compute how much work the forces on the faces of a small piece do.

3.6.2 The work of the stresses on a small box

Take any body that we load slowly, and a point \(P\) in its interior. At \(P\) we imagine a small box with edges \(\Delta x\), \(\Delta y\) and \(\Delta z\) parallel to the axes of a rectangular Cartesian coordinate system, and with volume \(\Delta V = \Delta x\,\Delta y\,\Delta z\) (figure 3.53). The material around the box acts on it with forces on its six faces. Let us follow a short stage of the loading, during which the displacement of every point \(Q\) of the box changes by a small vector \(\Delta\vek u(Q)\). How much work do the forces on the faces do?

We also need the rule for the work when the force does not necessarily point in the direction of the motion. When the point on which a constant force \(\vek F\) acts moves along a short segment \(\Delta\vek u\), the force does the work \(\vek F\cdot\Delta\vek u\), the scalar product of the force and the displacement (EP (3.1.1), p. 97). This is the length of the displacement times the component of the force in the direction of the displacement (definition 2.13). When the force points in the direction of the displacement, this is the school rule, force times distance. When the force is perpendicular to the displacement, the work is zero. When it points against the displacement, the work is negative. We will deal more carefully with work for displacements in any direction in chapter 4.

We simplify the computation with two assumptions, which hold approximately for a small box. First, the stress is the same at all points of the box and does not change noticeably during the short stage. Second, the change of displacement is a linear function of position: for every point \(Q\) of the box and for \(i = x, y, z\),

\[\begin{aligned} \Delta u_{\mathrm{i}}(Q) &= \Delta u_{\mathrm{i}}(P) + \Delta H_{\mathrm{ix}}\,(x_{\mathrm{Q}} - x_{\mathrm{P}}) \\ &\quad + \Delta H_{\mathrm{iy}}\,(y_{\mathrm{Q}} - y_{\mathrm{P}}) + \Delta H_{\mathrm{iz}}\,(z_{\mathrm{Q}} - z_{\mathrm{P}}) , \end{aligned} \tag{3.35}\]

where \(\Delta H_{\mathrm{ij}}\) is the change during the stage of the partial derivative \(\partial u_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}\) at the point \(P\), that is, the change of the entry of the displacement gradient in row \(i\) and column \(j\) (definition 1.109). Here \(x_{\mathrm{j}}\) stands for the coordinate \(x\), \(y\) or \(z\). By theorem 1.88 on the change of a field over a short step, the true change of displacement differs from this one by a remainder that is small compared with the size of the box. An exact computation also takes into account that the stress varies a little across the box, and the weight of the box. The weight does work when the box moves, but the difference of the forces on opposite faces, which balances the weight, does exactly as much work with the opposite sign (section 2.7). The other terms left out are smaller by a factor of the size of the box than the ones we will compute. The exact computation needs the integral over a body, which we will meet in chapter 4, and gives the same result (CM (5.4.7), PDF p. 237, SADD §6.1, PDF p. 131). A computation with a box like ours is also done in EP §3.2.1 (pp. 105-106).

First the two faces perpendicular to the \(x\) axis (figure 3.53).

  1. On the face with outward normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) take a small patch of area \(\Delta A\) around the point \(Q\). The force on it is \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\,\Delta A = (\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}})\,\Delta A\) (equation (2.23)). On the opposite face, with outward normal \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), an equal patch around the point \(Q'\) lies straight across. By lemma 2.21 on the traction on the opposite side of a cut, the force on it is \(-\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\,\Delta A\). The two points differ only in the coordinate \(x\), by \(x_{\mathrm{Q}} - x_{\mathrm{Q}'} = \Delta x\).
  2. During the stage the two forces do the works \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\,\Delta A\cdot\Delta\vek u(Q)\) and \(-\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\,\Delta A\cdot\Delta\vek u(Q')\). By lemma 2.12 on the properties of the scalar product, items 2 and 3, their sum is
    \[\Delta A\,\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\cdot\big(\Delta\vek u(Q) - \Delta\vek u(Q')\big) .\]
    Only the difference of the changes of displacement of the two points counts. If both points moved equally, the work would be zero, because the two forces are opposite.
  3. In equation (3.35), written for \(Q\) and for \(Q'\), all terms are equal except the one with the coordinate \(x\). Hence \(\Delta u_{\mathrm{i}}(Q) - \Delta u_{\mathrm{i}}(Q') = \Delta H_{\mathrm{ix}}\,\Delta x\) for \(i = x, y, z\), or in short
    \[\Delta\vek u(Q) - \Delta\vek u(Q') = \Delta x\,(\Delta H_{\mathrm{xx}},\ \Delta H_{\mathrm{yx}},\ \Delta H_{\mathrm{zx}}) .\]
  4. We compute the scalar product by components (equation (2.6)). The work of the pair of forces is
    \[\Delta A\,\Delta x\,\big(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\Delta H_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\Delta H_{\mathrm{yx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\,\Delta H_{\mathrm{zx}}\big) .\]
    It does not depend on where on the face the patch lies.
  5. We split the face into patches and add up the works of all pairs. The areas of the patches add up to the area of the face, \(\Delta y\,\Delta z\). Since \(\Delta y\,\Delta z\,\Delta x = \Delta V\), the work of the forces on the two faces perpendicular to the \(x\) axis is
    \[\Delta V\,\big(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\Delta H_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\Delta H_{\mathrm{yx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\,\Delta H_{\mathrm{zx}}\big) .\]
  6. We treat in the same way the faces perpendicular to the \(y\) axis, where \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}) = (\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}})\) and opposite points differ by \(\Delta y\) in the coordinate \(y\), and the faces perpendicular to the \(z\) axis. The work of the forces on all six faces is the sum of three such expressions:
    \[\begin{aligned} &\Delta V\,\big(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\Delta H_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\Delta H_{\mathrm{yx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\Delta H_{\mathrm{zx}} \\ &\qquad + \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\Delta H_{\mathrm{xy}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\Delta H_{\mathrm{yy}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}\Delta H_{\mathrm{zy}} \\ &\qquad + \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}\Delta H_{\mathrm{xz}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zy}}\Delta H_{\mathrm{yz}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\Delta H_{\mathrm{zz}}\big) . \end{aligned}\]
  7. We write the normal terms with strains. Since \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial x\) (definition 1.115), \(\Delta H_{\mathrm{xx}} = \Delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) is the change of the strain \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) during the stage. Likewise \(\Delta H_{\mathrm{yy}} = \Delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\) and \(\Delta H_{\mathrm{zz}} = \Delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\).
  8. We group the shear terms in pairs with swapped indices. By theorem 2.36 on the symmetry of stress, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\). By definition 1.115, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \tfrac12\,(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial y + \partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial x)\), so \(\Delta H_{\mathrm{xy}} + \Delta H_{\mathrm{yx}} = 2\,\Delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\). Together
    \[\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\Delta H_{\mathrm{yx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\,\Delta H_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,(\Delta H_{\mathrm{yx}} + \Delta H_{\mathrm{xy}}) = 2\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\Delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} .\]
    The same holds for the pairs with indices \(x\), \(z\) and \(y\), \(z\).
  9. The work of the forces on the faces of the box during the short stage is therefore
    \[\begin{aligned} &\Delta V\,\big(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\Delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\,\Delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\,\Delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} \\ &\qquad + 2\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}\,\Delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} + 2\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\,\Delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} + 2\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\Delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\big) = \Delta V \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\Delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} . \end{aligned} \tag{3.36}\]
    In the double sum the indices \(i\) and \(j\) run over \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\), so the sum has nine terms. Each shear pair appears in it twice, for example as \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\Delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\) and as \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\,\Delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yx}}\), which are equal. Hence the factor two on the left.

The work per unit volume during a short stage is therefore \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\Delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\). EP writes it the same way (p. 106). What has the computation shown?

First, a motion of the box as a whole does no work. In step 2 only the difference of the changes of displacement of opposite points counted, and the term \(\Delta u_{\mathrm{i}}(P)\) of equation (3.35) cancelled in step 3. The forces on opposite patches are opposite, and what one does in a common motion, the other takes away.

Second, a turn of the box does no work either. For a small turn about the \(z\) axis, \(\Delta H_{\mathrm{yx}} = -\Delta H_{\mathrm{xy}}\), which is the part of the gradient that describes the turn (definition 1.116). In step 8, however, \(\Delta H_{\mathrm{yx}}\) and \(\Delta H_{\mathrm{xy}}\) appear only in their sum, which is zero for a turn. Figure 3.54 shows why. In a counterclockwise turn the right face moves up relative to the left one, and the shear forces on them, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) up on the right and down on the left, do positive work. The top face, however, moves to the left relative to the bottom one, and the shear forces on them, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\) to the right at the top and to the left at the bottom, do negative work. The two works cancel exactly, because \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\). The stresses therefore do work only through a change of strain, not through a motion or a turn.

Third, the factor two in the shear terms is not a convention but a consequence of the fact that in shear both pairs of faces work (figure 3.54, left). The right face moves up relative to the left one by \(\Delta H_{\mathrm{yx}}\,\Delta x\), the top face moves to the right relative to the bottom one by \(\Delta H_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\Delta y\), and each pair contributes its own work. In shear, where \(\Delta H_{\mathrm{xy}} = \Delta H_{\mathrm{yx}} = \Delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\), the two contributions are equal.

Let us test equation (3.36) on the bar of the previous subsection. There only the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma\) is nonzero, so the work per unit volume during a short stage is \(\sigma\,\Delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), the stress times the change of strain. This is the same as the work of the force \(F = \sigma A\) over the motion of the end \(\Delta e = L\,\Delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), divided by the volume \(A L\).

During the whole loading the stresses change, so we must add up the works of the short stages. We describe the loading with a parameter \(t\), for example time, running from \(t_0\) to \(t_1\). The strain and stress components at \(P\) are then functions \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}(t)\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}(t)\). We split the interval \([t_0, t_1]\) into short stages. During the stage from \(t_{\mathrm{k}-1}\) to \(t_{\mathrm{k}}\) the work per unit volume is, by equation (3.36), approximately

\[\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}(t_{\mathrm{k}-1})\,\big(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}(t_{\mathrm{k}}) - \varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}(t_{\mathrm{k}-1})\big) .\]

By the mean value theorem 1.84 the change \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}(t_{\mathrm{k}}) - \varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}(t_{\mathrm{k}-1})\) equals \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}'(\xi_{\mathrm{ij},\mathrm{k}})\,(t_{\mathrm{k}} - t_{\mathrm{k}-1})\) for some number \(\xi_{\mathrm{ij},\mathrm{k}}\) between \(t_{\mathrm{k}-1}\) and \(t_{\mathrm{k}}\), which may be different for every component and every stage. The sum over the stages is therefore not exactly a Riemann sum of the function \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}(t)\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}'(t)\) (definition 3.32), which would take all parts of this function at the same point of the stage. It is close to one, though, and as the stages get shorter it approaches its integral. That it really does approach it would be shown with the uniform continuity of the stresses and of the derivatives of the strains as functions of the parameter (theorem 3.37), much as in the proof of theorem 3.38. We do not write this proof. EP writes the work per unit volume directly as an integral (EP (3.2.5), p. 103), while CM writes how fast this work grows, with the stress power (moč napetosti) (CM (5.4.11), PDF p. 238). We too define the work with an integral.

Definition 3.42 Work of the stresses per unit volume (delo napetosti na enoto prostornine)

Let \(P\) be a point of a body, and let the strain components \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) and the stress components \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\) at \(P\) change during a loading with a parameter \(t\) from a closed interval \([t_0, t_1]\). Let the functions \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}(t)\) be differentiable at every point of the interval \([t_0, t_1]\), at the endpoints with a one-sided derivative (definition 1.9), with derivatives continuous on \([t_0, t_1]\), and let the functions \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}(t)\) be continuous on \([t_0, t_1]\). The work of the stresses per unit volume (delo napetosti na enoto prostornine) at \(P\) during this loading is the number

\[\int_{\mathrm{t}_0}^{t_1} \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}(t)\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}'(t)\,\mathrm{d}t , \tag{3.37}\]

where the indices \(i\) and \(j\) run over \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\). If the loading consists of several successive stages, each with its own parameter, the work is the sum of the works of the stages.

Source: EP (3.2.4), (3.2.5) (p. 103) and (3.2.7) (p. 106), CM (5.4.11) (PDF p. 238), where the integrand \(\boldsymbol\sigma : \mathbf D\) is the stress power.

The integrand is a sum of nine terms, and each shear pair appears in it twice. Let us write it with the six different components. As in Voigt notation (definition 3.18), let \(\gamma_{\mathrm{yz}} = 2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}\), \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xz}} = 2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}\) and \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\) be the engineering shear strains. Then \(2\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}' = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}}'\), and likewise for the other two pairs, so

\[\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}' = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}' + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}' + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}' + \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}\,\gamma_{\mathrm{yz}}' + \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\,\gamma_{\mathrm{xz}}' + \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}}' . \tag{3.38}\]

The factor two has hidden itself in the engineering shear strain. This is why Voigt notation puts precisely the engineering shear strains into the column of strains: the integrand is the sum of the products of corresponding entries of the columns of definition 3.18

\[\{\sigma\} = (\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}},\ \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}},\ \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}},\ \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}},\ \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}},\ \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}) , \qquad \{\varepsilon\} = (\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}},\ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}},\ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}},\ \gamma_{\mathrm{yz}},\ \gamma_{\mathrm{xz}},\ \gamma_{\mathrm{xy}}) ,\]

which we write here in a row to save space. The entry of a column at place \(p\), where \(p\) is one of the numbers from \(1\) to \(6\), is written, as in the proof of lemma 3.22, \(\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{p}}\) and \(\{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{p}}\). Thus \(\{\varepsilon\}_1 = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\{\varepsilon\}_6 = \gamma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) and \(\{\sigma\}_6 = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\). The braces keep us from confusing these with the principal stresses \(\sigma_1\), \(\sigma_2\) and \(\sigma_3\). In this notation the integrand of equation (3.37) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{p}} \{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{p}}\,\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{p}}'\), where \(p\) runs from \(1\) to \(6\).

The simplest case is a uniaxial state, as in the bar, where only the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) is nonzero. The strains \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\) change too, in the steel bar both equal \(-\nu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), where \(\nu\) is Poisson's ratio (definition 3.2), but in the integrand (3.38) they are multiplied by the stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\), so they add nothing to the work. We then take the strain \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) itself as the parameter, running from \(0\) to the final value \(\varepsilon\). Its derivative with respect to the parameter is \(1\), so the work per unit volume is \(\int_0^{\varepsilon} \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), the area under the curve of stress against strain (EP, p. 104). In the steel bar \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), and the work is \(\int_0^{\varepsilon} E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \tfrac12 E\,\varepsilon^2 = \tfrac12\,\sigma\,\varepsilon\), which is 41 356 J/m³ as before.

3.6.3 Ideal elastic material

We have defined the work per unit volume for every loading that ends at a given strain. Does it depend only on the final strain, or also on the path by which we got there? Above the yield stress the material did not give the work back, so the work depended on what had happened to the material, not only on the final strain. Let us show that it can depend on the path even for a material whose stresses are linear functions of the strains, as in equation (3.21).

Let only \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) change, while the other four strain components stay zero. Then by equation (3.38) only the stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) do work. For a linear material they are

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = C_{11}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + C_{16}\,\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} , \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = C_{61}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + C_{66}\,\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} ,\]

where \(C_{11}\), \(C_{16}\), \(C_{61}\) and \(C_{66}\) are the entries of the material stiffness matrix in rows and columns \(1\) and \(6\) (definition 3.18). For steel \(C_{11} = \lambda + 2\mu = 263{,}8\) GPa, \(C_{66} = \mu = 75{,}38\) GPa and \(C_{16} = C_{61} = 0\), where \(\lambda\) and \(\mu\) are the Lamé constants of steel (definition 3.10). Imagine a material that differs from steel only in that \(C_{16} = 10\) GPa, while \(C_{61}\) stays zero. In such a material the shear \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) causes a normal stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), but the stretch \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) causes no shear stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\).

Let us go from zero strain to the strain with \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = a\) and \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} = b\), where \(a = b = 0{,}001\), along two paths (figure 3.55, left). On the first path we first increase \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) from \(0\) to \(a\) at \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\), then \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) from \(0\) to \(b\) at \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = a\). On the second path we first increase \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}}\), then \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\). On each leg of a path a single component changes, so we take it as the parameter \(t\), and its derivative with respect to the parameter is \(1\). We compute the integrals with the fundamental theorem of calculus and the function \(t^2/2\), whose derivative is \(t\). The constants \(C_{11}\) and \(C_{66}\) are rounded to four significant digits, so we round the works to four significant digits too.

  1. First path, first leg. The parameter is \(t = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) from \(0\) to \(a\), \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\), so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = C_{11}\,t\) and the work is \(\int_0^a C_{11}\,t\,\mathrm{d}t = \tfrac12 C_{11}\,a^2 = 131\,900\ \text{J/m}^3\).
  2. First path, second leg. The parameter is \(t = \gamma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) from \(0\) to \(b\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = a\), so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = C_{61}\,a + C_{66}\,t\) and the work is \(\int_0^b (C_{61}\,a + C_{66}\,t)\,\mathrm{d}t = C_{61}\,a\,b + \tfrac12 C_{66}\,b^2 = 0 + 37\,690\ \text{J/m}^3\).
  3. The work along the first path is the sum, \(131\,900 + 37\,690 = 169\,590\), rounded \(169\,600\ \text{J/m}^3\).
  4. Second path, first leg. The parameter is \(t = \gamma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) from \(0\) to \(b\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0\), so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = C_{66}\,t\) and the work is \(\tfrac12 C_{66}\,b^2 = 37\,690\ \text{J/m}^3\).
  5. Second path, second leg. The parameter is \(t = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) from \(0\) to \(a\), \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} = b\), so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = C_{11}\,t + C_{16}\,b\) and the work is \(\int_0^a (C_{11}\,t + C_{16}\,b)\,\mathrm{d}t = \tfrac12 C_{11}\,a^2 + C_{16}\,a\,b = 131\,900 + 10\,000 = 141\,900\ \text{J/m}^3\).
  6. The work along the second path is the sum, \(37\,690 + 141\,900 = 179\,590\), rounded \(179\,600\ \text{J/m}^3\).

Both paths end at the same strain, but the second costs \(10\,000\) J/m³ more. The difference is \((C_{16} - C_{61})\,a\,b\). Where does it come from? On the second path \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) grows when the shear is already \(b\), so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) contains the part \(C_{16}\,b\), which acts over the whole way of length \(a\) and contributes \(C_{16}\,a\,b\). On the first path \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) grows when the stretch is already \(a\), so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) contains the part \(C_{61}\,a\), which would contribute \(C_{61}\,a\,b\), but is zero for our material.

Now we load the material along the first path and unload it along the second, from the end back to the start. On the way back the stress at every strain has the same value as during loading along the same path, because it depends only on the strain, but the strain now changes in the opposite direction. Take the leg on which \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) returns from \(a\) to \(0\) at \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} = b\). We describe it with a parameter \(t\) from \(0\) to \(a\) and with \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = a - t\), whose derivative with respect to \(t\) is \(-1\). The function \(a\,t - t^2/2\) has the derivative \(a - t\), so the work is

\[\int_0^a \big(C_{11}\,(a - t) + C_{16}\,b\big)\cdot(-1)\,\mathrm{d}t = -\big(\tfrac12 C_{11}\,a^2 + C_{16}\,a\,b\big) ,\]

the opposite of the work on the same leg during loading. The same holds on the other leg. Unloading along the second path, the material therefore gives back about \(179\,600\) J/m³, while loading along the first path it received only about \(169\,600\) J/m³. After the round trip the material is in the same state as at the start, but it has given its surroundings \(10\,000\) J/m³ more than it received. A piece with a volume of one cubic decimetre, \(10^{-3}\) m³, would deliver 10 J of work in every round trip without anything being used up anywhere.

No such material exists. The second law of thermodynamics requires that in every cycle of deformation that ends at the same strain and the same internal energy as it started, the total work done on the material is positive or zero, when the cycle takes place at constant temperature or without heat exchange with the surroundings (BOWER §3.1.1, PDF p. 93). The internal energy (notranja energija) is the energy that a substance keeps within itself, in the small motions of its molecules and in its deformation (CM §5.4.2, PDF p. 236). The round trip in the opposite direction, loading along the second path and unloading along the first, gives the work \(+10\,000\) J/m³ and does not contradict this law, but the round trip in our direction does. For a material whose stress depends only on the current strain, the round trip in the opposite direction has the opposite work: on every leg the stress at every strain is the same, but the changes of strain have the opposite sign, as we computed on the leg above. The second law requires the work of both round trips to be positive or zero, so the work of both is zero. Any two paths from zero strain to the same strain, the first out and the second back, form such a round trip, so the work along both paths is the same. The general proof needs entropy (entropija), a thermodynamic quantity that we will not use in this book, so we do not develop it (CM §6.1.4, PDF pp. 253-254). We take the property we have found as a definition.

For the definition we still need an agreement on which strains are small. In what follows, a small strain is a strain for which each of the six components \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\), \(\gamma_{\mathrm{yz}}\), \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xz}}\) and \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) is smaller in absolute value than a chosen bound, for example \(0{,}0011\). With this bound a tension test of steel stays below the strain at the yield stress, \(220\ \text{MPa}/196\ \text{GPa} = 0{,}00112\), and the strains of size \(0{,}001\) that we compute with in this section are small. A function of the six strain components is continuous if it satisfies definition 1.67, except that the distance is measured between two columns of six numbers instead of between two points with three coordinates. The distance between two columns is the square root of the sum of the squares of the differences of corresponding entries (LEBL1 Example 7.1.5, PDF p. 258, and Def. 7.5.1, PDF p. 288).

Definition 3.43 Ideal elastic material (idealno elastičen material)

A material is ideal elastic (idealno elastičen) at a point \(P\) if two things hold.

  1. Each stress component at \(P\) is a function of the six strain components at \(P\), continuous at all small strains, that is, strains whose components are all smaller in absolute value than a chosen bound. At zero strain all stress components are zero.
  2. The work of the stresses per unit volume (definition 3.42) in every loading that meets the conditions of that definition, starts at zero strain and runs through small strains depends only on the strain at which the loading ends, not on the path along which the strain changed.

Source: BOWER §3.2.7 (PDF p. 102), where the work done to deform the material depends only on the final strain, and §3.1.1 (PDF p. 93), SADD §6.1 (PDF pp. 128 and 130-131). CM calls the first item Cauchy elasticity (CM §6.2.1, PDF p. 255).

The first item says that the material has no memory: the stress at a given strain is always the same, no matter how the material got there. The second item is the requirement we found with the cycle. A material that does not satisfy it, like our imagined material with \(C_{16} \ne C_{61}\), could deliver work from nothing in a cycle. Since for an ideal elastic material the work depends only on the final strain, it is a function of this strain.

Definition 3.44 Strain energy density (gostota deformacijske energije)

Let the material at the point \(P\) be ideal elastic. The strain energy density (gostota deformacijske energije) \(w\) at \(P\) at a small strain \(\{\varepsilon\}\) is the work of the stresses per unit volume in any loading that meets the conditions of definition 3.42, starts at zero strain, runs through small strains and ends at the strain \(\{\varepsilon\}\):

\[w(\{\varepsilon\}) = \int_{\mathrm{t}_0}^{t_1} \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}(t)\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}'(t)\,\mathrm{d}t . \tag{3.39}\]

Source: EP (3.2.7) (p. 106), SADD (6.1.3), (6.1.7) (PDF pp. 129 and 131), BOWER §3.2.7 (PDF p. 102).

The strain energy density is a number that depends on the six strain components, so a function of six variables, and its unit is J/m³, which is the same as Pa. At zero strain \(w = 0\), because a loading that does not leave zero strain does no work: the derivatives of the strains are zero, and so is the integral. The name says what the number means: the material received it as work during loading and keeps it until it gives it back on unloading. Thermodynamics also tells which energy this is. In loading without heat exchange \(w\) is the increase of the internal energy per unit volume, in loading at constant temperature the increase of the free energy (prosta energija), which is the internal energy reduced by a part tied to entropy (CM (5.4.11), PDF p. 238, CM (5.4.26), PDF p. 240, CM §6.2.2, PDF pp. 255-256). For small strains EP assumes constant temperature and calls \(w\) simply the stored elastic energy per unit volume (EP §3.2.1, p. 102). For the computations in this book we do not need this distinction, because we always compute \(w\) from the stresses and strains.

The bar of subsection 3.6.1 is made of steel, which is ideal elastic below the yield stress, and for it \(w = \tfrac12 E\,\varepsilon^2 = \tfrac12\,\sigma\,\varepsilon\). How much energy is that? Take steel at a strain of \(\varepsilon = 0{,}001\) in a tension test. The stress is then \(\sigma = E\,\varepsilon = 196\) MPa, still below the yield stress, and

\[w = \tfrac12 E\,\varepsilon^2 = \tfrac12 \cdot 196 \cdot 10^9\ \text{Pa} \cdot (0{,}001)^2 = 98\,000\ \text{J/m}^3 .\]

One cubic decimetre of such steel, \(10^{-3}\) m³, stores 98 J. That is the work it takes to lift a kilogram by about 10 m, since \(1\ \text{kg} \cdot 9{,}81\ \text{m/s}^2 \cdot 10\ \text{m} = 98{,}1\) J. The density grows with the square of the strain, because the stress and the strain grow together and \(w\) is half their product. For a material that stayed linear, twice the strain would therefore give four times the density. Steel at a strain of \(0{,}002\), however, would already be above the yield stress, where the formula \(\tfrac12 E\,\varepsilon^2\) no longer holds.

3.6.4 Stresses from the energy

In the bar of section 3.5 the force told how fast the work grows with the elongation: the work up to the elongation \(e\) is \(\tfrac12 k\,e^2\), and its derivative with respect to \(e\) is the force \(k\,e\). For an ideal elastic material it is the same, except that there are six variables. A material whose stresses are in this way derivatives of some function of the strain has a special name.

From history

In 1837 Green wrote a paper on the reflection and refraction of light at the surface between two media (LOVE, PDF p. 28). For the calculation he had to know how a vibration travels through the ether, the substance then believed to carry light (TIMO, PDF p. 190). He did not know how the particles of the ether act on each other, and he did not want to adopt molecular assumptions, because nature may behave quite differently (TIMO, PDF p. 191). He took a general principle instead: however the parts of a system act on each other, the sum of all internal forces multiplied by the small displacements is, for any part of the mass, the change of a single function (TIMO, PDF p. 191). This is the requirement that energy is conserved, since otherwise the work of the internal forces would depend on the path and a material, returned to the same state, could give out work. He expanded the function in the strain components. The term of first degree drops out, because an undeformed body must have the smallest energy, and the terms of third and higher degree are negligible for small strains (LOVE, PDF p. 28-29). What remains is a sum of terms, each of which is a product of two strain components and a number (TIMO, PDF p. 191). In general there are 21 such terms, six squares and 15 products of two different components, and for a body that is the same in all directions only two numbers remain (TIMO, PDF p. 191). William Thomson, later Lord Kelvin, justified the existence of such a function in 1855 by the first and second laws of thermodynamics: when a solid is deformed without a change of temperature, the stress components are derivatives of a function of the strain components (LOVE, PDF p. 29).

Let us return to a material whose stresses are derivatives of one function of the strain components, as for a bar the force is the derivative of work with respect to the elongation. The following definition describes it exactly.

Definition 3.45 Green-elastic material (Green-elastičen material)

A material is Green-elastic (Green-elastičen) or hyperelastic (hiperelastičen) at a point \(P\) if there is a function \(w\) of the six strain components \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\), \(\gamma_{\mathrm{yz}}\), \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xz}}\) and \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}}\), defined at all small strains, with the value zero at zero strain, whose partial derivatives are the stress components at \(P\):

\[\begin{aligned} \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} &= \frac{\partial w}{\partial\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}} , & \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} &= \frac{\partial w}{\partial\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}} , & \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} &= \frac{\partial w}{\partial\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}} , \\ \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} &= \frac{\partial w}{\partial\gamma_{\mathrm{yz}}} , & \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} &= \frac{\partial w}{\partial\gamma_{\mathrm{xz}}} , & \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} &= \frac{\partial w}{\partial\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}}} . \end{aligned} \tag{3.40}\]

Source: CM §6.2.2, (6.2.15) (PDF pp. 255 and 257), SADD (6.1.12) (PDF p. 132), EP (3.2.6) (p. 103), BOWER (PDF p. 104).

The partial derivative of a function of six variables is defined in the same way as for three variables (definition 1.25): we change one variable, hold the other five fixed, and take the limit of the difference quotient (LEBL2 Def. 8.3.8, PDF p. 39, for functions of \(n\) variables). Why do we differentiate with respect to \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) and not \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\)? When the shear strain \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\) grows by \(\Delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yx}}\) grows by the same amount, because it is the same number. By equation (3.36) the work per unit volume grows by \(2\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\Delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\Delta\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}}\). The energy density therefore grows at the rate \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) per unit change of \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}}\), or at the rate \(2\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) per unit change of \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\). The sources write \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}} = \partial w/\partial\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) for all nine components (CM (6.2.15), PDF p. 257, SADD (6.1.12), PDF p. 132). They treat \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yx}}\) as two variables and write \(w\) as a sum over all nine components, symmetric in \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yx}}\) (CM (6.3.1), PDF p. 258, SADD (6.1.9), PDF p. 131). If we change only \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\) and hold \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yx}}\) fixed, such a sum changes by half the work \(2\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\Delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\), and the derivative is again \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\). Both ways give the same stresses. We use six variables with engineering shear strains to stay clear of this trap.

Now let us show that every ideal elastic material is Green-elastic, and that the function \(w\) is precisely the strain energy density. The idea is the same as for the bar. The energy density at a strain that differs from \(\{\varepsilon\}\) in only one component is the density at a slightly smaller value of that component plus the integral of a single stress. By item 2 of the fundamental theorem of calculus, the derivative of such an integral with respect to its upper limit is the integrand itself.

Lemma 3.46 Stress as the derivative of the energy density (napetost kot odvod gostote energije)

Let the material at the point \(P\) be ideal elastic, and let \(w\) be its strain energy density. Then at every small strain \(w\) has partial derivatives with respect to all six strain components and satisfies equations (3.40), where the left sides are the stress components at this strain. The material is therefore Green-elastic.

Proof

We must show that the derivative of \(w\) with respect to each strain component is the corresponding stress component. We write the proof for the component \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), for the others it is the same.

  1. Let \(\{\varepsilon\}\) be a small strain. All its components are smaller in absolute value than the chosen bound, so there is a number \(r_0 > 0\) such that \(|\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}| + r_0\) is also smaller than the bound. For a number \(s\) with \(-r_0 \le s \le r_0\) let \(\{\varepsilon\}(s)\) be the strain that has the component \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + s\) and the other components equal to those of \(\{\varepsilon\}\). All these strains are small, and \(\{\varepsilon\}(0) = \{\varepsilon\}\).
  2. Let \(-r_0 < r < r_0\). We reach the strain \(\{\varepsilon\}(r)\) with a loading in two stages (figure 3.55, right). In the first stage we go from zero strain to \(\{\varepsilon\}(-r_0)\) along any path through small strains, for example straight, \(t\,\{\varepsilon\}(-r_0)\) for \(t\) from \(0\) to \(1\). In the second stage we go through the strains \(\{\varepsilon\}(s)\) as \(s\) runs from \(-r_0\) to \(r\). The path must meet the conditions of the definition of the work of the stresses 3.42.
  3. In this step and the next two we show that the straight first stage and the second stage meet these conditions. On both, the strain at the parameter \(t\) is \(\{\alpha\} + t\,\{\beta\}\), where \(\{\alpha\}\) and \(\{\beta\}\) are fixed columns of six numbers. In the first stage \(\{\alpha\}\) has all components zero and \(\{\beta\}\) is \(\{\varepsilon\}(-r_0)\). In the second stage, where we call the parameter \(s\), \(\{\alpha\} = \{\varepsilon\}\), and the column \(\{\beta\}\) has the component \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) equal to \(1\) and the others zero. Each strain component is therefore a fixed number plus a fixed number times the parameter, and it is differentiable with a constant, hence continuous, derivative. All these strains are small. In the first stage no component is larger in absolute value than the corresponding component of the small strain \(\{\varepsilon\}(-r_0)\), and in the second stage all the strains \(\{\varepsilon\}(s)\) with \(-r_0 \le s \le r_0\) are small by step 1.
  4. The distance between the strains at the parameters \(t\) and \(t'\) of the same stage is \(|t - t'|\,d\), where \(d\) is the distance of the column \(\{\beta\}\) from zero strain. Indeed, corresponding components differ by \(t - t'\) times the corresponding component of \(\{\beta\}\). The sum of the squares of the differences is therefore \((t - t')^2\) times the sum of the squares of the components of \(\{\beta\}\), which is \(d^2\), and its square root is \(|t - t'|\,d\).
  5. Each stress component is a continuous function of the parameter in both stages. Choose \(t\) and \(\eta > 0\). By item 1 of the definition of an ideal elastic material 3.43 the stress component is continuous at the small strain \(\{\alpha\} + t\,\{\beta\}\), so there is \(\delta_1 > 0\) such that its value at every small strain whose distance from \(\{\alpha\} + t\,\{\beta\}\) is less than \(\delta_1\) differs from its value at \(\{\alpha\} + t\,\{\beta\}\) by less than \(\eta\). We take \(\delta = \delta_1/(d + 1)\). For a parameter \(t'\) of the same stage with \(|t' - t| < \delta\) the distance is, by step 4, \(|t' - t|\,d \le \delta\,d < \delta_1\), so the values of the stress at \(t'\) and at \(t\) differ by less than \(\eta\). In the second stage this holds for all \(s\) with \(-r_0 \le s \le r_0\).
  6. The previous three steps show that the path meets these conditions. By item 2 of the definition of an ideal elastic material 3.43 and by the definition of the strain energy density 3.44, the work along this path therefore equals \(w(\{\varepsilon\}(r))\), and the work of the first stage equals \(w(\{\varepsilon\}(-r_0))\). By the definition of the work of the stresses 3.42, the work along the whole path is the sum of the works of the two stages.
  7. In the second stage the parameter is \(s\). The component \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) is \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + s\) and has the derivative \(1\), the other components are constant and have the derivative \(0\). By equation (3.38) only \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) is left of the integrand, the stress at the strain \(\{\varepsilon\}(s)\), which we call \(f(s)\). The work of the second stage is therefore \(\int_{-\mathrm{r}_0}^{r} f(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\), and by step 6
    \[w(\{\varepsilon\}(r)) = w(\{\varepsilon\}(-r_0)) + \int_{-\mathrm{r}_0}^{r} f(s)\,\mathrm{d}s .\]
  8. By step 5 the function \(f\) is continuous on \([-r_0, r_0]\). By the fundamental theorem of calculus 3.40, item 2, on the interval \([-r_0, r_0]\), the function \(r \mapsto \int_{-\mathrm{r}_0}^{r} f(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\) is therefore differentiable at the interior point \(r = 0\) with the derivative \(f(0)\). The first term on the right in step 7 does not depend on \(r\) and has the derivative zero (lemma 1.81). The function \(r \mapsto w(\{\varepsilon\}(r))\) therefore has the derivative \(f(0)\) at \(r = 0\), the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) at the strain \(\{\varepsilon\}\).
  9. The function \(r \mapsto w(\{\varepsilon\}(r))\) is \(w\) in which only the component \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) changes while the others are held fixed. Its derivative at \(r = 0\) is therefore the partial derivative \(\partial w/\partial\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) at the strain \(\{\varepsilon\}\). Hence \(\partial w/\partial\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\).
  10. For another component we repeat steps 1 to 9 with that component instead of \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\). For a shear component, for example \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}}\), what is left of the integrand (3.38) is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}}'\) with the derivative \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}}' = 1\), so we get \(\partial w/\partial\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\).

Source: SADD (6.1.12) (PDF p. 132) states the claim and leaves the proof to an exercise, CM §6.2.2 (PDF pp. 255-256) derives it from thermodynamics. The proof with a path in two stages is not from the sources.

The proof has shown why the stress is the derivative of the energy. When we increase the component \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) by a small \(r\), the material receives additional work of about \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,r\) per unit volume. In the bar this meant that the force tells how much work the next tenth of a millimetre of elongation adds. The converse holds too, provided the stresses, that is, the partial derivatives of \(w\), are continuous functions of the strain and zero at zero strain: such a Green-elastic material is ideal elastic, since the work along any path is the difference of the values of \(w\) at its ends. Item 1 of the definition of an ideal elastic material 3.43 asks for both conditions, while the definition of a Green-elastic material 3.45 does not contain them. EP calls such stresses conservative (EP §3.2.1, p. 103). The proof would need a rule for the derivative of a function of several variables along a path, which the book does not have yet, so we do not write it.

Now we can keep the promise of section 3.4. Let the material be linear elastic, with stresses that are linear functions of the strains, \(\{\sigma\} = \mat C\,\{\varepsilon\}\), where \(\mat C\) is the material stiffness matrix with entries \(C_{\mathrm{pq}}\), or entry by entry

\[\{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{p}} = \sum_{\mathrm{q}} C_{\mathrm{pq}}\,\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{q}} , \qquad p = 1, \dots, 6 ,\]

where \(q\) runs from \(1\) to \(6\). If such a material is ideal elastic, then by lemma 3.46 on the stress as the derivative of the energy density \(\{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{p}} = \partial w/\partial\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{p}}\). The derivative of this stress with respect to the component \(\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{q}}\) is \(C_{\mathrm{pq}}\). The derivative of the stress \(\{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{q}}\) with respect to the component \(\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{p}}\) is \(C_{\mathrm{qp}}\). Both are mixed derivatives of the same function \(w\), once first with respect to \(\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{p}}\) and then \(\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{q}}\), once in the opposite order, and by theorem 1.125 on the symmetry of mixed partial derivatives they are equal.

Theorem 3.47 Symmetry of the elastic constants (simetrija elastičnih konstant)

Let the material at the point \(P\) be ideal elastic and linear elastic, so that at every small strain \(\{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{p}} = \sum_{\mathrm{q}} C_{\mathrm{pq}}\,\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{q}}\) for \(p = 1, \dots, 6\), with the entries \(C_{\mathrm{pq}}\) of the material stiffness matrix \(\mat C\). Then:

  1. the matrix \(\mat C\) is symmetric, \(C_{\mathrm{pq}} = C_{\mathrm{qp}}\) for all \(p\) and \(q\) from \(1\) to \(6\), so the elastic constants satisfy \(C_{\mathrm{ijkl}} = C_{\mathrm{klij}}\) for all indices \(i\), \(j\), \(k\) and \(l\) running over \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\),
  2. the strain energy density at the strain \(\{\varepsilon\}\) is
    \[w = \frac12 \sum_{\mathrm{p}} \sum_{\mathrm{q}} C_{\mathrm{pq}}\,\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{p}}\,\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{q}} = \frac12 \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} . \tag{3.41}\]
Proof

We must show the symmetry of the matrix and the formula for \(w\). The first follows from the symmetry of mixed partial derivatives, the second from the work along a straight path.

1. Symmetry.

  1. By lemma 3.46 on the stress as the derivative of the energy density, at every small strain \(\partial w/\partial\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{p}} = \{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{p}} = \sum_{\mathrm{r}} C_{\mathrm{pr}}\,\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{r}}\), where \(r\) runs from \(1\) to \(6\).
  2. Let \(p \ne q\). If we increase the component \(\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{q}}\) by \(h\) and hold the others fixed, \(\{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{p}}\) increases by exactly \(C_{\mathrm{pq}}\,h\). The difference quotient is therefore \(C_{\mathrm{pq}}\) for every \(h \ne 0\), and its limit is \(C_{\mathrm{pq}}\). The mixed derivative of \(w\), first with respect to \(\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{p}}\) and then \(\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{q}}\), therefore exists at every small strain and equals the constant \(C_{\mathrm{pq}}\). Likewise the mixed derivative in the opposite order equals \(C_{\mathrm{qp}}\).
  3. Choose a small strain and hold the four components other than \(\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{p}}\) and \(\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{q}}\) at its values. Let \(f(x, y, z)\) be the value of \(w\) at the strain with \(\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{p}} = x\) and \(\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{q}} = y\). This is a function of three variables that does not depend on \(z\). It is defined around the point \((\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{p}}, \{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{q}}, 0)\), its partial derivatives with respect to \(x\) and \(y\) are the stresses \(\{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{p}}\) and \(\{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{q}}\), and its mixed derivatives are the constants \(C_{\mathrm{pq}}\) and \(C_{\mathrm{qp}}\), hence continuous.
  4. By theorem 1.125 on the symmetry of mixed partial derivatives, the two mixed derivatives of \(f\) are equal, so \(C_{\mathrm{pq}} = C_{\mathrm{qp}}\). For \(p = q\) the equality only says that a number equals itself.
  5. The entry \(C_{\mathrm{pq}}\) is the elastic constant \(C_{\mathrm{ijkl}}\), where \(ij\) is the pair of indices of place \(p\) and \(kl\) the pair of indices of place \(q\) (definition 3.18). The entry \(C_{\mathrm{qp}}\) is therefore \(C_{\mathrm{klij}}\), and step 4 gives \(C_{\mathrm{ijkl}} = C_{\mathrm{klij}}\). The elastic constants do not change when the indices within the first or the second pair are swapped (lemma 3.17 on swapping the indices within a pair), so the equality holds for all orders of the indices within the pairs.

2. Energy density.

  1. By definition 3.44 we may compute \(w\) along any path from zero strain to \(\{\varepsilon\}\). Take the straight path \(t\,\{\varepsilon\}\) as \(t\) runs from \(0\) to \(1\). All its strains are small, because \(|t\,\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{p}}| \le |\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{p}}|\).
  2. On this path the strain component \(p\) is \(t\,\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{p}}\), with the derivative \(\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{p}}\) with respect to \(t\), and the stress is \(\{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{p}}(t) = \sum_{\mathrm{q}} C_{\mathrm{pq}}\,t\,\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{q}}\). Both are a fixed number times \(t\), so the path meets the conditions of the definition of the work of the stresses 3.42: the strain components have constant derivatives, and the stresses are continuous.
  3. By equation (3.38) the integrand equals \(\sum_{\mathrm{p}} \sum_{\mathrm{q}} C_{\mathrm{pq}}\,\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{q}}\,\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{p}}\,t\), a constant number times \(t\). By the linearity of the integral (lemma 3.39, item 1) and the fundamental theorem of calculus 3.40 with the function \(t^2/2\),
    \[w = \sum_{\mathrm{p}} \sum_{\mathrm{q}} C_{\mathrm{pq}}\,\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{p}}\,\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{q}} \int_0^1 t\,\mathrm{d}t = \frac12 \sum_{\mathrm{p}} \sum_{\mathrm{q}} C_{\mathrm{pq}}\,\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{p}}\,\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{q}} .\]
  4. The inner sum \(\sum_{\mathrm{q}} C_{\mathrm{pq}}\,\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{q}}\) is \(\{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{p}}\), so \(w = \tfrac12 \sum_{\mathrm{p}} \{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{p}}\,\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{p}}\). By equation (3.38), applied to products instead of derivatives, this is \(\tfrac12 \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\).

Source: CM (6.3.1)-(6.3.3), (6.3.5) (PDF p. 258), SADD (6.1.13), (6.1.14) (PDF p. 132) and (6.1.7) (PDF p. 131), BOWER (PDF p. 104), EP (3.2.11) (p. 107). Replacing the function of six variables by a function of three variables in step 3 is not from the sources. LEBL2 Prop. 8.6.2 (PDF p. 60) swaps mixed derivatives for functions of any number of variables.

The stiffness matrix of an ideal elastic material is therefore symmetric, and only the entries on and above the diagonal are different, \(6 + 5 + 4 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 21\). A material whose inner structure has no symmetry at all needs all 21 constants, as we announced in section 3.4. The symmetry does not come from the symmetry of stress or strain, but from the requirement that the material does not deliver work in a cycle. Our imagined material with \(C_{16} = 10\) GPa and \(C_{61} = 0\) does not satisfy it, so no such material exists. The works along the two paths of figure 3.55 are \(\tfrac12 C_{11}\,a^2 + \tfrac12 C_{66}\,b^2 + C_{61}\,a\,b\) and \(\tfrac12 C_{11}\,a^2 + \tfrac12 C_{66}\,b^2 + C_{16}\,a\,b\), and with a symmetric matrix they are equal. The proof tells us a little more. Steps 3 and 4 did not need linearity, only continuous mixed derivatives. For a nonlinear ideal elastic material, too, the derivative of the stress \(\{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{p}}\) with respect to the component \(\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{q}}\) therefore equals the derivative of the stress \(\{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{q}}\) with respect to the component \(\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{p}}\), whenever these derivatives are continuous (SADD (6.1.13), PDF p. 132).

Formula (3.41) generalizes \(\tfrac12\,\sigma\,\varepsilon\) of the bar. The half has the same origin: along the straight path all stresses grow evenly from zero to their final values, so each is on average half its final value. The formula holds only for linear materials. In a nonlinear material the stresses do not grow evenly along the straight path, and \(w\) is not half the sum of the products, as we will see in figure 3.58.

3.6.5 Change of volume and change of shape

Let us apply formula (3.41) to an isotropic material. By Hooke's law (3.13) its stresses are

\[\begin{aligned} \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} &= \lambda\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} + 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} , & \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} &= \lambda\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} + 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} , & \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} &= \lambda\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} + 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} , \\ \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} &= 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} , & \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} &= 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} , & \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} &= 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} , \end{aligned}\]

where \(\lambda\) and \(\mu\) are the Lamé constants, \(\mu\) is the shear modulus \(G\), and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\) is the volumetric strain, which for small strains gives the relative change of volume of a small piece (section 1.17).

  1. By equation (3.41), \(w = \tfrac12\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} + 2\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} + 2\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} + 2\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}})\), where each shear pair is written once with a factor two.
  2. We insert the stresses. The terms with \(\lambda\) give \(\lambda\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}) = \lambda\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}^2\). The terms with \(\mu\) give \(2\mu\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}^2 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}^2 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}^2) + 2 \cdot 2\mu\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}^2 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}^2 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}^2)\).
  3. We multiply by \(\tfrac12\):
    \[\begin{aligned} w &= \tfrac12\,\lambda\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}^2 + \mu\,\big(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}^2 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}^2 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}^2 + 2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}^2 + 2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}^2 + 2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}^2\big) \\ &= \tfrac12\,\lambda\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}^2 + \mu \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}^2 . \end{aligned} \tag{3.42}\]

SADD (6.1.9) (PDF p. 131) and EP (3.2.16) (p. 107) write it this way, the latter also with a term for temperature, which we do not have. Let us check lemma 3.46 on the stress as the derivative of the energy density. Since \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}\) is a sum in which \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) appears with the factor one, the partial derivative of \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}^2\) with respect to \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) is \(2\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}\), so \(\partial w/\partial\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \lambda\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} + 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\). We write the shear term with the engineering shear strain, \(2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}^2 = \tfrac12\,\mu\,\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}}^2\), and its derivative with respect to \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) is \(\mu\,\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\). Hooke's law for an isotropic material therefore has an energy density, as it must.

Formula (3.42) can be written differently, so that it shows how much energy goes into the change of volume and how much into the change of shape. We split every strain into a part that changes only the volume and a part that changes only the shape (figure 3.56). The first part stretches the piece equally in all directions, by a third of the volumetric strain. The second part is the deviatoric strain of definition 2.79, which in section 3.2.8 we connected with the stress deviator, with the components

\[\begin{aligned} e_{\mathrm{xx}} &= \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} - \tfrac13\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} , & e_{\mathrm{yy}} &= \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} - \tfrac13\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} , & e_{\mathrm{zz}} &= \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} - \tfrac13\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} , \\ e_{\mathrm{xy}} &= \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} , & e_{\mathrm{xz}} &= \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} , & e_{\mathrm{yz}} &= \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} . \end{aligned}\]

Its volumetric strain is \(e_{\mathrm{xx}} + e_{\mathrm{yy}} + e_{\mathrm{zz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} - \varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} = 0\), so it does not change the volume.

  1. The sum of squares in equation (3.42) has a normal part \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}^2 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}^2 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}^2\) and a shear part. The shear part is the same for the components \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) and for the components \(e_{\mathrm{ij}}\), because their shear components are equal.
  2. In the normal part we insert \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = e_{\mathrm{xx}} + \tfrac13\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}\) and expand the square: \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}^2 = e_{\mathrm{xx}}^2 + \tfrac23\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}\,e_{\mathrm{xx}} + \tfrac19\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}^2\). Likewise for \(y\) and \(z\).
  3. We add up. The middle terms give \(\tfrac23\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}\,(e_{\mathrm{xx}} + e_{\mathrm{yy}} + e_{\mathrm{zz}}) = 0\), because the deviatoric part does not change the volume. The last terms give \(3 \cdot \tfrac19\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}^2 = \tfrac13\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}^2\). Hence \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}^2 = \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} e_{\mathrm{ij}}^2 + \tfrac13\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}^2\).
  4. We insert this into equation (3.42): \(w = \tfrac12\,\lambda\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}^2 + \tfrac13\,\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}^2 + \mu \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} e_{\mathrm{ij}}^2 = \tfrac12\,(\lambda + \tfrac23\,\mu)\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}^2 + \mu \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} e_{\mathrm{ij}}^2\).
  5. The number \(\lambda + \tfrac23\,\mu\) is the bulk modulus \(K = E/\big(3\,(1 - 2\nu)\big)\) (definition 3.7). Indeed, by definition 3.10, \(\lambda = E\nu/\big((1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)\big)\) and \(\mu = E/\big(2\,(1 + \nu)\big)\), so
    \[\lambda + \tfrac23\,\mu = \frac{3E\nu + E\,(1 - 2\nu)}{3\,(1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)} = \frac{E\,(1 + \nu)}{3\,(1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)} = \frac{E}{3\,(1 - 2\nu)} = K .\]
  6. Hence
    \[w = \tfrac12\,K\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}^2 + G \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} e_{\mathrm{ij}}^2 . \tag{3.43}\]

The first term is the volumetric part of the energy density (prostorninski del), the second the distortional part (del zaradi spremembe oblike) (SADD (6.1.15)-(6.1.17), PDF p. 132). A strain that changes only the volume has all components \(e_{\mathrm{ij}}\) equal to zero and stores only the first part, which the bulk modulus governs. A strain that changes only the shape has \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} = 0\) and stores only the second part, which the shear modulus governs. There is no term that contains both parts at once. In an isotropic material the change of volume and the change of shape are independent in energy, like two springs that we stretch each on its own. This agrees with the fact that in an isotropic material an even pressure changes only the volume and the deviatoric part of stress only the shape (SADD, PDF p. 132).

How much energy goes into the change of volume in a tension test? Take again steel at a strain of \(\varepsilon = 0{,}001\) in the direction \(x\), with \(E = 196\) GPa and Poisson's ratio \(\nu = 0{,}3\) (definition 3.2). The lateral strains are \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = -\nu\,\varepsilon = -0{,}0003\), and there are no shear strains.

  1. The volumetric strain is \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} = \varepsilon - 2\nu\,\varepsilon = (1 - 2\nu)\,\varepsilon = 0{,}4 \cdot 0{,}001 = 0{,}0004\).
  2. The components of the deviatoric part are \(e_{\mathrm{xx}} = \varepsilon - \tfrac13\,(1 - 2\nu)\,\varepsilon = \tfrac23\,(1 + \nu)\,\varepsilon = 0{,}000867\) and \(e_{\mathrm{yy}} = e_{\mathrm{zz}} = -\nu\,\varepsilon - \tfrac13\,(1 - 2\nu)\,\varepsilon = -\tfrac13\,(1 + \nu)\,\varepsilon = -0{,}000433\). The sum of their squares is \((\tfrac49 + \tfrac19 + \tfrac19)\,(1 + \nu)^2\,\varepsilon^2 = \tfrac23\,(1 + \nu)^2\,\varepsilon^2 = 1{,}1267 \cdot 10^{-6}\).
  3. With \(K = E/\big(3\,(1 - 2\nu)\big)\) the volumetric part is
    \[\begin{aligned} \tfrac12\,K\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}^2 &= \frac12 \cdot \frac{E}{3\,(1 - 2\nu)} \cdot (1 - 2\nu)^2\,\varepsilon^2 = \frac{1 - 2\nu}{3} \cdot \tfrac12 E\,\varepsilon^2 \\ &= \frac{0{,}4}{3} \cdot 98\,000\ \text{J/m}^3 = 13\,067\ \text{J/m}^3 . \end{aligned}\]
  4. With \(G = E/\big(2\,(1 + \nu)\big)\) the distortional part is
    \[\begin{aligned} G \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} e_{\mathrm{ij}}^2 &= \frac{E}{2\,(1 + \nu)} \cdot \tfrac23\,(1 + \nu)^2\,\varepsilon^2 = \frac{2\,(1 + \nu)}{3} \cdot \tfrac12 E\,\varepsilon^2 \\ &= \frac{2{,}6}{3} \cdot 98\,000\ \text{J/m}^3 = 84\,933\ \text{J/m}^3 . \end{aligned}\]

The shares \((1 - 2\nu)/3\) and \(2\,(1 + \nu)/3\) add up to \((1 - 2\nu + 2 + 2\nu)/3 = 1\), so the sum is \(98\,000\) J/m³, as we computed with the formula \(\tfrac12 E\,\varepsilon^2\). Only 13,3 % of the energy goes into the change of volume, 86,7 % into the change of shape. Why so little into the volume? Because the piece gets thinner, the lateral strains eat up \(2\nu = 60\) % of the stretch, and the volume changes only by \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} = 0{,}4\,\varepsilon\). A material with Poisson's ratio close to \(0{,}5\), like rubber, hardly changes its volume, the share \((1 - 2\nu)/3\) is almost zero, and almost all the energy goes into the change of shape.

In the pure shear of the cube of section 2.6.6, with the shear stress \(\tau_0 = 50\) MPa between the faces \(x\) and \(z\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} = 0\), and the only nonzero strain component is \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zx}} = \tau_0/(2G) = 3{,}3163 \cdot 10^{-4}\), with \(G = E/\big(2\,(1 + \nu)\big) = 196/2{,}6\) GPa \(= 75{,}38\) GPa. All the energy goes into the change of shape, \(w = G \cdot 2\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}^2 = \tau_0\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = 50 \cdot 10^6\ \text{Pa} \cdot 3{,}3163 \cdot 10^{-4} = 16\,582\) J/m³, because \(2G\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \tau_0\). Under an even pressure, on the other hand, all components \(e_{\mathrm{ij}}\) are zero, and all the energy goes into the change of volume.

From history

When does a material under load in several directions start to yield? James Clerk Maxwell wrote in a letter to his friend Thomson that he had never seen an investigation of the question when a small piece of material gives way, if the strain in three directions is given (TIMO, PDF p. 308). He believed that the piece starts to give way when the energy of change of shape reaches a limit (TIMO, PDF p. 308). He showed that the total energy per unit volume splits into two parts, the energy of uniform tension or compression and the energy of change of shape (TIMO, PDF p. 307). He did not return to the question, and his letters were published only later, in book form in 1937, so his idea was long unknown (TIMO, PDF p. 308).

Beltrami proposed in 1885 that the measure should be all the stored energy per unit volume (TIMO, PDF p. 308). Experiments did not confirm this, because under uniform pressure a large energy is stored without the material yielding or breaking (TIMO, PDF p. 308). In our example, in a tension test of steel only 13.3 % of the energy goes to the change of volume, and under uniform pressure all of it, and the shape does not change. Huber in 1904 took only the energy of change of shape, and von Mises independently of him in 1913 (TIMO, PDF p. 308-309). This energy is proportional to the sum of squares of the differences of the principal stresses, that is, to \(J_2\) from (2.74) (TIMO, PDF p. 308).

Let us return to the split of energy into a part due to the change of volume and a part due to the change of shape, which we computed for steel. The distortional part is connected with the von Mises equivalent stress (definition 2.76). The deviatoric part of stress in an isotropic material is \(s_{\mathrm{ij}} = 2G\,e_{\mathrm{ij}}\) (equation (3.14)), so \(G \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} e_{\mathrm{ij}}^2 = \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} s_{\mathrm{ij}}^2/(4G)\). The von Mises equivalent stress is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{vM}} = \sqrt{3 J_2}\), where \(J_2 = \tfrac12 \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} s_{\mathrm{ij}}^2\) is the second invariant of the deviator (equation (2.73)), so \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} s_{\mathrm{ij}}^2 = \tfrac23\,\sigma_{\mathrm{vM}}^2\) and \(G \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} e_{\mathrm{ij}}^2 = \sigma_{\mathrm{vM}}^2/(6G)\). In the tension test of steel at \(\varepsilon = 0{,}001\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{vM}} = \sigma = 196\) MPa. Since \(G = E/\big(2\,(1 + \nu)\big) = E/2{,}6\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{vM}}^2/(6G) = 2{,}6 \cdot (196 \cdot 10^6\ \text{Pa})^2/(6 \cdot 196 \cdot 10^9\ \text{Pa}) = 84\,933\) J/m³, as above. The criterion that predicts the onset of yield when \(\sigma_{\mathrm{vM}}\) reaches the yield stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{Y}}\) therefore says that the material starts to yield when the distortional energy reaches the value it has in a tension test at the onset of yield. SADD mentions such criteria (PDF p. 133), and we wrote down the yield condition with the von Mises equivalent stress in section 2.12.5.

3.6.6 Bounds on the elastic constants

Can the elastic constants be any numbers? In the bar, a positive Young's modulus meant that stretching takes pulling and doing work. A material with \(w < 0\) at some strain would give off work when deformed in that way, and it would deform by itself, without a force. A material with \(w = 0\) at a nonzero strain would deform in that way without any resistance. From a stable material we therefore require \(w > 0\) at every small strain that is not zero (SADD (6.1.11), PDF p. 131, where this property is stated for all elastic materials, BOWER, PDF p. 102). This is an assumption about the material, not a consequence of the previous theorems. What does it say about the constants of an isotropic material?

Theorem 3.48 Bounds on the elastic constants (meje elastičnih konstant)

Let the material be ideal elastic, linear elastic and isotropic, with Young's modulus \(E\) and Poisson's ratio \(\nu\), where \(\nu \ne -1\) and \(\nu \ne \tfrac12\), and let \(K = E/\big(3\,(1 - 2\nu)\big)\) be its bulk modulus and \(G = E/\big(2\,(1 + \nu)\big)\) its shear modulus. Then the following three statements are equivalent:

  1. the strain energy density is positive, \(w > 0\), at every small strain that is not zero,
  2. \(K > 0\) and \(G > 0\),
  3. \(E > 0\) and \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\).
Proof

We must show that statement 2 follows from statement 1 and conversely, and that statement 3 follows from statement 2 and conversely. We use equation (3.43), \(w = \tfrac12\,K\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}^2 + G \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} e_{\mathrm{ij}}^2\).

From 1 follows 2.

  1. Take a strain that changes only the volume: \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = c\), where \(c \ne 0\) is a small number, with no shear components. Then \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} = 3c\) and \(e_{\mathrm{xx}} = c - c = 0\), likewise \(e_{\mathrm{yy}} = e_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\), so \(w = \tfrac12\,K \cdot 9c^2\).
  2. By statement 1, \(w > 0\), and since \(\tfrac92\,c^2 > 0\), \(K > 0\).
  3. Take a strain that changes only the shape: \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yx}} = c \ne 0\), all other components zero. Then \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} = 0\), so \(e_{\mathrm{ij}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) for all indices and \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} e_{\mathrm{ij}}^2 = c^2 + c^2 = 2c^2\), and \(w = 2\,G\,c^2\).
  4. By statement 1, \(w > 0\), so \(G > 0\).

From 2 follows 1.

  1. Let \(K > 0\) and \(G > 0\). Then both terms of \(w\) are nonnegative, so \(w \ge 0\).
  2. Let \(w = 0\). A sum of two nonnegative terms is zero only if both are zero, so \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} = 0\) and \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} e_{\mathrm{ij}}^2 = 0\). A sum of squares is zero only if every square is zero, so all components \(e_{\mathrm{ij}}\) are zero.
  3. From \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = e_{\mathrm{xx}} + \tfrac13\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = e_{\mathrm{xy}}\) and the same relations for the other components it follows that the strain is zero.
  4. At a strain that is not zero, therefore, \(w = 0\) is impossible, and by step 1 \(w > 0\).

From 3 follows 2.

  1. Let \(E > 0\) and \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\). Then \(1 + \nu > 0\) and \(1 - 2\nu > 0\).
  2. The numerator and the denominator of \(G = E/\big(2\,(1 + \nu)\big)\) are positive, so \(G > 0\). Likewise \(K = E/\big(3\,(1 - 2\nu)\big) > 0\).

From 2 follows 3.

  1. Let \(K > 0\) and \(G > 0\). Since \(G\) is not zero, \(E\) is not zero either, and the reciprocals are \(1/G = 2\,(1 + \nu)/E\) and \(1/K = 3\,(1 - 2\nu)/E\).
  2. We add three times the first and the second. The terms with \(\nu\) cancel:
    \[\frac{3}{G} + \frac{1}{K} = \frac{6 + 6\nu + 3 - 6\nu}{E} = \frac{9}{E} .\]
  3. The left side is positive, so \(E = 9/(3/G + 1/K) > 0\).
  4. From \(G = E/\big(2\,(1 + \nu)\big)\) follows \(1 + \nu = E/(2G) > 0\), so \(\nu > -1\). From \(K = E/\big(3\,(1 - 2\nu)\big)\) follows \(1 - 2\nu = E/(3K) > 0\), so \(\nu < \tfrac12\).

Source: SADD §6.3, (6.3.3), (6.3.6), (6.3.9)-(6.3.11) (PDF pp. 134-135), where the bounds are derived from three stress states: tension, shear and even pressure. BOWER (PDF p. 102) states the bounds for a stable material. The proof through the split into a volumetric and a distortional part is not from the sources.

The proof used two test strains, one that changes only the volume and one that changes only the shape. These are the same strains as in figure 3.56, left and middle, and each tests one of the two constants. Figure 3.57, which extends figure 3.18 beyond both bounds, shows what happens at the bounds. As \(\nu\) approaches \(\tfrac12\) from below, \(K/E = 1/\big(3\,(1 - 2\nu)\big)\) grows beyond all bounds: the material resists a change of volume infinitely strongly compared with stretching, it is incompressible, as we said in section 3.2.9. As \(\nu\) approaches \(-1\) from above, \(G/E\) grows beyond all bounds. Above \(\tfrac12\), \(K\) would be negative, below \(-1\), \(G\) would be. For steel with \(\nu = 0{,}3\), \(K/E = 0{,}833\) and \(G/E = 0{,}385\), both positive.

The theorem says that Poisson's ratio can also be negative, but larger than \(-1\). Such a material gets wider when stretched. BOWER mentions that such materials exist (BOWER, PDF p. 102), although most real materials have \(0 < \nu < \tfrac12\) (SADD, PDF p. 135). For an anisotropic material the requirement \(w > 0\) means that the stiffness matrix \(\mat C\) is such that \(\tfrac12 \sum_{\mathrm{p}}\sum_{\mathrm{q}} C_{\mathrm{pq}}\,\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{p}}\,\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{q}} > 0\) at every strain that is not zero. One consequence is simple: at a strain whose only nonzero component is \(\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{p}}\), \(w = \tfrac12\,C_{\mathrm{pp}}\,\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{p}}^2\), so all diagonal entries \(C_{\mathrm{pp}}\) are positive. The complete conditions, which are written with determinants (section 2.10), are given in SADD (11.3.3) (PDF p. 340). We do not prove them.

3.6.7 Complementary strain energy density

In a uniaxial state the strain energy density is the area under the curve of stress against strain, between the curve and the strain axis. The curve splits the rectangle with sides \(\varepsilon_1\) and \(\sigma_1\), the final strain and the final stress, into two parts (figure 3.58). The other part, between the curve and the stress axis, gets a name of its own. In chapter 4 we will need it for methods that look for forces instead of displacements.

From history

Methods of structural analysis that came from energy always assumed that the displacements are linear functions of the forces, and for some structures this does not hold (TIMO, PDF p. 231). For such cases Friedrich Engesser introduced a new energy quantity in 1889 (TIMO, PDF p. 231).

Let us return to the curve of stress against strain in figure 3.58. Why do the classical methods fail? When the relation between force and displacement is a curve, the strain energy is not a quadratic function of the force, so its derivative with respect to the force does not give the displacement (TIMO, PDF p. 232). For a linear material the two parts of the rectangle in figure 3.58 are equal and the new quantity is not needed. For a curve that is not a straight line, however, they differ. The first part is the strain energy, and the second is the area on the other side of the curve, which Engesser introduced (TIMO, PDF p. 232).

Definition 3.49 Complementary strain energy density (gostota komplementarne energije)

During a loading that starts at zero strain and zero stress, let the strain components \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) and the stress components \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\) at a point \(P\) change with a parameter \(t\) from a closed interval \([t_0, t_1]\), and let all these functions be differentiable at every point of the interval \([t_0, t_1]\), at the endpoints with a one-sided derivative (definition 1.9), with derivatives continuous on \([t_0, t_1]\). The complementary strain energy density (gostota komplementarne energije) at \(P\) at the end of the loading is the number

\[w^* = \int_{\mathrm{t}_0}^{t_1} \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}(t)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}'(t)\,\mathrm{d}t . \tag{3.44}\]

Source: EP (3.2.9) (p. 106) and pp. 103-104 for the uniaxial state, where \(w^* = \int_0^{\sigma} \varepsilon\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\).

The roles are swapped: in \(w\) the stresses work on the changes of strain, in \(w^*\) the strains on the changes of stress. In a uniaxial state we take the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) itself as the parameter, running from \(0\) to \(\sigma_1\), and get \(w^* = \int_0^{\sigma_1} \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), the area between the curve and the stress axis (EP, p. 104). How are \(w\) and \(w^*\) related?

  1. For each pair of indices \(i\), \(j\) we use integration by parts (3.33) with the functions \(f = \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\) and \(g = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) (corollary 3.41):
    \[\int_{\mathrm{t}_0}^{t_1} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}'\,\mathrm{d}t = \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}(t_1)\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}(t_1) - \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}(t_0)\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}(t_0) - \int_{\mathrm{t}_0}^{t_1} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}'\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\mathrm{d}t .\]
  2. The loading starts at zero stress, so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}(t_0) = 0\), and the middle term drops out.
  3. We move the last integral to the left and add up over all nine pairs of indices. By the linearity of the integral (lemma 3.39, item 1) the left side is the sum of the integral (3.37) and the integral (3.44), the right side the sum of the products of the final stresses and strains:
    \[\int_{\mathrm{t}_0}^{t_1} \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}'\,\mathrm{d}t + w^* = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}(t_1)\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}(t_1) . \tag{3.45}\]

For an ideal elastic material the first term is the strain energy density \(w\) (definition 3.44), so \(w + w^* = \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\), taken at the end of the loading. Since \(w\) and the right side depend only on the final state, so does \(w^*\). In a uniaxial state this is \(w + w^* = \sigma_1\,\varepsilon_1\): the areas under and above the curve together fill the rectangle (figure 3.58).

For a linear material, by theorem 3.47 on the symmetry of the elastic constants, \(w = \tfrac12 \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\), so also \(w^* = \tfrac12 \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\), and the two densities are equal, \(w^* = w\). In figure 3.58, left, the line splits the rectangle into two equal triangles. For the steel bar at the force of 10 kN, therefore, also \(w^* = 41\,356\) J/m³. They are equal only in value, though. We write \(w\) as a function of the strains and \(w^*\) as a function of the stresses, and in chapter 4 this will matter (EP, p. 107).

For a material whose relation between stress and strain is not a straight line, the two densities differ, and \(w\) is no longer \(\tfrac12\,\sigma_1\,\varepsilon_1\). Rubber is such a material: it sustains large changes of shape and returns to its original shape, but its relation between force and elongation is not a straight line (BOWER §1.1.5, PDF p. 35). The rubber band of section 1.1, stretched by half its length, has such a curve. Its strains, however, are not small, so this example lies outside the model of this section, which works with small strains and with areas and volumes before loading. We cite it only as an example of a curve that is not a straight line. Rubber under large strains is described by hyperelastic models (BOWER §3.5, PDF pp. 120-121). Figure 3.58, right, shows a schematic curve that flattens as the strain grows. The area under it is larger than half the rectangle, so \(w > \tfrac12\,\sigma_1\,\varepsilon_1 > w^*\). For a curve that gets steeper as the strain grows it would be the other way round. The stresses are the partial derivatives of the strain energy density with respect to the strains (lemma 3.46). Are, conversely, the strains the partial derivatives of the complementary strain energy density with respect to the stresses, as EP and SADD write (EP, p. 106, SADD (6.6.3), PDF p. 139)? For a linear material and for a uniaxial state with any curve we prove this in the next lemma. For \(w^*\) to be a function of the stresses at all, we need the stresses to determine the strains uniquely.

Why would the strains be derivatives of the density \(w^*\)? Figure 3.59 shows the idea for a uniaxial state. The density \(w^*\) is the area between the curve and the stress axis up to the stress \(\sigma_1\). When we raise the stress by a small number \(h\), the area grows by a strip of height \(h\) and width \(\varepsilon_1\), the strain at the stress \(\sigma_1\), and by a small piece next to the curve. The increase divided by \(h\) is therefore close to \(\varepsilon_1\). This is the same argument as for the force, which is the derivative of the work with respect to the elongation (section 3.5.7), only with the roles of the axes swapped. For the steel bar at the force of 10 kN the stress is \(\sigma_1 = 127{,}3\) MPa and the strain \(\varepsilon_1 = \sigma_1/E = 6{,}496 \cdot 10^{-4}\). When the stress is raised by 1 MPa, the density \(w^* = \sigma^2/(2E)\) grows by \(652{,}2\) J/m³, while the strip has the area \(\varepsilon_1 \cdot 1\ \text{MPa} = 649{,}6\) J/m³. The difference of \(2{,}6\) J/m³ comes from the triangle next to the line, \((1\ \text{MPa})^2/(2E)\), which for a smaller step shrinks with the square of the step. For a linear material in several directions \(w^*\) is a quadratic function of the six stress components, and we compute its partial derivatives directly.

Lemma 3.50 Strains as derivatives of the complementary energy density (deformacije kot odvodi gostote komplementarne energije)
  1. Let a material at the point \(P\) be ideal elastic and linear elastic, with a material stiffness matrix \(\mat C\) that has an inverse \(\mat C^{-1}\). Then at every stress to which a small strain belongs,
    \[w^* = \frac12 \sum_{\mathrm{p}} \sum_{\mathrm{q}} (\mat C^{-1})_{\mathrm{pq}}\,\{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{p}}\,\{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{q}} , \tag{3.46}\]
    where \(p\) and \(q\) run from \(1\) to \(6\), and the partial derivatives of the density \(w^*\) with respect to the six stress components are the strain components:
    \[\begin{aligned} \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} &= \frac{\partial w^*}{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}} , & \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} &= \frac{\partial w^*}{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}} , & \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} &= \frac{\partial w^*}{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}} , \\ \gamma_{\mathrm{yz}} &= \frac{\partial w^*}{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}} , & \gamma_{\mathrm{xz}} &= \frac{\partial w^*}{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}} , & \gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} &= \frac{\partial w^*}{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}} . \end{aligned} \tag{3.47}\]
  2. Let a material at the point \(P\) be ideal elastic and the stress state uniaxial, with the only nonzero component \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma\). Let \(I\) be an open interval that contains \(0\), and let the strain be small at every stress \(\sigma \in I\), with each of its components a continuously differentiable function of the stress \(\sigma\) on \(I\) that is zero at \(\sigma = 0\). Denote the component \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) as a function of the stress by \(g\). Then the complementary strain energy density at the stress \(\sigma_1 \in I\) is
    \[w^*(\sigma_1) = \int_0^{\sigma_1} g(s)\,\mathrm{d}s \quad\text{for } \sigma_1 \ge 0 , \qquad w^*(\sigma_1) = -\int_{\sigma_1}^0 g(s)\,\mathrm{d}s \quad\text{for } \sigma_1 < 0 ,\]
    and \(w^*\) is a differentiable function of the stress with the derivative \(\mathrm{d}w^*/\mathrm{d}\sigma_1 = g(\sigma_1)\), the strain \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) at the stress \(\sigma_1\).
Proof

We have to show both items. In the first we write \(w^*\) with the stresses and differentiate a quadratic function. In the second we compute \(w^*\) along a path on which the parameter is the stress itself, and use the fundamental theorem of calculus.

1. Linear material.

  1. By theorem 3.47 on the symmetry of the elastic constants, \(\mat C\) is symmetric, and by equation (3.41), \(w = \tfrac12 \sum_{\mathrm{p}} \{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{p}}\,\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{p}}\).
  2. By (3.45), for an ideal elastic material \(w + w^* = \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\), which by (3.38), applied to products, is \(\sum_{\mathrm{p}} \{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{p}}\,\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{p}}\). With step 1, therefore, \(w^* = \tfrac12 \sum_{\mathrm{p}} \{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{p}}\,\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{p}}\).
  3. By lemma 3.19, \(\{\varepsilon\} = \mat C^{-1}\{\sigma\}\), so \(\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{p}} = \sum_{\mathrm{q}} (\mat C^{-1})_{\mathrm{pq}}\,\{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{q}}\). We insert this into step 2 and get (3.46).
  4. The matrix \(\mat C^{-1}\) is symmetric. Steps 1 to 4 of the proof of corollary 3.26 on the reciprocal relations use only that \(\mat C\) is symmetric and has an inverse, and they give \((\mat C^{-1})^\T = \mat C^{-1}\). Write \(a_{\mathrm{pq}} = (\mat C^{-1})_{\mathrm{pq}}\), so that \(a_{\mathrm{pq}} = a_{\mathrm{qp}}\).
  5. Choose a place \(r\). For the partial derivative with respect to \(\{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{r}}\) we change only \(\{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{r}}\), by a number \(h\), and keep the other components fixed. By step 3 the strain then changes by \(h\) times the column \(r\) of the matrix \(\mat C^{-1}\). The strain components at \(\{\sigma\}\) are strictly smaller in absolute value than the bound of small strains, so they stay so for small enough \(|h|\), as in step 1 of the proof of lemma 3.46 on the stress as the derivative of the energy density. At these stresses equation (3.46) holds, so the partial derivative of the density \(w^*\) is the partial derivative of the right side of (3.46).
  6. We split the right side into three parts: the term with \(p = q = r\), which is \(\tfrac12\,a_{\mathrm{rr}}\,\{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{r}}^2\), the terms in which exactly one of the indices \(p\) and \(q\) equals \(r\), which give \(\tfrac12 \sum_{\mathrm{q} \ne \mathrm{r}} a_{\mathrm{rq}}\,\{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{r}}\,\{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{q}} + \tfrac12 \sum_{\mathrm{p} \ne \mathrm{r}} a_{\mathrm{pr}}\,\{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{p}}\,\{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{r}}\), and the terms without \(\{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{r}}\), which do not change when \(\{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{r}}\) changes.
  7. By the rules of differentiation (lemma 1.81, items 1, 2, 4 and 5) the derivative of the first part is \(a_{\mathrm{rr}}\,\{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{r}}\), of the second \(\tfrac12 \sum_{\mathrm{q} \ne \mathrm{r}} a_{\mathrm{rq}}\,\{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{q}} + \tfrac12 \sum_{\mathrm{p} \ne \mathrm{r}} a_{\mathrm{pr}}\,\{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{p}}\), and of the third zero.
  8. By step 4, \(a_{\mathrm{pr}} = a_{\mathrm{rp}}\), so the second sum of the second part, after renaming the index \(p\) to \(q\), equals the first. The derivative is therefore \(a_{\mathrm{rr}}\,\{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{r}} + \sum_{\mathrm{q} \ne \mathrm{r}} a_{\mathrm{rq}}\,\{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{q}} = \sum_{\mathrm{q}} a_{\mathrm{rq}}\,\{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{q}}\), which by step 3 is the component \(\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{r}}\).
  9. By definition 3.18 the places \(1\) to \(6\) belong to the pairs \(xx\), \(yy\), \(zz\), \(yz\), \(xz\) and \(xy\), and in the column \(\{\varepsilon\}\) the places \(4\) to \(6\) hold the engineering shear strains. Step 8 for \(r = 1, \dots, 6\) is therefore (3.47).

2. Uniaxial state.

  1. For \(\sigma_1 = 0\) the strain is zero, the loading does not move, and \(w^* = 0\). The formula gives the same, because \(\int_0^0 g(s)\,\mathrm{d}s = 0\) by the convention of definition 3.34.
  2. Let \(\sigma_1 > 0\). As the parameter we take the stress itself, \(t\) from \(0\) to \(\sigma_1\). Then \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(t) = t\), the other stress components are zero, and the strain components are \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}(t) = g_{\mathrm{ij}}(t)\), where \(g_{\mathrm{ij}}\) are the functions of the hypothesis and \(g_{\mathrm{xx}} = g\). All these functions are differentiable on the interval \(I\), which contains \([0, \sigma_1]\), and have continuous derivatives, and the loading starts at zero stress and zero strain. This is therefore a loading as definition 3.49 requires.
  3. The derivative of the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(t) = t\) is \(1\), the derivatives of the other stress components are zero (lemma 1.81, item 4). In the integral (3.44) only the term \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}(t) \cdot 1 = g(t)\) remains, and \(w^*(\sigma_1) = \int_0^{\sigma_1} g(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\).
  4. The material is ideal elastic, so by (3.45) \(w^*\) depends only on the final state, and the final state is determined by the stress \(\sigma_1\). The value of step 3 is therefore the density \(w^*\) for every loading that ends at the stress \(\sigma_1\).
  5. Let \(\sigma_1 < 0\) and \(b = -\sigma_1 > 0\). As the parameter we take the magnitude of the compressive stress, \(t\) from \(0\) to \(b\). Then \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(t) = -t\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}(t) = g_{\mathrm{ij}}(-t)\).
  6. The reflection rule. Let a function \(f\) be differentiable at the point \(-t\). Then the function \(t \mapsto f(-t)\) is differentiable at \(t\) with the derivative \(-f'(-t)\). The difference quotient is
    \[\frac{f(-t - h) - f(-t)}{h} = -\,\frac{f(-t + k) - f(-t)}{k} , \qquad k = -h ,\]
    and \(0 < |h| < \delta\) holds exactly when \(0 < |k| < \delta\) holds. By definitions 1.5 and 1.9 the left side therefore approaches \(-f'(-t)\) as \(h\) approaches zero. If \(f\) is continuous, so is the function \(t \mapsto f(-t)\), because the distance between \(-t'\) and \(-t\) is \(|t' - t|\). The same holds for \(f'\).
  7. By step 6 the functions \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}(t) = g_{\mathrm{ij}}(-t)\) are differentiable with the continuous derivatives \(-g_{\mathrm{ij}}'(-t)\), and the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(t) = -t\) has the derivative \(-1\). The loading satisfies the requirements of definition 3.49, and as in step 3, \(w^*(\sigma_1) = \int_0^b g(-t) \cdot (-1)\,\mathrm{d}t = -\int_0^b g(-t)\,\mathrm{d}t\), by the linearity of the integral (lemma 3.39, item 1).
  8. We compute the integral with the fundamental theorem of calculus. Let \(G(s) = \int_{\sigma_1}^{s} g(u)\,\mathrm{d}u\) for \(s \in [\sigma_1, 0]\). By theorem 3.40, item 2, \(G\) is continuous on \([\sigma_1, 0]\) and has the derivative \(g\) on \((\sigma_1, 0)\). The function \(H(t) = -G(-t)\) is continuous on \([0, b]\) by step 6, and on \((0, b)\) it has the derivative \(-\big({-G'(-t)}\big) = g(-t)\). By item 1 of the same theorem, \(\int_0^b g(-t)\,\mathrm{d}t = H(b) - H(0) = -G(\sigma_1) + G(0) = \int_{\sigma_1}^0 g(u)\,\mathrm{d}u\), because \(G(\sigma_1) = 0\) by the convention of definition 3.34. With step 7 this is the formula for \(\sigma_1 < 0\).
  9. The derivative. The interval \(I\) is open and contains the numbers \(0\) and \(\sigma_1\), so it contains numbers \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) with \(\alpha < 0 < \beta\) and \(\alpha < \sigma_1 < \beta\). Let \(F(\sigma) = \int_\alpha^\sigma g(u)\,\mathrm{d}u\) for \(\sigma \in [\alpha, \beta]\). By the additivity of the integral (lemma 3.39, item 2), for \(\sigma \ge 0\) the integral \(\int_0^\sigma g\) equals \(F(\sigma) - F(0)\), and for \(\sigma < 0\), \(\int_\sigma^0 g = F(0) - F(\sigma)\). By steps 1, 3 and 8, therefore, \(w^*(\sigma) = F(\sigma) - F(0)\) for every \(\sigma \in [\alpha, \beta]\).
  10. By theorem 3.40, item 2, \(F\) has the derivative \(g\) at every point of the interval \((\alpha, \beta)\), and the constant function \(F(0)\) has the derivative zero (lemma 1.81). Therefore \(w^*\) is differentiable at \(\sigma_1\), and \(\mathrm{d}w^*/\mathrm{d}\sigma_1 = g(\sigma_1)\).

Source: EP (3.2.9) (p. 106) for \(w^*\), EP p. 104 for the uniaxial state, EP p. 106 and SADD (6.6.3) (PDF p. 139) for the relation between the strains and the derivatives. The proof is not from the sources.

The proof of item 1 showed where the symmetry is needed. By step 7 the derivative of the quadratic function \(\tfrac12 \sum_{\mathrm{p}}\sum_{\mathrm{q}} a_{\mathrm{pq}}\,\{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{p}}\,\{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{q}}\) with respect to \(\{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{r}}\) is the average of the row and the column \(r\), \(\tfrac12 \sum_{\mathrm{q}} (a_{\mathrm{rq}} + a_{\mathrm{qr}})\,\{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{q}}\). It agrees with the strain \(\sum_{\mathrm{q}} a_{\mathrm{rq}}\,\{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{q}}\) only when \(a_{\mathrm{qr}} = a_{\mathrm{rq}}\). Let us test the lemma on the thin composite plate of section 3.4.6, in the plane stress state of the oblique strip, with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 6{,}4\) MPa, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = 3{,}6\) MPa and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 4{,}8\) MPa. The other three stress components are zero, so in (3.46) only the terms with the elements of the material compliance matrix of (3.28) remain. With the reciprocal relation \(\nu_{\mathrm{yx}}/E_{\mathrm{y}} = \nu_{\mathrm{xy}}/E_{\mathrm{x}}\),

\[w^* = \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}^2}{2E_{\mathrm{x}}} - \frac{\nu_{\mathrm{xy}}}{E_{\mathrm{x}}}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} + \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}^2}{2E_{\mathrm{y}}} + \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}^2}{2G_{\mathrm{xy}}} = 2639\ \text{J/m}^3 .\]

The derivative with respect to \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/E_{\mathrm{x}} - \nu_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}/E_{\mathrm{x}}\), while by (3.28) \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/E_{\mathrm{x}} - \nu_{\mathrm{yx}}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}/E_{\mathrm{y}}\). The two expressions are equal precisely because of the reciprocal relation, and both give \(2{,}665 \cdot 10^{-5}\), as in section 3.4.6. The derivative with respect to \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}/G_{\mathrm{xy}} = 8{,}000 \cdot 10^{-4}\), which is \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}}\). With SymPy we also checked the difference quotients. When \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) is raised by \(0{,}01\) MPa, \(w^*\) grows by \(0{,}2668\) J/m³, and the quotient is \(2{,}668 \cdot 10^{-5}\). For \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) the quotient is \(8{,}008 \cdot 10^{-4}\). Item 2 holds for every curve described by a continuously differentiable function \(g\), also for the curve of figure 3.58, right.

What about a nonlinear material in several directions? EP and SADD write that then, too, the strains are the partial derivatives of the density \(w^*\) with respect to the stresses (EP, p. 106, SADD (6.6.3), PDF p. 139). A proof would need two things: that the strains are continuously differentiable functions of the six stress components, and the rule for the derivative of a function into which other functions of several variables are inserted. From (3.45) we would get \(w^* = \sum_{\mathrm{p}} \{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{p}}\,\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{p}} - w\), where \(w\) is a function of the strains and the strains are functions of the stresses. By the product rule and this rule the derivative with respect to \(\{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{r}}\) would be \(\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{r}} + \sum_{\mathrm{p}} \{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{p}}\,\partial\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{p}}/\partial\{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{r}} - \sum_{\mathrm{p}} (\partial w/\partial\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{p}})\,\partial\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{p}}/\partial\{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{r}}\), and by lemma 3.46 the last two sums are equal and cancel. We will prove this rule for functions of three variables in chapter 4, section 4.3.1, and for functions of six variables it is given in LEBL2 (theorem 8.3.7, PDF p. 38). The book does not use the statement for a nonlinear material in several directions.

With this we have answered the questions from the start of the section. The work that the machine does on the bar is stored below the yield stress as strain energy and given back on unloading, while above the yield stress part of it turns into heat. We saw this in the areas under the loading and unloading curves. On a small box we computed, from the forces on opposite faces and the differences of the changes of displacement, that the stresses do the work \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\Delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) per unit volume, while a motion and a turn do not count. An imagined material with a nonsymmetric stiffness matrix delivered work from nothing in a cycle, so we required of a material that the work depends only on the final strain. This requirement gave the strain energy density, the stresses as its partial derivatives and, through the symmetry of mixed partial derivatives, a symmetric stiffness matrix with 21 constants. In an isotropic material the energy splits into a part governed by the bulk modulus and a part governed by the shear modulus, and the requirement \(w > 0\) gave \(K > 0\) and \(G > 0\), hence \(E > 0\) and \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\). In a uniaxial state the complementary strain energy density is the other part of the rectangle under the final stress and strain, for a linear material its half. That its derivatives with respect to the stresses are the strains we proved for a linear material, where we differentiated a quadratic function of the stresses with a symmetric material compliance matrix, and for a uniaxial state with any curve, where we used the fundamental theorem of calculus. For a nonlinear material in several directions we have not proved it, and we do not use it. In chapter 4 we will add up the strain energy density over a whole body, compare it with the work of the loads, and obtain from this new routes to displacements and forces.
