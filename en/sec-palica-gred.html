Chapter 13 Vibration of continuous systems: string, shaft and beam

13.3 Bar and shaft (palica in gred)

Chapter 10 compared the frequencies of the finite element bar with those of the continuous bar, which it took as the yardstick without proof (equation (10.42)). Chapter 9 replaced a shaft that carries a disk at its end by one degree of freedom and promised the torsional stiffness \(k_{\mathrm{t}}\) of the shaft for later. This section pays both debts. We already have the bar: its equation of motion came from Hamilton's principle in chapter 8 (theorem 8.103). We obtain the shaft from the balance of moments of a piece, then show that both have the same equation as the string, and compute their natural vibrations. At the end we attach a disk to the shaft and ask how good the one degree of freedom model is.

13.3.1 The bar: equation and end conditions

Figure 13.13 shows what the model takes for a bar. At the top is the bar, fixed in a wall at \(x = 0\), with the cross sections at rest (dashed) and the displaced cross sections (blue), which stay plane and perpendicular to the axis. The displacement \(u(x, t)\) is the difference between them, greatly enlarged. On the right is the cross section \(A\), seen along the axis.

A bar (palica) here is a straight body with a constant cross section of area \(A\), density \(\rho\) and modulus of elasticity \(E\), loaded only along its axis. The model of chapter 8 has four assumptions, which we take from the source (CK PDF p. 386): the axis stays straight, cross sections stay plane and perpendicular to the axis, the material is linearly elastic, and the properties are constant over the cross section. The stress is therefore the same over the cross section and the lateral motion of the cross sections (contraction when stretched) is neglected. This is an idealization. It is sensible when the wavelength of the vibration is much larger than the largest dimension of the cross section. For a bar of length \(L = 1\) m and a section of 40 mm the wavelength of the third mode, which we meet below, is \(4L/5 = 0{,}8\) m, which is 20 times more. This section does not compute the error of the neglected contraction. How the bar is the limit of a slender body is shown by the section on elastodynamics later in the chapter.

Let \(u(x, t)\) be the axial displacement of the cross section at \(x\) at time \(t\). For \(\rho A\,u_{\mathrm{tt}} = EA\,u_{\mathrm{xx}} + q\) with \(q = 0\), dividing by \(\rho A\), theorem 8.103 gives the equation of free vibration

\[u_{\mathrm{tt}} = c^2\,u_{\mathrm{xx}} , \qquad c = \sqrt{\frac{E}{\rho}} . \tag{13.13}\]

This is the equation of the string (equation (13.2)) with another wave speed. The number \(c\) has the unit m/s, because \(E/\rho\) in N/m\(^2\) divided by kg/m\(^3\) is m\(^2\)/s\(^2\). For steel with \(E = 196\) GPa and \(\rho = 7800\) kg/m\(^3\) we get \(c = 5012{,}80\) m/s.

For the end conditions we need only what theorem 8.103 says about the end. A fixed end (pritrjen konec) has a prescribed displacement, \(u = 0\) at every time. This is the essential condition and admissibility of the function already requires it. A free end (prost konec) has no force, and theorem 8.103 gives for \(F = 0\) the natural condition \(EA\,u_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\), so \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\) (equation (8.98)). The theorem in chapter 8 is proved for the end at \(x = 0\) fixed and the one at \(x = L\) free. Steps 1 to 7 of its proof use only that \(\eta = 0\) at one end and arbitrary at the other, so the same holds with the ends exchanged. For \(q = 0\) and \(F = 0\) the action contains only \(u_{\mathrm{x}}^2\) and \(u_{\mathrm{t}}^2\), so the substitution \(x \mapsto L - x\) preserves it. With two fixed ends the boundary term in step 3 vanishes at both ends and only the equation remains. With two free ends step 3 has a boundary term at both ends. The variation \(\eta = (x/L)\,\psi\) of step 7 gives the natural condition \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\) at \(x = L\), and the variation \(\eta = (1 - x/L)\,\psi\) gives the same at \(x = 0\). The four pairs of ends we need are called fixed-fixed, fixed-free, free-free and free-fixed, which we get from fixed-free by reflection.

13.3.2 The shaft: balance of moments of a piece

A shaft (gred) is a bar with a full circular cross section, loaded by a moment about its axis. Its motion is the rotation of cross sections, not a displacement. Unlike the axial force, we do not have torsion in the book yet: chapter 16 comes later, so we take the torsional moment as a function of the twist as a model, as chapter 12 took it for the beam (equation (12.16)).

Figure 13.14 shows the model. On the left is the cross section of the shaft, seen along the axis. The dashed radial line is before the rotation, the blue one after it, and the angle between them is the rotation \(\varphi\), greatly enlarged. A point at the distance \(r\) from the axis rotates with it and has the tangential velocity \(r\,\varphi_{\mathrm{t}}\). The moment \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\) acts about the axis. On the right are the axes \(x\) and \(\varphi\) and the rotation \(\varphi(x)\) along the shaft, fixed at \(x = 0\), at a given instant.

Definition 13.15 Model of a shaft in torsion (model gredi pri torziji)

A shaft of length \(L\) is a straight bar with a full circular cross section of radius \(R\), constant along the axis \(x\), of a material with density \(\rho\) and shear modulus \(G\). Its motion is described by the rotation \(\varphi(x, t)\) of the cross section at \(x\) about the axis at time \(t\), a function of two variables with continuous derivatives \(\varphi_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\varphi_{\mathrm{t}}\), \(\varphi_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and \(\varphi_{\mathrm{tt}}\). The model has four assumptions: the axis stays straight, cross sections stay plane and perpendicular to the axis, radial lines of the cross section stay straight and radial, so the cross section rotates as a rigid body by the angle \(\varphi\), and the material is linearly elastic. The torsional moment (torzijski moment) \(M_{\mathrm{x}}(x, t)\) is the moment about the axis \(x\) with which the part of the shaft to the right of the cross section \(x\) acts on the part to the left of it. We take

\[M_{\mathrm{x}} = G\,I_{\mathrm{p}}\,\varphi_{\mathrm{x}} , \qquad I_{\mathrm{p}} = \int_{\text{section}} (y^2 + z^2)\,\mathrm{d}A = \frac{\pi d^4}{32} . \tag{13.14}\]

The number \(I_{\mathrm{p}}\) is the polar second moment of area (polarni vztrajnostni moment prereza), unit m\(^4\), \(d = 2R\) is the diameter and \(G I_{\mathrm{p}}\) is the torsional stiffness (torzijska togost) of the shaft.

Source: CK (12.10) (PDF p. 389) with the same assumptions 1 to 5 (axis straight, sections plane, radial lines straight, \(\tau = G\gamma\), \(G\) constant over the section). CK quotes equation (12.10) from elementary strength of materials for static torsion (Ref. [12.1], §4.2 and §4.3) and we take it as a model here, the proof will be in chapter 16. The value \(I_{\mathrm{p}} = I_{\mathrm{y}} + I_{\mathrm{z}} = 2\cdot\pi R^4/4 = \pi d^4/32\) follows from \(I = \pi d^4/64\) of chapter 5 (source ONATE2) and the definition \(I_{\mathrm{p}} = \int (y^2 + z^2)\,\mathrm{d}A\).

The model is an idealization with two requirements. The sections do not warp out of their plane (the cross section stays plane), which for a circle, unlike a rectangle, holds because of the axial symmetry (chapter 16), and the wavelength of the vibration must be much larger than the diameter, as for the bar. For a shaft with \(d = 40\) mm and \(L = 1\) m the wavelength of the third mode is \(0{,}8\) m, 20 times the diameter.

We obtain the equation that the twist of the shaft satisfies from the balance of moments of a piece \([a, b]\) of the shaft. The piece may have any length, the balance holds for every \(a < b\), and only when we look for the equation at a point and not an average over a piece do we shrink it (\(b \to a\)). Figure 13.15 shows the piece and the moments acting on it. At the top is the axis \(x\) and along it the shaft, fixed at \(x = 0\). The blue lines on the sections show the twist \(\varphi(x, t)\), greatly enlarged to be visible. The grey piece between \(a\) and \(b\) is the one for which we write the balance. Below is the piece alone. At the end \(b\) the moment \(M_{\mathrm{x}}(b, t)\) of the part of the shaft to the right of \(b\) acts on it, and at the end \(a\) the moment \(-M_{\mathrm{x}}(a, t)\) of the part to the left of \(a\).

Theorem 13.16 Equation of motion of the shaft (gibalna enačba gredi)

Let the shaft of definition 13.15 carry no external moment along its axis and let the law of angular momentum about the axis hold for every piece \([a, b] \subset [0, L]\): the time derivative of the angular momentum of the piece is the sum of the moments of the neighbouring parts. Then for every \(x \in [0, L]\) and every \(t\)

\[\varphi_{\mathrm{tt}} = c_{\mathrm{T}}^2\,\varphi_{\mathrm{xx}} , \qquad c_{\mathrm{T}} = \sqrt{\frac{G}{\rho}} . \tag{13.15}\]

At a fixed end \(\varphi = 0\), at a free end, where there is no external moment, \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\), so \(\varphi_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\).

Proof

We must show that the piece has the angular momentum \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \rho I_{\mathrm{p}}\,\varphi_{\mathrm{t}}\,\mathrm{d}x\), that its derivative equals the difference of the torsional moments at the ends, and then get the equation at a point from the integral over the piece.

  1. By the model a cross section rotates as a rigid body, so a point of the section at distance \(r\) from the axis has tangential velocity \(r\,\varphi_{\mathrm{t}}(x, t)\). Its angular momentum about the axis per unit mass is \(r\cdot r\,\varphi_{\mathrm{t}}\). The angular momentum of the piece is \(H(t) = \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b\!\int_{\text{section}} \rho\,r^2\,\varphi_{\mathrm{t}}\,\mathrm{d}A\,\mathrm{d}x = \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \rho I_{\mathrm{p}}\,\varphi_{\mathrm{t}}\,\mathrm{d}x\), because \(r^2 = y^2 + z^2\) and \(\int r^2\,\mathrm{d}A = I_{\mathrm{p}}\). We cut the piece of the shaft into three bodies between graphs, as the round bar in chapter 4 (definition 4.21). In each of them \(x\) is a rectangle coordinate, so by theorem 4.33 and corollary 4.34 the integral is an outer integral over \(x\) of the integral over the cross section of the piece, and the same theorem in the plane adds the three pieces of the section into \(\int r^2\,\mathrm{d}A\).
  2. Only the moments of the neighbouring parts act on the piece. At the end \(b\) this is by definition \(M_{\mathrm{x}}(b, t)\). At the end \(a\) the part to the left of \(a\) acts on the piece with a moment that by the law of action and reaction is opposite to the one with which the piece acts on the left part, that is \(-M_{\mathrm{x}}(a, t)\) (figure 13.15). The lateral surface is free.
  3. The law of angular momentum for the piece is \(H'(t) = M_{\mathrm{x}}(b, t) - M_{\mathrm{x}}(a, t)\) (moment at \(b\) minus moment at \(a\)). The derivative \(H'(t) = \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \rho I_{\mathrm{p}}\,\varphi_{\mathrm{tt}}\,\mathrm{d}x\) by lemma 4.19, because \(\varphi_{\mathrm{tt}}\) is continuous.
  4. By equation (13.14), \(M_{\mathrm{x}}(b, t) - M_{\mathrm{x}}(a, t) = G I_{\mathrm{p}}\,\big(\varphi_{\mathrm{x}}(b, t) - \varphi_{\mathrm{x}}(a, t)\big)\), which is the difference of the slopes at \(b\) and at \(a\) of the graph of the rotation, between \(a\) and \(b\). By the fundamental theorem (theorem 3.40, point 1, with the antiderivative \(\varphi_{\mathrm{x}}\) and the continuous function \(\varphi_{\mathrm{xx}}\)) this is \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b G I_{\mathrm{p}}\,\varphi_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}x\).
  5. From steps 3 and 4, \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \big(\rho\,\varphi_{\mathrm{tt}} - G\,\varphi_{\mathrm{xx}}\big)\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\) for every piece, because \(I_{\mathrm{p}} > 0\) is constant.
  6. Figure 13.16 shows on the axis \(x\) a continuous function \(g\) and a point \(x_0\). Let \(x_0 \in [0, L)\) and \(t\) be arbitrary and \(g = \rho\,\varphi_{\mathrm{tt}} - G\,\varphi_{\mathrm{xx}}\), a continuous function of \(x\) at this \(t\). By step 5, \(\int_{\mathrm{x}_0}^{s} g\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\) for every \(s \in (x_0, L]\), so also \(\frac{1}{s - x_0}\int_{\mathrm{x}_0}^{s} g\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\). As \(s \to x_0\) the left side tends by the fundamental theorem (point 2) to \(g(x_0)\): the average on the longer piece still differs from \(g(x_0)\), the one on the shorter piece comes close to it. So \(g(x_0) = 0\). For \(x_0 = L\) we take the pieces \([s, L]\) with \(s \to L\). Division by \(\rho\) gives (13.15). The piece is therefore short only because we look for the equation at a point and not for an average over a piece.
  7. A fixed end has \(\varphi = 0\), because the cross section cannot rotate. For the piece \([a, L]\), \(M_{\mathrm{x}}(L, t)\) is by step 2 the moment with which an external source acts on the end. At a free end it is zero, so by equation (13.14) \(\varphi_{\mathrm{x}}(L, t) = 0\), a horizontal tangent.

Source: CK (12.13) and (12.14) (PDF p. 390): the same equation, derived with a slice of length \(\Delta x\) and a limit. The proof by steps with a piece of finite length is not from the source. Boundary conditions: CK (12.15) (PDF p. 390).

Equation (13.15) is equation (13.13) with \(u \to \varphi\) and \(E \to G\). The density \(\rho\) is the same, the polar moment \(I_{\mathrm{p}}\) cancelled. The speed \(c_{\mathrm{T}}\) (speed of torsional waves) is therefore independent of the cross section. For steel with \(G = 75{,}385\) GPa, \(c_{\mathrm{T}} = 3108{,}8\) m/s.

Before we look for the natural vibrations, let us compute what chapter 9 promised: the stiffness \(k_{\mathrm{t}}\) of the shaft as a single term of the equation with one degree of freedom.

Figure 13.17 shows what we will compute. The shaft is fixed at \(x = 0\) and a moment \(M\) acts at the free end. On the middle graph with the axes \(x\) and \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\) is the shaft moment \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\), which equals \(M\) along the whole shaft. The rotation \(\varphi\) is therefore a straight line with slope \(\varphi_{\mathrm{x}}\), reaching the value \(\varphi(L)\) at the end.

Lemma 13.17 Torsional spring constant of a shaft (togost gredi proti zasuku)

Let the shaft of definition 13.15 be fixed at \(x = 0\) and at rest, with an external moment \(M\) about the axis acting at the end \(x = L\). Then \(\varphi(x) = M\,x/(G I_{\mathrm{p}})\) and the moment per unit rotation of the end is

\[k_{\mathrm{t}} = \frac{M}{\varphi(L)} = \frac{G\,I_{\mathrm{p}}}{L} . \tag{13.16}\]
Proof

We must show that at rest the torsional moment is constant, so the rotation is linear.

  1. At rest \(H = 0\). For the piece \([a, L]\) the law of angular momentum by steps 1 and 2 of theorem 13.16 is \(0 = M - M_{\mathrm{x}}(a)\), because the moment to the right of the end is the external moment \(M\). So \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = M\) along the whole shaft.
  2. By equation (13.14) \(\varphi_{\mathrm{x}} = M/(G I_{\mathrm{p}})\) is constant. The function \(\varphi - \varphi_{\mathrm{x}}\,x\) has derivative zero, so it is constant by corollary 1.85, and as \(\varphi(0) = 0\), \(\varphi = M\,x/(G I_{\mathrm{p}})\).
  3. At \(x = L\), \(\varphi(L) = M L/(G I_{\mathrm{p}})\), so \(k_{\mathrm{t}} = M/\varphi(L) = G I_{\mathrm{p}}/L\).

Source: Not from a source. CK (12.10) (PDF p. 389) is the model it follows from.

For a steel shaft of diameter \(40\) mm and length \(1\) m, \(I_{\mathrm{p}} = \pi\cdot 0{,}04^4/32 = 2{,}5133\cdot 10^{-7}\) m\(^4\) and \(k_{\mathrm{t}} = 75{,}385\cdot 10^9\cdot 2{,}51327\cdot 10^{-7}/1 = 18\,946{,}3\) N m/rad.

13.3.3 Frequencies of the bar and the shaft

By equations (13.13) and (13.15) the bar and the shaft have the same form \(w_{\mathrm{tt}} = c^2 w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and the same end conditions: fixed end \(w = 0\), free end \(w_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\). One computation is enough. We look for vibrations in which every point oscillates with the same frequency and the shape stays the same, that is solutions of the form \(w(x, t) = U(x)\,T(t)\). Solutions with \(T(t) = \cos\omega t\) and \(\omega > 0\) are the modes of a continuous system (definition 13.6) with the equation and end conditions of the bar or the shaft. The solution with \(T = a + b\,t\), which we will meet for two free ends, is not a mode, because it has no frequency \(\omega > 0\). For the string we obtained the modes by separation of variables (theorem 13.7), and now the same procedure holds for all four pairs of ends.

Figure 13.18 shows what a solution of the form \(U(x)\,T(t)\) means. At time \(t_0\) the bar has some shape, at time \(t_1\) the same shape is smaller, at time \(t_2\) it is reversed. The shape does not change, only the number it is multiplied by does. At the bottom this number is drawn as a function of time, the curve \(T(t)\), on which the three times \(t_0\), \(t_1\) and \(t_2\) from the top are marked. The number oscillates with period \(2\pi/\omega\).

Theorem 13.18 Natural vibrations of the bar and the shaft (lastna nihanja palice in gredi)

Let \(c > 0\), \(L > 0\) and let \(w = U(x)\,T(t)\) be a solution of \(w_{\mathrm{tt}} = c^2 w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) on \([0, L] \times \mathbb{R}\) with twice continuously differentiable \(U\) and \(T\), neither of them the zero function, and let each end \(x = 0\) and \(x = L\) be fixed (\(w = 0\) for every \(t\)) or free (\(w_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\) for every \(t\)). Then either \(T(t) = a\cos\omega t + b\sin\omega t\) with \(\omega = c\,k\), \(k > 0\), and \(U\) given by the table, or only in (c) \(k = 0\), \(U = 1\) and \(T = a + b\,t\) (a rigid motion):

ends \(k\) \(U(x)\)
(a) fixed-fixed \(k_{\mathrm{r}} = r\pi/L\), \(r = 1, 2, \dots\) \(\sin k_{\mathrm{r}} x\)
(b) fixed-free \(k_{\mathrm{r}} = (2r - 1)\pi/(2L)\), \(r = 1, 2, \dots\) \(\sin k_{\mathrm{r}} x\)
(c) free-free \(k_{\mathrm{r}} = r\pi/L\), \(r = 1, 2, \dots\) \(\cos k_{\mathrm{r}} x\)
(d) free-fixed \(k_{\mathrm{r}} = (2r - 1)\pi/(2L)\), \(r = 1, 2, \dots\) \(\cos k_{\mathrm{r}} x\)

The function \(U\) is determined up to a constant factor. Conversely every such function is a solution. The frequencies are \(f_{\mathrm{r}} = \omega_{\mathrm{r}}/(2\pi) = c\,k_{\mathrm{r}}/(2\pi)\), so \(f_{\mathrm{r}} = r\,c/(2L)\) in (a) and (c) and \(f_{\mathrm{r}} = (2r - 1)\,c/(4L)\) in (b) and (d).

Proof

We must show that \(U\) satisfies an equation of second order with the two end conditions, that this problem is solved only for these values of \(k\), and that \(T\) is an oscillation with the frequency \(c k\).

  1. Let \(U(x_0) \ne 0\) and \(T(t_0) \ne 0\). The equation is \(U(x)\,T''(t) = c^2 U''(x)\,T(t)\) for all \(x\), \(t\). At \(t = t_0\) we get \(U'' = -\lambda U\) for all \(x\), where \(\lambda = -T''(t_0)/(c^2 T(t_0))\) is a number. At \(x = x_0\) the equation and \(U''(x_0) = -\lambda U(x_0)\) give \(U(x_0)\,T'' = -c^2\lambda\,U(x_0)\,T\), so \(T'' = -c^2\lambda\,T\) for all \(t\).
  2. End conditions: at a fixed end \(U(\text{end})\,T(t) = 0\) for every \(t\), and as \(T(t_0) \ne 0\), \(U = 0\) at the end. At a free end likewise \(U' = 0\) at the end.
  3. We show \(\lambda \ge 0\). By integration by parts (corollary 3.41) \(\int_0^L U'^2\,\mathrm{d}x\)\( = \big[U U'\big]_0^L - \int_0^L U U''\,\mathrm{d}x\). The bracket is zero, because at each end \(U = 0\) or \(U' = 0\) (step 2). So \(\int_0^L U'^2\,\mathrm{d}x\)\( = \lambda\)\(\int_0^L U^2\,\mathrm{d}x\). Both integrals are areas between the curve and the axis \(x\) from \(0\) to \(L\). The integrand \(U^2\) is continuous, nonnegative and not zero everywhere, so its integral is positive (lemma 6.10, point 3). The integral on the left is nonnegative, so \(\lambda \ge 0\).
  4. The case \(\lambda = 0\). Then \(\int U'^2 = 0\), so \(U' = 0\) (lemma 6.10, point 3) and \(U\) is constant by corollary 1.85, \(U = U(x_0) \ne 0\). A fixed end would give \(U = 0\), while the constant \(U_0\) differs from zero everywhere. A free end allows it, so this is possible only in (c). For \(T\), \(T'' = 0\), so \(T'\) is constant and \(T = a + b\,t\) (corollary 1.85 twice).
  5. The case \(\lambda = k^2 > 0\), \(k > 0\). The characteristic roots of \(U'' + k^2 U = 0\) are \(\pm\mathrm{i}k\), so by theorem 9.39 and corollary 9.40 \(U = A\cos kx + B\sin kx\) with real \(A\), \(B\). Figure 13.20 shows both functions over the axis \(kx\), \(\sin kx\) in blue and \(\cos kx\) in orange. At \(x = 0\) a fixed end gives \(U(0) = A = 0\), a free end \(U'(0) = kB = 0\), so \(B = 0\).
  6. At \(x = L\). (a) Fixed at \(0\) (\(A = 0\)) and at \(L\): \(B\sin kL = 0\), \(B \ne 0\) (otherwise \(U = 0\)), so \(\sin kL = 0\). By lemma 8.10 (points 2 and 3) and the addition theorem (corollary 8.15, \(\sin(\theta + \pi) = -\sin\theta\)) the zeros of the sine are exactly the multiples of \(\pi\), so \(kL = r\pi\), \(r \ge 1\). (b) Fixed at \(0\) and free at \(L\): \(U'(L) = Bk\cos kL = 0\), \(\cos kL = 0\). From \(\cos\theta = \sin(\theta + \pi/2)\) the zeros of the cosine are \(\pi/2 + (r - 1)\pi\), so \(kL = (2r - 1)\pi/2\). (c) Free at \(0\) (\(B = 0\)) and at \(L\): \(U'(L) = -Ak\sin kL = 0\), \(\sin kL = 0\), \(kL = r\pi\), \(r \ge 1\). (d) Free at \(0\) (\(B = 0\)) and fixed at \(L\): \(U(L) = A\cos kL = 0\), \(A \ne 0\), so \(\cos kL = 0\) and \(kL = (2r - 1)\pi/2\) as in (b).
  7. For \(T\), \(T'' = -c^2k^2\,T\), and by theorem 9.39 and corollary 9.40 \(T = a\cos(ck\,t) + b\sin(ck\,t)\). The frequency is \(\omega = ck\) and \(f = \omega/(2\pi)\).
  8. Conversely, for these \(k\) the function \(U\,T\) satisfies the equation, because \(U'' = -k^2 U\) and \(T'' = -c^2k^2 T\), so \(U T'' = c^2 U'' T\), and it satisfies the end conditions by step 6.

Source: CK Example 13.1 (PDF pp. 407 to 409), (13.5) to (13.8): fixed-free, with the same result for case (b) and the shape \(\sin(\ldots)\). GR (4.129) to (4.134) (PDF pp. 263 to 264) the same, GR Table 4.2 (PDF p. 264) lists for the uniform bar \(\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2\) and the mode shapes for the pairs free-free, clamped-free and clamped-clamped, which is the same as rows (a), (b) and (c) of the table of theorem 13.18, except for a different numbering at free-free (\(r - 1\) instead of \(r\)). The proof for all four pairs is ours (step 3 for \(\lambda \ge 0\) is not from the sources).

The bar of case (b), fixed-free, has the frequencies \(c/(4L)\), \(3c/(4L)\), \(5c/(4L)\). The ratios \(1 : 3 : 5\) are not the ratios \(1 : 2 : 3\) of the string (section 13.2). The cause is the end condition: a fixed end has a zero of the shape, a free end a largest displacement with slope zero (there is no force). An odd number of quarter wavelengths therefore fits into the length \(L\), \(L = (2r - 1)\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}/4\), and \(k = 2\pi/\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) gives the condition of the theorem. In (a) and (c) a whole number of half wavelengths fits into \(L\). Figure 13.21 shows the first three modes of the pairs (a), (b) and (c). Pair (d) is the mirror image of pair (b), \(\sin k_{\mathrm{r}}(L - x) = \pm\cos k_{\mathrm{r}} x\). The left column is the bar fixed at both ends, with a whole number of half wavelengths over the length \(L\). The middle column is the fixed-free bar, with an odd number of quarter wavelengths. The right column is free at both ends and again has a whole number of half wavelengths.

Example 13.19 Steel bar fixed at one end: exact and finite-element frequencies (jeklena palica, pritrjena-prosta: točne in končnoelementne frekvence)

A steel bar of length \(L = 1\) m, \(E = 196\) GPa, \(\rho = 7800\) kg/m\(^3\), fixed at \(x = 0\) and free at \(x = L\). By theorem 13.18 (b) the frequencies are \(f_{\mathrm{r}} = (2r - 1)\,c/(4L)\) with \(c = \sqrt{E/\rho} = 5012{,}80\) m/s, so \(f_1 = 1253{,}20\), \(f_2 = 3759{,}60\), \(f_3 = 6266{,}01\) Hz. The cross section does not determine the frequencies, because \(A\) cancels in equation (13.13). The ratios between the models are the same as for bar C of chapter 10, because they depend only on \(\theta_{\mathrm{r}} = k_{\mathrm{r}} h\).

The consistent mass always gives a frequency above the exact one, the lumped mass below it, and the error of the first frequency drops by a factor 4 at each halving of the element (\(n \to 2n\)). The error of the second is about 9 times larger on the same mesh (\(5{,}83\) % against \(0{,}64\) % at \(n = 4\)), because its mode has a three times larger \(\theta_{\mathrm{r}}\) and the error grows with \(\theta_{\mathrm{r}}^2\) (the expansion (10.49), the error is about \(\theta_{\mathrm{r}}^2/24\)). It also drops at each halving, first by a factor \(3{,}3\), then by \(4{,}0\) (\(5{,}83\) % to \(1{,}45\) %), once \(\theta_{\mathrm{r}}\) is small enough. These are the same percentages as in the table of chapter 10 (\(+10{,}27\) %, \(+2{,}59\) %, \(+0{,}64\) %, \(+0{,}16\) % for \(n = 1, 2, 4, 8\)).

The boundary conditions of both ends choose the frequencies. Take the bar of example 13.19, fixed at \(x = 0\) and free at \(x = L = 1\) m, \(c = 5012{,}80\) m/s, and test two frequencies that are not its own.

  1. The frequency \(c/(2L) = 2506{,}40\) Hz, that is \(k = \pi/L\) and \(U = \sin kx\), is the first frequency of a bar fixed at both ends (case (a) of theorem 13.18). It satisfies the equation and the condition \(U(0) = 0\) (step 5 of the proof). A free end requires \(U'(L) = 0\) (step 2), but here \(U'(L) = k\cos\pi = -\pi/L = -3{,}1416\) 1/m, the steepest slope that \(\sin kx\) has at all. The axial force at the free end is proportional to \(U'(L)\) and would be as large as possible, while nothing acts on the end. This frequency is not a frequency of the bar.
  2. A closer frequency: \(k\) \(10\) % above the first root, \(k = 1{,}1\cdot\pi/(2L) = 1{,}7279\) 1/m. Then \(f = c\,k/(2\pi) = 1378{,}5\) Hz against \(1253{,}20\) Hz. The free-end condition gives \(U'(L) = k\cos kL\), and \(\cos(1{,}7279) = -0{,}1564\), that is \(15{,}6\) % of the largest slope, again not zero (figure 13.22).

The condition \(\cos kL = 0\) selects \(kL = \pi/2, 3\pi/2, \dots\), where the tangent at the free end is horizontal. A \(10\) % error in \(k\) does not fulfil it approximately: the force at the free end is proportional to \(\cos kL\) and is zero only at a root. This is why the frequencies of the bar are not multiples of the frequencies of the string but odd multiples of \(c/(4L)\).

Figure 13.22 extends figure 13.21 by three values of \(kL\), each in its own row, each with the fixed end on the left and the free end (open circle) at the distance \(L\). In the first row, \(kL = \pi/2\), the sine reaches its peak exactly at \(x = L\), the tangent is horizontal and \(U'(L) = 0\). In the second, \(kL = 1{,}1\,\pi/2\), the peak is just before the end and the tangent already tilts, \(U'(L) = -0{,}156\,k\). In the third, \(kL = \pi\), this is the first frequency of a bar fixed at both ends, and the tangent falls with the largest slope \(-k\).

What is proved by this and what is not? Corollaries 10.38 and 10.39 were stated with respect to the frequencies (10.42), which chapter 10 took as given. By theorem 13.18 (b) these are really the frequencies of the bar, since \(\omega_{\mathrm{r}} = c\,k_{\mathrm{r}}\) with \(k_{\mathrm{r}} = (2r - 1)\pi/(2L)\). Both corollaries are therefore now theorems for the uniform bar, each with its own hypothesis. By corollary 10.39 the lumped mass gives no frequency above the exact one for any \(r\). By corollary 10.38 the consistent mass gives no frequency below the exact one only for \(\theta_{\mathrm{r}}^2 < 6\), that is \(\theta_{\mathrm{r}} < 2{,}449\). At \(n = 8\) these are the modes \(r = 1\) to \(6\), and for \(r = 7\) and \(8\) (\(\theta_{\mathrm{r}} = 2{,}553\) and \(2{,}945\)) the corollary says nothing. For all modes of the consistent mass this is shown by the Ritz theorem for continuous systems (section 13.9). For other bars and other elements we do not have a general theorem yet. For the lumped mass there is no such theorem: the difference \(\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}^{\mathrm{z}} - \mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\) is positive semidefinite (lemma 10.33), which gives only \(\lambda^{\mathrm{z}} \le \lambda^{\mathrm{k}}\) and does not say whether the exact value lies above the lumped one. COOK (PDF p. 397) therefore writes that lumped masses "usually, but not always" underestimate frequencies.

The free-free pair has by theorem 13.18 (c) the frequencies \(r\,c/(2L)\) and the zero frequency of the rigid motion. The bar without supports of section 10.7.6 has at \(c = 5012{,}80\) m/s the exact frequencies \(2506{,}40\) and \(5012{,}80\) Hz and the zero frequency. Both errors there are \(+10{,}27\) %, and the explanation is now this: with two elements the modes \(r = 1, 2\) have \(\theta_{\mathrm{r}} = r\pi/2\), so \(\theta = \pi/2\) (\(1 - \cos\theta = 1\)) and \(\theta = \pi\) (\(1 - \cos\theta = 2\)), and equation (10.45) gives for both the same ratio \(\lambda^{\mathrm{k}}/\omega^2 = 12/\pi^2 = 1{,}216\), which is \(1{,}1027\) in frequency. That this equation also holds for free ends we do not prove here, it was checked numerically in section 10.7.6.

13.3.4 The time \(h/c\) and the time step

Chapter 10 computed, for the time stepping, that the critical step of central differences on a bar of \(n\) equal elements of length \(h\) with lumped mass is \(h/(c\sin(\theta/2))\) with \(\theta\) close to \(\pi\), so close to \(h/c\), and promised an explanation of the number \(h/c\). The number has the unit of time: \(h\) is a length, \(c\) a speed. The explanation is that \(h/c\) is the time in which a wave crosses one element.

Let us first ask where a disturbance spreads along the bar. Let the bar be infinite, so that no wave is reflected, and let the initial displacement be \(f\) and the initial velocity zero.

Lemma 13.20 Spreading of a disturbance along the bar (razširjanje motnje po palici)

Let \(f\) be a twice continuously differentiable function on \(\mathbb{R}\) and \(c > 0\). The function

\[u(x, t) = \tfrac12\big(f(x - ct) + f(x + ct)\big) \tag{13.17}\]

satisfies equation (13.13), \(u(x, 0) = f(x)\) and \(u_{\mathrm{t}}(x, 0) = 0\). The value \(u(x, t)\) depends only on the values of \(f\) at two points, \(x - ct\) and \(x + ct\).

Proof

We must show the equation and the initial conditions with the chain rule.

  1. By the chain rule (lemma 8.29, item 1, with \(k = 1\)) \(u_{\mathrm{t}} = \tfrac{c}{2}\big(-f'(x - ct) + f'(x + ct)\big)\) and \(u_{\mathrm{tt}} = \tfrac{c^2}{2}\big(f''(x - ct) + f''(x + ct)\big)\).
  2. Likewise \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = \tfrac12\big(f'(x - ct) + f'(x + ct)\big)\) and \(u_{\mathrm{xx}} = \tfrac12\big(f''(x - ct) + f''(x + ct)\big)\), so \(u_{\mathrm{tt}} = c^2 u_{\mathrm{xx}}\).
  3. At \(t = 0\), \(u = \tfrac12(f(x) + f(x)) = f(x)\) and \(u_{\mathrm{t}} = \tfrac{c}{2}(-f'(x) + f'(x)) = 0\).
  4. The dependence on two points is immediate from the form (13.17).

Source: Not from a source. KREYSZIG §12.4 (PDF pp. 579 to 582) knows this form for strings, the equation is the same.

The disturbance \(f\) therefore splits into two halves that travel in opposite directions with the speed \(c\): the position of the half that started at \(x_1\) is \(x_1 \pm ct\). The time in which a half crosses an element of length \(h\) is \(h/c\). For \(h = 62{,}5\) mm and \(c = 5012{,}80\) m/s, which is the element of bar C of chapter 10 at \(n_{\mathrm{e}} = 16\), \(h/c = 12{,}468\ \mu\)s, and the critical step from the table computed there is \(12{,}483\ \mu\)s.

Now let us look at the scheme. With lumped mass, for an interior node, by definition 10.68, with row \(j\) of the matrix \(\mat M^{\mathrm{z}}\), which is \(\rho A h\) times the identity (definition 10.32), and the row of the matrix \(\mat K\) from step 1 of the proof of theorem 10.36,

\[q_{\mathrm{j}}^{k+1} = 2q_{\mathrm{j}}^{k} - q_{\mathrm{j}}^{k-1} + \Big(\frac{c\,\Delta t}{h}\Big)^2\big(q_{\mathrm{j}-1}^{k} - 2q_{\mathrm{j}}^{k} + q_{\mathrm{j}+1}^{k}\big) , \tag{13.18}\]

where \(q_{\mathrm{j}}^k\) is the displacement of the node \(x_{\mathrm{j}} = jh\) at time \(k\,\Delta t\). Each new displacement at node \(j\) needs the old displacements at \(j - 1\), \(j\) and \(j + 1\). By induction on \(k\): \(q_{\mathrm{j}}^k\) depends only on the initial values at the nodes \(i\) with \(|i - j| \le k\). Information in the scheme therefore moves by at most one element per step, that is \(h\) in the time \(\Delta t\).

Let us compare with lemma 13.20. At time \(t = k\,\Delta t\) the scheme has seen only points up to the distance \(k h = (h/\Delta t)\,t\), while the solution (13.17) depends on points at the distance \(c\,t\). If \(\Delta t > h/c\), then \(c > h/\Delta t\) and \(c\,t > k h\): the solution depends on the point \(x + ct\), which the scheme does not see. Take an initial displacement \(f\) that is zero on \([x - kh, x + kh]\) and at \(x - ct\), but not zero at \(x + ct\). Such an \(f\) exists because \(ct > kh\): a twice continuously differentiable bump around \(x + ct\), for example \((s - \alpha)^3(\beta - s)^3\) on \([\alpha, \beta]\) and zero elsewhere, narrow enough not to reach \(x + kh\). The scheme gives the value zero at \(x\), the solution (13.17) gives \(\tfrac12 f(x + ct) \ne 0\). We refine the mesh at a fixed \(t\) and a fixed ratio \(\Delta t/h\), so that \(x\) is a node of all meshes (for example \(x = 0\)) and \(t/\Delta t\) is an integer. Then \(kh = (h/\Delta t)\,t\) is the same on all meshes and the same \(f\) works for all of them. For \(\Delta t > h/c\) the scheme therefore does not converge to the solution (13.17), however fine the mesh is. The step must not be larger than about \(h/c\), which agrees with the critical step \(h/(c\sin(\theta/2))\) of chapter 10, which for lumped mass is always a little above \(h/c\) and approaches it with a finer mesh (table there: \(50{,}849\) against \(49{,}872\ \mu\)s at \(n_{\mathrm{e}} = 4\) and \(3{,}117\) against \(3{,}117\ \mu\)s at \(n_{\mathrm{e}} = 64\)).

What is proved here and what only explained? Proved are the dependence of the scheme on its neighbours (induction) and the dependence of the solution (13.17) on the points \(x \pm ct\) (lemma), and with them that the scheme does not converge to this solution for \(\Delta t > h/c\). Only explained is that this is the only solution with the given initial conditions (the book has no uniqueness theorem for the infinite bar), that non-convergence means instability (this is the theorem on the equivalence of convergence and stability, which the book does not have), and that the same judgement holds for a finite bar with reflections. For the consistent mass the argument does not hold, because the matrix \(\mat M^{-1}\) is not diagonal and one step couples all nodes. The critical step \(h/(c\sqrt3)\) of chapter 10 is stricter, and why exactly by the factor \(\sqrt3\) we do not explain here.

13.3.5 A shaft with a disk at its end

A drive is often not a shaft alone but a shaft with a mass at its end. Chapter 9 replaced such a system, a shaft with a disk, by the equation \(J\ddot\varphi + k_{\mathrm{t}}\varphi = M_{\mathrm{t}}\), in which the shaft contributes only its stiffness and has no mass. Now we have \(k_{\mathrm{t}} = G I_{\mathrm{p}}/L\) (lemma 13.17) and can ask: how large is the error from neglecting the mass of the shaft?

At the end \(x = L\) of a shaft fixed at \(x = 0\) let a thin disk (disk) with moment of inertia \(J_{\mathrm{d}}\) about the axis of the shaft be rigidly attached (figure 13.23). On the left is the shaft, fixed in the wall, of length \(L\), and at its end the disk. On the right is the disk alone. The disk rotates by the angle \(\varphi(L, t)\) of the end section and turns as a rigid body about its own axis, which passes through its centre of mass. By corollary 8.75, \(J_{\mathrm{d}}\,\varphi_{\mathrm{tt}}(L, t)\) therefore equals the moment of all forces on it about this axis. The shaft acts on the disk with the moment \(-M_{\mathrm{x}}(L, t)\), because the disk acts on the shaft with \(M_{\mathrm{x}}(L, t)\) by the definition of the torsional moment (figure 13.23, right). In the figure this is the moment \(-M_{\mathrm{x}}(L, t)\) that turns the disk, and the angular acceleration that the disk gets from it. The equation of motion of the disk is therefore

\[J_{\mathrm{d}}\,\varphi_{\mathrm{tt}}(L, t) = -G I_{\mathrm{p}}\,\varphi_{\mathrm{x}}(L, t) . \tag{13.19}\]

This is a dynamic boundary condition, as for a bar with a point mass at its end (CK Example 12.1 (PDF pp. 387 to 388) and Example 12.2 (PDF p. 391) for a shaft with a disk). For \(J_{\mathrm{d}} \to 0\) it goes over into a free end.

Theorem 13.21 Frequency equation of a shaft with a disk (frekvenčna enačba gredi z diskom)

Let the shaft of definition 13.15 be fixed at \(x = 0\) and carry at \(x = L\) a disk with moment of inertia \(J_{\mathrm{d}} > 0\) with the boundary condition (13.19), and let \(\mu = \rho I_{\mathrm{p}} L/J_{\mathrm{d}}\). Let \(\varphi = U(x)\,T(t)\) be a solution of equation (13.15) with twice continuously differentiable \(U\) and \(T\), neither of them the zero function. Then \(U = B\sin\beta x\) with \(\beta > 0\) and \(s = \beta L\) satisfies

\[s\,\sin s = \mu\,\cos s , \tag{13.20}\]

\(T = a\cos\omega t + b\sin\omega t\) with \(\omega = c_{\mathrm{T}}\,\beta\). Equation (13.20) has in each interval \(\big((r - 1)\pi,\ (r - 1)\pi + \pi/2\big)\), \(r = 1, 2, \dots\), exactly one solution \(s_{\mathrm{r}}\) and none elsewhere on \((0, \infty)\). The natural frequencies are \(f_{\mathrm{r}} = c_{\mathrm{T}}\,s_{\mathrm{r}}/(2\pi L)\). Conversely every such function is a solution.

Proof

We must show that step 1 of theorem 13.18 gives the same \(U'' = -\lambda U\), that the disk turns the condition into an equation for \(\lambda\), and that this equation has exactly the stated roots.

  1. As in step 1 of theorem 13.18 (with \(c = c_{\mathrm{T}}\), equation (13.15)), \(U'' = -\lambda U\) and \(T'' = -c_{\mathrm{T}}^2\lambda\,T\). The fixed end gives \(U(0) = 0\).
  2. Equation (13.19) gives \(J_{\mathrm{d}}\,U(L)\,T'' = -G I_{\mathrm{p}}\,U'(L)\,T\). We insert \(T'' = -c_{\mathrm{T}}^2\lambda T\), divide by \(T(t_0) \ne 0\) and use \(c_{\mathrm{T}}^2 = G/\rho\) to get \(U'(L) = \ell_{\mathrm{d}}\,\lambda\,U(L)\), where \(\ell_{\mathrm{d}} = J_{\mathrm{d}}/(\rho I_{\mathrm{p}})\) is the length of the shaft whose mass has the moment of inertia \(J_{\mathrm{d}}\).
  3. We show \(\lambda > 0\). Integration by parts (corollary 3.41) and \(U(0) = 0\) give \(\int_0^L U'^2\,\mathrm{d}x = U(L)\,U'(L) - \int_0^L U U''\,\mathrm{d}x = \lambda\big(\ell_{\mathrm{d}}\,U(L)^2 + \int_0^L U^2\,\mathrm{d}x\big)\). The bracket is positive, because \(U\) is not zero and is continuous (lemma 6.10, point 3). So \(\lambda \ge 0\). For \(\lambda = 0\) we would have \(\int U'^2 = 0\), \(U' = 0\) and \(U = U(0) = 0\), which is excluded. So \(\lambda = \beta^2 > 0\).
  4. As in step 5 of theorem 13.18 and because of \(U(0) = 0\), \(U = B\sin\beta x\), \(B \ne 0\). The condition of step 2 is \(B\beta\cos\beta L = \ell_{\mathrm{d}}\beta^2 B\sin\beta L\), so \(\cos s = \ell_{\mathrm{d}}\beta\,\sin s\). Since \(\ell_{\mathrm{d}}\beta = (L/\mu)(s/L) = s/\mu\) (\(\mu = L/\ell_{\mathrm{d}}\)), we get (13.20).
  5. There are no roots at the discontinuities of the tangent, because at \(\cos s = 0\) the equation gives \(s\sin s = 0\) with \(\sin s = \pm 1\), which is impossible for \(s > 0\). For \(s = (r - 1)\pi + \sigma\), \(\sigma \in (0, \pi]\), the addition theorem gives \(\sin s = (-1)^{r-1}\sin\sigma\) and \(\cos s = (-1)^{r-1}\cos\sigma\). The left side minus the right side is therefore \((-1)^{r-1}\,q_{\mathrm{r}}(\sigma)\) with \(q_{\mathrm{r}}(\sigma) = \big((r - 1)\pi + \sigma\big)\sin\sigma - \mu\cos\sigma\).
  6. On \([\pi/2, \pi]\), \(\sin\sigma \ge 0\) and \(\cos\sigma \le 0\) (lemma 8.10, point 3, and \(\cos(\pi/2) = 0\)), so both terms of \(q_{\mathrm{r}}\) are nonnegative. The sum is zero only if both are zero, that is \(\sin\sigma = 0\) and \(\cos\sigma = 0\), which is not possible at once (point 1 of the lemma). So \(q_{\mathrm{r}} > 0\) and there are no roots there.
  7. On \((0, \pi/2)\), \(q_{\mathrm{r}}'(\sigma) = (1 + \mu)\sin\sigma + \big((r - 1)\pi + \sigma\big)\cos\sigma > 0\) (rules of differentiation, lemma 1.81, derivative of sine and cosine, theorem 8.12, and positivity from lemma 8.10), so by the mean value theorem (theorem 1.84) \(q_{\mathrm{r}}\) strictly increases. As \(q_{\mathrm{r}}(0) = -\mu < 0\) and \(q_{\mathrm{r}}(\pi/2) = (r - 1)\pi + \pi/2 > 0\), by the intermediate value theorem (theorem 2.57) it has exactly one zero \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}}\) in \((0, \pi/2)\) and \(s_{\mathrm{r}} = (r - 1)\pi + \sigma_{\mathrm{r}}\).
  8. \(T\) and the converse as in steps 7 and 8 of theorem 13.18, with \(\omega = c_{\mathrm{T}}\beta\).

Source: CK Example 12.2 (PDF p. 391) for the boundary condition of a shaft with a disk, and the same form as for a bar with a mass (CK Example 12.1, PDF pp. 387 to 388). The frequency equation and the proof about the roots are ours, not from a source.

The blackwhite curve is \(s\tan s\), which has one branch between neighbouring verticals. The grey dashed verticals are at \((2r - 1)\pi/2\), where \(\tan s\) grows beyond all bounds from the left. The blue horizontal at height \(\mu\) is the right-hand side of the equation. The roots are the intersections of the horizontal with the curve, and their vertical segments to the axis are drawn too. Corollary 13.22 Shaft with a disk: the one degree of freedom limit (omejitev na eno prostostno stopnjo)

For the shaft with a disk of theorem 13.21 let \(\omega_1 = c_{\mathrm{T}}\,s_1/L\) be the first natural circular frequency and \(\omega_{\mathrm{1DOF}} = \sqrt{k_{\mathrm{t}}/J_{\mathrm{d}}}\) the frequency of the one degree of freedom model of chapter 9 with the stiffness \(k_{\mathrm{t}} = G I_{\mathrm{p}}/L\) (lemma 13.17). Then:

  1. \(\omega_{\mathrm{1DOF}} = c_{\mathrm{T}}\sqrt\mu/L\) and \(\omega_1 < \omega_{\mathrm{1DOF}}\).
  2. For \(\mu \to \infty\), \(s_{\mathrm{r}} \to\) \((2r - 1)\pi/2\), which are the frequencies of the shaft without a disk (theorem 13.18 (b)).
Proof

We must show the equality, then the bound with the function \(q_1\) from the proof of theorem 13.21, then the limit.

  1. From \(c_{\mathrm{T}}^2 = G/\rho\), \(k_{\mathrm{t}}/J_{\mathrm{d}} = G I_{\mathrm{p}}/(L J_{\mathrm{d}}) = (G/\rho)\cdot\rho I_{\mathrm{p}}/(L J_{\mathrm{d}}) = c_{\mathrm{T}}^2\mu/L^2\), so \(\omega_{\mathrm{1DOF}} = c_{\mathrm{T}}\sqrt\mu/L\).
  2. For \(r = 1\), \(q_1(\sigma) = \sigma\sin\sigma - \mu\cos\sigma\) is strictly increasing on \([0, \pi/2]\) (step 7 of the proof) with the zero \(s_1\). If \(\sqrt\mu \ge \pi/2\), then \(s_1 < \pi/2 \le \sqrt\mu\). If \(\sqrt\mu < \pi/2\), let \(g(t) = \sin t - t\cos t\), \(g(0) = 0\) and \(g'(t) = t\sin t > 0\) on \((0, \pi/2)\), so by theorem 1.84 \(g(t) = g'(\xi)\,t > 0\) for \(0 < t \le \pi/2\), with \(\xi \in (0, t)\). Then \(q_1(\sqrt\mu) = \sqrt\mu\,g(\sqrt\mu) > 0 = q_1(s_1)\), and as \(q_1\) increases, \(s_1 < \sqrt\mu\). Division by \(L/c_{\mathrm{T}}\) gives \(\omega_1 < \omega_{\mathrm{1DOF}}\).
  3. For the roots \(s_{\mathrm{r}} = (r - 1)\pi + \sigma_{\mathrm{r}}\) of (13.20), \(\cos\sigma_{\mathrm{r}} = (s_{\mathrm{r}}/\mu)\sin\sigma_{\mathrm{r}}\), so \(0 < \cos\sigma_{\mathrm{r}} \le s_{\mathrm{r}}/\mu < r\pi/\mu\), which tends to zero as \(\mu \to \infty\). Since the cosine on \([0, \pi/2]\) is strictly decreasing with a zero at \(\pi/2\) (lemma 8.10, point 3), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}} \to \pi/2\).

Source: Not from a source.

Point 1 gives the direction of the error: the one degree of freedom model overestimates the frequency. The reason is the inertia of the shaft, which the model neglected: an added moment of inertia lowers the frequency. By how much is told by a calculation with an expansion in \(\mu\).

Example 13.23 Steel shaft with a disk (jeklena gred z diskom)

The shaft is steel, \(G = 75{,}385\) GPa, \(\rho = 7800\) kg/m\(^3\), \(d = 40\) mm, \(L = 1\) m, fixed at \(x = 0\), with a disk \(J_{\mathrm{d}} = 0{,}05\) kg m\(^2\) at \(x = L\). Without the disk the shaft would by theorem 13.18 (b) have the frequencies \(f_{\mathrm{r}} = (2r - 1)\,c_{\mathrm{T}}/(4L)\) with \(c_{\mathrm{T}} = 3108{,}8\) m/s, so \(f_1 = 777{,}2\), \(f_2 = 2331{,}6\), \(f_3 = 3886{,}0\) Hz, for every diameter. With the disk, by lemma 13.17, \(k_{\mathrm{t}} = 18\,946{,}3\) N m/rad and \(\rho I_{\mathrm{p}} L = 7800\cdot 2{,}5133\cdot 10^{-7}\cdot 1 = 1{,}9604\cdot 10^{-3}\) kg m\(^2\), which is the moment of inertia of the shaft about its axis, so \(\mu = 1{,}9604\cdot 10^{-3}/0{,}05 = 0{,}03921\). The root of equation (13.20) in \((0, \pi/2)\) is \(s_1 = 0{,}19672\) (SymPy), so \(f_1 = c_{\mathrm{T}} s_1/(2\pi L) = 97{,}34\) Hz. The second root is \(s_2 = 3{,}1540\), \(f_2 = 1560{,}6\) Hz.

The one degree of freedom model gives \(\omega_{\mathrm{1DOF}} = \sqrt{k_{\mathrm{t}}/J_{\mathrm{d}}} = 615{,}57\) 1/s, so \(f = 97{,}97\) Hz. This is \(0{,}65\) % above \(97{,}34\) Hz, as corollary 13.22 claims. The leading term of the expansion of the root in \(\mu\) is \(s_1 = \sqrt\mu\,(1 - \mu/6 + \dots)\) (SymPy, we neither prove the expansion nor bound its remainder here, so this is a numerical observation), and \(\mu/6 = 0{,}65\) %, which is the same as adding a third of the moment of inertia of the shaft to the disk, \((1 + \mu/3)^{-1/2} \approx 1 - \mu/6\). Checked numerically (nzs_palica_gred.out): at \(\mu = 0{,}0392\) we have \(s_1/\sqrt\mu = 0{,}993513\) and \(1 - \mu/6 = 0{,}993467\), a difference of \(4{,}7\cdot 10^{-5}\) (about \(0{,}03\,\mu^2\)). The two quantities we compare agree even better: \(\omega_{\mathrm{1DOF}}/\omega_1 - 1 = 0{,}6530\) % and \(\mu/6 = 0{,}6535\) %, a difference of \(0{,}0004\) percentage points. The reason is that the terms with \(\mu^2\) nearly cancel: from \(s_1 = \sqrt\mu\,(1 - \mu/6 + 11\mu^2/360 + \dots)\) we get \(\omega_{\mathrm{1DOF}}/\omega_1 = \sqrt\mu/s_1 = 1 + \mu/6 - \mu^2/360 + \dots\) (SymPy). At \(\mu = 1\) the difference is already \(0{,}43\) percentage points (\(16{,}23\) % against \(16{,}67\) %).

For this disk the one degree of freedom model is wrong by \(0{,}65\) %, because the moment of inertia of the shaft about its axis is only \(3{,}9\) % of the moment of inertia of the disk (\(\mu = 0{,}0392\)). The model has only one frequency, and the higher modes of the shaft that it neglected are far away: \(f_2 = 1560{,}6\) Hz is about 16 times higher than \(f_1\).
