Chapter 3 Constitutive equations

3.2 Hooke's law in space

The tension test of section 3.1 gave us two numbers for every material. Young's modulus \(E\) (definition 3.1) tells us which stress along the bar causes a given strain along the bar, \(\sigma = E\,\varepsilon\). Poisson's ratio \(\nu\) (definition 3.2) tells us by what fraction of this strain the bar gets thinner across. For steel \(E = 196\) GPa and \(\nu = 0{,}3\) (BOWER, Table 3.1, PDF p. 100). But the test knows only one stress, the stress along the bar. At a point of a body the stress is described by six numbers, three normal and three shear stress components (definition 2.25 and theorem 2.36 on the symmetry of stress). The strain is described by six numbers as well, the components of the small strain tensor (definition 1.115). Our examples have shown all kinds. The sheared cube of section 1.6.2 had only a shear strain, in still water all three normal stresses are equal (section 2.6.6), and in the hanging bar a single stress comes with three normal strains. The question of this section is: how do all six strain components at a point depend on all six stress components at the same point?

We will build the answer from tension tests alone, without new measurements. First we say for what kind of material the answer will hold. Then we look at what the tension test says about all six strain components, and on the way we see what it means that a material is the same in all directions. Next we add three tensions in three directions and get from this the response to an even pressure. Pure shear we split into a tension and a compression along the diagonals. Together this gives the relation between stresses and strains, which we test on the hanging bar. At the end we look at how the relation splits into a part that changes the volume and a part that changes the shape, and where it stops holding.

3.2.1 The model and its assumptions

We look at a small piece of material around a point \(P\) inside a body. We choose a rectangular Cartesian coordinate system with the axes \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\). The stress at \(P\) is described by the stress components \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\), ..., \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\), where \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ab}}\) is the component \(b\) of the traction vector on the face with the normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{a}}\). By theorem 2.36 on the symmetry of stress, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zy}}\), so only six of them are different. The strain of the material close to \(P\) is described by the components of the small strain tensor ((1.51)),

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial x} , \qquad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial y} , \qquad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \frac12\left(\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial x}\right)\]

and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}\) built in the same way. Here too \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yx}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zx}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}\). We look for a rule whose input is the six stress components at \(P\) and whose output is the six strain components at \(P\). Such a rule for a given material is called a constitutive equation (konstitutivna enačba), hence the name of the chapter.

We will derive the rule for a material for which these assumptions hold.

  1. Elasticity. The strain close to \(P\) depends only on the stress at \(P\) at this moment, not on the stress elsewhere, and not on how fast and along which path the material was loaded. Without stress there is no strain: we measure strains from the state before loading, in which the material carries no stress. Most materials behave like this in the tension test as long as the stresses are small enough (BOWER §3.2.1, PDF p. 96). The stresses must therefore stay below the yield stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{Y}}\) (definition 2.7), above which the material deforms permanently.
  2. Linearity. Definition 3.3 requires stress and strain to be proportional in the tension test. For loading in several directions we extend this as follows. If the stress state \(A\) causes the strains \(a\) and the stress state \(B\) the strains \(b\), then both states together cause the strains \(a + b\), and \(c\) times the state \(A\) causes \(c\) times the strains \(a\), for every number \(c\), also a negative one. We add states by adding their corresponding stress components, which means that the forces on each face of the piece add up. That it also holds when the material is loaded in several directions at once is an assumption of the model, confirmed by tests for many materials at small strains (SADD §4.1-4.2, PDF p. 91). With it we will build complicated stress states from simple ones.
  3. Isotropy. Definition 3.4 requires that bars cut in different directions give the same \(E\) and \(\nu\) in the tension test. For loading in several directions we extend this as follows: the rule that gives the strain components from the stress components is the same in every rectangular Cartesian coordinate system, with the same two numbers \(E\) and \(\nu\), however the axes are turned (SADD §4.2, PDF p. 92, CM §6.3.5, PDF p. 266). Most metals and many plastics behave like this, because the tiny crystals and grains they consist of point in random directions (SADD, PDF p. 92). Wood and materials reinforced with fibres are not isotropic. We will use isotropy in the proofs of two lemmas and say each time where.
  4. Small strains and small rotations. All nine partial derivatives \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial x\), \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial y\), ..., \(\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}/\partial z\) are smaller in size than about \(0{,}001\). Then the small strain tensor describes the changes of lengths and right angles well (section 1.17). Also the stress computed per area before loading and the stress computed per area after loading differ for steel by less than a tenth of a percent, so we do not distinguish them (section 2.13). For a large rotation the small strain tensor shows a strain even where no length changes, as for the rigid rotation in section 1.17, and the rule we will derive no longer holds there (BOWER §3.2.2, PDF p. 97).
  5. Constant temperature. A heated material expands even without stress. How to add this we will see in section 3.3.

We do not need homogeneity. The material may have different \(E\) and \(\nu\) at different points (definition 3.5), and the rule holds at each point with the two numbers there. In our examples the steel is homogeneous.

3.2.2 Tension in one direction

Let us start with the stress we know from the tension test. In a bar pulled by an axial force the only nonzero stress component is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma\), if the axis \(x\) lies along the bar (section 2.6.6). Such a state is called uniaxial stress (enoosna napetost). The tension test gives two of the six strain components. By the definition of Young's modulus the strain along the bar is \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma/E\), and by the definition of Poisson's ratio the lateral strain is \(-\nu\) times this, \(-\nu\,\sigma/E\). Two questions remain. Does the bar get thinner in the direction of the axis \(y\) by as much as in the direction of the axis \(z\)? Are the shear strains really zero, or does one of the right angles between fibres along the axes change after all? The test measures only the change of length and thickness, so it does not answer these questions. In a material that is not the same in all directions, for example a plate with fibres pulled at a slant to the fibres, the right angles do change (CM, Fig. 6.3.4, PDF pp. 267-268). The answer comes from isotropy.

Figure 3.10 shows the idea. We pull a cube of the material in the direction of the axis \(x\). Let us watch two fibres in the plane of the axes \(x\) and \(y\), along the diagonals with the unit vectors \(\vek n_1 = (1, 1, 0)/\sqrt2\) and \(\vek n_2 = (1, -1, 0)/\sqrt2\). By (1.53) their strains are

\[\varepsilon(\vek n_1) \approx \tfrac12\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \tfrac12\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} , \qquad \varepsilon(\vek n_2) \approx \tfrac12\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \tfrac12\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} - \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} .\]

Suppose that \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} > 0\). Then the fibre along \(\vek n_1\) would stretch more than the fibre along \(\vek n_2\) (figure 3.10, middle). Now we turn the cube together with the forces by half a turn about the axis \(x\). The forces on the left and right faces still pull in the direction of the axis \(x\), so the loading is the same as before. The material is the same in all directions, so the turned cube is the same test, and again the fibre along \(\vek n_1\) would have to stretch more. But the turn has carried the more stretched fibre from the direction \(\vek n_1\) to the direction \(\vek n_2\) (figure 3.10, right). The same test cannot have two different outcomes, so \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\). Every turn that leaves the loading unchanged gives such a condition. We write this precisely with the components in turned axes, which we met in section 2.9.

Lemma 3.6 Uniaxial stress in an isotropic material (enoosna napetost v izotropnem materialu)

Let the material at the point \(P\) satisfy assumptions 1 to 5 of section 3.2.1: elasticity, linearity, isotropy (the rule that gives the strain components from the stress components is the same in every rectangular Cartesian coordinate system, with the same \(E\) and \(\nu\)), small strains and constant temperature. Here \(E\) is Young's modulus of the material and \(\nu\) its Poisson's ratio. Let the only nonzero stress component at the point \(P\) in some rectangular Cartesian coordinate system be \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma\). Then the strain components at \(P\) in this system are

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \frac{\sigma}{E} , \qquad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = -\nu\,\frac{\sigma}{E} , \qquad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0 . \tag{3.6}\]
Proof

We take the components \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\) from the tension test and get the others with two turns about the axis \(x\) that leave the loading unchanged.

  1. In the bar of the tension test the only nonzero stress component is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) (section 2.6.6). By definition 3.1 the strain of the bar along its axis is \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma/E\). By assumption 1 (elasticity) the strain close to a point depends only on the stress there, so the same holds at \(P\). For compression, \(\sigma < 0\), the same follows by assumption 2 (linearity) with the factor \(-1\).
  2. By definition 3.2 the lateral strain is \(-\nu\) times the strain along the bar. For the lateral direction \(y\) we therefore have \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = -\nu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = -\nu\,\sigma/E\).
  3. Take new axes with the unit vectors \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{x}} = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{y}} = -\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{z}} = -\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), that is, axes turned by half a turn about the axis \(x\). By definition 2.37 the rows of the rotation matrix are the components of these vectors in the old axes, so \(\mat Q\) is diagonal with the entries \(Q_{\mathrm{xx}} = 1\), \(Q_{\mathrm{yy}} = -1\) and \(Q_{\mathrm{zz}} = -1\).
  4. By theorem 2.42 on the transformation of stress components, the stress components in the new axes are \(\bar\sigma_{\mathrm{kl}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} Q_{\mathrm{ki}}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,Q_{\mathrm{lj}}\), where the indices \(i\) and \(j\) run over \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\) ((2.44)). Since \(\mat Q\) is diagonal, \(Q_{\mathrm{ki}}\) is nonzero only for \(i = k\) and \(Q_{\mathrm{lj}}\) only for \(j = l\), so a single term remains, \(\bar\sigma_{\mathrm{kl}} = Q_{\mathrm{kk}}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{kl}}\,Q_{\mathrm{ll}}\). The only nonzero component is \(\bar\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 1 \cdot \sigma \cdot 1 = \sigma\). In the new axes the stress has the same components as in the old ones.
  5. By lemma 2.44 on the transformation of strain components the same rule holds for strains, \(\bar{\ten\varepsilon} = \mat Q\,\ten\varepsilon\,\mat Q^\T\), and by the same argument as in step 4, \(\bar\varepsilon_{\mathrm{kl}} = Q_{\mathrm{kk}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{kl}}\,Q_{\mathrm{ll}}\). Therefore \(\bar\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 1 \cdot \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} \cdot (-1) = -\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\) and \(\bar\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = -\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}\).
  6. Here we use isotropy (assumption 3). The rule that gives the strain components from the stress components is the same in the new axes as in the old ones. By step 4 the stress components in the new axes are the same numbers as in the old ones, so the strain components in the new axes are also the same numbers as in the old ones: \(\bar\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\) and \(\bar\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}\). With step 5, \(-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\), so \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\), and likewise \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = 0\).
  7. Now take the axes turned by a quarter turn about the axis \(x\): \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{x}} = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{y}} = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) and \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{z}} = -\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\). The rows of the rotation matrix are \((1, 0, 0)\), \((0, 0, 1)\) and \((0, -1, 0)\). Each row has a single nonzero entry: \(Q_{\mathrm{xx}} = 1\), \(Q_{\mathrm{yz}} = 1\) and \(Q_{\mathrm{zy}} = -1\).
  8. In the sum of step 4, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\) is nonzero only for \(i = j = x\), so \(\bar\sigma_{\mathrm{kl}} = Q_{\mathrm{kx}}\,\sigma\,Q_{\mathrm{lx}}\). The entry \(Q_{\mathrm{kx}}\) is nonzero only for \(k = x\), so again the only nonzero component is \(\bar\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma\).
  9. By the lemma on the transformation of strain components, \(\bar\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} Q_{\mathrm{yi}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\,Q_{\mathrm{yj}}\). In the row \(y\) only \(Q_{\mathrm{yz}} = 1\) is nonzero, so \(\bar\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\). Likewise \(\bar\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = Q_{\mathrm{yz}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zy}}\,Q_{\mathrm{zy}} = -\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zy}} = -\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}\), since the small strain tensor is symmetric.
  10. Again we use isotropy as in step 6: \(\bar\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\) and \(\bar\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}\). Therefore \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = -\nu\,\sigma/E\) and \(-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}\), so \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\).

Source: SADD §4.3.1 (PDF p. 95), CM (6.3.40) (PDF p. 267). The sources read the result off the general law for an isotropic material (SADD (4.2.10), PDF p. 93), which we are only building here. The argument with turns is not from the sources.

The proof has shown what isotropy demands of a material. A turn that leaves the loading unchanged must not change the strain either. The strain components whose sign such a turn reverses must therefore be zero, and the components that the turn exchanges must be equal. For a bar pulled along the axis \(x\) such turns are the half turn and the quarter turn about this axis. In steel with \(\nu = 0{,}3\) a piece that gets longer by a thousandth along the axis \(x\) gets shorter by \(0{,}3\) thousandths in all lateral directions, and the right angles between fibres along the axes stay right.

3.2.3 Three tensions at once

What if we pull the piece in the directions of all three axes at once, with different stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\), and there are no shear stress components? Such a state is the sum of three uniaxial stresses, and by linearity we add their strains (figure 3.11).

  1. We split the state into three. In the first the only nonzero stress component is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), in the second \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\), in the third \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\). The sum of the corresponding components of all three is the given state.
  2. By lemma 3.6 on uniaxial stress the first state gives the strains \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/E\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = -\nu\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/E\) and no shear strains.
  3. The lemma holds in every rectangular Cartesian coordinate system, and we choose the names of the axes ourselves. If we rename the axis \(y\) to \(x\), the axis \(z\) to \(y\) and the axis \(x\) to \(z\), the only nonzero stress component of the second state is the one along the first axis. The second state therefore gives \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}/E\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = -\nu\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}/E\) and no shear strains.
  4. In the same way the third state gives \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}/E\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = -\nu\,\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}/E\) and no shear strains.
  5. By linearity we add the strains of all three states. The component \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) receives \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/E\) from the first state, \(-\nu\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}/E\) from the second and \(-\nu\,\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}/E\) from the third, and likewise the other two:
    \[\begin{aligned} \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} &= \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} - \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}})}{E} , \\ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} &= \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} - \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}})}{E} , \\ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} &= \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} - \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}})}{E} . \end{aligned} \tag{3.7}\]
    The shear strains are sums of zeros, so they are zero.

Each normal stress stretches the material in its own direction and makes it thinner in the other two. A tension in a lateral direction therefore reduces the thinning. A steel cube pulled with the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 100\) MPa in one direction only has, by (3.7), the strain

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \frac{100 \cdot 10^6\ \text{Pa}}{196 \cdot 10^9\ \text{Pa}} = 5{,}102 \cdot 10^{-4} .\]

If we also pull it with the same stress in the direction of the axis \(y\), then

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \frac{(100 - 0{,}3 \cdot 100) \cdot 10^6\ \text{Pa}}{196 \cdot 10^9\ \text{Pa}} = 3{,}571 \cdot 10^{-4} ,\]

\(30\) % less, because the tension in the direction of the axis \(y\) makes the cube thinner in the direction of the axis \(x\) by \(0{,}3\) times its own strain. In the direction of the axis \(z\), in which the cube is not pulled, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = -0{,}3 \cdot (100 + 100) \cdot 10^6\ \text{Pa}/(196 \cdot 10^9\ \text{Pa}) = -3{,}061 \cdot 10^{-4}\), because both tensions make it thinner. In figure 3.11 the stresses are in the ratio \(3 : 2 : 1\), and the edge along the axis \(z\) gets shorter although it is pulled: its own tension lengthens it by \(1\) part, the tensions in the other two directions shorten it by \(0{,}3 \cdot (3 + 2) = 1{,}5\) parts.

3.2.4 Even pressure and the bulk modulus

In still water the stress at every point is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = -p\), and there are no shear components (section 2.6.6). We sink a small steel cube with the edge \(h = 10\) mm into the water. The water pushes on each of its faces perpendicularly, with the pressure \(p\). We assume that the stress in the whole cube is the same too, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = -p\) without shear components. This state agrees with the forces of the water on all six faces, since by Cauchy's formula the traction vector on a face with the outward normal \(\vek n\) is \(-p\,\vek n\), exactly the force of the water per unit area. It also satisfies the equilibrium equations of section 2.7, since its components do not change from point to point if we neglect the weight of the cube. The weight of the cube changes the stress between its top and bottom by \(\rho g h = 7800 \cdot 9{,}81 \cdot 0{,}01\) Pa \(= 765\) Pa, which we will compare with the pressure below. By how much does the cube shrink?

  1. We put \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = -p\) into (3.7):
    \[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \frac{-p - \nu\,(-p - p)}{E} = -\frac{(1 - 2\nu)\,p}{E} ,\]
    and likewise \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\). There are no shear strains.
  2. All edges get shorter by the same fraction, and the right angles stay right. The cube stays a cube, only smaller (figure 3.12).
  3. By how much does the volume decrease? A piece in the shape of a box with the edges \(\Delta x\), \(\Delta y\) and \(\Delta z\) along the axes, which has only normal strains, has after loading the edges \((1 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}})\,\Delta x\), \((1 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}})\,\Delta y\) and \((1 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}})\,\Delta z\), which are still perpendicular. Its volume therefore changes from \(V_0 = \Delta x\,\Delta y\,\Delta z\) to
    \[V_1 = V_0\,(1 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}})(1 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}})(1 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}) .\]
  4. We multiply out the brackets, multiplying each term of the first by each term of the second and then by the third:
    \[\frac{V_1 - V_0}{V_0} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} .\]
    When the strains are smaller in size than \(0{,}001\), each product of two strains is smaller than \(10^{-6}\), so about a thousand times smaller than the single strains, and we drop the products. The relative change of volume is approximately the sum of the normal strains,
    \[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} ,\]
    the volumetric strain of definition 2.79. We did the same computation already in section 1.17.4. That the same holds for a piece with shear strains is the content of lemma 2.55 on the change of volume.
  5. Under even pressure, by step 1, therefore
    \[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} = -\frac{3\,(1 - 2\nu)\,p}{E} .\]

The pressure and the relative decrease of volume are thus proportional. Their ratio is a number that tells how strongly the material resists a change of volume.

Definition 3.7 Bulk modulus (modul stisljivosti)

Let a pressure \(p\) act equally from all sides on a small piece of a linear elastic isotropic material, so that the stress in it is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = -p\) without shear components, and let \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\) be the volumetric strain that this pressure causes. The bulk modulus (modul stisljivosti) of the material is the number

\[K = \frac{p}{-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}} .\]

Source: SADD (4.3.1) (PDF p. 96), BOWER §3.2.5 (PDF p. 101).

The minus sign is there because pressure decreases the volume, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} < 0\), while the bulk modulus should be positive. The ratio does not depend on \(p\), since by step 5 \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}\) is proportional to \(p\). Putting in \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}\) from step 5 we get

\[K = \frac{E}{3\,(1 - 2\nu)} . \tag{3.8}\]

For steel \(K = 196\ \text{GPa}/\big(3\,(1 - 2 \cdot 0{,}3)\big) = 196\ \text{GPa}/(3 \cdot 0{,}4) = 163{,}3\) GPa, somewhat less than \(E\). BOWER notes that the bulk modulus is usually larger than Young's modulus (BOWER §3.2.6, PDF p. 102). This holds only for \(\nu > 1/3\). By (3.8), \(K/E = 1/\big(3\,(1 - 2\nu)\big)\). For \(\nu < 0{,}5\) the denominator is positive, so this ratio is larger than \(1\) exactly when \(3\,(1 - 2\nu) < 1\), that is, when \(\nu > 1/3\). For copper with \(\nu = 0{,}34\), \(K = 1{,}04\,E\), and for steel with \(\nu = 0{,}3\), \(K = 0{,}833\,E\). SADD too gives a bulk modulus of \(164\) GPa for steel, less than its Young's modulus of \(207\) GPa (SADD, Table 4.2, PDF p. 97).

How much does our cube shrink at a depth of 100 m? The pressure in the water there is larger by \(\rho_{\mathrm{w}} g d\) than at the surface (section 2.7), where \(\rho_{\mathrm{w}}\) is the density of water, about \(1000\) kg/m³ (VK, Appendix A.4, PDF p. 637), and \(d = 100\) m the depth:

\[p = 1000\ \text{kg/m}^3 \cdot 9{,}81\ \text{m/s}^2 \cdot 100\ \text{m} = 981\,000\ \text{Pa} = 0{,}981\ \text{MPa} .\]

At the surface the air already pushes with about \(100\) kPa (VK, Appendix A.4, PDF p. 637). By linearity an additional pressure causes an additional strain, whatever pressure acted before, so we compute only the change from the surface. The volumetric strain is

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} = -\frac{p}{K} = -\frac{0{,}981 \cdot 10^6\ \text{Pa}}{163{,}3 \cdot 10^9\ \text{Pa}} = -6{,}006 \cdot 10^{-6} .\]

The cube with the volume \(1000\) mm³ loses \(0{,}006006\) mm³. Each edge gets shorter by a third of this fraction, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}/3 = -2{,}002 \cdot 10^{-6}\), that is, by \(10\ \text{mm} \cdot 2{,}002 \cdot 10^{-6} = 2{,}002 \cdot 10^{-5}\) mm, which is \(20{,}02\) nm. A nanometre, nm, is a thousandth of a micrometre. The weight of the cube changes the stress by \(765\) Pa, which is \(0{,}08\) % of the pressure \(981\,000\) Pa, and the difference of the water pressure between the top and the bottom of the cube is \(\rho_{\mathrm{w}} g h = 98{,}1\) Pa, \(0{,}01\) % of the pressure. We were right to neglect both.

The volume also changes under tension in one direction. By lemma 3.6 on uniaxial stress,

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} = \frac{\sigma}{E} - \nu\,\frac{\sigma}{E} - \nu\,\frac{\sigma}{E} = (1 - 2\nu)\,\frac{\sigma}{E} = (1 - 2\nu)\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} .\]

A steel bar that gets longer by a thousandth increases its volume by \((1 - 2 \cdot 0{,}3) \cdot 0{,}001 = 0{,}4 \cdot 0{,}001 = 0{,}0004\), that is, by \(0{,}04\) %. This is exactly the number we computed in section 1.6 from the thinning by \(0{,}3\) thousandths. For \(\nu = 0{,}5\), however, \(1 - 2\nu = 0\), the volume does not change, and the bulk modulus in (3.8) grows without bound. We return to this at the end of the section.

3.2.5 Pure shear and the shear modulus

The shear stress components remain. Take the pure shear of section 2.6.6. We load a cube only with forces along its faces, the axis \(x\) points to the right, the axis \(z\) up, and the corners of the front face are \(A\) at the bottom left, \(B\) at the bottom right, \(C\) at the top right and \(D\) at the top left. The only nonzero stress components are \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}} = \tau_0\). None of the lemmas so far tells us what the strain is. In section 2.9, however, we put axes along the diagonals of the front face,

\[\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{x}} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\,(1, 0, 1) , \qquad \bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{y}} = (0, 1, 0) , \qquad \bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{z}} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\,(-1, 0, 1) ,\]

the axis \(\bar x\) from \(A\) toward \(C\) and the axis \(\bar z\) from \(B\) toward \(D\), and computed that the components of the same stress in these axes are

\[\bar\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \tau_0 , \qquad \bar\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = -\tau_0 , \qquad \text{all other } \bar\sigma_{\mathrm{kl}} = 0 .\]

On the faces perpendicular to the diagonal \(AC\) a tension \(\tau_0\) acts, on the faces perpendicular to the diagonal \(BD\) a compression \(\tau_0\), and in these axes there are no shear components. Pure shear is thus the sum of a tension \(\tau_0\) in the direction of the diagonal \(AC\) and a compression \(\tau_0\) in the direction of the diagonal \(BD\) (figure 3.13). For tension and compression in one direction we already know the strains. We write them in the diagonal axes and carry them back to the axes of the cube.

Lemma 3.8 Strain in pure shear (deformacija pri čistem strigu)

Let the material at the point \(P\) satisfy assumptions 1 to 5 of section 3.2.1: elasticity, linearity, isotropy (the rule that gives the strain components from the stress components is the same in every rectangular Cartesian coordinate system, with the same \(E\) and \(\nu\)), small strains and constant temperature. Here \(E\) is Young's modulus of the material and \(\nu\) its Poisson's ratio. Let the only nonzero stress components at the point \(P\) in some rectangular Cartesian coordinate system be \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}} = \tau_0\). Then the only nonzero strain components at \(P\) in this system are

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zx}} = \frac{(1 + \nu)\,\tau_0}{E} . \tag{3.9}\]
Proof

We write the stress in axes along the diagonals, where it is the sum of two uniaxial stresses, add the two strains and carry them back.

  1. Take new axes with the unit vectors \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{x}} = (1, 0, 1)/\sqrt2\), \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{y}} = (0, 1, 0)\) and \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{z}} = (-1, 0, 1)/\sqrt2\). The rows of the rotation matrix \(\mat Q\) are these three vectors (definition 2.37).
  2. By theorem 2.42 on the transformation of stress components, in the new axes \(\bar\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \tau_0\) and \(\bar\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = -\tau_0\), and all other components are zero, as we computed in section 2.9.
  3. This is the sum of two states: in the first the only nonzero component is \(\bar\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \tau_0\), in the second \(\bar\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = -\tau_0\).
  4. Here we use isotropy (assumption 3). Lemma 3.6 on uniaxial stress holds in every rectangular Cartesian coordinate system with the same \(E\) and \(\nu\), so also in the new axes. The first state gives \(\bar\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \tau_0/E\), \(\bar\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \bar\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = -\nu\,\tau_0/E\) and no shear components.
  5. For the second state we use the lemma with the axis \(\bar z\) in the place of the first axis, as in section 3.2.3, and with the stress \(-\tau_0\). It gives \(\bar\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = -\tau_0/E\), \(\bar\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \bar\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \nu\,\tau_0/E\) and no shear components.
  6. By linearity we add:
    \[\bar\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \frac{(1 + \nu)\,\tau_0}{E} , \qquad \bar\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \frac{-\nu\,\tau_0 + \nu\,\tau_0}{E} = 0 , \qquad \bar\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = -\frac{(1 + \nu)\,\tau_0}{E} ,\]
    and there are no shear components in the new axes.
  7. By lemma 2.44 on the transformation of strain components, \(\bar{\ten\varepsilon} = \mat Q\,\ten\varepsilon\,\mat Q^\T\). We multiply this equation by \(\mat Q^\T\) from the left and by \(\mat Q\) from the right. Since \(\mat Q\) is orthogonal, \(\mat Q^\T\mat Q = \mat I\) (definition 2.39), and since the matrix product is associative (lemma 1.99), we get \(\mat Q^\T\bar{\ten\varepsilon}\,\mat Q = \mat I\,\ten\varepsilon\,\mat I = \ten\varepsilon\).
  8. By definition 1.98 the entry in row \(i\) and column \(j\) of the product \(\mat Q^\T\bar{\ten\varepsilon}\,\mat Q\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} \sum_{\mathrm{l}} Q_{\mathrm{ki}}\,\bar\varepsilon_{\mathrm{kl}}\,Q_{\mathrm{lj}}\). The matrix \(\bar{\ten\varepsilon}\) has only \(\bar\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and \(\bar\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\) nonzero, which we write as \(\pm g\) with \(g = (1 + \nu)\,\tau_0/E\). Two terms remain:
    \[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} = g\,(Q_{\mathrm{xi}}\,Q_{\mathrm{xj}} - Q_{\mathrm{zi}}\,Q_{\mathrm{zj}}) .\]
  9. The row \(x\) of the matrix \(\mat Q\) is \((1, 0, 1)/\sqrt2\), the row \(z\) is \((-1, 0, 1)/\sqrt2\). For \(i = j = x\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = g\,(\tfrac12 - \tfrac12) = 0\), for \(i = j = z\) likewise \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\), and for \(i = x\) and \(j = z\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = g\,\big(\tfrac12 - (-\tfrac12)\big) = g\). Where \(i\) or \(j\) equals \(y\), \(Q_{\mathrm{xy}} = Q_{\mathrm{zy}} = 0\), so \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}\) are zero. Only \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zx}} = (1 + \nu)\,\tau_0/E\) remains.

Source: SADD §4.3.2 (PDF p. 95), CM (6.3.40) (PDF p. 267). The sources read the result off the general law for an isotropic material. The route through a tension and a compression along the diagonals is not from the sources.

Where does the factor \(1 + \nu\) come from? The tension along the diagonal \(AC\) lengthens it by \(\tau_0/E\). The compression along the diagonal \(BD\) shortens the material in its own direction and widens it in all directions perpendicular to it, among them the direction \(AC\), by \(\nu\,\tau_0/E\). The two contributions add. In the same way the diagonal \(BD\) gets shorter by \(\tau_0/E\) because of the compression and by \(\nu\,\tau_0/E\) because of the tension along \(AC\), which makes it thinner. In the direction of the axis \(y\), however, the tension makes the material thinner by \(\nu\,\tau_0/E\), the compression widens it by the same amount, and \(\bar\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0\). The square \(ABCD\) becomes a rhombus, a quadrilateral with four equal sides, longer along \(AC\) and shorter along \(BD\), and its right angle at the corner \(A\) gets smaller (figure 3.14). That \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}\) is exactly the strain of the diagonal \(AC\) we also see from (1.53): the fibre in the direction \((1, 0, 1)/\sqrt2\) has the strain \(\tfrac12\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \tfrac12\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = 0 + 0 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}\).

Shear stress and shear strain are thus proportional, just like stress and strain in the tension test. Their ratio has its own name. Books measure it with the engineering shear strain \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xz}} = 2\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}\) of section 1.17.4, and so do we.

Definition 3.9 Shear modulus (strižni modul)

Let the only nonzero stress components in a small piece of a linear elastic isotropic material be \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}} = \tau_0\) (pure shear), and let \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xz}} = 2\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}\) be the engineering shear strain that this stress causes. The shear modulus (strižni modul) of the material is the number

\[G = \frac{\tau_0}{\gamma_{\mathrm{xz}}} .\]

Source: SADD §4.3.2 (PDF p. 95), CM (6.3.40) (PDF p. 267), BOWER §3.2.5 (PDF p. 101).

By lemma 3.8 on strain in pure shear, \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xz}} = 2\,(1 + \nu)\,\tau_0/E\), so

\[G = \frac{E}{2\,(1 + \nu)} , \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = 2G\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} . \tag{3.10}\]

The ratio does not depend on \(\tau_0\). For steel \(G = 196\ \text{GPa}/\big(2\,(1 + 0{,}3)\big) = 75{,}38\) GPa, somewhat less than \(E\), as BOWER notes (§3.2.6, PDF p. 102). With the engineering shear strain the relation has the form \(\tau = G\,\gamma\), the same as \(\sigma = E\,\varepsilon\) in the tension test (CM (6.3.40), PDF p. 267). With our component \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}\), which is half of \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xz}}\), it carries a factor \(2\): \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = 2G\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}\). SADD writes the same, with the shear modulus \(\mu\) (SADD, PDF p. 95). When reading the sources one must therefore check which shear strain they use. The shear modulus can be measured directly, for example by twisting a thin tube, in whose wall pure shear arises (SADD §4.3.2, PDF p. 95, chapter 16). For an isotropic material it need not be measured, since \(E\) and \(\nu\) determine it.

Let us test this on a steel cube with the edge \(h = 10\) mm in pure shear with \(\tau_0 = 50\) MPa, as in section 2.6.6. By the lemma on strain in pure shear,

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \frac{\tau_0}{2G} = \frac{50 \cdot 10^6\ \text{Pa}}{2 \cdot 75{,}38 \cdot 10^9\ \text{Pa}} = 3{,}316 \cdot 10^{-4} , \qquad \gamma_{\mathrm{xz}} = 6{,}633 \cdot 10^{-4} .\]

The diagonal \(AC\) gets longer by the fraction \(3{,}316 \cdot 10^{-4}\), the diagonal \(BD\) shorter by the same fraction. Let the cube be glued to the table by its bottom face, like the sheared cube of section 1.6.2. The displacement field \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = \gamma_{\mathrm{xz}}\,z\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\) has zero displacement on the bottom face, where \(z = 0\), and by (1.51) exactly these strains: \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \tfrac12\,(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial z + \partial u_{\mathrm{z}}/\partial x) = \tfrac12\,(\gamma_{\mathrm{xz}} + 0)\), and all other components are zero. The top face moves by

\[s = \gamma_{\mathrm{xz}}\,h = 6{,}633 \cdot 10^{-4} \cdot 10\ \text{mm} = 6{,}633\ \text{µm} .\]

By (2.48) of section 2.9.5 the sine of the angle by which the right angle at the corner \(A\) decreases is approximately \(2\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = 6{,}633 \cdot 10^{-4}\), which is an angle of \(0{,}038^\circ\) (computed with NumPy). Compared with the rhombus of figure 3.14, the glued cube also turns a little, which does not change the strains.

Now we can also answer a question that we did not ask in section 1.6.2: what forces are needed to push the top face of the steel cube by \(s = 10\) µm? There \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xz}} = s/h = 0{,}001\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = 0{,}0005\). By the definition of the shear modulus, such a strain is caused by pure shear with

\[\tau_0 = G\,\gamma_{\mathrm{xz}} = 75{,}38 \cdot 10^9\ \text{Pa} \cdot 0{,}001 = 75{,}38\ \text{MPa} .\]

On each face with the area \(100\) mm² \(= 10^{-4}\) m² the force is therefore \(75{,}38 \cdot 10^6\ \text{Pa} \cdot 10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2 = 7538\) N (figure 3.15). We push the top face to the right, the glue pulls the bottom face to the left, we pull the right face up and the left face down. Without the forces on the side faces the forces on the top and bottom faces would turn the cube, as we saw for pure shear in section 2.6.6, and the field \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = 0{,}001\,z\) would not hold near the free side faces. The field of chapter 1 is thus an idealization that requires these four forces. Does the steel stay elastic? In pure shear the principal stresses are \(\tau_0\), \(0\) and \(-\tau_0\), so the largest shear stress over all faces is \(\tau_0\) (theorem 2.70). In the tension test steel starts to yield at \(\sigma_{\mathrm{Y}} = 220\) MPa, when the largest shear stress on oblique cuts is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{Y}}/2 = 110\) MPa (lemma 2.19 on the largest shear stress in a bar). By the rule of section 2.4, that the largest shear stress roughly decides when metals start to yield, \(75{,}38\) MPa is below the limit, and the cube stays elastic.

3.2.6 The law for all components

Now we know the response to all simple stress states. Every state is the sum of three uniaxial stresses along the axes and three pure shears, and by linearity we add their strains. Often we also need the reverse rule, which gives the stresses from the strains, for example when we know the displacement field and look for the stresses.

From history

Navier, Poisson and Cauchy built the theory on molecules with forces along the lines joining them (figure 1.1), as we saw in section 3.1.3. For a general substance, in which each of the six stress components is a linear function of the six strain components, Poisson and Cauchy assumed \(6 \cdot 6 = 36\) constants. The molecular assumption reduced them to 15, and for a substance that is the same in all directions to one (TIMO, PDF p. 189). From this theory Poisson also derived that the shear modulus is \(0{,}4\,E\) and the bulk modulus \(\tfrac23 E\) (TIMO, PDF p. 189, LOVE, PDF p. 29). Indeed, at \(\nu = 1/4\) equations (3.10) and (3.8) give \(G = E/(2 \cdot 1{,}25) = 0{,}4\,E\) and \(K = E/(3 \cdot 0{,}5) = \tfrac23 E\). The ratio \(K/G\) is therefore \(5:3\) for every such substance.

Two constants were also obtained by George Green, who did not want to assume molecules but started from the principle of conservation of energy (TIMO, PDF p. 190-191, section 3.6.4). George Gabriel Stokes noticed that resistance to compression and resistance to shearing are the two fundamental resistances of a substance that is the same in all directions, and introduced both moduli (LOVE, PDF p. 29). He would not accept Poisson's conclusion that the ratio of the two is always \(5:3\), because liquids, soft solids and hard solids resist compression and change of shape in very different ratios (LOVE, PDF p. 29). We test Stokes' objection on the table in section 3.1.3. From (3.10) and (3.8) it follows that \(K/G = 2\,(1 + \nu)/\big(3\,(1 - 2\nu)\big)\), which is \(5:3\) only at \(\nu = 1/4\), for example for nylon. For mild steel with \(\nu = 0{,}30\) we get \(K/G = 2{,}17\). For natural rubber with \(\nu = 0{,}49\) we have \(K = 16{,}7\,E\) and \(G = 0{,}34\,E\), so \(K/G = 49{,}7\): rubber is hard to compress but changes its shape easily in shear (BOWER, Table 3.1, PDF p. 100).

Gabriel Lamé in a book of 1852 gave up molecular forces and assumed that the stresses are linear functions of the strains. For a substance that is the same in all directions two constants are then needed, which we obtain from a simple tension test and from a test in which we twist a bar (TIMO, PDF p. 125-126, LOVE, PDF p. 30). This is exactly what we have done: we built the law without molecules, from the tension test and the pure shear.

Let us return to the reverse rule, which gives the stresses from the strains. Two combinations of the numbers \(E\) and \(\nu\) appear in the reverse rule, which books name after Lamé (SADD, PDF p. 93, CM, PDF p. 266).

Definition 3.10 Lamé constants (Laméjevi konstanti)

The Lamé constants (Laméjevi konstanti) of a linear elastic isotropic material with Young's modulus \(E\) and Poisson's ratio \(\nu\), where \(\nu \neq -1\) and \(\nu \neq \tfrac12\), are the numbers

\[\lambda = \frac{\nu\,E}{(1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)} , \qquad \mu = \frac{E}{2\,(1 + \nu)} . \tag{3.11}\]

Source: CM (6.3.33) (PDF p. 266), SADD (4.2.7) (PDF p. 93) and Table 4.1 (PDF p. 97), BOWER §3.2.5 (PDF p. 101).

The constant \(\mu\) has the same formula as the shear modulus in (3.10), so \(\mu = G\). For steel \(\lambda = 196\ \text{GPa} \cdot 0{,}3/\big((1 + 0{,}3)(1 - 2 \cdot 0{,}3)\big) = 113{,}1\) GPa and \(\mu = 75{,}38\) GPa. The letter \(\lambda\) is the established one for this constant in books (CM (6.3.32), SADD (4.2.7)), although in section 2.11 we used it for an eigenvalue, and in section 2.13 for a ratio of lengths. Where the meanings meet, we will say which one is meant.

Below, \(\tr\ten\varepsilon = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\) denotes the trace of the matrix of the small strain tensor, the sum of its diagonal entries (section 1.17.4), and \(\tr\ten\sigma = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) the trace of the stress matrix. The trace of the strain matrix is the volumetric strain \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}\).

Theorem 3.11 Hooke's law for an isotropic material (Hookov zakon za izotropen material)

Let the material at every point of the body satisfy assumptions 1 to 5 of section 3.2.1: elasticity, linearity, isotropy (the rule that gives the strain components from the stress components is the same in every rectangular Cartesian coordinate system, with the same \(E\) and \(\nu\)), small strains and constant temperature. Here \(E\) is Young's modulus of the material and \(\nu\) its Poisson's ratio. Then at every point of the body and in every rectangular Cartesian coordinate system:

1. Strains from stresses.

\[\begin{aligned} \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} &= \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} - \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}})}{E} , \qquad & \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} &= \frac{1 + \nu}{E}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} , \\ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} &= \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} - \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}})}{E} , \qquad & \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} &= \frac{1 + \nu}{E}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} , \\ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} &= \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} - \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}})}{E} , \qquad & \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} &= \frac{1 + \nu}{E}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} . \end{aligned} \tag{3.12}\]

2. Stresses from strains. If \(\nu \neq -1\) and \(\nu \neq \tfrac12\), then

\[\ten\sigma = \lambda\,(\tr\ten\varepsilon)\,\mat I + 2\mu\,\ten\varepsilon , \tag{3.13}\]

where \(\lambda\) and \(\mu\) are the Lamé constants of the material and \(\mat I\) is the identity matrix, in components

\[\begin{aligned} \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} &= \lambda\,\tr\ten\varepsilon + 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} , \qquad & \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} &= 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} , \\ \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} &= \lambda\,\tr\ten\varepsilon + 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} , \qquad & \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} &= 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} , \\ \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} &= \lambda\,\tr\ten\varepsilon + 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} , \qquad & \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} &= 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} . \end{aligned}\]
Proof

1. Strains from stresses. We split the stress into six simple states and add their strains by linearity.

  1. By theorem 2.36 on the symmetry of stress, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zy}}\). We therefore write the stress matrix as the sum of four matrices, added entry by entry:
    \[\ten\sigma = \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0 & \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} & 0 \\ \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} \\ 0 & \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} & 0 \end{bmatrix} .\]
  2. The first matrix is three normal stresses without shear. By (3.7) it causes the normal strains of the left column of (3.12) and no shear strains.
  3. The third matrix is pure shear with \(\tau_0 = \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\). By lemma 3.8 on strain in pure shear it causes only \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zx}} = (1 + \nu)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}/E\).
  4. The lemma on strain in pure shear holds in every rectangular Cartesian coordinate system. If we rename the axis \(y\) to \(x\), the axis \(z\) to \(y\) and the axis \(x\) to \(z\), the second matrix becomes pure shear between the first and the third axis, so it causes only \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yx}} = (1 + \nu)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}/E\).
  5. If we rename the axis \(z\) to \(x\), the axis \(x\) to \(y\) and the axis \(y\) to \(z\), the fourth matrix becomes pure shear between the first and the third axis, so it causes only \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zy}} = (1 + \nu)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}/E\).
  6. By linearity the strains are the sums of the strains of steps 2 to 5. Each component receives a contribution from one step only, and we get (3.12).

2. Stresses from strains. We solve the equations of item 1 for the stresses, first for the sum of the normal stresses.

  1. We add the three equations of the left column of (3.12). Each normal stress appears once with the factor \(1\) and twice with the factor \(-\nu\), so \(\tr\ten\varepsilon = (1 - 2\nu)\,\tr\ten\sigma/E\).
  2. Since \(\nu \neq \tfrac12\), we may divide by \(1 - 2\nu\): \(\tr\ten\sigma = E\,\tr\ten\varepsilon/(1 - 2\nu)\).
  3. In the first equation of the left column we write \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = \tr\ten\sigma - \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and multiply by \(E\): \(E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} - \nu\,(\tr\ten\sigma - \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}) = (1 + \nu)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} - \nu\,\tr\ten\sigma\).
  4. Since \(\nu \neq -1\), we may divide by \(1 + \nu\), and we put in step 2:
    \[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \frac{E}{1 + \nu}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \frac{\nu}{1 + \nu}\,\tr\ten\sigma = \frac{E}{1 + \nu}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \frac{\nu\,E}{(1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)}\,\tr\ten\varepsilon = 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \lambda\,\tr\ten\varepsilon ,\]
    where we used (3.11), from which \(E/(1 + \nu) = 2\mu\).
  5. In the same way the second and third equations of the left column give \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\).
  6. From the right column, multiplied by \(E/(1 + \nu) = 2\mu\), follow \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}\).
  7. The matrix \(\lambda\,(\tr\ten\varepsilon)\,\mat I + 2\mu\,\ten\varepsilon\) has on its diagonal \(\lambda\,\tr\ten\varepsilon + 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\lambda\,\tr\ten\varepsilon + 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\) and \(\lambda\,\tr\ten\varepsilon + 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\), and off the diagonal \(2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\) and so on, because the identity matrix \(\mat I\) has ones on the diagonal and zeros elsewhere (definition 1.105). By steps 4 to 6 it agrees with the matrix \(\ten\sigma\) in all nine entries, which is (3.13).

Source: SADD (4.2.7), (4.2.8), (4.2.10), (4.2.11) (PDF pp. 93-94), CM (6.3.32)-(6.3.35) (PDF pp. 266-267), BOWER §3.2.2 (PDF pp. 97-98). SADD and CM write the law with indices, CM with the trace as we do. SADD derives it from the condition that the material constants do not change when the axes turn, and then explains the meaning of the constants from it with tension, shear and pressure (SADD §4.3, PDF pp. 94-96). We went the other way.

The law is named after Hooke, whom we met in section 1.1 and who wrote in 1678 that the deformation of an elastic body is proportional to the force (SADD, PDF p. 93). What does item 1 say? The normal strains depend only on the normal stresses, and each shear strain only on the shear stress with the same two indices. A shear stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) therefore changes no length along the axes, and a normal stress no right angle between the axes. A material with all these properties is described by only two numbers, \(E\) and \(\nu\). All the others, \(G\), \(K\), \(\lambda\) and \(\mu\), we compute from them (SADD, Table 4.1, PDF p. 97).

Let us test the law on a small piece of a thin steel plate in the plane of the axes \(x\) and \(y\). We pull the plate with the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 100\) MPa in the direction of the axis \(x\) and with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = 50\) MPa in the direction of the axis \(y\), and at the same time shear it with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 30\) MPa. The two faces of the plate are free, so by Cauchy's formula \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) on them. The plate is thin, so we assume that these components are zero inside it too. Such a state is called plane stress (ravninsko napetostno stanje) (BOWER §3.2.3, PDF p. 99). By item 1, with all stresses in MPa and \(E = 196\,000\) MPa,

\[\begin{aligned} \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} &= \frac{100 - 0{,}3 \cdot 50}{196\,000} = 4{,}3367 \cdot 10^{-4} , \\ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} &= \frac{50 - 0{,}3 \cdot 100}{196\,000} = 1{,}0204 \cdot 10^{-4} , \\ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} &= \frac{0 - 0{,}3 \cdot (100 + 50)}{196\,000} = -2{,}2959 \cdot 10^{-4} , \\ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} &= \frac{(1 + 0{,}3) \cdot 30}{196\,000} = 1{,}9898 \cdot 10^{-4} , \end{aligned}\]

and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\). The plate gets thinner, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} < 0\), although \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\). Now we go back with item 2, with \(\lambda = 113\,080\) MPa and \(2\mu = 150\,770\) MPa. The trace is \(\tr\ten\varepsilon = (4{,}3367 + 1{,}0204 - 2{,}2959) \cdot 10^{-4} = 3{,}0612 \cdot 10^{-4}\), so \(\lambda\,\tr\ten\varepsilon = 34{,}62\) MPa and

\[\begin{aligned} \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} &= 34{,}62 + 150\,770 \cdot 4{,}3367 \cdot 10^{-4} = 34{,}62 + 65{,}38 = 100\ \text{MPa} , \\ \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} &= 34{,}62 - 150\,770 \cdot 2{,}2959 \cdot 10^{-4} = 34{,}62 - 34{,}62 = 0 . \end{aligned}\]

In the same way we get \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = 50\) MPa and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 150\,770 \cdot 1{,}9898 \cdot 10^{-4} = 30\) MPa (checked with NumPy). For \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) the term \(\lambda\,\tr\ten\varepsilon\), which belongs to the change of volume, and the term \(2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\), which belongs to the thinning, cancel exactly.

Books write the law in different ways. SADD writes the normal stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{x}}\), the shear stresses \(\tau_{\mathrm{xy}}\) and the strains \(e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(e_{\mathrm{xy}}\), with the same meaning as our \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\) (SADD (4.2.8), (4.2.11), PDF pp. 93-94). CM writes item 2 as \(\ten\sigma = 2\mu\,\ten\varepsilon + \lambda\,\tr(\ten\varepsilon)\,\mat I\) (CM (6.3.32), PDF p. 266). BOWER (PDF pp. 97-98) and CM ((6.3.39), PDF p. 267) also write the law with a \(6 \times 6\) matrix, in which \(2\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\) stands in place of \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\), and BOWER warns about it explicitly. We will meet this form in section 3.4.

3.2.7 The hanging bar

Let us return to the steel bar of length \(L = 10\) m with a diameter of 10 mm that hangs under its own weight. It is held in the idealized way of section 1.7.1: only the point \(O\), the centre of the top face, is fixed, and an upward force distributed evenly over the whole top face acts on that face. The axis \(x\) points from \(O\) vertically down, the axes \(y\) and \(z\) lie horizontally. In chapter 1 we took the displacement field from the books,

\[\begin{aligned} u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y, z) &= a\,(2Lx - x^2) - 0{,}3\,a\,(y^2 + z^2) , \\ u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y, z) &= -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\,y , \\ u_{\mathrm{z}}(x, y, z) &= -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\,z , \end{aligned}\]

with \(a = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\) m⁻¹ ((1.18)). Does this field agree with Hooke's law?

We know the stress from chapter 2. At the depth \(x\) the normal stress on the cross section is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \rho g\,(L - x)\) ((2.5)), and all other stress components are zero, because each cross section carries the part of the bar below it evenly, and neighbouring parts of the bar pass only vertical forces to each other (CM Example 7.3.2, equation (2), PDF p. 305). At every point of the bar there is thus uniaxial stress (figure 3.16). By Hooke's law, item 1, the strains are

\[\begin{aligned} \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} &= \frac{\rho g\,(L - x)}{E} = 2a\,(L - x) , \\ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} &= -\nu\,\frac{\rho g\,(L - x)}{E} = -\nu \cdot 2a\,(L - x) , \\ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} &= 0 , \end{aligned}\]

where we used \(a = \rho g/(2E)\) from section 3.1.

Now we compute the strains from the field by (1.51). We computed the partial derivatives in section 1.7.2.

  1. \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial x = 2a\,(L - x)\).
  2. \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial y = -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{z}}/\partial z = -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\).
  3. \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \tfrac12\,(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial y + \partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial x) = \tfrac12\,(-0{,}6\,a\,y + 0{,}6\,a\,y) = 0\), and likewise \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \tfrac12\,(-0{,}6\,a\,z + 0{,}6\,a\,z) = 0\).
  4. \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = \tfrac12\,(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial z + \partial u_{\mathrm{z}}/\partial y) = \tfrac12\,(0 + 0) = 0\).

At every point of the bar the field gives exactly the strains that Hooke's law demands, if \(-\nu \cdot 2a = -0{,}6\,a\), that is, \(\nu = 0{,}3\) (checked with SymPy). The number \(0{,}3\) in the field is indeed Poisson's ratio of steel, as section 3.1 anticipated, and the field agrees with the stress and with Hooke's law at every point. At the depth \(x = 5\) m, for example at the point \(P\) of section 1.7.1,

\[\begin{aligned} \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} &= 7800 \cdot 9{,}81 \cdot 5\ \text{Pa} = 0{,}3826\ \text{MPa} , \\ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} &= \frac{0{,}3826 \cdot 10^6\ \text{Pa}}{196 \cdot 10^9\ \text{Pa}} = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6} , \\ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} &= -0{,}3 \cdot 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6} = -5{,}856 \cdot 10^{-7} . \end{aligned}\]

What does this test not show? For this to really be the field of the hanging bar, the stress must also satisfy the equilibrium equations, which we checked in section 2.7, and the conditions on the surface of the bar and where it is held. How to find such a field without taking it from books we will see in chapter 11.

3.2.8 Volume and shape

Under even pressure the cube only got smaller, in pure shear it only changed its shape. Can every strain be split into these two parts, and is each caused by its own part of the stress? As in section 2.12 we split the stress into the mean stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{m}} = \tfrac13\,\tr\ten\sigma\) and the deviator \(\ten s = \ten\sigma - \sigma_{\mathrm{m}}\,\mat I\) (definition 2.73). In the same section (definition 2.79) we split the strain in the same way into the spherical part \(\tfrac13\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}\,\mat I\), where \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} = \tr\ten\varepsilon\) is the volumetric strain, and the deviatoric strain

\[\ten e = \ten\varepsilon - \tfrac13\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}\,\mat I .\]

The spherical part lengthens every edge of a cube by the same fraction, \(\tfrac13\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}\), and changes only the size. The deviatoric strain has the trace \(\tr\ten e = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} - \tfrac13\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} \cdot 3 = 0\), so by lemma 2.55 on the change of volume it does not change the volume, only the shape (for normal strains alone step 4 of section 3.2.4 already shows this). How are the two parts related to the stress?

  1. By (3.13), \(\ten\sigma = \lambda\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}\,\mat I + 2\mu\,\ten\varepsilon\). The trace of a sum is the sum of the traces, and the trace of the identity matrix is \(3\), so \(\tr\ten\sigma = 3\lambda\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} + 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}\).
  2. The mean stress is a third of this, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{m}} = (\lambda + \tfrac23\,\mu)\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}\).
  3. We put in (3.11) and add the fractions over the common denominator \(3\,(1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)\):
    \[\lambda + \tfrac23\,\mu = \frac{3\nu\,E + (1 - 2\nu)\,E}{3\,(1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)} = \frac{(1 + \nu)\,E}{3\,(1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)} = \frac{E}{3\,(1 - 2\nu)} = K\]
    by (3.8). Therefore \(\sigma_{\mathrm{m}} = K\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}\).
  4. The stress deviator is \(\ten s = \ten\sigma - \sigma_{\mathrm{m}}\,\mat I = \lambda\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}\,\mat I + 2\mu\,\ten\varepsilon - (\lambda + \tfrac23\,\mu)\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}\,\mat I = 2\mu\,\ten\varepsilon - \tfrac23\,\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}\,\mat I = 2\mu\,(\ten\varepsilon - \tfrac13\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}\,\mat I)\).
  5. The expression in brackets is \(\ten e\), and \(\mu = G\), so \(\ten s = 2G\,\ten e\).

Hooke's law thus splits into two independent parts,

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{m}} = K\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} , \qquad \ten s = 2G\,\ten e . \tag{3.14}\]

The mean stress changes only the volume, and the bulk modulus tells how strongly the material resists this. The stress deviator changes only the shape, and the shear modulus tells how strongly the material resists a change of shape (figure 3.17). In pure shear \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} = 0\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{m}} = 0\), everything is a change of shape. Under even pressure \(\ten e = 0\) and \(\ten s = 0\), everything is a change of volume. None of our sources writes the split in this form in a section of its own. We assembled it from the split of the strain (SADD (2.5.1), (2.5.2), PDF p. 49), the split of the stress (SADD (3.5.1), (3.5.2), PDF p. 72) and the bulk modulus (SADD (4.3.1), PDF p. 96).

One more property follows from the law, which isotropic materials have and others need not have. Every principal direction of stress is also a principal direction of strain (section 2.11). In this corollary we write the eigenvalues \(\sigma_1\) and \(\varepsilon_1\), not \(\lambda\), because in this section \(\lambda\) is a Lamé constant.

Corollary 3.12 Principal directions of stress and strain (glavne smeri napetosti in deformacije)

Let Hooke's law for an isotropic material hold (theorem 3.11) with \(\nu \neq -1\) and \(\nu \neq \tfrac12\), and let \(\vek n\) be a unit vector. If \(\vek n\) is a principal direction of stress, that is, \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \sigma_1\,\vek n\) for some number \(\sigma_1\), then \(\vek n\) is also a principal direction of strain, \(\ten\varepsilon\,\vek n = \varepsilon_1\,\vek n\) for some number \(\varepsilon_1\), and conversely.

Proof

We get both directions of the argument from the law written with matrices, because the identity matrix leaves every vector as it is.

  1. We write item 1 of the theorem with matrices: \(\ten\varepsilon = \frac{1 + \nu}{E}\,\ten\sigma - \frac{\nu}{E}\,(\tr\ten\sigma)\,\mat I\). Indeed, the entry \((x, x)\) of the right side is \(\big((1 + \nu)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} - \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}})\big)/E = \big(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} - \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}})\big)/E\), which is the first equation of (3.12), and the entry \((x, y)\) is \((1 + \nu)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}/E\), because \(\mat I\) has zeros off the diagonal. Likewise for the other entries.
  2. Let \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \sigma_1\,\vek n\). The product of a sum of matrices with a vector is the sum of the products (lemma 1.99), a multiple of a matrix is multiplied by a vector by taking the multiple out, and \(\mat I\,\vek n = \vek n\) (definition 1.105). Therefore
    \[\ten\varepsilon\,\vek n = \frac{1 + \nu}{E}\,\sigma_1\,\vek n - \frac{\nu}{E}\,(\tr\ten\sigma)\,\vek n = \frac{(1 + \nu)\,\sigma_1 - \nu\,\tr\ten\sigma}{E}\,\vek n ,\]
    and \(\vek n\) is a principal direction of strain.
  3. Let \(\ten\varepsilon\,\vek n = \varepsilon_1\,\vek n\). By item 2 of the theorem and the same rules, \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \lambda\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}\,\vek n + 2\mu\,\varepsilon_1\,\vek n = (\lambda\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} + 2\mu\,\varepsilon_1)\,\vek n\), and \(\vek n\) is a principal direction of stress.

Source: SADD §4.2 (PDF p. 94), where the result is Exercise 4.7. SADD adds that it also holds for some, but not all, anisotropic materials.

We have already seen this in pure shear. In the axes along the diagonals \(AC\) and \(BD\) in the proof of lemma 3.8 on strain in pure shear both matrices, \(\bar{\ten\sigma}\) and \(\bar{\ten\varepsilon}\), were diagonal, so the principal directions of stress and strain are the same: the directions of the two diagonals and the axis \(y\). In a plate with fibres pulled at a slant to the fibres, however, the tension also causes a shear strain between the direction of the tension and the direction perpendicular to it (CM, Fig. 6.3.4, PDF p. 268). The direction of the tension is a principal direction of stress, but not a principal direction of strain.

3.2.9 Where the law stops holding

The formulas (3.8) and (3.10) have \(1 - 2\nu\) and \(1 + \nu\) in their denominators. Figure 3.18 shows what happens when \(\nu\) approaches the two limits.

As \(\nu\) approaches \(0{,}5\), \(1 - 2\nu\) approaches zero, and the bulk modulus grows without bound (figure 3.18, blue curve). The volume of the material can hardly be compressed. At \(\nu = 0{,}5\) the volume does not change under any stress, and the material is called incompressible (nestisljiv) (SADD, PDF p. 96, BOWER §3.2.6, PDF p. 102). Rubber has \(\nu = 0{,}49\) (BOWER, Table 3.1, PDF p. 100), and its bulk modulus is \(K = E/\big(3\,(1 - 2 \cdot 0{,}49)\big) = E/(3 \cdot 0{,}02) = 16{,}7\,E\). For an incompressible material item 2 of Hooke's law does not hold, since \(\lambda\) divides by \(1 - 2\nu = 0\) and is not defined (as \(\nu\) approaches \(0{,}5\) it grows without bound). The reason is physical: since \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} = 0\) under every stress, we cannot compute the mean stress from the strains, because the strain does not determine it. As \(\nu\) approaches \(-1\), on the other hand, the shear modulus grows without bound. Materials with negative \(\nu\) exist, and a bar of such a material gets thicker under tension (BOWER, PDF p. 102). For a stable material \(-1 < \nu < 0{,}5\) (BOWER, PDF p. 102). Why, we will show in section 3.6 with the work needed to deform a material.

We derived Hooke's law under the assumptions at the beginning of the section. Where one of them fails, the law fails too.

  1. Above the yield stress steel deforms permanently. This happens at a strain of about \(\sigma_{\mathrm{Y}}/E = 220\ \text{MPa}/196\ \text{GPa} = 0{,}00112\) (section 2.2).
  2. Rubber stays elastic even at large strains, but there the stress is no longer proportional to the strain (section 3.1).
  3. For large rotations the small strain tensor no longer describes the changes of lengths, even if the material is elastic and the changes of lengths are small (BOWER §3.2.2, PDF p. 97).
  4. Crystals, wood and materials reinforced with fibres are not isotropic (SADD, PDF p. 92). They need more than two numbers, which we look at in section 3.4.
  5. When the temperature changes, a material expands even without stress.

We continue with the last assumption in the next section, where we extend Hooke's law to heated materials.
