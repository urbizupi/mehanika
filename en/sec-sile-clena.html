Chapter 28 Flexible robot links

28.5 Forces on the link

In section 28.6 the motion of the link will come from Lagrange's equations (theorem 8.99) for each coordinate \((\vek o, \theta, \vek q_{\mathrm{d}})\). Their left side, the kinetic energy, was built in section 28.4. The right side is the generalized forces (definition 8.51). The name is so because such a force is not always a force in newtons: to a coordinate that is an angle belongs a moment in newton metres, and the coordinate itself is not always a length. Such a force is generalized because one notion covers force, moment and everything else that does work when a coordinate changes. Two questions must be answered. The first: what does the bent link itself pull back with, and does that depend on where in space the link is and how it is turned. The second: into which of the three groups of coordinates, the shift \(\vek o\), the rotation \(\theta\) or the bending \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\), does each external load enter, that is, the force at the end of the link, the weight of the link and the motor moment.

We prepare the link. It is an Euler-Bernoulli beam (definition 5.14) of length \(L\) with constant bending stiffness \(EI\) and mass per length \(\rho A\), in the plane of motion. The planar case is the only one the book proves, because it has no rotations in space yet. The frame is chosen as in section 28.2. The link is fixed to the hub (pest), a rigid body that the motor turns about the point \(\vek o\). The hub has this name in the way of a wheel: it is the central part to which the rest is fixed, the spokes of a wheel, here the link. The link is clamped into the hub, so the root of the link turns as the hub does. The frame is the clamped one, which by definition 28.5 means \(v(0) = 0\) and \(v'(0) = 0\), and the transverse displacement of the axis, measured in the frame, is \(v(x) = \vek\psi(x)^\T\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\) with the shapes \(\psi_{\mathrm{k}}\) of definition 28.4. The position of the point of the link with local coordinate \(x\) is by that definition

\[\vek r(x) = \vek o + \mat R(\theta)\,\big(\bar{\vek r}_0(x) + \mat\Psi(x)\,\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\big) , \qquad \bar{\vek r}_0 = \begin{pmatrix} x \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} , \quad \mat\Psi = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ \psi_1 & \cdots & \psi_{\mathrm{n}} \end{pmatrix} . \tag{28.25}\]

28.5.1 Elastic energy of the link

We begin with what the link pulls back with. We take the steel strip of section 28.1, \(L = 1\) m, \(EI = 141{,}12\) N m\(^2\), \(\rho A = 1{,}872\) kg/m, and bend it into the shape of the first cantilever mode (chapter 13), so that the tip is \(10\) mm off the straight line. We draw it twice (figure 28.34). On the left the hub is at the point \(\vek o\) with the frame axes \(\bar x\) and \(\bar y\) and rotation \(\theta = 0\), the straight dashed line is the undeformed link, the blue curve is the bent link, and the tip displacement \(q_{\mathrm{d}}\) is measured from the line to the tip. On the right is the same bent link in another position: its rotation \(\theta\) is \(40^\circ\), the hub is elsewhere, the frame axes are turned with the link and the displacement \(q_{\mathrm{d}}\) is measured perpendicular to the turned line. The shape relative to the frame is the same in both placements. The question is whether the energy is the same in both.

If we computed the energy in fixed axes from the small strain tensor, we would get different energies. Section 28.1 showed that for a rigid rotation by the angle \(\theta\) this tensor is \((\cos\theta - 1)\,\mat I\) (lemma 28.1). At \(\theta = 40^\circ\) that is a false stretch of \(-0{,}2340\). Multiplied by \(E = 196\) GPa it is a stress of \(45{,}9\) GPa that exists nowhere, while the bending of the link gives in the most strained fibre at the root a strain of only \(1{,}055 \cdot 10^{-4}\), which is \(2218\) times less. In the frame there is no false stretch. Theorem 28.7 says that the Green-Lagrange tensor of the link is computed from the displacement gradient \(\bar{\vek u} = \mat\Psi\,\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\) in the axes of the frame, and neither \(\vek o\) nor \(\theta\) is in it.

For the energy we must still say what the model takes for the displacement of the axis along the link. The model has three named simplifications.

All three simplifications are shown in figure 28.35. On the left panel is the bent axis of the link, the cross-sections are lines perpendicular to it, and a fibre at distance \(y\) from the axis shortens or stretches in proportion to \(y\) and to the curvature. On the middle panel are the axis of length \(L\) and its horizontal projection, shorter by the shortening \(s\). On the right panel are the graph \(v(x)\) and its slope \(v'\), the angle between the tangent and the horizontal, which must stay small. The simplifications follow in order.

  1. The Euler-Bernoulli model (definition 5.14): the cross-sections stay plane and perpendicular to the axis, the shear deformation and the rotation of the sections are neglected. The bound is in chapter 13: section 13.10 computes that at \(r/L = 0{,}0115\) the rotation of the sections lowers the first frequency of the beam by \(0{,}066\) %, our strip has \(r = h/\sqrt{12}\), so \(r/L = 0{,}0017\), and the error is smaller still.
  2. The axis does not change length: the axial stiffness \(EA/L\) is \(1{,}11 \cdot 10^5\) times the tip stiffness \(3EI/L^3\). When the tip moves away by \(q_{\mathrm{d}}\), an unstretched axis must come closer to the hub by the shortening of the axis \(\tfrac12\int_0^L v'^2\,\mathrm{d}x = 58{,}1\) \(\mu\)m, which is \(0{,}58\) % of the displacement (the estimate of step 2 of the proof of lemma 8.149, error at most \(v'^2_{\max}/4\)). The tip is free in the axial direction, so it can approach without work. If it were held at the length \(L\), the axis would stretch by that much and store the energy \(\tfrac12 (EA/L)\,s^2 = 0{,}0794\) J, which is \(3{,}64\) times the whole bending energy \(0{,}0218\) J. The axis therefore stays unstretched. This displacement we leave out of the position (28.25). It is \(6 \cdot 10^{-5}\) of the link length, and for the forces on \(\vek o\) and \(\theta\) that is also the relative error. For the forces on \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\) it is not that small. The shortening is quadratic in \(q\), so its derivative with respect to \(q\) is of first order, \(\partial u(L)/\partial q = -q\int_0^L\psi_1'^2\,\mathrm{d}x = -0{,}0116\) at \(10\) mm. An axial force component therefore changes the stiffness by about \(1\) %. With an axial force of \(-5\) N at the tip, the tip deflection by the beam-column formula is \(1{,}44\) % larger than the linear one. This term is the geometric stiffness. The model of this section leaves it out, section 28.7 brings it back, where an axial force pulls on it.
  3. Small slope: the curvature of the graph is \(v''(1 + v'^2)^{-3/2}\), and we write the energy with \(v''\). At the largest slope \(0{,}0138\) this error is \(2{,}8 \cdot 10^{-4}\) (relative), at the slope \(0{,}1\) it would be \(1{,}5\) %.

For the proof of the theorem we look at the energy as a function of two coordinates (figure 28.36). On the left panel are the level curves of the energy \(U = \tfrac12\,\vek q^\T\mat K\,\vek q\), ellipses around zero, because the energy is never negative and is zero only at \(\vek q = \vek 0\). At the state \(\vek q\) the force \(-\mat K\vek q\) points to the centre, perpendicular to the level curve. On the right panel is a coordinate that costs no energy: the level curves are parallel lines, and along the valley of zero energy the energy does not change.

We check steps 3 and 4 of the proof on the strip of the example with two shapes, the first two modes of the cantilever, at \(q_1 = 10\) mm and \(q_2 = 3\) mm (figure 28.37). We draw all curves over the interval \([0, L]\). First we draw the second derivative of the first shape, multiplied by \(q_1\), \(q_1\psi_1''\), and the second derivative of the second shape, multiplied by \(q_2\), \(q_2\psi_2''\). Their sum is the second derivative of the displacement \(v'' = \vek\psi''^\T\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\), since the derivative of a sum is the sum of the derivatives. Near the root \(v''\) is negative. Below it we draw \(EI\,(v'')^2\). The square of a negative number is positive, so this curve never drops below the axis, not even near the root, and the area under it, \(2U = 197{,}8\) mJ, is positive. The square of a sum of two terms splits into four products \(EI\,q_{\mathrm{j}} q_{\mathrm{k}}\,\psi_{\mathrm{j}}''\psi_{\mathrm{k}}''\), which we draw in a two by two table, with \(j\) for the row and \(k\) for the column. On the diagonal are the squares with areas \(q_1^2 K_{11} = 43{,}6\) mJ and \(q_2^2 K_{22} = 154{,}2\) mJ. Off the diagonal the same curve appears twice, because \(\psi_1''\psi_2'' = \psi_2''\psi_1''\), so \(K_{12} = K_{21}\). For these shapes its positive and negative parts cancel, and \(K_{12} = 0\). The areas of the table add up to \(43{,}6 + 154{,}2 = 197{,}8\) mJ, the area under the square. Each area in the table is the product of the coordinates \(q_{\mathrm{j}} q_{\mathrm{k}}\) and a number \(K_{\mathrm{jk}} = \int_0^L EI\,\psi_{\mathrm{j}}''\psi_{\mathrm{k}}''\,\mathrm{d}x\) that depends only on the shapes, not on the state.

The step of the proof that \(\vek c^\T\mat K\,\vek c = 0\) is possible only for \(\vek c = \vek 0\) is shown in figure 28.38 with three graphs on \([0, L]\), one under the other. The top graph is \(\vek c^\T\vek\psi''\): if the integral is zero, this function is zero everywhere. So the middle graph \(\vek c^\T\vek\psi'\) is horizontal, and since it is zero at \(x = 0\), it is zero everywhere. The same holds for the bottom graph \(\vek c^\T\vek\psi\), which is likewise zero at \(x = 0\).

Under these assumptions the following holds.

Theorem 28.18 Elastic energy of a link (elastična energija člena)

Let the link be an Euler-Bernoulli beam of length \(L\) with constant stiffness \(EI\) in a floating frame with the reference conditions \(v(0) = 0\) and \(v'(0) = 0\) (definition 28.5), the transverse displacement \(v = \vek\psi^\T\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\) with linearly independent \(\psi_{\mathrm{k}} \in C^2[0, L]\) satisfying \(\psi_{\mathrm{k}}(0) = \psi_{\mathrm{k}}'(0) = 0\), and let the axis not change length, while its shortening is left out of the position (28.25) (simplification 2). Then:

  1. The strain energy of the link in the placement \((\vek o, \theta, \vek q_{\mathrm{d}})\) is
    \[U = \tfrac12\,\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}^\T\,\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}\,\vek q_{\mathrm{d}} , \qquad \mat K_{\mathrm{dd}} = \int_0^L EI\,\vek\psi''(x)\,\vek\psi''(x)^\T\,\mathrm{d}x , \tag{28.26}\]
    independent of \(\vek o\) and \(\theta\).
  2. The matrix \(\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}\) is symmetric and positive definite.
  3. The generalized forces of the elastic forces, that is \(-\partial U/\partial(\vek o, \theta, \vek q_{\mathrm{d}})\), are \(\big(\vek 0,\ 0,\ -\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}\,\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\big)\).
Proof

We must show that \(\vek o\) and \(\theta\) do not appear in the energy, that the energy is a quadratic form in \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\) and that this form is positive definite.

  1. By theorem 28.7 the Green-Lagrange tensor of a point of the link is a function of the displacement gradient \(\bar{\vek u} = \mat\Psi\,\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\) in the axes of the frame, and \(\vek o\) and \(\theta\) are not in it. By definition 4.85 the energy is the integral of a density \(w\) that is a function of the strain. So \(U\) does not depend on \(\vek o\) and \(\theta\).
  2. In the displacement field (5.16) we have \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial x = u' - y\,v''\) and \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial x = v'\), so by theorem 28.7, item 2, the Green-Lagrange strain of the fibre at distance \(y\) is \(E_{\mathrm{xx}} = u' - y\,v'' + \tfrac12\big((u' - y\,v'')^2 + v'^2\big)\). The axis does not change length, which means \(E_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0\) at \(y = 0\), that is \(u' + \tfrac12(u'^2 + v'^2) = 0\). Subtracting this leaves \(E_{\mathrm{xx}} = -y\,v''(1 + u') + \tfrac12 y^2 v''^2\). For the strip of the example \(|u'| < 10^{-4}\) and \(\tfrac12|y\,v''| < 6 \cdot 10^{-5}\), so \(E_{\mathrm{xx}} = -y\,v''\) with a relative error below \(2 \cdot 10^{-4}\). This is the strain of the field (5.16) with \(u' = 0\), and lemma 5.77 with \(u' = 0\) gives \(U = \tfrac12\int_0^L EI\,(v'')^2\,\mathrm{d}x\).
  3. For \(v = \vek\psi^\T\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\) we have \(v'' = \vek\psi''^\T\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\) and \((v'')^2 = \vek q_{\mathrm{d}}^\T\,\vek\psi''\vek\psi''^\T\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\), the sum of the products \(q_{\mathrm{j}} q_{\mathrm{k}}\,\psi_{\mathrm{j}}''\psi_{\mathrm{k}}''\) on the diagonal and off it. Since \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\) depends only on time, by the linearity of the integral (lemma 3.54) we take it out of the integral and obtain (28.26). The integrands \(EI\,\psi_{\mathrm{j}}''\psi_{\mathrm{k}}''\) are continuous, so the integrals exist.
  4. The matrix is symmetric because \(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}''\psi_{\mathrm{k}}'' = \psi_{\mathrm{k}}''\psi_{\mathrm{j}}''\). For a column \(\vek c \ne \vek 0\) we have \(\vek c^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}\,\vek c\) \(= \int_0^L EI\,(\vek c^\T\vek\psi'')^2\,\mathrm{d}x \ge 0\), the area under a square that never drops below the axis.
  5. If this integral is zero, the continuous nonnegative function \(EI\,(\vek c^\T\vek\psi'')^2\) is zero on \([0, L]\), hence so is \(\vek c^\T\vek\psi''\) (lemma 6.10, item 3). By corollary 1.86 \(\vek c^\T\vek\psi'\) is constant, equal to \(\vek c^\T\vek\psi'(0) = 0\). By the same corollary \(\vek c^\T\vek\psi\) is constant, equal to \(\vek c^\T\vek\psi(0) = 0\). The functions \(\psi_{\mathrm{k}}\) are linearly independent, so \(\vek c = \vek 0\). Hence \(\vek c^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}\,\vek c > 0\) for every \(\vek c \ne \vek 0\).
  6. The internal forces of an ideally elastic body have the potential \(U\) (definition 4.85, definition 8.58), so the generalized force is \(-\partial U/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}\). Since \(U\) does not depend on \(\vek o\) and \(\theta\) (step 1), these derivatives are zero. By the rule for the derivative of a quadratic form with a symmetric matrix, \(\partial U/\partial\vek q_{\mathrm{d}} = \mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}\,\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\).

Source: SHABANA (5.113) to (5.117), PDF pp. 225 to 227: the virtual work of the elastic forces is \(-\vek q_{\mathrm{f}}^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{ff}}\,\delta\vek q_{\mathrm{f}}\) with a symmetric positive definite stiffness matrix, because a rigid motion corresponds to a constant strain and the deformation is defined relative to the frame (PDF p. 225). Example 5.4 (PDF p. 226) is the same computation for a beam with an axial and a bending coordinate. The proof in steps is not from the source, because the source does not prove positive definiteness.

We sum up the theorem in words. The elastic energy is a quadratic form of the elastic coordinates in which neither the position nor the rotation of the link appears, so a shift and a rotation of the link without bending do not change the energy. This is exactly the question we asked at figure 28.34: both states have the same \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\), hence \(U = \tfrac12\,K_{11}\,q_1^2 = \tfrac12 \cdot 436{,}1 \cdot 0{,}010^2 = 0{,}0218\) J in both. For the shapes \(\psi_{\mathrm{r}}\) of the first three cantilever modes, normalized so that \(\psi_{\mathrm{r}}(L) = 1\) (so \(q_{\mathrm{r}}\) is the contribution of mode \(r\) to the tip displacement in metres), \(\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}} = \operatorname{diag}(436{,}1,\ 17\,129,\ 134\,295)\) N/m. The matrix is diagonal because the cantilever modes are orthogonal also with respect to the energy (theorem 13.70), and the computation gives zero off-diagonal entries. The static tip stiffness is \(3EI/L^3 = 423{,}4\) N/m, and \(K_{11}\) is \(3{,}0\) % larger. It is larger because, for a given tip displacement, the shape of the static deflection has the least energy (the principle of minimum potential energy, theorem 4.91, applied to a force at the tip), and the shape of the first mode is another shape.

Now the condition the theorem requires, and a case where it fails. The conditions \(\psi_{\mathrm{k}}(0) = \psi_{\mathrm{k}}'(0) = 0\) are not decoration. Take only one condition, \(v(0) = 0\), and the shape \(\psi = x/L\). It satisfies \(\psi(0) = 0\) but not \(\psi'(0) = 0\), and it is a rigid rotation of the link by the angle \(q/L\). Then \(\psi'' = 0\) and \(K_{\mathrm{dd}} = 0\): for \(q = 10\) mm we get \(U = 0\), the matrix is not positive definite and step 5 of the proof stops already at the derivative: \(\vek c^\T\vek\psi' = c/L\) is constant, but equal to \(\vek c^\T\vek\psi'(0) = c/L \ne 0\), so \(\vek c^\T\vek\psi\) need not be constant and \(c = 0\) does not follow. Such a coordinate does not measure bending but a rotation, which \(\theta\) already measures. The same shape gave in section 28.4 a mass matrix with the coordinates \((\theta, q)\) that is singular at \(q = 0\) and nearly singular at \(q = 10\) mm (determinant \(3{,}9 \cdot 10^{-5}\), (28.24)), and here it gives zero stiffness. A coordinate that costs no energy is not elastic, it duplicates \(\theta\).

28.5.2 Generalized forces of the link

We have the elastic forces. The external ones are three: the force at the end of the link, the own weight of the link and the motor moment on the hub. We draw them (figure 28.39). The link lies at the angle \(\theta\) from the horizontal, and at \(\theta = 30^\circ\) it is bent downward from the straight position, the hub is at \(\vek o\) and the frame axes are turned with the link. The weight acts on every point of the link downward, so the arrows of the weight are drawn from a point of the link downward: weight is a pull of the earth, and the arrow of a pulling force starts at the point on which it acts. At the tip acts the force \(\vek F\), \(10\) N downward, in fixed axes. Since the axis of the link is not horizontal, we split it into the components along \(\bar x\) and \(\bar y\), \(-F\sin\theta\) and \(-F\cos\theta\). The lever arm of the force about the hub is the horizontal distance \(L\cos\theta\) between \(\vek o\) and the tip. On the hub acts the motor moment \(\tau\) in the direction of increasing angle.

Since the link is not a rigid bar, a generalized force for the coordinate \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\) has a meaning only if we define it for the link. Definition 8.51 is stated for rigid bodies, but its formula \(\vek F\cdot\partial\vek r/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}\) needs only the position of the point of application as a function of the coordinates, and we have that in (28.25).

Figure 28.40 shows where the division comes from. On the top panel is the link in its position and at the tip the force \(\vek F\). The three virtual changes move the tip each in its own way: the shift of the hub \(\delta\vek o\) moves all points equally, the rotation \(\delta\theta\) moves the tip perpendicular to the line to the hub, and the bending \(\delta q\) moves it transverse to the link. The sum of the three is \(\delta\vek r\), and the work of the force is its projection on \(\delta\vek r\) times \(F\). On the bottom panel is a distributed force: the link is cut into pieces, and each piece is replaced by a point force \(\vek f(\xi_{\mathrm{i}})\,h_{\mathrm{i}}\).

For the weight the expressions of the lemma simplify, as figure 28.41 shows. We draw the link at \(\theta = 30^\circ\) with the hub at \(\vek o\), bent under its own weight. We choose a point of the link and write it with local coordinates: along the axis \(\bar x\) to the foot is \(x\), from the foot transversely to the point is the deflection \(v\), which is negative for a sagging link. On the piece of the link at this point acts the weight \(\rho A g\,h\) vertically down, where \(h\) is the length of the piece. The moment of a vertical force about the hub is the force times the horizontal distance of its point of application from \(\vek o\). We build this distance from two parts. The foot is at the horizontal distance \(x\cos\theta\) from the hub, and the transverse deflection adds \(-v\sin\theta\), which is positive for \(v < 0\) because the sagging point lies to the right of the foot. Together this is the lever arm \(x\cos\theta - v\sin\theta\). The same number is the height of the position vector of the point turned by a quarter turn, since a turn by \(90^\circ\) stands the horizontal component upright. On the right panel we enlarge the point and draw the transverse direction \(\bar y\) of the frame. Bending along the coordinate \(q_{\mathrm{k}}\) moves the point along this direction by \(\psi_{\mathrm{k}}\,\delta q_{\mathrm{k}}\). The transverse direction is tilted from the vertical by the angle \(\theta\), so the vertical component of this displacement is \(\psi_{\mathrm{k}}\cos\theta\,\delta q_{\mathrm{k}}\). The weight is vertical, so it does work only on this component.

Lemma 28.19 Generalized forces of the external loads on a link (posplošene sile zunanjih obremenitev člena)

Let the link be as in theorem 28.18 and \(\vek r(x)\) the position (28.25).

  1. A force \(\vek F\) (a column of two numbers in fixed axes) at the point with local coordinate \(a \in [0, L]\) has the generalized forces
    \[\vek Q_{\mathrm{o}} = \vek F , \qquad Q_\theta = \vek F\cdot\mat R(\theta)\,\mat J\,\big(\bar{\vek r}_0(a) + \mat\Psi(a)\,\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\big) , \qquad \vek Q_{\mathrm{d}} = \mat\Psi(a)^\T\,\mat R(\theta)^\T\,\vek F , \tag{28.27}\]
    with \(\mat J = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\) (the quarter turn).
  2. A distributed force with continuous density \(\vek f(x)\) per length (in fixed axes) has the generalized forces
    \[\vek Q_{\mathrm{o}} = \int_0^L \vek f\,\mathrm{d}x , \quad Q_\theta = \int_0^L \vek f\cdot\mat R\,\mat J\,(\bar{\vek r}_0 + \mat\Psi\vek q_{\mathrm{d}})\,\mathrm{d}x , \quad \vek Q_{\mathrm{d}} = \int_0^L \mat\Psi^\T\mat R^\T\vek f\,\mathrm{d}x . \tag{28.28}\]
    For the own weight, \(\vek f = -\rho A g\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\),
    \[\vek Q_{\mathrm{o}} = -m g\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} , \quad Q_\theta = -\rho A g \int_0^L \big(x\cos\theta - v\sin\theta\big)\,\mathrm{d}x , \quad \vek Q_{\mathrm{d}} = -\rho A g\,\cos\theta\int_0^L \vek\psi\,\mathrm{d}x . \tag{28.29}\]
  3. A motor moment \(\tau\) on the hub, which is rigidly fixed to the root of the link, has
    \[\vek Q_{\mathrm{o}} = \vek 0 , \qquad Q_\theta = \tau , \qquad \vek Q_{\mathrm{d}} = \tau\,\vek\psi'(0) = \vek 0 . \tag{28.30}\]
Proof

We must show that the virtual work of each load is a linear function of the virtual changes of the coordinates, and read off the coefficients. By definition 4.71 the work of the force \(\vek F\) at a virtual change of the position of the point of application \(\delta\vek r\) is \(\vek F\cdot\delta\vek r\).

  1. The position \(\vek r(a)\) from (28.25) is a continuously differentiable function of the coordinates \((\vek o, \theta, \vek q_{\mathrm{d}})\). By the chain rule (lemma 8.30) \(\delta\vek r = \sum_{\mathrm{s}} (\partial\vek r/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}})\,\delta q_{\mathrm{s}}\), and as in step 4 of the proof of lemma 8.52 the virtual work is \(\sum_{\mathrm{s}} Q_{\mathrm{s}}\,\delta q_{\mathrm{s}}\) with \(Q_{\mathrm{s}} = \vek F\cdot\partial\vek r/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}\).
  2. Derivatives: \(\partial\vek r/\partial\vek o\) is the identity matrix. By theorem 8.13, \(\mat R'(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} -\sin\theta & -\cos\theta \\ \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \end{pmatrix} = \mat R(\theta)\,\mat J\), so \(\partial\vek r/\partial\theta = \mat R\,\mat J\,(\bar{\vek r}_0 + \mat\Psi\vek q_{\mathrm{d}})\). The position is linear in \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\), so \(\partial\vek r/\partial q_{\mathrm{k}} = \mat R\,\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{k}}\) with \(\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{k}}\) the \(k\)-th column of the matrix \(\mat\Psi\).
  3. We insert into \(Q_{\mathrm{s}} = \vek F\cdot\partial\vek r/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}\). For \(s = \vek o\) we get \(\vek Q_{\mathrm{o}} = \vek F\), for \(s = \theta\) we get \(Q_\theta = \vek F\cdot\mat R\mat J(\ldots)\), and for \(q_{\mathrm{k}}\) we get \(\vek F\cdot\mat R\,\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{k}} = (\mat R^\T\vek F)\cdot\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{k}}\), because \(\mat R\) is orthogonal by lemma 2.47. This is (28.27).
  4. We divide the distributed force into pieces \([x_{\mathrm{i}-1}, x_{\mathrm{i}}]\) of length \(h_{\mathrm{i}}\). We replace a piece by the point force \(\vek f(\xi_{\mathrm{i}})\,h_{\mathrm{i}}\) at a point \(\xi_{\mathrm{i}}\) of the piece. By definition 4.71 its work is \(\vek f(\xi_{\mathrm{i}})\cdot\delta\vek r(\xi_{\mathrm{i}})\,h_{\mathrm{i}}\). Definition 4.71 covers only point forces, so we define the work of a distributed force as the limit of the work of these substitute forces. The sum over the pieces is a Riemann sum of the continuous function \(\vek f\cdot\delta\vek r\), its limit at \(\max h_{\mathrm{i}} \to 0\) is the integral (definition 3.49), so the work of the distributed force is \(\int_0^L \vek f\cdot\delta\vek r\,\mathrm{d}x\). We insert step 1 and exchange the finite sum over \(s\) with the integral (linearity of the integral): \(Q_{\mathrm{s}} = \int_0^L \vek f\cdot\partial\vek r/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}\,\mathrm{d}x\). By step 3, (28.28) follows.
  5. For the weight, \(\vek f = (0, -\rho A g)\). With \(\mat R\mat J\,(x, v)^\T\) \(= (-v\cos\theta - x\sin\theta,\ x\cos\theta - v\sin\theta)\) the second component is the lever arm \(x\cos\theta - v\sin\theta\), and with \(\mat R\,(0, \psi_{\mathrm{k}})^\T\) \(= (-\psi_{\mathrm{k}}\sin\theta,\ \psi_{\mathrm{k}}\cos\theta)\) the second component is \(\psi_{\mathrm{k}}\cos\theta\). This is (28.29).
  6. The hub is a rigid body rigidly fixed to the root of the link, so its angle of rotation is the angle of rotation of the cross-section at \(x = 0\). By theorem 5.15 the cross-section turns by \(v'\) relative to the axis of the frame, so \(\varphi_{\text{hub}} = \theta + v'(0) = \theta + \vek\psi'(0)^\T\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\). By definition 8.51 (the term with moments) the moment \(\tau\) has \(Q_{\mathrm{s}} = \tau\,\partial\varphi_{\text{hub}}/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}\), so \(Q_\theta = \tau\), \(\vek Q_{\mathrm{d}} = \tau\,\vek\psi'(0)\) and \(\vek Q_{\mathrm{o}} = \vek 0\). With the reference condition \(\psi_{\mathrm{k}}'(0) = 0\) the last is \(\vek 0\).

Source: SHABANA (5.118) to (5.119), PDF p. 227, and Example 5.5 (PDF pp. 227 to 228): \(\delta W_{\mathrm{e}} = \vek Q_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\delta\vek q\) split into \(\vek Q_{\mathrm{R}}\), \(\vek Q_\theta\), \(\vek Q_{\mathrm{f}}\), a force at a point written with the shape matrix and the matrix \(\mat B\) of columns of derivatives with respect to the rotation. The distributed force, the weight and the moment on the hub are not from the source.

The lemma tells how each load divides among the three groups of coordinates. The force \(\vek F\) goes whole into \(\vek o\), into the rotation as a moment about \(\vek o\) (\(Q_\theta\) is the scalar product with the position vector turned by a quarter turn) and into \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\) as the transverse component of the force, evaluated with the shapes at the point of application. The axial component \(-F\sin\theta\) does not go into \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\), because the matrix \(\mat\Psi\) has no axial row: the unstretchable axis carries it, and the hub takes it as a reaction. This holds in the model of this section, which leaves out the shortening of the axis (simplification 2). The effect of the axial force on bending, the geometric stiffness, is brought back in section 28.7.

Figure 28.42 shows the case we shall compute. The hub is at \(\vek o\), the link is horizontal and, under its own weight, bent downward. The weight acts on every point of the link downward, its resultant \(mg\) is at the middle, so the lever arm is \(L/2\), and the hub moment \(\tau\) opposes it.

We test it on an example with the same strip, \(L = 1\) m, \(EI = 141{,}12\) N m\(^2\), \(\rho A = 1{,}872\) kg/m, \(m = 1{,}872\) kg. The plane of motion is now vertical, so the own weight acts in the plane of bending, in which the strip is \(6\) mm wide. The three shapes are the first three cantilever modes, normalized to \(\psi_{\mathrm{r}}(L) = 1\).

Example A: weight at \(\theta = 0\). The link is horizontal, \(\cos\theta = 1\) and \(\sin\theta = 0\). By (28.29), \(Q_\theta = -\rho A g\,L^2/2 = -9{,}182\) N m and \(Q_{\mathrm{d}}\) has components \(-7{,}190\), \(3{,}985\) and \(-2{,}336\) N. At rest the hub is held by the moment \(\tau = 9{,}182\) N m, which is the equilibrium of the hub (\(\tau + Q_\theta = 0\), in the equation for \(\theta\) there are no elastic forces by theorem 28.18, item 3), and equals the root moment of a cantilever under its own weight, \(\rho A g L^2/2\) (equilibrium of the whole link about the hub, chapter 5). The elastic tip displacement from \(\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}\,\vek q_{\mathrm{d}} = \vek Q_{\mathrm{d}}\) with the first \(k\) modes:

number of modes tip displacement (mm) ratio to \(-\rho A g L^4/(8EI) = -16{,}267\) mm

With the modes the displacement approaches the static deflection of the cantilever (chapter 5, theorem 5.57). With one mode it is \(1{,}3\) % too large. Theorem 28.18 and lemma 28.19 are thus checked together on a case we know from chapter 5.

Example B: at \(\theta = 30^\circ\) (\(\cos\theta = 0{,}8660\)). By (28.29), \(Q_{\mathrm{d}}\) is \(\cos 30^\circ\) times smaller, \(-6{,}226\), \(3{,}451\) and \(-2{,}023\) N, and the displacement is also \(0{,}8660\) times smaller, \(14{,}09\) mm. The moment \(Q_\theta\) depends on the bending too: for a rigid link it is \(-\rho A g\,L^2\cos\theta/2 = -7{,}952\) N m, with the bending \(\int_0^L v\,\mathrm{d}x = -5{,}635\) mm m it is \(-8{,}004\) N m, which is \(0{,}65\) % more. The force at the tip from figure 28.39: \(\vek F = (0, -10)\) N. By (28.27) the component of \(\vek F\) in the frame is transverse \(-F\cos\theta = -8{,}660\) N and axial \(-F\sin\theta = -5{,}000\) N. So \(\vek Q_{\mathrm{d}} = \psi_{\mathrm{r}}(L)\cdot(-8{,}660) = -8{,}660\) N for every \(r\), and the moment about the hub is \(Q_\theta = -F\,(L\cos\theta - v(L)\sin\theta)\). At \(v(L) = 0\) it is \(-F\,L\cos\theta = -8{,}660\) N m, with the lever arm \(L\cos\theta = 0{,}8660\) m from the figure. At the true displacement \(v(L) = -20{,}4\) mm it is \(-8{,}762\) N m, which is \(1{,}18\) % more, because the tip has dropped and the lever arm is longer. The tip displacement with three modes is \(-20{,}426\) mm, the exact value \(-F\cos\theta\,L^3/(3EI)\) is \(-20{,}456\) mm, the ratio is \(0{,}9985\). Since the bending is \(2\) % of the length, the effect on the lever arm is measurable, and the lemma includes it, while a frozen lever arm \(L\cos\theta\) does not. An effect of the same size is the one the model leaves out: by the beam-column formula the axial component \(-5\) N increases the tip deflection by \(1{,}44\) %, to \(-20{,}750\) mm (simplification 2). Likewise the axial weight component \(\rho A g\sin\theta\,(L - x)\) at \(30^\circ\) softens mode 1 by about \(0{,}8\) % (one-mode estimate \(\dfrac{\rho A g\sin\theta}{K_{11}}\int_0^L (L - x)\,\psi_1'^2\,\mathrm{d}x\), with the integral \(0{,}393\) m and \(K_{11} = 436{,}1\) N/m). The model of this section leaves this out too, and section 28.7 returns it as geometric stiffness.

Now a case that shows that the claim of the lemma, item 3 (\(\vek Q_{\mathrm{d}} = \vek 0\) for a moment on the hub), is a property of the frame and not of the moment. Take the chord frame: the axis \(\bar x\) goes from \(\vek o\) to the tip of the link, and the reference conditions are \(v(0) = 0\) and \(v(L) = 0\) (section 28.2, reference conditions). The shapes are \(\psi_{\mathrm{k}} = \sin(k\pi x/L)\), and \(\psi_{\mathrm{k}}'(0) = k\pi/L \ne 0\). Figure 28.43 shows the same link in both frames. Above is the clamped frame: the axis \(\bar x\) lies on the tangent at the root, which is the direction of the hub, and the moment \(\tau\) acts only on the rotation. Below is the chord frame: the axis \(\bar x\) goes through the tip, the tangent at the root, which is the direction of the hub, is turned relative to the axis by the slope \(v'(0)\), the rotation of the axis from the horizontal is \(\theta\), and the angle of the hub is \(\theta + v'(0)\). The moment \(\tau\) acts on the hub, that is on the angle \(\theta + v'(0)\).

In the chord frame \(\tau = 6{,}24\) N m (the moment that accelerates a rigid link with moment of inertia \(0{,}624\) kg m\(^2\) about the hub at \(10\) rad/s\(^2\)) and by (28.30) \(Q_{\mathrm{d},\mathrm{k}} = \tau\,k\pi/L\) equals \(19{,}60\), \(39{,}21\) and \(58{,}81\) N for \(k = 1, 2, 3\), while in the clamped frame it is \(0\). For the first mode the chord stiffness is \(K_{11} = EI\,(\pi/L)^4\,L/2 = 6873\) N/m, so the force \(19{,}60\) N alone would give an elastic displacement of \(2{,}85\) mm. This is not an error but bookkeeping: the same physical rotation of the hub is \(\theta\) in the clamped frame and the sum \(\theta + v'(0)\) in the chord frame. For the bent link of the figure (tip \(10\) mm, tangent at the root horizontal) the angle of the chord is \(d/L = 0{,}0100\) rad, the slope of the root against the chord is \(v'(0) = -0{,}0100\) and the angle of the hub is \(0{,}0100 - 0{,}0100 = 0\), equal to the rotation of the clamped frame. The rule "the moment enters only the equation for \(\theta\)" therefore holds only in the clamped frame. If we used it in the chord frame, we would leave out the force \(19{,}60\) N in the first elastic equation, and the model would pass the moment only to the rotation of the frame axis and would not impose the slope of the root on the link, as if the link were joined to the hub by a hinge.

For this reason we shall use the clamped frame from here on (section 28.6). In it the total generalized force of the link on the hub is

\[\vek Q = \begin{pmatrix} \vek Q_{\mathrm{o}} \\ Q_\theta \\ \vek Q_{\mathrm{d}} \end{pmatrix}_{\text{external}} + \begin{pmatrix} \vek 0 \\ 0 \\ -\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}\,\vek q_{\mathrm{d}} \end{pmatrix}_{\text{elastic}} ,\]

where the external loads have the groups of lemma 28.19. This column will be the right side of Lagrange's equations in section 28.6. The effects of the turning of the link are not in this section. In Lagrange's equations they are given by the terms of the kinetic energy that depend on \(\dot\theta\,\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\) and \(\dot\theta^2\,\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\) (section 28.4), and at fast turning also by the axial force at the shortening of the axis (section 28.7).
