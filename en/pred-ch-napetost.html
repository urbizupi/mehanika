Chapter 2 Stress and equilibrium

Chapter 2Lecture: Stress and equilibrium

1 Stress is force per area, and it depends on the cut

The whole chapter stands on one move. We cut the body in thought, draw the cut-off piece alone and write Newton's law for it. Today we do it with half a bar.

We start with a question. Let us draw a steel bar. We pull it at both ends with a force of 10 kN, the weight of about one tonne. Below it we draw a thin steel wire and pull it with the same force. What happens? The bar holds. The wire does not: when we let go of the force, it stays longer than it was. The same force, the same steel, a different outcome. So what tells us how hard the material is loaded? And is it a single number?

Let us look at the two from the end. The cross section of the bar is a circle of diameter 10 mm and area \(78{,}54\ \text{mm}^2\). The cross section of the wire has half the diameter, 5 mm, and so a quarter of the area, \(19{,}63\ \text{mm}^2\). Now we divide the force by the area. On a scale we put how many newtons come to one square millimetre. For the bar that is 127,3 MPa, for the wire 509,3 MPa. Steel carries about 220 MPa before it stays permanently longer. The bar is below this limit, the wire far above it.

The answer to both questions is in one picture. The picture lies in the plane of the axes \(x\) and \(y\). We draw the left part of the bar, pulled at its left end by the force \(N\). The right part we only outline. We do not cut it straight along the cross section but obliquely, by the angle \(\theta\). Across the cut the right part pulls the left one with the vector \(\vek t\), the force per unit area. This vector points along the bar, not perpendicular to the cut. The cut faces the direction of the normal \(\vek n\), tilted from the axis by the same angle. So \(\vek t\) splits into a part that pulls the two pieces apart and a part that slides them.

The claim in one sentence: stress (napetost) is the internal force divided by the area that carries it, \(\sigma = N/A\) ((2.4)), and on a cut tilted by \(\theta\) the same force gives the normal stress \(\sigma\cos^2\theta\) and the shear stress \(|\sigma\sin\theta\cos\theta|\) ((2.13), (2.14)). So there is no single number. Only the cut gives it.

The claim uses four things: a force inside the bar, dividing by the area, a smaller value on the oblique cut and two parts. For each we ask why.

1.1 Why is there a force in the bar at all, if nothing moves?

With the wire we simply divided a force. But which force? Let us draw a bar and pull it with the force \(F\) at both ends. We cut it in thought, here, at the distance \(x\) from the left end. The bar stays whole, the cut is only in our head. Now we draw the left part by itself. What acts on it? The force at the left end, pulling it to the left. Does the left part move to the left? No, it is at rest. Newton's law says that for a body at rest the sum of the forces is zero ((2.2)). So the right part must pull it across the cut to the right, with an equally large force \(N\).

This is the axial force (osna sila). We put the cut anywhere, so in every cross section this bar is pulled with \(N = F\). We do not see this force until we cut the body in thought. We left out the weight of the bar here. Why we may, we show with the assumptions at the end. In the book: 2.1.

1.2 Why does the force alone not tell whether the bar holds?

We have the axial force. Why must we still divide it? Let us take two equal thin bars of cross section \(A\) and place them side by side. At the ends we fix them to two rigid plates and pull the plates apart with the force \(F\). How much does each carry? The bars are equal and the force pulls in the middle between them. Nothing tells one from the other, so they carry the same, \(F/2\) each.

Now we glue them into one bar of cross section \(2A\) and pull it with the same force \(F\). Both halves stretch as before, the glue has nothing to carry. Each half still carries \(F/2\) on the area \(A\). The thick bar carries twice the force, but it has twice the material. What is the same in both cases? The force per square millimetre, \(F/(2A)\). This is what the material feels. That is why the stress is \(\sigma = N/A\), and why the wire with a quarter of the area is under four times the stress. In the book: 2.2.

1.3 Why is the stress on an oblique cut smaller?

We claimed that the stress on an oblique cut is smaller. Does less force cross such a cut, or is the same force only shared out differently? We draw three left parts of the same bar, and each is pulled by the same force \(N\). We cut the first straight, the second by \(30^\circ\), the third by \(60^\circ\), each time from the cross section. How large is the force across the oblique cut? The same as across the cross section. Why? The left part is the same piece with the same force at its left end, and Newton again demands that the right part holds it with \(N\).

So what changes? The length of the cut. The straight cut is \(b\) long, the cut at \(30^\circ\) is \(1{,}155\,b\) long, the cut at \(60^\circ\) is \(2\,b\), exactly twice as long. The same force spreads over a longer and longer cut. So the force per unit area is \(\sigma\) on the straight cut, only \(0{,}866\,\sigma\) at \(30^\circ\) and just half, \(0{,}5\,\sigma\), at \(60^\circ\).

Why exactly \(1/\cos\theta\)? Let us look in space. We draw the bar along the axis \(x\), with a cross section of width \(b\) and height \(h\). Through the origin we draw the cross section, and then the oblique cut with the normal \(\vek n\), tilted by the angle \(\theta\). The height of the cut, along \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), stays \(h\). The width, along \(\vek d\) in the cut, becomes the slanted side \(b/\cos\theta\), the hypotenuse of a right triangle with the leg \(b\) next to the angle \(\theta\). The area of the cut is therefore \(A_\theta = A/\cos\theta\) ((2.9)), and the force per unit area is

\[|\vek t| = \frac{N}{A_\theta} = \sigma\cos\theta .\]

In the book: 2.4, with a round cross section in 2.35.

1.4 Why two parts, normal and shear?

The force per unit area is one single vector. Why then do we split it into two parts? Let us go back to the picture of the oblique cut. The vector \(\vek t\) points along the bar. The cut, however, faces the direction of the normal \(\vek n\). What does that do to the material at the cut? The part of the force that points along the normal pulls the right piece away from the left one, as on the cross section. The rest lies in the face of the cut and tries to slide the two pieces past each other, like a deck of cards pushed along its top. The first we call the normal stress (normalna napetost) \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}}\), because it points along the normal, the second the shear stress (strižna napetost) \(\tau\) (2.18).

How large are they? The projection on the normal adds one more factor \(\cos\theta\), because \(\vek n\) is tilted by \(\theta\) from the direction of \(\vek t\). The rest gets the factor \(\sin\theta\). So

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{n}} = \sigma\cos\theta \cdot \cos\theta = \sigma\cos^2\theta , \qquad \tau = |\sigma\cos\theta \cdot \sin\theta| .\]

In the book: 2.4, steps 1 to 5 with the dot product.

1.5 The ground everything stands on

Where do the questions why stop? At four things that need no more proof. First: a body at rest has a zero sum of forces, that is Newton, and the left part of the cut bar showed it. Second: two equal bars share a load equally, because nothing tells one from the other, as with the thin bars. Third: the slanted side of a rectangle is longer than the straight one by \(1/\cos\theta\), a right triangle on the cut in space. Fourth: a vector splits into a part along a given direction and a rest perpendicular to it (2.14), as in the parts of \(\vek t\).

1.6 Back up

Now we build the claim again, in three steps. The same force \(N\) across every cut, because the left part is at rest. A larger area, \(A/\cos\theta\), so \(|\vek t| = \sigma\cos\theta\). We split \(\vek t\): one more \(\cos\theta\) for the normal part, \(\sin\theta\) for the shear part. This gives \(\sigma\cos^2\theta\) and \(|\sigma\sin\theta\cos\theta|\), the two arrows of the picture at the start.

1.7 The view by numbers: a graph

What do the numbers add? We draw a horizontal axis for the tilt \(\theta\) and a vertical one for the stress, with the angles marked. The normal stress starts at \(\sigma\) and falls to zero. The shear stress starts at zero, grows and falls again. The dots are the values at \(0^\circ\), \(30^\circ\), \(45^\circ\), \(60^\circ\) and \(90^\circ\). The shear dots show a peak. Where? At \(45^\circ\), where the shear equals \(\sigma/2\) and the two curves cross (2.19).

What does this mean for our bar with \(127{,}3\) MPa? On the cut at \(45^\circ\) the normal and the shear stress are both half of \(127{,}3\), so \(63{,}7\) MPa. In metals such as steel, the largest shear stress roughly decides when yielding starts (in the book: 2.4, the Tresca criterion). So we must be able to compute the shear also where the cross section has none. The stress at one point of the bar is therefore not one number but a whole curve, and the cut picks the value.

1.8 The view by Pythagoras: a check

Do the two parts really add back up to \(\vek t\)? In the picture of the oblique cut the two parts are the legs of a right triangle, \(\vek t\) is the hypotenuse. Pythagoras must hold:

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{n}}^2 + \tau^2 = \sigma^2\cos^4\theta + \sigma^2\sin^2\theta\cos^2\theta = \sigma^2\cos^2\theta = |\vek t|^2 .\]

It holds. This view is for whoever would rather see a triangle than a formula.

1.9 Assumptions: why they hold, when not and what they cost

First assumption: the force is spread evenly over the cut. We draw a flat bar and pull it with the force \(F\) by pins in the middle of its ends. Right at the end the force is gathered near the middle of the cross section. A little further it is already more even, far from the end almost fully even. Why does it hold? Saint-Venant's principle (Saint-Venantovo načelo) says that two loads with the same resultant and the same tendency to turn the body spread the force almost the same way far from where they act. When does it not hold? Near grips, pins and holes. What does it cost? There \(N/A\) is only the average, locally the force per unit area is larger. By how much the book does not compute here, the figure is schematic. In the book: 2.2.

Second assumption: \(A\) is the area before loading. We draw the cross section before loading grey dashed and after it orange. Why does it hold? When a steel bar stretches by one thousandth of its length, the area of the cross section drops only by 0,06 %, to \(0{,}9994\,A\), and the two squares overlap in the picture. When does it not hold? For rubber stretched by half, the area drops to \(0{,}667\,A\), by a third, because rubber nearly keeps its volume. What does it cost? Our \(N/A\) is the nominal stress. How the two are told apart, 2.13 shows.

Third assumption: the weight of the bar is left out. Why does it hold? We draw the force at the end, 10 kN, and on the same scale the weight of a bar of diameter 10 mm and length 10 m, 60 N. We can hardly see it, it is 0,6 % of the force. When does it not hold? For hanging bars, where the weight is the only force. What does it cost? The force a cross section carries differs from the one without weight by at most 60 N, so by 0,6 %.

Fourth assumption: the cut does not reach the ends of the bar. As \(\theta\) goes to \(90^\circ\) the cut gets longer and longer, and at \(90^\circ\) it is a lengthwise cut that splits the bar into two halves side by side. There the derivation does not hold, but the formulas give zero, and that is exactly what we saw with the glue of the thick bar: nothing has to be carried across a lengthwise cut.

What do we keep? We cut, draw the piece alone, write Newton. Half a bar gave us the force \(N\), dividing by the area gave the stress, and the oblique cut showed that the stress depends on the cut. Next time we take a small wedge around a point and ask whether we need infinitely many numbers to know all the cuts through the point.

2 Three vectors tell everything (Cauchy's formula)

The thread of the whole chapter is one single move. We cut the body in thought, draw the cut-off piece alone and write Newton's law for it. In the previous part this piece was half a bar. Today it is a tiny pyramid at one point.

Let us take again a bar pulled by an axial force, and on it a point \(P\). We cut through \(P\) perpendicular to the axis. The normal of the cut points along the bar, and so does the traction vector. Now through the same point we draw a cut tilted by \(30^\circ\). It has its own normal and its own traction vector, which is shorter. At a tilt of \(60^\circ\) the vector is shorter still. Infinitely many cuts pass through one point, and each has its own vector. Do we then need to know infinitely many vectors for one point?

No. Three are enough. That is today's claim, and one picture will show it.

From the body at the point \(P\) we cut out in thought a tiny pyramid with a triangular base, a tetrahedron (tetraeder). We place it so that one corner is at \(P\). One of its faces is slanted, with the normal \(\vek n\). This is the cut we are interested in. Across it the rest of the body pulls the tetrahedron with the force \(\Delta\vek F_{\mathrm{n}}\). The other three faces lie in the coordinate planes: the face \(PBC\) with outward normal \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) carries the force \(\Delta\vek F_{-\mathrm{x}}\), the face \(PCA\) with normal \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) carries the force \(\Delta\vek F_{-\mathrm{y}}\), and the face \(PAB\) with normal \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) the force \(\Delta\vek F_{-\mathrm{z}}\). The tetrahedron is at rest, so these four forces add up to zero. The claim will come from this:

\[\vek t(\vek n) = n_{\mathrm{x}}\,\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) + n_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}) + n_{\mathrm{z}}\,\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}) .\]

This is Cauchy's formula (Cauchyjeva formula, (2.25)). The traction vectors on three faces perpendicular to the axes give the traction vector on every cut. We multiply each of the three by a component of the normal and add them.

The claim uses four things we have not explained yet: what stress at a point is, why a sum, why the weights are exactly the components of the normal, and where the weight of the tetrahedron went. Let us go in order.

2.1 What is stress at a point?

In the bar we divided the force by the area of the whole cross section. What do we divide in a body where the force is not the same all over the cut? We cut the body by a plane and look at the left part. On the cut we choose the point \(P\). Its outward normal points out of it, toward the right part. Around \(P\) we choose on the cut a small patch of area \(\Delta A\). The right part pulls the left one across it with the force \(\Delta\vek F\). We divide the force by the area and shrink the patch toward \(P\). What is left is the traction vector (vektor napetosti) \(\vek t(\vek n)\), the force per unit area at the point itself ((2.15)). Why do we shrink? Because in a general body the force changes from point to point, and only a small patch tells what is at \(P\). Why does \(\vek n\) stand in the notation? Because we saw earlier in L2.10 that the same \(P\) gives a different vector for a different cut. In the book: 2.5.

2.2 Why would the vector on a slanted cut be a sum of three?

Where would a sum come from in the formula at all, and where the weights \(n_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(n_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(n_{\mathrm{z}}\)? Let us first try where we already know everything, in the bar.

We draw a bar pulled by the axial force \(N\), with the \(x\) axis along the bar and the \(y\) axis up. Far from the ends we choose the point \(P\). Next to it we cut out a small wedge (klin) and enlarge it so that we can see it. On the left it is bounded by the cross section through \(P\). Its outward normal is \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), its area \(\Delta A_{\mathrm{x}}\). The rest of the bar pulls the wedge across it to the left with the force \(\sigma\,\Delta A_{\mathrm{x}}\). On the right is the slanted face with normal \(\vek n\) and area \(\Delta A\), tilted by the angle \(\theta\). Across it the bar pulls the wedge to the right with the force \(\sigma\cos\theta\,\Delta A\). On the bottom face with normal \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) there is no force, since the bar carries no force across a lengthwise cut.

The wedge is at rest. What does that tell us? The forces to the left and to the right are equal, \(\sigma\,\Delta A_{\mathrm{x}} = \sigma\cos\theta\,\Delta A\). This holds exactly when

\[\Delta A_{\mathrm{x}} = \cos\theta\,\Delta A = n_{\mathrm{x}}\,\Delta A .\]

So equilibrium asks nothing special of the bar. It only asks that the cross face is smaller than the slanted one by the factor \(n_{\mathrm{x}}\). When we divide both forces by the area of the slanted face, the quotient \(\Delta A_{\mathrm{x}}/\Delta A\) becomes just \(n_{\mathrm{x}}\), and the vector on the slanted face is the vector on the cross face multiplied by \(n_{\mathrm{x}}\). Hence the weighted sum. In the book: 2.6.1.

What is the assumption here? That there is no force across the lengthwise cut, \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}) = \vek 0\). Why it holds: this is so in the solutions the books give for a uniformly pulled bar and for a hanging bar. When not: near the grips that hold the bar, where the force is gathered on part of the section. What it does: without it the sum would get one more term, \(n_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}})\). The general formula keeps this term, so the assumption concerns only the bar, not the claim.

2.3 Why are the areas in the ratio \(n_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(n_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(n_{\mathrm{z}}\)?

In a general body the normal has three components, so we need three faces at \(P\), and the wedge becomes a tetrahedron. Why are its faces in exactly the right ratio?

We draw the axes and put the point \(P\) at the origin. On the axes we choose the vertices \(A\), \(B\) and \(C\), for our normal \(PA\) of length \(3\) mm, \(PB\) of length \(1{,}5\) mm and \(PC\) of length \(1{,}5\) mm. We join them into the slanted face \(ABC\). Its normal is \(\vek n = (\tfrac13, \tfrac23, \tfrac23)\). Now we light the face along the \(x\) axis. Each of its points moves along a ray straight back to the plane \(x = 0\). The vertex \(A\) falls into \(P\), \(B\) and \(C\) stay where they are. The shadow is the face \(PBC\).

Remember a sheet of paper under a lamp. As we tilt it, its shadow shrinks. The shadow of a tilted face is smaller by the cosine of the tilt. And the cosine of the angle between the normal and the \(x\) axis is exactly the component \(n_{\mathrm{x}}\). Let us check with numbers. The shadow has the area \(1{,}125\ \text{mm}^2\), the slanted face \(3{,}375\ \text{mm}^2\):

\[\frac{\Delta A_{\mathrm{x}}}{\Delta A} = \frac{1{,}125}{3{,}375} = \frac13 = n_{\mathrm{x}} .\]

In the same way light along the \(y\) axis casts the shadow \(PCA\) with area \(n_{\mathrm{y}}\,\Delta A\), along the \(z\) axis the shadow \(PAB\) with area \(n_{\mathrm{z}}\,\Delta A\) ((2.21)). The exact proof computes the volume of the tetrahedron in two ways, once with the base \(ABC\), once with \(PBC\). In the book: 2.6.2.

Where is the assumption here? There is none. The slanted face and its shadow are flat, and this is only geometry. So this step is exact at every size of the tetrahedron.

2.4 Why may we forget the weight?

Not only forces across the faces act on the tetrahedron. It also has a weight. Why is it missing in L2.11?

We draw a tetrahedron of height \(h = 1\) mm. Across the slanted face acts a force, which is stress times area. On all its material acts the weight, which for steel is \(25{,}51\) Pa per unit area of the slanted face. Now we make the tetrahedron ten times smaller, \(h = 0{,}1\) mm, and magnify it ten times under a lens so that it looks the same size in the picture. The area dropped a hundred times, so the force across the face per unit area stayed the same. But the volume dropped a thousand times, and the weight per unit area is only \(2{,}551\) Pa. Once more ten times, to \(h = 0{,}01\) mm: the force stays, the weight is \(0{,}2551\) Pa, almost a dot.

Why so? Area goes with the square of the size, volume with the cube. In a picture magnified each time so that the area looks the same, the weight therefore drops ten times per step. The weight per unit area is \(\rho g h/3\) and goes to zero with \(h\). Stresses in steel, on the other hand, are measured in millions of pascals. In the book: 2.6.2, the table at 2.50.

Assumption: the only force on all of the material is the weight, and the density is the same everywhere near \(P\). Why it may be: the weight per unit area falls like \(\rho g h/3\), at \(h = 0{,}01\) mm it is already \(0{,}2551\) Pa. When not: when the force on the material of a small piece is not bounded by a constant times the volume of the piece. What it does: at \(h = 1\) mm the error would be \(25{,}51\) Pa against stresses of millions of pascals, and in the limit the weight term vanishes and there is no error. The same holds for a moving body. Mass goes with volume, so the term with the acceleration vanishes like the weight. Cauchy's formula therefore holds in an accelerating body too (the book, after 2.26).

2.5 Why does the minus at the faces at \(P\) disappear?

On the faces at \(P\) the outward normals are \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), not \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\). How do pluses stand in the formula then?

Let us go back to the cut body. On the right part is the same patch, only its outward normal is \(-\vek n\). The left part pulls the right one with the force \(-\Delta\vek F\), opposite to the force on the left part. This is the law of action and reaction. The point \(P\) is the same on both parts. Opposite forces on the same area give opposite traction vectors:

\[\vek t(-\vek n) = -\vek t(\vek n)\]

((2.16)). So \(\vek t(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = -\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\), and the minus goes to the other side of the equation as a plus. In the book: 2.21.

2.6 The ground and the way back

We have arrived at statements that need no further explanation:

  • a piece of a body at rest has zero net force (Newton),
  • a force and its counterforce are equal in size (action and reaction),
  • the shadow of a tilted face is smaller by the cosine of the tilt,
  • area goes with the square of the size, volume with the cube, so the weight per unit area drops with size.

Now we go back up, to the tetrahedron. First: the tetrahedron is at rest, so \(\Delta\vek F_{\mathrm{n}}\), \(\Delta\vek F_{-\mathrm{x}}\), \(\Delta\vek F_{-\mathrm{y}}\), \(\Delta\vek F_{-\mathrm{z}}\) and the weight add up to zero ((2.22)). Second: we divide everything by the area of the slanted face. The areas of \(PBC\), \(PCA\) and \(PAB\) become \(n_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(n_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(n_{\mathrm{z}}\), because they are shadows ((2.23)). Third: we shrink the tetrahedron. The weight vanishes, and the quotients force over area become traction vectors at \(P\). Fourth: action and reaction turn three minuses into pluses. What is left is Cauchy's formula.

The third step hides the last assumption. The average force per unit area on a small face of the tetrahedron approaches the traction vector at \(P\). Why it holds: we assume that stress changes continuously from point to point, without jumps, as continuum mechanics assumes. When not: for a force gathered on a line there is no traction vector at the point at all (2.40), and neither at the scale of grains and atoms, where matter is not continuous. What it does: without it there is no stress at a point, and the formula has nothing to talk about. The exact proof with limits: 2.6.4.

2.7 Nine numbers

Three vectors, each with three components, are nine numbers, the stress components (komponente napetosti). Where do they live? Let us draw them.

We draw the axes and at \(P\) a small cube. On the face with normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) lies the vector \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\), split into three parts: \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) points out of the face, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\) lie in it. On the face with normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) are \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}\), and on the face with normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zy}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\). The first index tells the face, the second the direction ((2.26)). On the board we write them as a table where each row is one face:

\[\begin{array}{c|ccc} & x & y & z \\ \hline \vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) & \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} & \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} & \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} \\ \vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}) & \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}} & \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} & \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} \\ \vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}) & \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}} & \sigma_{\mathrm{zy}} & \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} \end{array}\]

The thicker arrows, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\), are normal stresses, the six thinner ones are components of the shear parts. What does this add? Each number has its own face and its own direction that we can point at with a finger. In the book: 2.6.5.

2.8 A machine that turns a direction into a force

Algebraically the formula is a machine. We put in the direction of the cut, the normal \(\vek n\), and out comes the force per unit area across that cut. In components ((2.27)):

\[t_{\mathrm{x}}(\vek n) = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\,n_{\mathrm{y}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}\,n_{\mathrm{z}} ,\]

and the same for \(t_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(t_{\mathrm{z}}\). This is the \(x\) column of the table, dotted with the normal. What does this add? The nine numbers are the whole machine, for every direction we will still need.

2.9 The bar on a cut we could not handle before

Let us go back to the bar. In it only \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma\) is nonzero. For the slanted cut with \(\vek n = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta, 0)\) the formula gives \(\sigma\cos\theta\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), which we already found with the wedge. We assumed nothing new, the bar already obeyed the formula. Now let us take a cut with the normal \((\tfrac13, \tfrac23, \tfrac23)\), not tilted about any axis. The formula gives \(\vek t = (\sigma/3, 0, 0)\). For the bar with \(\sigma = 127{,}3\) MPa this is about \(42{,}4\) MPa along the bar. Without the formula such a cut would be hard to handle. In the book: 2.6.6, where this vector of \(42{,}44\) MPa is drawn in figure 2.54.

3 A small cube: the sum of forces gives the equilibrium equations, the sum of moments gives symmetry

The thread of the chapter stays the same: we cut the body in thought, draw the cut-off piece alone and write Newton's laws for it. Today the piece is a small cube. There are two questions. Stress changes from point to point. Can it change any way it likes, or does something tie it to the stress right next to it? And there are nine stress components. Are these really nine different numbers? We already know the answer to the first, we just have not named it yet. It is hidden in the hanging bar.

Let us draw a steel bar, of length \(L = 10\) m, hanging under its own weight. The axis \(x\) points down. At the top it is held by a force that pulls the top face up uniformly. Now we cut out in thought a slice between the depths 4 m and 6 m, with its centre at the depth \(x\), and draw it alone, enlarged. Who pulls on it? The part above pulls it up with a force of \(36{,}06\) N. The part below pulls it down with \(24{,}04\) N. And on every bit of it acts the weight, \(12{,}02\) N in all. The slice is at rest, so the forces add up to zero:

\[36{,}06\ \text{N} = 24{,}04\ \text{N} + 12{,}02\ \text{N} .\]

This is the whole claim of today's lecture in small: the top and the bottom face do not carry the same load, and the difference carries the weight. For a general body it reads like this. On opposite faces of a small piece the stress is not the same, and the difference per unit length balances the body force (prostorninska sila). These are the equilibrium equations (ravnotežne enačbe) (2.38). Besides, the shear stresses on neighbouring faces are equal, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\) ((2.48)), or the cube would spin. We reach both by asking "why" of every claim we use.

3.1 Why does the stress in the hanging bar drop with depth?

Why does the upper part pull harder than the lower one? Anyone who has carried a bag knows: what hangs below must be carried from above. The part above the slice carries everything that hangs below it, and the slice itself too.

How much is that in stress? Stress is force per area. The cross-section has the area \(7{,}854 \cdot 10^{-5}\) m², so the difference of stress between the top and the bottom face is

\[\frac{12{,}02\ \text{N}}{7{,}854 \cdot 10^{-5}\ \text{m}^2} \approx 0{,}153\ \text{MPa} .\]

The thickness of the slice is 2 m, so the stress drops by \(0{,}0765\) MPa for every metre. That is \(\rho g\), the weight of a cubic metre of steel, \(7800 \cdot 9{,}81 = 76\,518\) N/m³.

Let us draw the graph too. On the right we set up the axes: depth downwards as in the bar, stress horizontally. The line is the stress \(\sigma(x) = \rho g\,(L - x)\), largest at the top, zero at the free end. We mark the top and bottom faces of the slice, and between them the drop of stress \(\rho g\,\Delta x\), our \(0{,}153\) MPa. What is the drop divided by the thickness? The slope of the line. What happens when we make the slice thinner? The quotient stays a slope, now at a single depth, so a derivative. The equation of the slice becomes \(\sigma'(x) + \rho g = 0\). In the book: 2.7.2 and 2.7.7.

3.2 What changes in a body that is not a bar?

In the bar only two faces did any work, because nothing acts on the mantle. In a general body we cut out a cube around a point \(P\). How many faces are loaded? All six. We look only at the forces in the direction \(x\), the other two directions go the same way.

Let us draw the cube from the front, with its centre \(P\) and edge \(h\). The axis \(x\) points right, the axis \(y\) up, the axis \(z\) towards us. On the right face acts the force \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) at its centre \(Q_{+\mathrm{x}}\), times the area \(h^2\). On the left face acts the same kind of force with the value at the centre \(Q_{-\mathrm{x}}\). It points left, because the outward normal there is \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) (lemma 2.21). We drew it longer, because we take the stress to decrease in the direction \(x\), as in the hanging bar. How much does not cancel? We carry the shorter arrow over to the left, grey dashed, and what is left is the blue excess.

Now the top and the bottom face. Their normal forces point in the direction \(y\), so we do not draw them here. In the direction \(x\) the shear component \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\) acts on them: on the top face to the right, on the bottom face to the left. This pair does not cancel fully either, the excess is blue again. The front and back faces carry the shear component \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}\), which we cannot see in this view, but it behaves the same way. What balances the excesses? The body force on the material of the cube, \(h^3 b_{\mathrm{x}}\), drawn from \(P\). In the picture, where we do not see the third pair, it is exactly as long as both excesses together.

On the board we write that the \(x\) components add up to zero:

\[\big[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(Q_{+\mathrm{x}}) - \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(Q_{-\mathrm{x}})\big] h^2 + \big[\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}(Q_{+\mathrm{y}}) - \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}(Q_{-\mathrm{y}})\big] h^2 + \big[\sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}(Q_{+\mathrm{z}}) - \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}(Q_{-\mathrm{z}})\big] h^2 + h^3 b_{\mathrm{x}} = 0 .\]

We divide by the volume \(h^3\). Each bracket is now the difference of stress on two faces a distance \(h\) apart, divided by \(h\). That is a slope, as for the line of the bar, and as \(h \to 0\) a partial derivative:

\[\frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}}{\partial z} + b_{\mathrm{x}} = 0 .\]

The directions \(y\) and \(z\) give two more such equations. Do you see the pattern of the indices? The second index is \(x\) everywhere, the direction of the forces we add. The first index tells by which coordinate we differentiate: in the direction of the normal of its face. In the book: 2.7.6, the proof in 2.7.8.

Where did we assume something? First, that the cube is at rest, like the whole body. Why right: a part of a body in equilibrium is in equilibrium itself. When not: a body that accelerates, where the forces do not add up to zero. Effect: without acceleration the right side is exactly zero. Second, that \(P\) is an interior point. Why: only then does the whole cube lie in the body for small \(h\). When not: on the surface, where the force of the surroundings per unit area gives the stress vector, and we do not write the equation there.

3.3 Why may we take the force on a face as the stress at its centre times the area?

In the last equation we wrote each force as the value at the centre of the face times \(h^2\). But the stress varies over the face. Why are we allowed to do this?

Let us draw the face from the side, with the edge \(h\) and the centre \(Q_{+\mathrm{x}}\). The arrows are the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) at points of the face, and their tips lie on a line: the stress varies linearly. Whatever the upper half carries above the value at the centre, the lower half carries less. The average is exactly the value at the centre. This is the ground of this step: for a load that grows evenly, the average is in the middle.

And when the distribution is not a line? On the right it is curved. Near the centre we replace it by the line through the value at the centre, and only a small remainder is left.

Here an assumption enters: the stress in the body varies smoothly, without jumps. More precisely, the stress components are continuously differentiable fields. Why is that right? Inside one material the stress changes gradually. When does it fail? At a point force, at the tip of a crack and at the interface of two materials, and there the equations do not hold in this form. What does it do to the result? The remainder in the cube is smaller than \(\eta h\) for any small chosen \(\eta\), since the farthest corner is only \(0{,}866\,h\) from \(P\). After dividing by \(h\) it goes to zero and drops out of the equation.

But why do we need an error that falls faster than \(h\)? Because the excess we are after is itself of size \(h\). In the hanging bar the stress between the faces of a cube with edge 1 mm changes by only \(76{,}52\) Pa. An error of that size would hide it completely. The book takes this step as the assumption (2.35) until it has exact summation over a face.

3.4 Why must the sum of moments be zero?

Now the second question: why six numbers and not nine? For that we need the second condition of equilibrium.

Take a ruler, 30 cm long, on a table, with its centre \(O\) and axes. At the end \(A_1\) we push across with a force \(\vek F\), at the end \(A_2\) with the opposite force \(-\vek F\). The forces add up to zero. Does the ruler stay at rest? No, it turns. You have all done this with a pencil on a desk. Each force turns with its arm, \(\vek r_1\) and \(\vek r_2\), and both moments point up, the ruler turns counterclockwise. But if the forces act along the ruler, they are parallel to the arms and the sum of moments is zero. A body at rest does not start to turn, so the sum of moments is zero ((2.45)). In the book: 2.8.3.

3.5 Why must \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\)?

The faces of the cube carry six shear components. Which two of them try to turn it about the axis \(z\), and each which way? We replace the ruler by our cube and look along the axis \(z\).

Let us draw the cube and the axis \(z\) through \(P\), pointing towards us. The axis \(x\) points right, the axis \(y\) up. On the right face acts the shear force \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}h^2\) upwards, on the left face the same force downwards. Each has an arm \(h/2\). Together they turn the cube counterclockwise, with the moment

\[2 \cdot \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} h^2 \cdot \frac h2 = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} h^3 .\]

We repeat this on a second copy of the cube. On the top face \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}h^2\) acts to the right, on the bottom face to the left, the arm is again \(h/2\), and the pair turns it clockwise, with the moment \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}h^3\). And the normal forces? Their lines pass through \(P\), there is no arm and no moment. What is left is the difference of the two pairs, the \(z\) component in (2.46):

\[h^3\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} - \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}) .\]

It must be zero, so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\). But only if nothing else gives a moment. Is that really so? That is the next why.

3.6 Why can neither the weight nor the change of stress fix this?

What did we leave out? The weight of the cube and the fact that the stress changes a little across the cube. Both give a moment, but smaller by one power of \(h\). The weight is proportional to the volume \(h^3\), the arm to \(h\), together \(h^4\). The change of stress across the cube is proportional to \(h\), times the area \(h^2\), times the arm \(h\), again \(h^4\). But the pair of shear forces is \(h^3\).

Let us look at numbers. Suppose that in a steel cube \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) were 1 MPa larger than \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\). On the horizontal axis is the edge \(h\), on the vertical axis the moment, both on a logarithmic scale. The moment of the pair at \(h = 1\) mm is \(1 \cdot 10^{-3}\) N m. The bound for the moment of the weight is only \(6{,}627 \cdot 10^{-8}\) N m, the ratio is \(6{,}627 \cdot 10^{-5}\). And for every cube ten times smaller the ratio is ten times smaller. For a small enough cube the weight cannot balance any difference that is not zero. For steel with the same density throughout the cube the moment of the weight about the centre is even exactly zero. In the book: 2.8.4, the proof with bounds in 2.8.5.

The theorem 2.41 has three assumptions. Let us state each with what it does.

  1. The stress components have continuous partial derivatives near \(P\). Why right: inside one material the stress varies smoothly. This is more than the equilibrium equations needed, they used derivatives only at \(P\). When not: at the same three places as before. Effect: the moment from the change of stress falls below the line of the pair.
  2. The body force density is bounded near \(P\). Why right: the weight of steel is \(76\,518\) N/m³ everywhere. Effect: its moment stays below the bound in the picture.
  3. Only forces act on each part of the body, across the surface and on the volume, and there is no other source of moment. When not: materials whose inner structure also carries distributed moments that are not moments of forces, body couples (telesni momenti). Such materials need extra equations, this book does not treat them. Effect: without this, nothing can balance the pair \(h^3(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} - \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}})\), so the difference is zero.

3.7 Back up: from the slice to six numbers

Now we go back up. The slice: one pair of faces, the difference carries the weight. The cube: three pairs for each direction, divide by the volume, three equilibrium equations. The same cube, which must not turn: the pairs of shear forces must balance each other, so the shear components are symmetric. Shear on one pair of faces therefore never comes alone, an equal shear on the neighbouring pair always goes with it (in the book: 2.8.8). What does that mean for counting?

Let us draw the table of components: the row says which axis the face is perpendicular to, the column the direction of the component. There are nine numbers. On the diagonal are the normal components, they stay alone. The shear components across the diagonal are equal in pairs. Six different numbers are left. But there are only three equilibrium equations. Does equilibrium alone then determine the stress? No. The missing equations come from the properties of the material, which tie stresses to strains, and from the requirement that the strains fit together. The next chapters lead there.

3.8 Another view: pressure in water

One equation can have several faces. You know from school that the pressure in water grows with depth. Is this the same equation?

Let us draw a container with water up to the surface, the axis \(x\) points down. In the water we choose a small cube with centre \(P\) and enlarge it. The surrounding water pushes up on the bottom face harder than down on the top face, and the difference is the weight of the cube. On the side faces it pushes from the left and from the right equally, because nothing changes in the horizontal direction. This is exactly the picture of the cube from before, only the stress is a pressure, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -p\). The equilibrium equation gives \(p = p_0 + \rho g\,x\), the line on the graph, which starts at the air pressure \(p_0\) at the surface. For water the slope of the line is \(\rho g = 9810\) N/m³, and at a depth of 10 m the pressure is larger by \(98\,100\) Pa, about as much as the air pressure. What does this view add? You already know the equilibrium equation, under the name of the law of hydrostatics. In the book: 2.7.9.

4 The turned cube: principal stresses, largest shear, Mohr's circle

One last time, the move of the chapter: we cut the body in thought, draw the cut-off piece alone and write Newton's laws for it. Today the piece is a small cube that we turn until the shear vanishes from its faces. Why do we care? A material fails when the normal or the shear stress exceeds its strength (trdnost). So we must know on which cut through a point the material pulls most and on which it slides most.

Let us draw the \(x\) and \(y\) axes and around the point a small square piece. On the faces perpendicular to the \(x\) axis acts the normal stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 67\) MPa, on the faces perpendicular to the \(y\) axis \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = 53\) MPa. Along the faces there is also the shear \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 24\) MPa. All this is the left picture. What happens if we turn the piece? On the new faces the stresses change. Is there a turn in which the picture is simplest?

There is. Let us draw the piece once more, turned so that one normal points along \(\vek n_1 = (\tfrac45, \tfrac35, 0)\) and the other along \(\vek n_2 = (-\tfrac35, \tfrac45, 0)\). On it only the normal stresses remain, 85 MPa along \(\vek n_1\) and 35 MPa along \(\vek n_2\), and there is no shear anywhere. This is the right picture.

The claim of the segment on this picture: at every point there are three mutually perpendicular faces without shear ((2.80)), their normal stresses \(\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \sigma_3\) are the largest and the smallest normal stress of all cuts, and the largest shear is \((\sigma_1 - \sigma_3)/2\) on the cut half-way between the first and the third principal direction ((2.93)). Now we ask this claim why, one question after another.

4.1 What does a face without shear mean?

On the turned piece there is no shear. At which turn does it vanish, and what is special about the traction vector then? We turn the cut about the \(z\) axis and compute the stresses for each angle \(\theta\). On the horizontal axis we put the angle, on the vertical one the stress. The blackwhite curve is the normal stress: 67 MPa at \(0^\circ\), already 84,28 MPa at \(30^\circ\). The blue curve is the shear: 24 MPa at \(0^\circ\), only 5,94 MPa at \(30^\circ\). Where is the normal stress largest? At about \(37^\circ\), and exactly there the shear is zero. A quarter turn later the normal stress is smallest, and again the shear is zero.

What happens to the traction vector on such a cut? Let us draw the axes and the point \(P\), and around it the same cuts, each as a short segment with the arrow of its traction vector. Almost everywhere the arrow is tilted against the segment: a part of the vector lies along the cut, and that is the shear. Only on the four blue cuts does it stand perpendicular. These are the cuts with the normals \(\pm\vek n_1\) and \(\pm\vek n_2\). Let us check by computing. For the normal \(\vek n_1\) the Cauchy formula gives \(\vek t(\vek n_1) = (68, 51, 0)\). Compare with the normal: \(85 \cdot \tfrac45 = 68\) and \(85 \cdot \tfrac35 = 51\). The traction vector points exactly along the normal, \(\vek t = 85\,\vek n_1\). A face without shear is therefore a face with \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \lambda\,\vek n\) ((2.77)). The normal is an eigenvector of the stress matrix and the number \(\lambda\) is the normal stress on it. We call them the principal direction (glavna smer) and the principal stress (glavna napetost). In the book: 2.11.1.

4.2 Why does such a face exist at all?

In the numerical example we found it by computing. Do we find it for every stress? Let us turn the question round: first find the cut with the largest normal stress, then show that it cannot carry shear.

Let us draw the cut through the point \(P\) from the side, as a line. On it is the unit normal \(\vek n\). Suppose that among all cuts through \(P\) this one has the largest normal stress. The traction vector \(\vek t\) acts on it. Let us split it: the normal part \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}}\vek n\) points perpendicular to the cut, the shear part \(\tau\) lies in the cut. Suppose the shear part is not zero. What then?

Tilt the normal by a small angle \(\alpha\) toward the shear part, into \(\vek n'\). The normal stress on the new cut is the projection of the traction vector on \(\vek n'\). Drop perpendiculars from the end of the normal part and from the end of \(\vek t\) onto the orange line. The normal part becomes only a little shorter on the new line, by a term of order \(\alpha^2\), for \(\alpha = 0{,}1\) by half a percent. The shear part, however, now contributes for the first time, about \(\tau\alpha\). When we tilt the normal, the traction vector itself changes too, since \(\vek t(\vek n') = \ten\sigma\,\vek n'\). This change contributes as well, and its contribution equals the first, another \(\tau\alpha\). So the normal stress grows by about \(2\tau\alpha\). But it was already the largest. A contradiction, so there is no shear part.

Why is the second contribution equal to the first? Here the assumption enters: the stress matrix is symmetric, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\) ((2.79)). Why does it hold? We got it in the previous segment from the balance of moments on a small cube. When does it hold? When the body is in equilibrium, the stress changes smoothly around the point, and only forces act on the parts of the body, no distributed moments (2.41). What without it? The two contributions could cancel, there would be no gain, and the argument would fail. The book shows a non-symmetric matrix that has no eigenvector at all (2.11.1).

One question remains. Does a largest normal stress exist at all? Let us draw all possible unit normals from one point \(O\). Their ends form the sphere of radius 1, a closed surface without an edge. The normal stress changes over it without jumps. When we go round the whole sphere, it is therefore largest somewhere, and that is where \(\vek n_1\) points. In the book: 2.11.2.

4.3 Why are there three, and mutually perpendicular?

We have one face without shear. Where do two more come from, and why are they perpendicular to the first? Let us stay on the sphere of directions. We got the first principal direction \(\vek n_1\) as the direction of the largest normal stress. We find the second by the same argument, only that we search among the normals perpendicular to \(\vek n_1\), so on the circle. This gives \(\vek n_2\). The third direction \(\vek n_3\) is perpendicular to both. On the turned piece, \(\vek n_2\) is at \(126{,}87^\circ\). Check: \(\vek n_1\cdot\vek n_2 = -\tfrac{12}{25} + \tfrac{12}{25} = 0\). The third direction is here \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), which points out of the picture, and the stress on it is 0. So we have three principal stresses, \(\sigma_1 = 85\) MPa, \(\sigma_2 = 35\) MPa and \(\sigma_3 = 0\). Symmetry enters here too: only because of it the second step does not spoil the first. In the book: 2.11.2.

4.4 Why is the largest shear half-way between two principal directions and equal to half the difference?

On which cut does the material slide most, and by how much? This is the core of the segment, so let us take our time. Put the axes along the principal directions. Then the normal of a cut gives three numbers, \(w_1 = n_1^2\), \(w_2 = n_2^2\) and \(w_3 = n_3^2\). Each is a square, so it is not negative, and their sum is 1. Let us draw a number line with the principal stresses \(\sigma_3\), \(\sigma_2\) and \(\sigma_1\). On each we put a weight of mass \(w_{\mathrm{i}}\): \(w_3\), \(w_2\) and \(w_1\), here 0,3, 0,2 and 0,5. Where do we support the line so that it balances? At the point \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}}\), since \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}} = w_1\sigma_1 + w_2\sigma_2 + w_3\sigma_3\) ((2.81)). The normal stress is the centre of mass of the weights. And the shear? Its square is the mean of the squared grey distances from the support, with the same weights ((2.92)). Shear is the spread of the weights.

When are the weights spread most? All lie between \(\sigma_3\) and \(\sigma_1\). None is farther from the midpoint of this interval than half its length. We get the largest spread when half the mass goes to each end and nothing to the middle, \(w_2 = 0\). Then each weight is exactly \((\sigma_1 - \sigma_3)/2\) from the support, and that is the shear. The weights \(w_1 = w_3 = \tfrac12\) mean \(n_1 = n_3 = 1/\sqrt2\): the normal lies half-way between \(\vek e_1\) and \(\vek e_3\). On such a cut the normal stress is \((\sigma_1 + \sigma_3)/2\). In the book: 2.12.2.

Where is this cut on the cube? Let us draw the cube in the principal axes \(\vek e_1\) and \(\vek e_3\). The stress \(\sigma_1\) pulls the faces perpendicular to \(\vek e_1\), the stress \(|\sigma_3|\) pushes the faces perpendicular to \(\vek e_3\). The cut of largest shear is a diagonal. We cut off a triangular piece along it. On it act the same pull \(\sigma_1\) and the same push \(|\sigma_3|\) as on the cube. The piece is at rest, so across the slanted face there must act the traction vector that balances them. Its normal part is the mean, its shear part is half the difference.

4.5 Why a circle?

The theorem says how large the largest shear is, but not how the stresses change as we turn the cut. What do we get if we draw every cut as a single point?

On the horizontal axis we put the normal stress, on the vertical one the shear. The shear gets a sign through the direction \(\vek d = (-n_{\mathrm{y}}, n_{\mathrm{x}}, 0)\) along the cut. The cross-section of the bar is the point \((\sigma, 0)\): all the stress is normal. The cut at \(45^\circ\) is the point with normal stress \(\sigma/2\) and shear of size \(\sigma/2\), for the bar of diameter 10 mm under a force of 10 kN that is \(127{,}3/2 = 63{,}7\) MPa. In the bar \(\sigma_1 = \sigma\) and the other two principal stresses are zero, so by the theorem with the weights we know that this is the largest shear of all cuts through the point, not only of those in the picture. The longitudinal cut at \(90^\circ\) is the point \((0, 0)\). The cut at \(30^\circ\) is the point \((0{,}75\,\sigma;\ -0{,}433\,\sigma)\), and the cut at \(60^\circ\) lies on the same circle. All lie on a circle with centre at \(\sigma/2\) and radius \(\sigma/2\).

Why a circle? The normal stress is \(\sigma\cos^2\theta\), the shear \(\sigma\sin\theta\cos\theta\). We write the first as \(\tfrac{\sigma}{2} + \tfrac{\sigma}{2}\cos 2\theta\) and the second as \(\tfrac{\sigma}{2}\sin 2\theta\). That is a point on the circle of radius \(\sigma/2\) at the angle \(2\theta\), since \(\cos^2 2\theta + \sin^2 2\theta = 1\). So the cut at \(30^\circ\) is \(60^\circ\) round the circle from the cross-section, twice as far. A quarter turn of the cut is half the circle. That is why in the numerical example \(37^\circ\) and \(127^\circ\) were a quarter turn apart: on the circle they are opposite ends. In the book: 2.12.3, (2.98).

4.6 The ground

Where did we reach the ground? The table of the first segment for the bar at \(0^\circ\), \(45^\circ\) and \(90^\circ\) is a computation anyone can repeat: those are three dots on the circle. A quantity that changes without jumps over the sphere of directions has a largest value on it. And \(\cos^2 + \sin^2 = 1\) gives the circle. We need nothing more.

4.7 Back up

Now we go from the ground back up to the turned cube, on a new example. A thin plate has \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 90\) MPa, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = 30\) MPa and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 40\) MPa. We draw the axes. The cut with normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) is the point \((90, 40)\), the cut with normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) the point \((30, -40)\). A quarter turn of the cut is half the circle, so these are opposite ends of a diameter. The centre is half-way, \(m = (90 + 30)/2 = 60\) MPa, and the radius comes from the right triangle with legs 30 and 40, \(R = \sqrt{30^2 + 40^2} = 50\) MPa. We draw the circle. Where is the shear zero? Where the circle meets the horizontal axis, at \(60 + 50 = 110\) MPa and at \(60 - 50 = 10\) MPa. These are the principal stresses. From the cut \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) to the first principal face we turn by the angle \(2\theta\) on the circle, so by \(\theta\) on the plate. This is a turned piece as at the start of the segment: its faces are the points where the circle meets the axis. At the top and bottom of the circle the shear is 50 MPa.

Is 50 MPa the largest shear? No. The theorem gives \((110 - 0)/2 = 55\) MPa. Where is the difference? The circle of the previous picture covers only the cuts that contain the \(z\) axis. The third principal stress of the plate is 0, on the unloaded face. On the axis of normal stress let us put all three principal stresses. The cuts about each principal direction give their own circle: the blue one of radius 50 MPa, the large one of radius 55 MPa and the small one of radius 5 MPa. All other cuts lie between them ((2.102)). The top of the large circle is the largest shear at the point, \(\tau_{\max} = 55\) MPa. For our example from the start it is the same: \(\sigma_1 = 85\) MPa, \(\sigma_3 = 0\), so the largest shear is \((85 - 0)/2 = 42{,}5\) MPa, not \((85 - 35)/2\).

4.8 The view of algebra: numbers that do not change when we turn

The principal stresses are eigenvalues, so we also get them from the characteristic equation \(\lambda^3 - I_1\lambda^2 + I_2\lambda - I_3 = 0\) ((2.85)). For the plate it is \(-\lambda(\lambda - 110)(\lambda - 10) = 0\). The coefficients \(I_1\), \(I_2\), \(I_3\) ((2.87)) do not change when the axes turn. On the plate's circle we see it for \(I_1\): \(90 + 30 = 120\) and \(110 + 10 = 120\), both twice the centre \(m\). When we turn the axes, the points \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) move round the circle, but the centre stays. What does this view add? We get the principal stresses by computing, without drawing, and a check with them: the sum of the normal stresses is the same in all axes. In the book: 2.11.3.

4.9 Assumptions

  • Symmetric stress, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\): we stated it where it enters, at the second contribution when the normal is tilted. Effect on the result: the principal stresses are real and the principal directions perpendicular.
  • The circle holds only for cuts that contain a principal direction, condition \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\). When not: a cut tilted toward all three axes is on no circle, it lies in the region between the circles. Effect: whoever looked at only one circle of the plate would get 50 MPa instead of 55 MPa, 9 percent too little, on the unsafe side.
  • The plate: the stress is the same at every point of the plate, and the plate's weight is neglected. Why we may: the forces are spread evenly along the edges and the plate is thin. Effect: the edge forces per unit area become the stress components directly, the points \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) on the circle.
  • One point: the principal directions hold at the point where we took the nine numbers. At another point of the same body the faces without shear are in general turned differently. So we must turn the piece at each point separately.
  • Equal principal stresses: the ordering \(\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \sigma_3\) always exists, but with equal values the directions are not unique. In a fluid at rest all three are \(-p\), and the largest shear is \((-p - (-p))/2 = 0\). On the picture of weights all three weights would stand at one point, there is no spread, the shear is zero on every cut, and every direction is principal.
