Chapter 3 Constitutive equations

Chapter 3Lecture: Constitutive equations

1 We ask the material by a tension test: a line and two numbers

Let us take a steel bar and a rubber cord and load both with the same stress. Equilibrium gives both the same stress, since it never asks what the body is made of. And yet the cord stretches roughly two thousand to twenty thousand times more than the bar. So where does the number come from that tells steel from rubber? Today we get it, and not by a calculation but by a measurement.

Let us draw a steel bar, more precisely its middle part between two marks. The distance between the marks is called the gauge length (merilna dolžina), and we denote it by \(L\). The diameter of the bar we denote by \(d\). Now we clamp the bar in a machine and pull it. Below we draw it once more, first grey dashed, as it was. The machine pulls it with a force \(F\) at both ends. What happens? The bar gets longer and at the same time thinner, we draw it orange, strongly magnified. How much longer it got is shown by the blue distance \(\Delta L\) between the old and the new mark.

Now the graph. Horizontally we plot the strain \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \Delta L/L\), vertically the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = F/A\), where \(A\) is the area of the cross-section. The machine raises the force slowly, and at each force we write down one pair of numbers. Each pair is one point. What do we see? The points lie on a straight line through the origin.

That is the answer of the whole test in one sentence: as long as the stress is not too large, the measured points lie on a line through the origin, so the material is described by two numbers, the slope \(E\) of the line, which says how strongly it resists stretching, and the number \(\nu\), which says how much it thins meanwhile. We measure both, we derive neither. Now we go down through the questions this sentence opens.

1.1 Why measure, why not compute?

We could compute the stress. Why do we not compute this number as well? Recall the hanging bar. From equilibrium we got the stress \(\sigma(x) = \rho g\,(L - x)\), and we never asked what the bar is made of. So equilibrium tells us the force that pulls the bar, but not how much the bar gets longer. Let us also count. What do we look for at each point of a body, and how many equations do we have?

\[\underbrace{3}_{\text{displacement}} + \underbrace{6}_{\text{strain}} + \underbrace{6}_{\text{stress}} = 15\ \text{unknowns}, \qquad \underbrace{6}_{\text{strain from displacement}} + \underbrace{3}_{\text{equilibrium}} = 9\ \text{equations} .\]

Six equations are missing. These six the material must tell by itself, how much it stretches under a given force. We call them constitutive equations (konstitutivne enačbe), after the Latin verb constituere, to put together: they say how the substance is made up in terms of its response. We do not get them from equilibrium or geometry, so we set them up from measurements. In the book: 3, introduction.

1.2 Why a bar?

Why do we ask the material with a bar and not with a piece of some other shape? Let us look at what happens inside it. We pull the bar at both ends with a force \(F\). Let us cut it anywhere between the marks. The right part is at rest, so the left part holds it with the axial force \(N = F\), the same in every cross-section. Far from the grips this force is spread evenly over the cross-section. If the steel is the same everywhere, every piece responds the same. Let us split the gauge length into three equal pieces. After loading they are again equally long, each got longer by a third of the whole elongation \(\Delta L\).

What do we gain? From one measured force and one measured elongation we get the stress \(F/A\) and the strain \(\Delta L/L\), valid at every point between the marks. One measurement is one point of the graph. In a body of a more complicated shape the stress would change from point to point, and we would not know which stress belongs to which strain.

Four assumptions entered here, each with its cost.

  • The force is spread evenly over the cross-section between the marks. It holds because the marks are on the thinner middle part, far from the heads, which the grips squeeze only on their surface. It fails near the grips and where the bar necks before it breaks. Without it, \(F/A\) and \(\Delta L/L\) are only averages and belong to no point.
  • The axial force is \(F\) in every cross-section. It holds because the machine pulls slowly and the weight is negligible: the middle part with diameter 10 mm and length 50 mm weighs \(0.30\) N, while the machine pulls with several thousand newtons. It fails in the hanging bar, where the weight is the only load. We return to it below.
  • The steel is the same everywhere between the marks, homogeneous (homogen). We check this by cutting two bars from different parts of a block in the same direction: they give the same line. It fails for soil, whose properties depend on depth. Without this assumption the pieces would stretch differently: in a block glued from steel with 196 GPa and steel with 190 GPa the strains would differ by about 3 %, and \(\Delta L/L\) would be only an average.
  • We compute the stress with the area \(A\) before loading, although the bar gets thinner. At the strain \(0.001\) the area shrinks by \(0.06\) %, up to the yield stress by less than a tenth of a percent, and that is the error in the stress. At large strains it is no longer negligible. In the book: 3.1.1.

1.3 Why stress and strain, not force and elongation?

The machine measures a force and an elongation. Why do we not simply plot these two? Let us try, with three bars of the same steel.

Let us draw the graph of force against elongation. A bar of length \(L\) and cross-section \(A\) gives a line. Now let us take a bar of the same steel twice as long. What happens at the same force? It is made of two bars of length \(L\), one after the other. Each carries the same force and gets longer by the same \(\Delta L\), the whole by \(2\,\Delta L\), and its line is half as steep. Now a bar twice as thick. It is like two thin bars side by side, each carries half the force. For the same elongation it needs twice the force, and its line is twice as steep. The same steel, three lines. So the graph of force and elongation describes the bar, not the material.

How do we remove the bar? We divide the force by the area, the elongation by the length. The bar twice as long has twice the elongation and twice the length, the quotient stays the same. The bar twice as thick has twice the force and twice the area, here too the quotient stays the same. All three bars give one single line. What is left is the material alone. The ground of this step is two bars one after the other and two side by side: both we can check with our hands. In the book: 1.11 and 2.12.

1.4 What does the slope \(E\) say?

The line has one single number, its slope. What does this number say about the steel? Back to the graph of the tension test. Let us draw a triangle on the line: over the horizontal run \(\Delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) the line rises by \(E\,\Delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\). The slope \(E\) is called the modulus of elasticity, Young's modulus (modul elastičnosti), and the line is

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} .\]

Let us read one point. Our bar of diameter 10 mm, pulled with 10 kN, has the stress \(127.3\) MPa and the strain \(6.496 \cdot 10^{-4}\), this is the orange point. Their quotient is

\[E = \frac{127.3\ \text{MPa}}{6.496 \cdot 10^{-4}} \approx 196\,000\ \text{MPa} = 196\ \text{GPa} .\]

In the book: 3.1.2, (3.1).

Why such a big number? \(E\) is the quotient of stress and strain, and strain has no unit and is small, so the quotient is large and has the unit of stress. Put \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = 1\) and we get \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\): the modulus is the stress at which the bar would get longer by its whole length, if the line reached that far. It does not. At the yield stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{Y}} = 220\) MPa the graph bends, and that already at the strain \(0.00112\):

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{Y}}}{E} = \frac{220\ \text{MPa}}{196\ \text{GPa}} = 0.00112 .\]

So the line is only a little more than a thousandth of strain long.

The assumption that the relation is a line has a cost. It holds because we measured it, up to the yield stress. Our bar is at \(127.3\) MPa, at 58 % of the yield stress, so we may use \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\). It fails above the yield stress. There, at the strain \(0.002\), the line would predict \(196\,000 \cdot 0.002 = 392\) MPa, while the idealized steel carries only 220 MPa.

1.5 Young's view: a column of the substance

In 1807 Young was the first to write down this number, not as a stress but as a column of the same substance: the weight of the column is to the weight that compresses the substance by a given amount as the length of the substance is to its shortening. In our terms the weight of the column is \(E A\), the slope of the line times the area of the cross-section. What does this view add? A picture for the big number: \(E\) is a column that would compress the substance by its whole length. In the book: 3.1.2, history.

1.6 What does all this stand on?

And why do the points lie on a line? Here the why questions stop. We do not derive this, we measure it. At the bottom are three observations.

  1. The points lie on a line through the origin. At twice the stress the strain is twice as large.
  2. When we reduce the force, the points return along the same line. At zero force the strain is zero, the bar returns to its original length.
  3. The strain depends only on the stress, not on how fast we pull and not on how the bar was loaded before: each stress has one single point of the line.

From the first and the third observation we make a model, the linear elastic material (linearno elastičen material), and the second follows from them. In the book: 3.1.9.

1.7 Why a second number?

The bar does not only get longer. What happens to its thickness, and does \(E\) already tell us? Let us look at the diameter \(d\) of the bar. After loading it is smaller by \(\Delta d\). The change of diameter divided by the diameter is the transverse strain \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \Delta d/d\). The diameter shrinks, so it is negative.

Horizontally we plot the axial strain, vertically the transverse one, positive upwards. At each force we measure both. What do we see? Again a line through the origin, only it falls. Over the horizontal run \(\Delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) it drops by \(\nu\,\Delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\). This constant number is Poisson's ratio (Poissonovo število):

\[\nu = -\frac{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}}{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}} .\]

Why the minus? For a material that thins, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\) is negative, and the minus makes \(\nu\) positive. For ordinary structural steel \(\nu = 0.3\). Our bar therefore has

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = -0.3 \cdot 6.496 \cdot 10^{-4} = -1.949 \cdot 10^{-4}, \qquad \Delta d = -1.949 \cdot 10^{-4} \cdot 10\ \text{mm} = -1.949 \cdot 10^{-3}\ \text{mm} .\]

The diameter shrinks by almost two micrometres, from 10 mm to \(9.998\) mm. \(E\) says how much longer the bar gets, \(\nu\) how much thinner meanwhile.

Could we derive \(\nu\)? Poisson derived with molecules that for a material behaving the same in all directions it is always \(1/4\). In 1848 Wertheim pulled glass and metal tubes, and the measurements did not agree with \(1/4\). So we measure \(\nu\) for each material. In the book: 3.1.8, (3.12).

Here too is an assumption. The round cross-section stays a circle, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\), because steel behaves the same in all directions. It fails for wood, where the transverse strains in two directions can differ and the material needs more Poisson's ratios (3.4). For steel one single number is enough.

1.8 Back up: from one point to the hanging bar

One bar, one force and one elongation give one point. All points on the line give \(E\). The thinning gives \(\nu\). With this we compute the elongation of any bar pulled by an axial force \(N\):

  1. the stress is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = N/A\),
  2. on the line, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/E = N/(EA)\),
  3. the elongation is \(\Delta L = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\,L\), so
    \[\Delta L = \frac{N L}{E A} .\]

The bar is a spring with constant \(EA/L\). Our bar at 10 kN shows \(\Delta L = 0.03248\) mm on the gauge length 50 mm, a little over three hundredths of a millimetre, while a bar 10 m long gets longer by \(6.496\) mm. In the book: (3.2).

The formula assumes that the axial force is the same in all cross-sections. In the hanging bar it is not, the top carries the whole weight, the bottom nothing. If we put the whole weight into the formula, we would get a value twice too large. So how do we compute there?

Let us draw the hanging bar. At the top an even pull holds it, only the point \(O\) is fixed, the axis \(x\) points down. We choose a short piece between the depths \(x\) and \(x + \Delta x\) and magnify it. The part above pulls it up, the part below pulls it down, and its own weight pulls it down too. What does it remind us of? Of a specimen pulled by two forces \(F\). So we say that the stress at a point depends only on the strain right next to it, and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/E\) holds at each point separately.

Why may we? The stress at the top is \(0.765\) MPa, 288 times below the yield stress, so we are deep on the line. And over a piece 1 cm long the stress changes only by \(\rho g \cdot 0.01\ \text{m} = 765\) Pa, a thousandth of the stress at the top, and its weight is exactly this difference. So the piece is loaded almost evenly, like a specimen. What would it cost if this did not hold? The stress at a point would depend on the strain elsewhere too, and a single \(E\) would not give the strain.

We draw the bar of length \(L\) once more. The stress falls from \(\rho g L\) at the top to zero at the bottom. We divide it by \(E\) and get the strain, a shape of the same form, \(3.904 \cdot 10^{-6}\) at the top. We add up the elongations of the pieces from the top down and get the displacement, which at the bottom reaches

\[u(L) = \frac{\rho g L^2}{2E} = 1.952 \cdot 10^{-5}\ \text{m} .\]

At half the length the displacement is already three quarters of this, because the upper half is stretched more. The formula for a weightless bar pulled by half the weight gives the same: \(\Delta L = (\rho g A L/2)\,L/(EA) = \rho g L^2/(2E)\). This closes the promise of chapter 1, where we took the displacement of the hanging bar from the books. In the book, step by step: 3.1.10, (3.14).

1.9 By eye: four lines

This is the view for someone who has only a few minutes. In the same graph we draw the lines of four materials. At the same strain \(0.001\) steel carries 196 MPa, titanium 116 MPa, aluminium 74 MPa, wood along the grain only about 12 MPa. Stiffness is a steepness you see with your eyes. Rubber with a modulus of \(0.01\) to \(0.1\) GPa would lie on the horizontal axis in this scale. The numbers in tables are typical, not exact: SADD gives 207 GPa for steel instead of 196 GPa, a difference of a little over 5 %, and any computed elongation can be off by that much if we do not know the actual steel. In the book: 3.18.

1.10 Where the line ends

What happens if we go past the limit? The axis of strain and the axis of stress as before. We load along the line up to the yield stress, above it the stress stays 220 MPa in the idealization, and we pull to the strain \(0.002\). Now we unload. The point does not return along the same path but along a line with the same slope \(E\). At zero stress a permanent strain \(0.000878\) remains. At the strain \(0.00139\) the stress was 220 MPa during loading, and during unloading it is 100 MPa. The stress 100 MPa, on the other hand, the bar reached during loading already at the strain \(0.000510\). So the same strain has two stresses, and such a material is not elastic. This view shows the edge of the model: the two numbers \(E\) and \(\nu\) describe the material only on the straight part. In the book: 3.1.9.

We got the line with the slope \(E\) and the thinning with \(\nu\) from a bar pulled in one direction only. Are these two numbers enough when we pull, press and shear the material in all directions at once? That is the question of the next part.

2 From one bar to every load: Hooke's law in space

The tensile test gave us two numbers, Young's modulus (modul elastičnosti) \(E\) and Poisson's ratio (Poissonovo število) \(\nu\). But we pulled the bar in one direction only. A small piece inside a body is pulled, pressed and sheared at once, and its stress is described by six numbers, three normal and three shear components. Do we need a new test for each? No. One picture shows why not.

Let us draw the axis \(x\) to the right and the axis \(z\) up, and in their plane the front face of a small cube with the corners \(A\), \(B\), \(C\) and \(D\). Acting on it are only forces along the faces, \(\tau_0\) per unit area on each face. We call this pure shear (čisti strig). At first sight nothing is less like pulling a bar. Now we draw both diagonals. The claim of today's part is that this cube equals a piece pulled by a tension \(\tau_0\) along the diagonal \(AC\), together with a piece pressed by a compression \(\tau_0\) along the diagonal \(BD\).

In one sentence: if the effects of loads add and the material has no preferred direction, every state of stress is a sum of tensions and compressions, even shear, so \(E\) and \(\nu\) give all six strain components, and the shear modulus (strižni modul) is not a new measurement but

\[G = \frac{E}{2\,(1 + \nu)} .\]

This sentence holds five things we have not yet justified. May we add? Does one pull leave the cube unskewed? What does one pull give? Why is shear a pull plus a push? Where does \(1 + \nu\) come from? Let us take them in turn.

2.1 Why may we add?

The test knows one pull only. What if we pull the cube in three directions at once: may we simply add three tests?

Let us draw three axes, \(x\) toward us, \(y\) to the right, \(z\) up, and the cube as it is before loading. The first cube we pull only along the axis \(x\), with the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\). A second equal cube only along the axis \(y\), with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\), a third only along the axis \(z\), with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\). And if all three forces act on the same cube? The forces on each face add, so the stresses add too, which we write with plus and equals signs. We get the cube with all three forces. Linearity (linearnost) says that its change of shape is also the sum of the three separate changes.

Why should this hold? In one direction it is the straight line of the test itself: twice the force, twice the elongation. In several directions at once it is an assumption of the model, which tests confirm at small strains. Here we stand on the ground, on a measured fact. In the book: 3.2.1, assumption 2.

The assumption of linearity, briefly. Why it holds: the measured straight line and tests in several directions. When not: at large strains, for example a rubber band. What it does: without it we could not add the three cubes into the fourth.

2.2 Why does one pull not skew the cube?

Can a cube that we pull straight skew to one side? The test measured only the elongation and the thinning, not the right angles.

Let us draw the axes \(x\) and \(y\) and a cube, seen along the axis \(z\), and pull it along the axis \(x\). We watch a fibre along one diagonal and a fibre along the other. Suppose the cube did skew. We draw it orange, skewed, over its outline before loading, in the middle. Then the first fibre would stretch more than the second.

Now we turn the whole cube together with its forces by half a turn about the axis \(x\). What changes in the load? Nothing, the forces still pull along the axis \(x\). The material is the same in all directions, so this is the same test and must give the same result. But after the turn the fibre stretched more lies along the other diagonal, on the right. One test, two results. The skew would have to equal itself with the opposite sign, and the only such number is zero.

The ground of this answer: a symmetric cause in a material with no preferred direction cannot pick a side. In the book: 3.2.2, where the same argument is made with the rotation matrix, and a quarter turn also shows that the cube gets thinner in the direction \(y\) just as in the direction \(z\).

The assumption of isotropy (izotropnost). Why it holds for steel: a metal is made of small crystals and grains oriented at random, so on average no direction is special. When not: wood and fibre reinforced materials. There one pull can really skew the cube, there are more constants (3.4), and the shear modulus is a number of its own. What it does: it reduces the constants to two, \(E\) and \(\nu\). It will enter twice more, at the renaming of the axes and at the piece at \(45^\circ\), where it gives \(G = E/(2\,(1 + \nu))\).

2.3 What does one pull give?

So what does one single pull do? Now we know everything about the first cube. It gets longer as the test says, thinner equally in both transverse directions, and it does not skew:

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}}{E} , \qquad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = -\nu\,\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}}{E} , \qquad \text{no shear strains} .\]

And the second and the third cube? We choose the names of the axes ourselves, and the material is the same in all directions. We rename the axis \(y\) as \(x\) and get the same answer. Let us add what all three cubes give to the stretch along the axis \(x\): its own tension \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/E\), and from the other two a thinning, \(-\nu\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}/E\) and \(-\nu\,\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}/E\). For all three directions this is (3.19), for example

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} - \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}})}{E} .\]

Look at the edge along the axis \(z\) on the last cube. We pull it, and still it gets shorter. Why? The stresses are in the ratio \(3 : 2 : 1\). Its own tension lengthens it by \(1\) part, the tensions in the other two directions shorten it by \(0.3 \cdot (3 + 2) = 1.5\) parts.

And our steel bar from the test? Only \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) \(= 127.3\) MPa, no other stresses. The formula returns exactly what we measured:

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = 6.496 \cdot 10^{-4} , \qquad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = -0.3 \cdot 6.496 \cdot 10^{-4} = -1.949 \cdot 10^{-4} .\]

In the book: 3.2.4.

2.4 Why is shear a pull plus a push?

In shear nothing pulls and nothing pushes, the forces only slide along the faces. Where could a pull hide here? Let us cut the cube and see what the cut carries.

Let us take the square \(ABCD\), the corners as before, and cut it along the diagonal \(BD\). We keep the triangle \(ABD\). Which forces act on it? On the bottom side \(AB\) a force to the left, on the left side \(AD\) a force downward. A side is \(h\) long, so each force is \(\tau_0 h\) per unit depth.

We add the forces as arrows, the tail of one at the tip of the other. The sum is \(\sqrt2\,\tau_0 h\) long and points toward the corner \(A\), perpendicular to the cut. The triangle is at rest, so by Newton the cut acts on it with an equal force in the opposite direction, away from the triangle. So the cut pulls it, perpendicular to the cut, with no force along it. The cut is \(\sqrt2\,h\) long, so the stress on it is \(\sqrt2\,\tau_0 h / (\sqrt2\,h) = \tau_0\). Perpendicular to \(BD\) means along \(AC\): this is the tension \(\tau_0\) along \(AC\).

If we cut the square along \(AC\), the forces on \(AB\) and \(BC\) add to a force that pushes the triangle \(ABC\) toward the cut. That is the compression \(\tau_0\) along \(BD\). The ground: forces add as arrows, and a body at rest has zero sum of forces. In the book: 3.2.6, where the same follows from turning the axes by \(45^\circ\).

2.5 Where does \(1 + \nu\) come from?

Why does the shear modulus have \(1 + \nu\) in it and not just \(1\)? Let us count everything that lengthens the diagonal.

First, what happens to the square. Let us draw the axes, the square with its corners and diagonals. In shear the right angle at \(A\) does not stay right. The square becomes a rhombus, longer along \(AC\) and shorter along \(BD\), and the angle at \(A\) gets smaller by \(\gamma\). This is the shear strain we are looking for.

How much longer does \(AC\) get? Take a piece with axes along the diagonals, before loading, with edge \(a\). On it act the tension \(\tau_0\) along \(AC\) and the compression \(\tau_0\) along \(BD\). This is it after loading. The tension lengthens it along \(AC\), as in the test, by \(a\,\tau_0/E\). And the compression? The compression shortens it in its own direction and widens it in the transverse directions, by \(\nu\) times as much. One transverse direction is \(AC\), so the compression lengthens \(AC\) by a further \(\nu\,a\,\tau_0/E\). Together this is an elongation \((1 + \nu)\,a\,\tau_0/E\). In the same way \(BD\) gets shorter by \((1 + \nu)\,a\,\tau_0/E\): the compression shortens it, the tension thins it.

One diagonal gets longer by the fraction \((1 + \nu)\,\tau_0/E\), the other shorter by the same fraction. By how much does the right angle get smaller? By twice that fraction, which is the geometry of the rhombus at small strains (In the book: 3.2.6). So

\[\gamma = \frac{2\,(1 + \nu)\,\tau_0}{E} , \qquad G = \frac{\tau_0}{\gamma} = \frac{E}{2\,(1 + \nu)} .\]

For steel \(G = 196\ \text{GPa}/(2 \cdot 1.3) = 75.38\) GPa. This is (3.21) and (3.22).

2.6 Back up

Now we walk back along the same path. One pull is the test. Three pulls are a sum of three tests. Shear is a pull and a push at \(45^\circ\), so the same two numbers give it. Every state of stress is a sum of three pulls along the axes and three shears, and a shear stress gives no normal strain, for the same reason as above, the cube cannot pick a side. This is Hooke's law (Hookov zakon) for all six components, (3.27): three normal strains from (3.19) and each shear strain from its own shear stress, \(\gamma = \tau/G\).

And the picture from the top? The third constant \(G\) was never free. The diagonals of the rhombus fix it. In the book the law is tested on the hanging bar, 3.2.10.

2.7 Perspective 1: the geometry in two minutes

If you are in a hurry, remember the rhombus. Shear is a tension along one diagonal and a compression along the other. The diagonal gets longer by \(\tau_0/E\) from the tension, by a further \(\nu\,\tau_0/E\) from the compression in the transverse direction, and the angle gets smaller by twice this. What does this view add? The shear modulus of an isotropic material need not be measured separately.

2.8 Perspective 2: size and shape

Another view of the same law. Let us draw the axes, a cube and its whole strain. It equals a change of size, where each edge gets longer by the same fraction, plus a change of shape with no change of volume. What causes which part? Under the cubes we write the stress. The size is changed by the mean stress (srednja napetost), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{m}} = K\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}\), the shape by the deviator, \(\ten s = 2G\,\ten e\). This is (3.29), In the book: 3.2.11.

An example for size. Into still water we lower a steel cube with edge \(h = 10\) mm and look at it from the front, the axis \(x\) to the right, the axis \(z\) up. The water pushes on each face with the pressure \(p\). At a depth of 100 m, \(p = 0.981\) MPa. The bulk modulus (modul stisljivosti) of steel is \(K = E/(3\,(1 - 2\nu)) = 163.3\) GPa, and the cube shrinks by

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} = -\frac{p}{K} = -6.006 \cdot 10^{-6} .\]

Each edge becomes \((1 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}})\,h\), with a third of this fraction, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = -2.002 \cdot 10^{-6}\). What does this view add? Two independent resistances: \(K\) against squeezing and \(G\) against shearing. Again two numbers, only a different two.

2.9 Perspective 3: by numbers along \(\nu\)

On the horizontal axis we put \(\nu\) from \(-1\) to \(0.5\), vertically \(K/E\) and \(G/E\). We draw the curve for \(K\) and the curve for \(G\). Steel at \(\nu = 0.3\) has \(K/E = 0.833\) and \(G/E = 0.385\). What happens as \(\nu\) goes to \(0.5\)? \(K\) grows without bound, and toward \(-1\) \(G\) grows without bound. Rubber with \(\nu = 0.49\) has \(K/E = 16.7\), far above the picture: it changes shape easily and volume hardly at all. What does this view add? What the number \(\nu\) means for the feel of a material.

2.10 Perspective 4: history

Navier and Poisson built the theory on molecules and got a single constant for a substance that is the same in all directions. Green, Stokes and Lamé arrived at two, and two is exactly what we got from the tensile bar and from shear. What does this view add? That "two numbers" is a hard won finding, not a matter of course.

2.11 Assumptions

Which assumptions did we use without naming them? Linearity and isotropy we judged where they entered. Of the five, three remain.

  1. Elasticity. The strain at a point depends only on the stress there now. Why: that is how the bar in the test behaves. When not: above the yield stress, for steel \(\sigma_{\mathrm{Y}} = 220\) MPa. What it does: without it there is no rule, the history of loading would count.
  2. Small strains and rotations. All derivatives of the displacements below about \(0.001\). Why: steel yields already at a strain of \(0.00112\). When not: a rubber band with a strain of \(0.5\). What it does: the stress on the old or the new area differs by less than \(0.1\) %, and the angle of the rhombus gets smaller by twice the fraction only to first order.
  3. Constant temperature. When not: a heated material expands even without stress, steel by 15 millionths of its length per kelvin. What it does: otherwise we add the thermal expansion to each normal strain, shear stays as it is (3.3).

Homogeneity is not needed: the rule holds at each point with that point's numbers \(E\) and \(\nu\).

3 Where the work goes: strain energy and the bounds on the constants

Let us go back to the steel bar of the tension test. It will tell us something no gauge shows: how much energy is inside it, and why the two elastic constants cannot be just any numbers.

We clamp the left end of the bar in the grip, which does not move. We draw the bar in blackwhite, as it is before pulling, with the length \(L = 10\) m and a diameter of 10 mm. Now the machine slowly pulls the right end with the force \(F\), which grows from zero to 10 kN. The bar gets longer, its right end moves by \(e\). At the end \(e = \Delta L = 6.496\) mm.

The machine pulled and the end moved, so the machine did work. A question for you: where did this work go? Is it in the bar now? And if it is, do we get it back? We read the answer off a single graph.

We draw the axes: the elongation \(e\) across, the force \(F\) up. Below the yield stress the force is proportional to the elongation, so the relation is a line from the origin \(O\). Its steepness is the stiffness \(k\), \(k = EA/L = 1.539 \cdot 10^6\) N/m, so many newtons for each metre of elongation. The line ends at the final force \(F_1 = 10\) kN and the final elongation \(\Delta L = 6.496\) mm.

The claim of this segment in one sentence: the work is the area of the triangle under the line, 32.48 J, the bar stores all of it and gives all of it back, per cubic metre it is \(\tfrac12\,\sigma\,\varepsilon\), and the demand that this energy be positive for every deformation fences the constants in to \(E > 0\) and \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\). Each part of that sentence raises a new "why?". Let us take them in turn.

3.1 Why exactly one half?

Is the work simply force times distance, 10 kN times \(6.496\) mm, so \(64.96\) J? That is what we learned at school, but it holds for a constant force. Ours was not 10 kN all the way. At the start it was zero.

The force grew evenly, along the line. Its mean is therefore halfway, at \(F_1/2\). The rectangle under this line has the area \(\tfrac12\,F_1\,\Delta L\). Is it equal to the triangle? Look at the top right: this corner of the triangle sticks out above the mean. On the left this gap is missing below it. Both are right triangles with legs \(\Delta L/2\) and \(F_1/2\), so they are equal. The corner fills the gap, and the triangle has the same area as the rectangle:

\[W = \tfrac12\,F_1\,\Delta L = \tfrac12 \cdot 10\,000\ \text{N} \cdot 6.496 \cdot 10^{-3}\ \text{m} = 32.48\ \text{J} .\]

We can see the same thing another way, without the mean. We cut the path into short pieces.

We draw the same axes and the same line twice. On each short piece the force is nearly constant, so the work on it is a thin rectangle, force times a short distance. The blue rectangles take the force at the start of the piece and slightly underestimate the work. The grey rectangles are what is missing up to the force at the end of the piece. On the left there are four pieces, on the right sixteen. When the pieces are four times shorter, there is four times less grey area. The rectangles squeeze into the triangle under the line. That is the idea of the integral. In the book: 3.5.1 and (3.57).

Assumption: the force is a line. Why it holds: the stress in the bar is 127.3 MPa, below the yield stress of steel, 220 MPa, and there the test measured a line. When not: above the yield stress and for rubber the relation is curved. What it does: the area under the curve is still the work, only the shape is no longer a triangle and the factor \(\tfrac12\) goes.

3.2 Where does the work go, and does it come back?

Where are these 32.48 J now, and do we get them back? Let us try: we unload the bar slowly. What does the test show? Below the yield stress the points come back down the same line. This is a measured fact, the second observation of the tension test from segment 01. The end of the bar moves back towards the grip, and the bar still pulls the machine's grip towards itself. The bar's force and the displacement point the same way, so now the bar does work on the machine. At every elongation the force is the same as before, so this work is the same triangle, 32.48 J.

The bar stored the work and gave it all back, like a spring. Such energy is called strain energy (deformacijska energija) \(U\), because the body has it for being deformed. For our bar \(U = 32.48\) J. The spring is not only a figure of speech: the bar is a spring with the stiffness \(k\), and the school energy of a spring, \(\tfrac12\,k\,\Delta L^2\), is the same triangle, since \(k\,\Delta L = F_1\).

Assumption: we pull slowly. When the end moves, the material of the bar moves too, and part of the work could go into motion. How much?

The bar in the grip stretches uniformly, so a point at the distance \(x\) moves with the velocity \(v\,x/L\), from zero at the grip to \(v\) at the end at \(x = L\), where the machine pulls with the force \(F\). Why it holds, with a number: if we stretch the bar in one second, \(v = 6.496\) mm/s, and the kinetic energy of the bar is

\[\frac{m\,v^2}{6} = 4.3 \cdot 10^{-5}\ \text{J} \approx 1.3 \cdot 10^{-6} \cdot 32.48\ \text{J} ,\]

about one millionth of the work. When not: when the load changes noticeably within the time a disturbance needs to cross the bar ten times, here \(10\,L/c = 0.032\) s, for example in an impact. What it does: we drop the kinetic energy, and all the work goes into the material, with an error of the order of one millionth. We also drop the weight of the bar, only 0.6 % of the final force. In the book: 3.6.1.

And if we pulled the bar past the yield stress? There the claim fails.

On the left, for comparison, is our bar once more: the axes, the line from the origin up to the force \(F_1\) at the elongation \(\Delta L\), the triangle of work and the way back along the same line. On the right we draw the axes of strain \(\varepsilon\) and stress \(\sigma\). From the origin \(O\) we go along the loading curve past the yield stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{Y}}\) to the point \(B\). Now we unload. The material does not come back along the curve but along a line parallel to the initial line, and at zero stress it stays permanently stretched by \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{p}}\). It gives back only the blue area under the unloading line. The grey area between the curve and the line is not returned. By the first law of thermodynamics it goes into heat.

Assumption: the material is elastic. Why it holds: our bar is at 127.3 MPa, which is 58 % of the yield stress. When not: above 220 MPa. What it does: without it part of the work is lost, and the energy no longer depends only on the present strain but on what the material went through.

3.3 Why per unit volume?

Is 32.48 J a property of steel or only of this bar? Only of this bar. A bar twice as long, of the same steel at the same stress, would store twice as much. What then belongs to the steel? On the graph we divide the force by the area \(A\) and the elongation by the length \(L\). The line becomes the stress-strain line of segment 01, and the triangle stays a triangle, divided by the volume \(AL\):

\[w = \frac{U}{A L} = \frac12 \cdot \frac{F_1}{A} \cdot \frac{\Delta L}{L} = \tfrac12\,\sigma\,\varepsilon = \frac{\sigma^2}{2E} = 41\,356\ \text{J/m}^3 .\]

The bar is gone from the formula, only stress and strain are left. It is the same step as in segment 01, when we went from force and elongation to stress and strain. The units agree: \(1\ \text{Pa} = 1\ \text{N/m}^2 = 1\ \text{J/m}^3\).

Assumption: every part of the bar is stretched equally. Why it holds: the points are evenly spaced and the axial force is the same in every cross-section, since we drop the weight. When not: in the hanging bar, where the stress grows towards the top. What it does: only then is \(w\) simply \(U/V\). Otherwise every piece has its own \(w\), and \(U\) is the sum over the pieces.

3.4 And a piece inside a body, loaded in every direction?

What about a piece inside a body, with all six stress components acting on it at once? In the bar only one was non-zero. How do we add up the work then?

Inside the body we imagine at the point \(P\) a small box with the edge \(\Delta x\), in the axes \(x\), \(y\), \(z\). The surrounding material pulls the patch at the point \(Q\) on the right face with a force, and the patch at \(Q'\) on the left with the opposite force. In a short step \(Q\) moves by \(\Delta\vek u(Q)\) and \(Q'\) by \(\Delta\vek u(Q')\). If both points moved equally, the box would only shift, and the works of the opposite forces would cancel. The work of the pair therefore depends only on the difference of the displacements, and that difference divided by \(\Delta x\) is the change of strain. Per unit volume each stress works on its own strain: the three normal ones on stretching, the three shear ones on changes of right angles. In the book: 3.6.3.

For a linear isotropic material the six terms sort themselves into two squares:

\[w = \tfrac12\,K\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}^2 + G \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} e_{\mathrm{ij}}^2 .\]

The first part is for a change of volume, the second for a change of shape. Let us draw what this means.

A small cube in the \(xy\) plane can stretch equally in every direction and stay a cube. This changes only the volume, and we pay for it with the first part, with the bulk modulus \(K\). Or its right angles can change while the volume stays. That is a change of shape, and we pay for it with the second part, with the shear modulus \(G\). There is no term with both at once: two springs, each stretched on its own. The piece from the tension test gets longer and thinner, and that is the sum of the two. For steel at \(\varepsilon = 0.001\) only 13.3 % of the energy goes into volume, 86.7 % into shape. Why so little into volume? The thinning in two transverse directions eats \(2\nu = 60\) % of the stretch, so the volume changes only by \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} = 0.4\,\varepsilon\). In the book: 3.6.6 and (3.72).

Assumption: the material is isotropic. Why it holds: metals are made of small crystals turned every which way, as in segment 02. When not: wood, fibre composites. What it does: only for an isotropic material does the energy split into two independent parts with \(K\) and \(G\). For an anisotropic one there are up to 21 constants, and the condition applies to the whole stiffness matrix.

3.5 Why must the energy be positive?

What if some material gave off work for some deformation, \(w < 0\)? For the bar we had to pull to stretch it and give work in doing so. Let us draw how such a material would behave.

In the axes \(x\) and \(y\) we take a cube and press on it from all sides with the pressure \(p\). An ordinary material shrinks. This one inflates. The pressure forces point inwards while the faces move outwards, so the work the cube receives is negative: the cube gives energy off. In the same way a cube loaded by the shear stresses \(\tau\) tilts the other way, against the force. A material that gives off energy while deforming would deform like that by itself, with no force. A material with \(w = 0\) would deform with no resistance at all. The ground we stand on: things around us do not crumple or inflate by themselves.

Assumption: the material is stable, \(w > 0\) for every small deformation that is not zero. Why it holds: we see it in every material we use, and the sources state it for elastic materials. When not: it is not a theorem but a demand we place on the material. What it does: it rules out every value of the constants for which a cube like this one or this one would exist.

3.6 What does this say about \(E\) and \(\nu\)?

Which numbers \(E\) and \(\nu\) does the demand \(w > 0\) still allow at all? The idea of the proof: we test the demand \(w > 0\) on two deformations, each testing one constant. First: we lengthen every edge of a cube by the same fraction \(c\), this fraction here. Only the volume changes, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} = 3c\), there is no deviatoric part, and \(w = \tfrac92\,K\,c^2\). Since \(c^2 > 0\), the demand \(w > 0\) means \(K > 0\). Second: we change only the shape of a cube, the right angle shrinks by \(2c\). The volume stays, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} = 0\), and \(w = 2\,G\,c^2\). So \(G > 0\).

Now we write both moduli with \(E\) and \(\nu\):

\[K = \frac{E}{3\,(1 - 2\nu)} > 0 , \qquad G = \frac{E}{2\,(1 + \nu)} > 0 .\]

From both together \(3/G + 1/K = 9/E > 0\), so \(E > 0\). Then the denominators must be positive too, \(1 + \nu > 0\) and \(1 - 2\nu > 0\), so

\[-1 < \nu < \tfrac12 .\]

The converse holds as well: if \(K\) and \(G\) are positive, \(w\) is a sum of two non-negative squares that are both zero only for zero deformation. In the book: 3.64, all four directions of the proof.

What happens just outside? At \(\nu = 0.55\) we have \(1 - 2\nu = -0.1\) and \(K = 196\ \text{GPa}/(3 \cdot (-0.1)) = -653\) GPa. Pressure then gives a positive volumetric strain \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} = -p/K\), and the cube inflates. At \(\nu = -1.1\) we have \(1 + \nu = -0.1\) and \(G = 196\ \text{GPa}/(2 \cdot (-0.1)) = -980\) GPa. A shear stress \(\tau > 0\) gives a shear strain \(\gamma = \tau/G < 0\), and the cube tilts against the force.

On the \(\nu\) axis we mark both bounds, \(-1\) and \(\tfrac12\). Between them is the band of allowed values. As \(\nu\) approaches \(\tfrac12\), \(K/E\) grows without bound: the material resists a change of volume ever more strongly, it is nearly incompressible, like rubber. As \(\nu\) approaches \(-1\), \(G/E\) grows without bound. Outside the band one of the ratios is negative, and those are the cubes of the previous figure. Steel with \(\nu = 0.3\) has \(K/E = 0.833\) and \(G/E = 0.385\), both positive, and sits well inside the band. A negative \(\nu\) is allowed, above \(-1\): such a material widens sideways when stretched, and such materials exist. Most real materials, though, have \(0 < \nu < \tfrac12\).

3.7 Back up

Work is force times distance, and for a force growing along a line it is a triangle. Since the material comes back along the same line, the triangle is stored energy. Dividing by the volume gives \(\tfrac12\,\sigma\,\varepsilon\), a property of the material. In space each stress works on its own strain, and for an isotropic material everything sorts into the volume part and the shape part. The material must not give off energy, so both parts must be positive, \(K > 0\) and \(G > 0\), and that is the band \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\) with \(E > 0\). This keeps the promise of segment 01 that every stable material has a Poisson's ratio between \(-1\) and \(\tfrac12\).

3.8 The area view: the explanation in a few minutes

If you keep one picture, let it be the blue triangle. Energy is the area under the line, visible on the graph of the tension test. When the line comes back, we get the area back. When the way back is different, the grey area stays lost. And an area that came out negative would be a material that deforms by itself.

3.9 The path view: why the energy is a function of the state

Does it matter along which path we reach the final deformation?

Across we plot the stretch \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), up the shear \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}}\). From zero deformation \(O\) we go to the deformation \(C\) with \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = a\) and \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} = b\), both 0.001. The first path stretches first, up to the point \(A\), then shears. The second path shears first, up to the point \(B\), then stretches. For an invented material in which shear causes a normal stress but stretching causes no shear stress, the second path costs 10 000 J/m³ more. Now we load along the first path and unload along the second, back to \(O\). The material gives back 10 000 J/m³ more than it received, and in every round trip: a source of energy from nothing. An ideally elastic material does not allow this, so its energy depends only on the final deformation, and the stiffness matrix is symmetric.

The right picture shows why the stresses are then derivatives of the energy. From zero deformation we go along any path to a deformation that has one component, \(\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{p}}\), smaller by \(r_0\) than the deformation \(\{\varepsilon\}\) we are interested in. From there we go straight through it and by \(r\) further, to this deformation. On the straight part one single component changes, so one single stress component does work there, and it is the slope at which the energy grows along that component. In the book: 3.6.4 and (3.69).
