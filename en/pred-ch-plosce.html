Chapter 18 Plates

Chapter 18Lecture: Plates

1 Why a plate is not a wide beam

Take a steel plate 10 mm thick. We cut a strip 10 mm wide out of it. We bend the strip and the plate equally hard. A question for you: does the strip inside the plate carry the same stress as the strip cut free? No. The strip inside the plate carries 9.9 % more stress, and we will draw both stresses in figure L18.4. Why? The answer is in one picture.

First we draw the strip cut free in cross-section, as we see it looking along its length. Its width \(b\) and thickness \(h\) are both 10 mm. We bent the strip downwards, as a loaded floor bends. But the section also curls sideways, into an arch turned against the bending. Now we draw the same strip in the middle of a plate. To its left and right are its neighbours (soseda), two equal strips. They hold it with a couple, so its cross curvature stays zero.

This is the claim of the segment: a stretched layer wants to shrink sideways, the neighbours do not allow it, and we pay for that ban with an extra stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \nu\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\). That is why the stiffness of a plate is \(D = E h^3/(12(1-\nu^2))\) and not \(E h^3/12\) as for a beam of unit width. The claim uses four things: a layer stretches, a stretched layer wants to shrink, in the plate it cannot, and the ban costs something. We ask "why?" of each.

1.1 Why is a layer stretched at all?

A question to start with: we only bend the plate, nobody pulls it along its length. Where does a stretch come from then? Let us draw and see.

We put the \(x\) axis to the right and \(z\) up. From the plate we cut a piece between two normals (normali), straight lines through the thickness. Before bending the piece is a rectangle, the mid-plane dashed in the middle. Now we bend the piece, this time bulging upwards as in the book's example: the top face gets longer, the bottom one shorter. For the strip of the opening figure it is the other way round, but the computation is the same. The normals stay straight and perpendicular to the curved mid-plane, so they lean towards each other at the bottom. We extend them downwards and they meet at the centre of curvature, the radius is \(R = 50\) m. What happens to the mid-plane? It keeps its length, its stretch is zero. How high above it is the top face? The height is \(z = 5\) mm. At this height the top fibre stretches, by \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = z/R = 0.005/50 = 1.00\cdot 10^{-4}\). Why exactly \(z/R\)? Each normal turns by the slope \(w_{\mathrm{x}}\), so its end at height \(z\) moves sideways by \(z\,w_{\mathrm{x}}\). Neighbouring normals have different slopes, so the fibre between them gets longer, and that is \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = -z\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}\). For \(w = -x^2/(2R)\) we have \(w_{\mathrm{xx}} = -1/R = -0.02\) 1/m, that is the same radius, and \(-z\,w_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0.005 \cdot 0.02 = 1.00\cdot 10^{-4}\). In the book: 18.1.2, theorem 18.3 and equation (18.3).

Two assumptions entered here. Let us show where.

Normals stay straight and perpendicular. These are the two thick sides of the piece. Why may we? The plate is thin, \(h/a = 10/500 = 0.02\), and the model asks for \(h/a \le 0.05\). When does it fail? In a moderately thick plate, \(0.05 \le h/a < 0.10\), and a very thick one, \(h/a \ge 0.10\), the normal warps. What does it cost? The shear strains \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}\) are set to zero, so the deflection comes out too small. By how much? Let us draw it.

On the horizontal axis we put the ratio of thickness and side, on the vertical one the excess of deflection that the theory with a turned normal gives. For the simply supported square the excess is the parabola \(5.18\,(h/a)^2\). Our plate at \(0.02\) is off by \(0.2\) %. At the limit of a thin plate, \(0.05\), the error is \(1.3\) %, at the limit of a very thick one, \(0.10\), already \(5.2\) %. The cost also depends on the supports: the clamped circle has a steeper parabola, \(18.3\,(h/l)^2\) with the diameter \(l\), at \(0.10\) already \(18.3\) %. In the book: 18.8.5.

The mid-plane does not stretch. That is the zero label in the figure of the piece. Why may we? At a midspan deflection \(w_0 = h/5\) the stretch of the mid-plane is \(3.95\cdot 10^{-5}\) and the bending strain of the face \(3.95\cdot 10^{-4}\), so ten percent. When does it fail? At \(w_0 = h\) the ratio is already \(0.50\) and the model is wrong. There the theory of von Kármán applies. In the book: the table in 18.1.2.

1.2 Why does a stretched layer want to shrink sideways?

A question for you: what happens to the width of a rubber band when we pull it? It gets thinner. That is a measured fact, Poisson's ratio (Poissonovo razmerje): for steel the width shrinks by \(0.3\) of the stretch. Back to the strip cut free. The strip is bent downwards, so its bottom fibres are stretched and get narrower. The top ones are squeezed and get wider. What becomes of the section? The section curls the other way, with \(w_{\mathrm{yy}} = -\nu\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}\), in size \(0.3 \cdot 0.02 = 0.006\) 1/m. We call this anticlastic curvature (antiklastična ukrivljenost), from the Greek anti, opposite, and klasis, breaking. The edge of the strip moves relative to its middle by only \(0.075\ \mu\mathrm{m}\), so the arch is strongly exaggerated in the figure. In the book: 18.1.4 and definition 3.8.

1.3 Why can it not in the plate?

So the strip cut free curls sideways. A question: what does the strip in the middle of the plate have that the free one lacks? Neighbours. Look at the strip inside the plate. Next to it are its neighbours. Do they differ from it? No. The load and the supports do not depend on \(y\), so every strip bends exactly like its neighbour. Suppose the top fibres of our strip widened sideways. Then the fibres of the neighbour would have to widen too, since it is the same. The two strips would push into each other. Since they are equal, neither moves sideways relative to the other. Distances in the \(y\) direction do not change. What holds this? The contact between neighbours. By Newton's third law a strip pushes on its neighbour with the same force as the neighbour on it. Along the top face they press, along the bottom face they pull. Together this is the couple \(M_{\mathrm{y}}\), and that is why the cross curvature is zero. In the book: 18.4.

The neighbours hold the sides, \(\kappa_{\mathrm{y}} = 0\). That is the straight right section. Why may we? The plate is long and the load does not change along \(y\), so all strips are equal. When does it fail? For a narrow beam, about as wide as it is thick. There \(EI\) applies. What does it cost? For a narrow beam the plate formula would overestimate the stress by \(9.9\) %. Where the boundary between the two cases lies, the book does not compute.

1.4 What does it cost?

So the neighbours hold the width. A question: is that free? How much stress does it take to keep a piece of a layer from narrowing?

We put the \(x\) axis to the right and \(y\) up. From the top layer we take a small square. First we leave it free and stretch it along \(x\). Both squares get the same stretch, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = 1.00\cdot 10^{-4}\). What do the sides of the free square do? They move in, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = -\nu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = -0.30\cdot 10^{-4}\). This stretch needs \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = 19.600\) MPa. Now we draw the same square in the plate, with neighbours above and below. We stretch it equally, but the width stays. What must the neighbours do? They must pull the sides outwards, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \nu\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 6.462\) MPa. By Poisson this sideways pull shortens the square along \(x\). So the same stretch needs a larger force, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 21.538\) MPa.

We write two lines on the board. Hooke's law along \(x\) with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \nu\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\):

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} - \nu\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}}{E} = \frac{(1 - \nu^2)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}}{E} ,\]
\[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \frac{E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}}{1 - \nu^2} .\]

Same stretch, more stress. The ratio of the two stresses is \(0.91 = 1 - \nu^2\). In the book: theorem 18.7, steps 1 and 2, equation (18.7).

The layer is in plane stress, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\). Both lines stand on this assumption. Why may we? The faces of the plate carry at most the load \(q = 10\) kPa, the layers \(21.5\) MPa, two thousand times more. When does it fail? When the load on the face would be comparable with the stresses in the layers. What does it cost? The model has \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\), while Hooke's law gives \(-4.29\cdot 10^{-5}\) at the face. That changes the thickness by only \(0.107\ \mu\mathrm{m}\). In the book: 18.1.5.

1.5 How does stress become a moment?

We know the stress in each layer separately. A question: what do all these layers together do to the edge of a piece, push it or turn it?

In panel (a) we draw the axes \(x\) and \(z\) and a piece of plate with its mid-plane. On a face of the piece acts the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\). On one side of the mid-plane it pushes, on the other it pulls, and it grows with the distance. What is the sum of the forces? Zero, since push and pull cancel. A couple remains, the moment \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\), measured per metre of edge. The integral of \(z^2\) over the thickness gives \(h^3/12\), so for \(\kappa_{\mathrm{y}} = 0\) the moment is \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = D\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}\). In the book: definitions 18.5 and 18.6.

We have already drawn the numbers under the squares in figure L18.4. The strip cut free has the stiffness \(EI = 16\,333.3\) N m per metre of width, the strip in the plate \(D = 17\,948.7\) N m. At \(R = 50\) m the beam moment is \(-326.67\) N m/m, the plate moment \(-358.97\) N m/m. The sign is negative because in this example the top face is in tension.

1.6 Back up

Now we put it all together. Bending stretches the top fibre by \(-z\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}\). The stretched fibre wants to shrink sideways. The neighbours do not allow it. The ban is the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\), which raises \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) by the factor \(1/(1-\nu^2) = 1.0989\). The sum over the thickness gives \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = D\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) when \(\kappa_{\mathrm{y}} = 0\). In general \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = D\,(\kappa_{\mathrm{x}} + \nu\,\kappa_{\mathrm{y}})\). The term \(\nu\,\kappa_{\mathrm{y}}\) is the same story told from the other direction, in the section \(x = \text{const}\). In the opening figure: on the left \(EI\) applies, on the right \(D\) applies.

1.7 By hand: an eraser

Take a rubber eraser and bend it. The top face turns saddle shaped, like the left section. A wide sheet of the same rubber does not, like the right strip. What does this add? The effect is visible to the eye, it is not just a result of the algebra.

1.8 By numbers: how wrong we would be

If we compute a narrow beam with the plate formula, we overestimate the stress by \(1/0.91 - 1 = 9.9\) %. If we compute a plate as a beam, we underestimate the stress by \(9\) %. What does this add? The size of the effect: not a small correction, almost ten percent.

1.9 The third curvature: twist

A card first lies flat. We hold it by its four corners, pull two up and two down, with forces at the corners. We get a twisted surface \(w = c\,x\,y\). Every grid line on it stays straight, so \(w_{\mathrm{xx}} = w_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0\). Yet the plate resists. With what? Look at the face \(x = \text{const}\) from the front. The shear stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) points one way at the top and the opposite way at the bottom, and its couple is the twisting moment \(M_{\mathrm{xy}}\), at \(c = 0.01\) 1/m equal to \(D\,(1-\nu)\,c = 125.64\) N m/m. What does this add? A third curvature, which a beam does not have. The next two segments stand on it.

1.10 Algebraic: where \(1 - \nu^2\) comes from

Hooke's law of a layer has two equations, for \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and for \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\). To the first we add \(\nu\) times the second:

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \nu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \frac{(1 - \nu^2)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}}{E} .\]

For the held square \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0\), and we get exactly the second stress. What does this add? We see that the factor \(1 - \nu^2\) comes from \(\nu\) acting twice: first when the neighbours need \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \nu\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), and second when this \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) by Poisson shortens the fibre along \(x\) by \(\nu\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}/E\). In the book: theorem 18.7, step 2.

2 Who carries the load

For a beam it is simple: the load travels along the beam to its two ends. A plate, though, rests on four edges. Along which paths does a load in the middle reach them? And why does the plate equation have exactly three terms, \(w_{\mathrm{xxxx}} + 2\,w_{\mathrm{xxyy}} + w_{\mathrm{yyyy}}\)? One picture answers this.

We draw a square plate in plan view, seen from above. The hatching along all four sides means that the plate rests there on a support that allows no vertical displacement. Next to the plate we put the axes \(x\) and \(y\). The \(z\) axis points out of the board towards us.

Now we load the plate only on a small patch in the middle, with the load \(q\). The load (obtežba) is a force per unit area, in N/m². We count it positive in the \(z\) direction, upwards. Weight therefore brings \(q < 0\). We agree on this once and it holds for the whole segment.

Where does this force go? The first path is the strip along \(x\), a narrow band of the plate through the patch. It bends like a beam and carries the force to the left and right edges. The second path is the strip along \(y\), which carries it to the bottom and top edges. The third path is new. The neighbouring strip sags less than the strip through the middle, so the plate twists between them. The twist (zvoj) carries the moment \(M_{\mathrm{xy}}\), and it too takes part of the force away. Under the plate we write the three terms of the equation, each in the colour of its path.

The claim of the segment is therefore: the load is carried along three paths, bending along \(x\), bending along \(y\), and twist between neighbouring strips, and the plate equation is this sharing written term by term:

\[q = M_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{xx}} + 2\,M_{\mathrm{xy},\mathrm{xy}} + M_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{yy}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad D\,\nabla^4 w = q .\]

Why so? Let us go down question by question.

2.1 Why can moments alone not carry the load?

From the previous segment we have the moments. A question for you: can moments alone hold a load that presses vertically? We cut a small piece out of the plate and look at what acts on it.

First the axes: \(x\) and \(y\) in the mid-plane, \(z\) upwards. Then the piece: a rectangle of the mid-plane and all the material above and below it. We draw the thickness much too large, so that we see what happens through it. On the top face acts the load \(q\), vertically.

On the side faces the piece is cut off from the rest of the plate, and there the rest of the plate holds it. What did the previous segment give us? Moments. On the right face there is the moment \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\): at the top the stress pushes the face, at the bottom it pulls it. On the front face there is \(M_{\mathrm{y}}\), and on both faces also the couple \(M_{\mathrm{xy}}\) from the shear stresses in the plane of the plate.

Can these couples carry the load? Look at the forces in a couple. Both are horizontal. Horizontal forces have no vertical component, so their sum in the \(z\) direction is zero. The load, though, is vertical. By Newton the piece stays at rest only if the sum of all forces on it is zero. So a vertical force must also pass through the side faces. It is the transverse shear force \(Q_{\mathrm{x}}\) on the right and \(Q_{\mathrm{y}}\) on the front face. We call them transverse (prečni), because they act across the mid-plane. They are forces per unit length of edge, in N/m. They come from the shear stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}\), summed over the thickness. In the book: 18.2.1, 18.9.

Here the first assumption enters. The Kirchhoff normal stays perpendicular to the mid-plane, so the shear strain \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}\) is zero. Hooke's law would turn that into a zero stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\) and so \(Q_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\). Why do we not listen to it here? We accepted the same contradiction for the Euler-Bernoulli beam: the shear force is fixed by equilibrium, not by the strain, and without it the piece is not in equilibrium. When does this fail? For thick plates, where the normal really turns. What does it cost us? We neglect the deflection due to shear. How much, shows the blue parabola of the clamped circle of the previous segment: for a diameter of \(50\) thicknesses the deflection with shear is only \(0.73\) % larger, for a diameter of \(10\) thicknesses already \(18.3\) %. In the book: 18.1.5, 18.8.5.

2.2 Why is the shear force the derivative of the moment?

So there must be a shear force. A question: what fixes its size? Look at turning. The piece does not turn. So the sum of the moments on it is zero, again by Newton. We stay with the picture of the piece. The moment \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\) on the right face is not the same as the one on the left, because the moment changes across the plate. Someone has to pay the difference. It is paid by the shear force \(Q_{\mathrm{x}}\), whose lever arm about the left face is as long as the piece. About the same axis, though, the couple \(M_{\mathrm{xy}}\) on the front and back faces turns too. We shrink the piece to a point and get:

\[Q_{\mathrm{x}} = -\big(M_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{x}} + M_{\mathrm{xy},\mathrm{y}}\big) , \qquad Q_{\mathrm{y}} = -\big(M_{\mathrm{xy},\mathrm{x}} + M_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{y}}\big) .\]

The second term in each line is what a beam does not have: the twist on the neighbouring faces. The idea of the proof is the equilibrium of a finite piece, each face integrated over the thickness, then shrinking. In the book: 18.2.2, 18.10.

Are the moments really not enough? We test it with numbers.

We draw the axes in metres and on them the piece \(R\), from \(0.1\) to \(0.3\) m along \(x\) and from \(0.2\) to \(0.4\) m along \(y\). On the plate we put the deflection \(w = q(x^4 + y^4)/(48D)\), which solves no supported plate but has simple moments. On the right we draw the zero from which we measure the moments about the \(x\) axis (left) and about the \(y\) axis (right).

First only the moment couples on the edge of the piece: \(+60\) N m and \(+40\) N m. Then the moment of the load about the same axes: \(+120\) N m and \(+80\) N m. Both turn the same way. With nothing else the sum would be \(180\) N m and \(120\) N m, the piece would turn. Equilibrium comes only with the shear forces, \(-180\) N m and \(-120\) N m. Now it adds up: \(60 + 120 - 180 = 0\) and \(40 + 80 - 120 = 0\). In the book: 18.2.2, the test of the lemma.

2.3 Why a sum of three terms?

A question for you: we got the two shear forces from three moments. How do we get from them back to the load \(q\)? We add up the vertical forces. What the shear forces bring in through the four sides must be equal and opposite to the load on the piece. On the same piece this is through the sides \(-300\), \(+100\), \(-400\) and \(+200\) N, in total \(-400\) N, and the load \(+400\) N. The sum is zero. At a point this account reads

\[Q_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{x}} + Q_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{y}} + q = 0 .\]

This is (18.18). We put both shear forces from above into it:

\[q = M_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{xx}} + M_{\mathrm{xy},\mathrm{yx}} + M_{\mathrm{xy},\mathrm{xy}} + M_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{yy}} = M_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{xx}} + 2\,M_{\mathrm{xy},\mathrm{xy}} + M_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{yy}} .\]

Where does the two come from? The twist \(M_{\mathrm{xy}}\) appears in both shear forces, once in \(Q_{\mathrm{x}}\) and once in \(Q_{\mathrm{y}}\), because it acts on both pairs of faces. In the plan view this is the strip along \(x\), the strip along \(y\) and the twist between neighbours, which both of them feel. The idea of the proof: we turn the integral over the edge into an integral over the piece and use that a function whose integral over every rectangle is zero is itself zero. In the book: 18.2.3, 18.12, steps 1 to 3.

Here the second assumption enters: the deflection \(w\) has four continuous derivatives and the load \(q\) is continuous. Why does it hold here? Under a load spread over an area, such as weight or snow, the moments are smooth. When not? Under a point force, where the moment next to the point grows without bound (18.60). What does it cost us? There the equation does not hold at the point of the force, the equilibrium of a finite piece still does.

2.4 Why does \(\nu\) drop out?

A question: every moment contains \(\nu\), the plate equation does not. Where does it go? Every moment is the stiffness \(D\) times a curvature, that is the previous segment: \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = D(w_{\mathrm{xx}} + \nu w_{\mathrm{yy}})\), \(M_{\mathrm{y}} = D(w_{\mathrm{yy}} + \nu w_{\mathrm{xx}})\), \(M_{\mathrm{xy}} = D(1-\nu)\,w_{\mathrm{xy}}\). We differentiate and write each path separately, as the three terms under the plan view:

\[\begin{aligned} M_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{xx}} &= D\,\big(w_{\mathrm{xxxx}} + \nu\,w_{\mathrm{xxyy}}\big) \\ 2\,M_{\mathrm{xy},\mathrm{xy}} &= D\,\big(2\,w_{\mathrm{xxyy}} - 2\nu\,w_{\mathrm{xxyy}}\big) \\ M_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{yy}} &= D\,\big(w_{\mathrm{yyyy}} + \nu\,w_{\mathrm{xxyy}}\big) \end{aligned}\]

We add the three lines:

\[q = D\,\big(w_{\mathrm{xxxx}} + 2\,w_{\mathrm{xxyy}} + w_{\mathrm{yyyy}}\big) = D\,\nabla^4 w .\]

The parts with \(\nu\) are \(+\nu\), \(+\nu\) and \(-2\nu\), their sum is zero. What bending gains because the neighbours hold the sides of a strip, the twist loses. This is (18.17). In the book: 18.12, steps 4 and 5.

Here the third assumption enters: \(D\) is the same everywhere, the plate is homogeneous and of constant thickness. Why here? Only then can we take \(D\) out in front of the derivatives. When not? For a plate of varying thickness. What does it cost us? There terms with derivatives of \(D\) would remain, and the equation would be longer.

2.5 Back up

Now we go the way back, from the ground to the top. The load on the patch must leave through its sides as a shear force, because horizontal couples cannot balance a vertical force. A shear force exists only where a moment changes, because the piece does not turn. Three moments change: \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\) along the \(x\) strip, \(M_{\mathrm{y}}\) along the \(y\) strip, and \(M_{\mathrm{xy}}\) across both, as the twist. Every moment is \(D\) times a curvature, so the three paths are three fourth derivatives, exactly these three terms.

2.6 The plate as a strip

First view: what is left when nothing changes in the \(y\) direction?

We cover a ditch with a long steel plate. We look at it from the side: the \(x\) axis across the ditch, the \(z\) axis upwards, the mid-plane is a line. The plate rests on two supports, the edges of the ditch. The load \(q\) presses on it, \(10\) kPa downwards. Across the span \(L\) the mid-plane curves, in the middle by \(|w(L/2)|\).

Below is the same plate in an oblique view. The flat mid-plane continues far back along the \(y\) axis. The curved surface is the same at every \(y\), so we take one strip of width \(1\). In the plan view from the top this means: the \(y\) strip and the twist carry nothing, because \(w_{\mathrm{yy}}\) and \(w_{\mathrm{xy}}\) are zero. What is left is

\[D\,w'''' = q ,\]

the beam equation, only with \(D\) in place of \(EI\). What does this view add? The plate contains the beam. How much it deflects we draw in the next view. In the book: 18.4, (18.30).

2.7 By numbers

Second view: how much does each path carry?

On the left we draw the strip over the ditch, supported only on its two long edges, and under it its deflection, \(0.4534\) mm. Next to it a square of the same plate, supported on all four edges. Its centre deflection is only \(0.1415\) mm, about three times less, \(0.312\) of the strip's. The square is not three times thicker. It only has two new paths, the \(y\) strip and the twist.

At the deflection of the square we also check the assumption that lies under the whole segment, from the previous segment: the mid-plane does not stretch, the deflections are small, \(|w| \le h/5\). Why does it hold here? \(0.1415\) mm is \(0.014\) of the thickness of \(10\) mm, and at such a deflection the stretch of the mid-plane is only \(0.7\) % of the bending strain. When not? At a deflection of \(h/5\), that is \(2\) mm, the stretch is already \(10\) % and the model starts to miss. What does it cost us? Only because of it is the equation linear in \(w\), at larger deflections the mid-plane stretches, and with immovable edges an axial force arises in the plane of the plate, which the model does not have.

How much does the twist carry? Take a single shape, the sine hill \(w \propto \sin(\pi x/a)\sin(\pi y/a)\) on a square. Every derivative in \(x\) or \(y\) brings a factor \(\pi/a\), so all three fourth derivatives, \(w_{\mathrm{xxxx}}\), \(w_{\mathrm{xxyy}}\) and \(w_{\mathrm{yyyy}}\), are equal. The moments from above then give the shares \(32.5\) %, \(35\) % and \(32.5\) %: \((1+\nu)\), \(2(1-\nu)\) and \((1+\nu)\) out of a total of \(4\). For this hill the twist carries more than either bending alone. So the twist is not a small correction. This holds for this hill, other shapes share differently. In the book: 18.5, 18.41.

2.8 By hand

Third view, without calculation. Imagine a floor of planks laid along \(x\). At first the planks just lie side by side. We step on one: it sags alone, like the strip along \(x\), its neighbours stay flat. Now we glue the planks together. We step on the same plank. The neighbours have to sag with it, but less, so the glued joint between them twists, like the twist between the strips. This twist passes part of our weight to the neighbours. This is the feel of the term \(2\,w_{\mathrm{xxyy}}\).

2.9 Historically

Fourth view: the equation was first found from an energy, and a wrong one at that. Sophie Germain took for the energy of the plate the square of the sum of curvatures, \(\tfrac{D}{2}\int(\nabla^2 w)^2\,\mathrm{d}A\), and got the same equation. Why is the energy still wrong?

We take a flat cardboard plate and hold it by the four corners, we lift two diagonal ones and lower the other two. The cardboard twists into the surface \(w = c\,x\,y\), in which every grid line is straight. So \(w_{\mathrm{xx}} = w_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0\) and \(\nabla^2 w = 0\). Germain's energy is zero, yet the cardboard does not stay twisted without the forces at the corners. The plate equation for this \(w\) says \(0 = q\) and it holds, because there is no load in the interior. So the equation alone does not see the twist, its work is at the edge, where the forces at the corners take it over. How the edge and the corner do this is the next segment. In the book: 18.2.4, 18.19.

3 Edges and corners: three quantities, two conditions

A square plate lies on four walls and a load pushes it down. Where must the walls hold it, and in which direction? The first answer would be: everywhere, and everywhere upwards. Let us see whether that holds.

We draw one edge of the plate, seen from the side, of length \(a\). We divide it into ten equal pieces. On each piece the support pushes up, most strongly in the middle of the edge, least near the corner. And what happens at the corner itself? The arrow there points down. The support must pull the corner down, with the force \(0.065\,q_0a^2\). For our steel plate, \(a = 500\) mm and \(q = 10\) kPa, that is \(162.4\) N at each corner. The plate would lift at the corners if nothing held it.

Where does a force that pulls down come from, when everything else pushes up? The claim in one sentence: at an edge the twisting moment cannot be prescribed separately, because a row of twisting couples along the edge is the same thing as vertical forces, which along the edge change the shear force to \(V_{\mathrm{x}} = Q_{\mathrm{x}} - M_{\mathrm{xy},\mathrm{y}}\), and at the end of the edge one force \(M_{\mathrm{xy}}\) is left over, which the two edges at the corner double to \(F_{\mathrm{c}} = 2\,M_{\mathrm{xy}}\). There are four whys in this claim. We take them in turn.

3.1 Why are there only two conditions on an edge?

A question for you: what can a hand do to the edge of a plate? Let us draw all the options.

We look at the edge from the side. Dashed we draw the middle plane before loading, with a thick line where it bends to. A hand can do three things to an edge: hold it at its height and at its angle, hold only its height, or let it go. First: we build the edge into a wall. Such an edge is clamped (vpet). The wall allows neither deflection nor rotation, so \(w = 0\) and \(\partial_{\mathrm{n}} w = 0\). Second: we lay the edge on a hinged support. The edge is simply supported (prostoležeč). The support holds the height but not the angle, so the plate tilts on the support. Since a hinge gives no moment, \(w = 0\) and \(M_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\). Third: we leave the edge free (prost). Nothing holds it. What must hold there? The moment is zero, and one more thing.

Here is the catch. The edge of a plate carries three quantities: the bending moment \(M_{\mathrm{n}}\), the shear force \(Q_{\mathrm{n}}\) and the twisting moment \(M_{\mathrm{xy}}\). At a free edge we would therefore expect three conditions: all three are zero. But the plate equation \(D\,\nabla^4 w = q\) is of fourth order, like the beam equation, and takes only two conditions on each edge. So under the free edge stands \(M_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\) and \(V_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\), where \(V_{\mathrm{n}}\) is the effective shear force (nadomestna prečna sila). Three quantities are squeezed into two. How, if each of them really acts on the edge? That is the next why.

The ground of this step is what the hand does: it holds the height, holds the angle, or holds nothing. Poisson did demand three conditions for an edge with given forces, and the book shows on the twisted plate \(w = c\,xy\), held by forces at its corners, that three conditions have no solution. In the book: 18.3.2, 18.19, 18.3.5.

3.2 Why can the twisting moment be replaced by forces?

A question: is the twisting moment at the edge really a third thing, or can we say it with forces? We cut a small piece out of the edge and put it on the board.

This is one piece of the edge, seen straight onto the face of the edge. It is \(\Delta s\) long and \(h\) thick, the thickness drawn much enlarged. What is the twisting moment on this piece? Shear stresses in the plane of the plate, pointing one way at the top and the other way at the bottom. We draw them as two horizontal forces, each \(M_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\Delta s/h\), a distance \(h\) apart. Their moment is force times lever arm:

\[\frac{M_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\Delta s}{h} \cdot h = M_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\Delta s .\]

Now we draw the same piece again, this time with a couple of vertical forces: at the left end \(M_{\mathrm{xy}}\) up, at the right end \(M_{\mathrm{xy}}\) down, a distance \(\Delta s\) apart. The moment is \(M_{\mathrm{xy}} \cdot \Delta s\). The same as before, and the sense of rotation is the same too. Does the plate notice the difference?

Why not? A couple does not move a body, because its two forces add up to zero. It only turns the body, and how strongly is given by the moment alone. Two couples with the same moment therefore act on the piece in the same way. These are Newton's laws for moving and for turning, and they are the ground of this step. The Kirchhoff plate model knows on each piece of the edge only the force and the moment, not how the stresses are spread over the thickness. For the model the replacement is just another way of writing the same load on the edge. In the book: 18.3.4.

The assumption that enters here: we replace the stresses over the thickness by others that are statically equivalent (statično enakovredne). Why is it right? Both loads have the same force and the same moment on every piece, and the model sees only these two. When does it fail? In a narrow strip along the edge itself the real stresses differ. Boussinesq treated the replacement as a local disturbance at the edge. The book gives no width for that strip. What does it do to the result? Nothing to the deflection and the moments of the model, it changes only the stresses in that strip along the edge, which a thin plate does not describe anyway.

3.3 Why does only the difference remain?

A question: every piece of the edge now has a pair of vertical forces. How many of them remain when we put the pieces in a row? We put three such pieces side by side along the edge, each \(\Delta s\) long. The twisting moment grows along the edge, so the couples grow from left to right: \(0.6\), \(0.95\) and \(1.3\). Look at the first joint. The first piece pushes down there with \(0.6\), the second piece pushes up at the same point with \(0.95\). Two forces at one point add. How much is left? What remains is \(0.95 - 0.6 = 0.35\) upwards. At the second joint \(1.3 - 0.95 = 0.35\).

What if the twisting moment were the same all along the edge? The forces at the joints would cancel exactly, and nothing would be left on the edge. What remains is only as much as \(M_{\mathrm{xy}}\) changes from piece to piece, \(\Delta M_{\mathrm{xy}}\). Divided by the length of a piece it is a force per unit length of edge. As the pieces get shorter, \(\Delta M_{\mathrm{xy}}/\Delta s\) tends to the derivative of the twisting moment along the edge, on the edge \(x = a\) to \(M_{\mathrm{xy},\mathrm{y}}\). This force joins the shear force:

\[V_{\mathrm{x}} = Q_{\mathrm{x}} - M_{\mathrm{xy},\mathrm{y}} .\]

This is the effective shear force of (18.25). So at a free edge the second condition is \(V_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\), not two separate conditions \(Q_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\) and \(M_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\). The ground: counting at the joints, and two opposite forces at one point cancel.

3.4 Why is there a force at the corner?

A question: what about the force at the very end of the edge, where there is no neighbour any more? Look at the two ends of the edge. The first piece on the left has no neighbour, so its force \(0.6\) has nothing to cancel against. The same holds for the last piece on the right and its force \(1.3\). At each end of the edge one whole force \(M_{\mathrm{xy}}\) remains. Now we draw a corner of the plate. Two edges meet in it, and each brings its own end force \(M_{\mathrm{xy}}\), both in the same direction. Together:

\[F_{\mathrm{c}} = 2\,M_{\mathrm{xy}} = 2 \cdot 81.21\ \mathrm N = 162.4\ \mathrm N ,\]

since for our plate the twisting moment at the corner is \(M_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0.03248\,q\,a^2 = 81.21\) N m/m, a moment per metre of edge, which is a force in newtons. This is the force at the corner of the first picture.

Is it really the force with which the support holds the corner, or only a leftover of the calculation? The idea of the proof: we move the plate virtually so that the supports are violated, and the work of all forces on that displacement shows that the support holds the edge with the force \(V_{\mathrm{n}}\) per unit length and the corner with the force \(F_{\mathrm{c}}\) (18.22). In the book: 18.3.4.

Two assumptions enter here. First: the edges are straight and meet at a right angle. Why is it right? Our square is like that. When does it fail? A smooth curved edge has no end, so it has no corner force, as in a circular plate (18.6). What does it do to the result? The factor \(2\) in \(F_{\mathrm{c}} = 2\,M_{\mathrm{xy}}\) comes from two edges meeting at one point. Second: the support can also pull the corner, not only push it. Why is it right? We prescribed the simply supported edge by \(w = 0\), so the support holds the plate both ways. When does it fail? A plate merely laid on the walls lifts off at the corners, and the solution is different. What does it do to the result? Without the corner forces \(650\) N are missing, a quarter of the load, as the next picture shows.

3.5 Back up: equilibrium of the whole plate

Now we put everything back together. The twist along the edge is couples of forces, the inner forces of the couples cancel, the differences along the edge and whole forces at the ends remain. Let us check this on the whole plate.

We draw the plate in an oblique view. The load pushes on it, in all \(q\,a^2 = 2500\) N downwards. Each edge pushes up, each with \(787.3\) N, the four together with \(3149.1\) N. That is more than the whole load. Where does the surplus go? Into the corners, where the support pulls down, each with \(162.4\) N, the four together with \(649.6\) N. We add up:

\[2500 - 3149.1 + 649.6 \approx 0 ,\]

within \(0.02\,\%\), because the series of the solution is cut off. The edges carry more than the plate's load, the corners give the surplus back. This is (18.29). And it is also the answer to the first question: the orange arrow at the corner is the end of the twisting couples of two edges.

3.6 By hand: a card on a box

Lay a square piece of card on the rim of an open box and press on the middle. The book says that the corners of the plate lift at the support. On the card you see it: the corners come off. This experiment adds the sign that the calculation hides in \(n_{\mathrm{x}} n_{\mathrm{y}}\): the force at the corner points down, the way the load points, not up like the other support forces. One more detail from the arrows along the edge: if each edge carried only a quarter of the load, the force on a tenth of the edge would be \(0.025\,q_0a^2\). The middle carries more:

\[\frac{0.0417\,q_0a^2}{0.025\,q_0a^2} \approx 1.7 ,\]

because near a corner two edges share the load.

3.7 A free plate held only at the corners

Corner forces are not only a notion of the calculation. We can make them by hand.

We take a plate with no supports, like a card in our hands. At two opposite corners we push it up, at the other two down, with four equal forces. The plate twists. The edges carry no load, the lower edge stays straight, and yet the plate is in equilibrium. The only forces on the plate are the four corner forces, each \(F = 2\,M_{\mathrm{xy}}\). If we lift one corner of our plate 1 mm above the other three, each force is \(100.5\) N. What does this view add? Corner forces are real forces that we can apply by hand. This is the twisted card of the first two parts, now with a name for the forces that hold it. In the book: 18.3.6.

3.8 By energy: why the count is forced

One more view from above. The boundary conditions are what the energy leaves free. When we change the deflection slightly and integrate the energy by parts twice, three terms remain on the edge: the moment times the rotation of the edge, the effective shear force times the deflection of the edge, and the term of the twisting moment, which after integration along the edge shrinks to its values at the ends of the edge. These are the corner terms, the same we got by counting forces at the joints. This view adds that two conditions and the corner force are no trick but follow from the energy itself. In the book: 18.3.3, 18.20, 18.21.
