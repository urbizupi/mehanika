Chapter 7 Basics of FEM for bars and trusses

Chapter 7Lecture: Basics of FEM for bars and trusses

1 The element is a spring: what the 2 × 2 matrix says

Let us cut one piece out of a bar, one finite element (končni element), and take it by both ends. With which two forces must we hold it so that the ends are displaced by \(d_1\) and \(d_2\)? One simple experiment, done twice, gives the answer.

We hold the left end firmly and pull the right end to the right. We draw the element as it is now, a thick line from node 1 to node 2. The grey tick shows where node 2 was before. We have moved it by \(d_2 = 1\), one metre, and the element is longer by exactly that much. In the figure this metre is greatly exaggerated. What do we feel in our hands? The right hand must pull to the right with the force \(EA/h\). The left hand must pull to the left, with the force \(-EA/h\), or else the element would drag node 1 along. The minus only gives the direction: we count forces positive to the right, along the axis \(x\).

We repeat the experiment with the other end. Now we hold the right end and push the left end to the right, towards it. We draw the element after this, from node 1 to node 2. Node 1 was here before. We have moved it by \(d_1 = 1\), and the element is shorter by that much. Now we must push node 1 to the right with the force \(EA/h\) and push node 2 to the left with the force \(-EA/h\).

We write the two numbers of each experiment as a column:

\[\text{bottom } (d_1 = 1): \begin{pmatrix} EA/h \\ -EA/h \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \text{top } (d_2 = 1): \begin{pmatrix} -EA/h \\ EA/h \end{pmatrix} .\]

We put the two columns side by side and get the stiffness matrix (togostna matrika) of the element:

\[\mat k_{\mathrm{e}} = \frac{EA}{h}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} .\]

That is the whole claim of this part of the lecture. Column \(j\) of the matrix is the two forces we feel in our hands when we move node \(j\) by 1 and hold the other one in place (7.4). A general state \((d_1, d_2)\) is just the sum of the two experiments, so \(\vek F_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\).

The claim uses four things we have not explained. Why are two numbers enough? Why is the force exactly \(EA/h\)? Why are the two forces opposite? And why does moving the whole element cost nothing? Let us take them in turn.

1.1 Why are two numbers, \(d_1\) and \(d_2\), enough?

The element has infinitely many points and each could move differently. How can we describe the whole element by the displacements of its ends alone?

We draw the axis \(x\) along the bar and on it the ends of the element, \(x_{\mathrm{a}}\) and \(x_{\mathrm{b}}\). The displacement is along the bar, but we draw it as a height above the axis. We decide that the displacement between the ends is a straight line. Through two points there is only one straight line, so it is fixed by exactly two numbers, the displacements of the ends. That is the ground of this question: through two points goes one line.

We write the line as the sum of two parts. The first part is the line that equals 1 at node 1 and 0 at node 2, the shape function \(\psi_1^e\) (oblikovna funkcija). The second part is the other way round, \(\psi_2^e\), 1 at node 2 and 0 at node 1. In the middle panel we raise the left end by the displacement \(d_1\) and the right end by the displacement \(d_2\). We multiply the first part by \(d_1\), \(d_1\psi_1^e\), and the second by \(d_2\), \(d_2\psi_2^e\). Their sum is the field \(u_{\mathrm{h}}\):

\[u_{\mathrm{h}}(x) = \psi_1^e(x)\,d_1 + \psi_2^e(x)\,d_2 .\]

At node 1 the second part is zero and \(d_1\) is left. At node 2 the first part is zero and \(d_2\) is left. So \(d_1\) and \(d_2\) are simply the heights of the line at the ends. In the book: 7.1.1, (7.3).

The straight line is an assumption, so let us say what it costs. Take the second element of the hanging steel bar of 7.1, from \(x = 5\) m to \(x = 10\) m, hanging under its own weight.

We draw the axis \(x\) from 5 m to 10 m and the vertical axis of displacement \(u\). The true displacement of the bar under its own weight is a parabola, because the weight along the bar makes the axial force grow from the bottom upwards. The nodal displacements of the element are exact, \(14.64\) µm and \(19.52\) µm. Through them we draw the straight line \(u_{\mathrm{h}}\). In the middle, at 7.5 m, the line is at \(17.08\) µm and the parabola at \(18.30\) µm. The line is \(1.220\) µm below the parabola. That is the price of the assumption.

When is the straight line exact? When no load acts along the element and \(EA\) is constant, since then the axial force is constant and the displacement grows evenly. When does it fail? When a load acts along the element, like the weight here, which bends the displacement into a parabola. What does this do to the result? Inside the element there is an error, at the nodes there is none. With a smaller element the error falls like \(h^2\), since the difference is \(a\,h^2/4\) with \(a = \rho g/(2E)\). In the book: 7.1.1.

1.2 Why is the force exactly \(EA/h\) per unit displacement?

How much force does one metre of elongation cost? Back to the top experiment of figure L7.1. The ends move apart by \(d_2 - d_1 = 1\), the element is longer by that metre. We divide the elongation by the length \(h\) and get the strain:

\[\varepsilon = \frac{d_2 - d_1}{h} = \frac{1}{h} .\]

Since the displacement is a straight line, this strain is the same along the whole element. Why do we divide by the length? Because strain means, by the meaning of the word, elongation per unit length.

The bar answers the strain with the axial force \(N = EA\,\varepsilon\). This is the measured law of the material (Hooke's law), \(E\) is the stiffness of the material, \(A\) the area of the cross-section. With \(\varepsilon = 1/h\)

\[N = \frac{EA}{h} ,\]

and that is exactly the force in the right hand. In the book: (7.4).

Here we assumed a linear elastic material. Why is it right? Steel responds linearly at small strains, and our element of the hanging bar stretches only by the difference of its nodal displacements, \(19.52 - 14.64 = 4.88\) µm over 5 m. The strain is about \(10^{-6}\). When does it fail? When the steel starts to yield. Effect: without linearity the forces of the two experiments could not be added, and a matrix independent of \(\vek d\) would not exist at all.

1.3 Why are the two forces opposite, where does the minus in the matrix come from?

In each column one entry is positive and the other negative. Why?

We draw the element alone, without the rest of the bar. At the left end and at the right end we have cut it off. What we removed we replace by forces. On the right end acts the force \(F_2\), on the left end the force \(F_1\), both on the axis of the bar, because a bar carries only an axial force. There are no other forces, because no load acts along the element. The element is at rest. By Newton, the sum of the forces on a body at rest is zero, so \(F_1 + F_2 = 0\). That is why each column of the matrix sums to zero, and that is the minus.

Which of the two is \(N\)? We cut the element once more, anywhere inside, and draw the pieces apart. The left piece is pulled by the force \(F_1\), the right piece by the force \(F_2\). At the new cut the axial force \(N\) acts, to the left on the right piece and to the right on the left piece. Each piece is at rest by itself, so \(F_2 = N\) and \(F_1 = -N\). Since we could choose the cut anywhere, \(N\) is the same along the whole element, just as the straight line says. In the book: proof of 7.5, step 5.

The assumption here is that no load acts along the element. Why is it right? On an element without its own weight only the two end forces act. When does it fail? In the hanging bar, where each element carries its weight. Effect: the sum of the forces of the surroundings is no longer zero but minus the weight of the element, as the numbers below show.

1.4 Why does moving both ends by the same distance cost nothing?

What do we feel in our hands if we move both ends at once, by the same distance? We add the two experiments of figure L7.1: node 1 by 1 and node 2 by 1 at the same time. The forces add, \(EA/h - EA/h = 0\) at each node:

\[\mat k_{\mathrm{e}} \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \frac{EA}{h}\begin{pmatrix} 1 - 1 \\ -1 + 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} .\]

Why? The distance between the ends does not change, nothing stretches, no force is needed. The ground: when we carry a ruler across the room, it does not stretch.

We see the same on the shape functions. The sum \(\psi_1^e + \psi_2^e\) is 1 everywhere, so with \(d_1 = d_2 = c\) we get \(u_{\mathrm{h}} = c\), the element moves as a whole. In the book: 7.3, 7.6, points 3 and 4.

What follows? The element alone cannot be solved. If \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) is a solution, so is \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} + c\,(1, 1)^\T\). We must fix the element somewhere, which is the next part of the lecture (7.3).

1.5 Back up: every state is the sum of two experiments

Now we have all the parts. We get the state \((d_1, d_2)\) by multiplying the bottom experiment by \(d_1\) and the top one by \(d_2\) and adding them. Since the material is linear, the forces add too:

\[\vek F_{\mathrm{e}} = d_1 \frac{EA}{h}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix} + d_2 \frac{EA}{h}\begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \frac{EA}{h}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} d_1 \\ d_2 \end{pmatrix} = \mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} .\]

That is equation (7.6) for one element. Let us check it with numbers on the second element of the hanging bar.

We draw the axis \(x\) downwards, from 5 m to 10 m. We draw the element before loading grey dashed. Under its weight the bar stretches, and we draw the element after loading in orange, moved down. The upper node moves by \(d_1 = 14.64\) µm, the lower one by \(d_2 = 19.52\) µm. Part of this is only a move as a whole, which costs nothing. The element gets longer by \(d_2 - d_1 = 4.88\) µm, and only that is what it feels. Its stiffness is \(EA/h = 3.079\cdot 10^6\) N/m. The upper node needs the force \(-15.02\) N, upwards, the lower one the force \(15.02\) N, downwards:

\[3.079\cdot 10^6\ \tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{m}} \cdot 4.88\cdot 10^{-6}\ \text{m} = 15.02\ \text{N} .\]

These would be the forces if the element had no weight. The real element has the weight \(q\,h = 30.05\) N along it, and here the assumption from the free-body diagram comes back. We replace the weight by two forces at the nodes, for constant \(q\) half the weight at each node, \(q\,h/2 = 15.02\) N (7.1.3). The surroundings only have to add the rest: at the lower node \(15.02 - 15.02 = 0\), because the lower end is free, at the upper node \(-15.02 - 15.02 = -30.05\) N, exactly the weight of the element, carried by the first element. In the book: 7.9, (7.10). We need this in the next part.

From the same two numbers we also get the force in the element. The elongation divided by \(h\) and multiplied by \(EA\) gives \(N_{\mathrm{e}} = EA\,(d_2 - d_1)/h = 15.02\) N, the same along the whole element ((7.11)).

1.6 By hand: the element is a spring

If we had to tell you all this in two minutes, we would say this. The element is a spring. A spring with constant \(k\) needs the force \(k\) per metre of elongation, at both ends, in opposite directions. For the element the constant is \(EA/h\). The 2 × 2 matrix is Hooke's spring written for both ends at once, the first column for moving the left end, the second for moving the right one.

From \(EA/h\) we read at once when the spring is stiffer. A shorter element (\(h\) smaller), a thicker one (\(A\) larger) or one of a stiffer material (\(E\) larger). The whole hanging bar, \(h = 10\) m, has \(1.539\cdot 10^7\ \text{N} / 10\ \text{m} = 1.539\cdot 10^6\) N/m, half as much as its half.

1.7 By energy: the same matrix stores the energy

A stretched spring stores energy. An element with the elongation \(d_2 - d_1\) has

\[U_{\mathrm{e}} = \frac{EA}{2h}\,(d_2 - d_1)^2 = \tfrac12\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} .\]

For the element of the hanging bar this is \(36.66\) µJ. The energy is a square of the elongation, so it is never negative and it is zero exactly when \(d_1 = d_2\), for a move as a whole. What does this view add? The term \(-2\,d_1 d_2\) of the expanded energy splits into two equal halves, at the places \((1,2)\) and \((2,1)\), so the matrix is symmetric. And the derivative of the energy with respect to \(d_2\) is \(\frac{EA}{h}(d_2 - d_1)\), exactly the force \(F_2\). In the book: (7.5), 7.6.

1.8 By formula: the recipe for all later elements

The slopes of the shape functions are \(-1/h\) for \(\psi_1^e\) and \(1/h\) for \(\psi_2^e\). We put them in the row \(\mat b_{\mathrm{e}} = \frac1h(-1,\ 1)\), and the strain is \(\varepsilon = \mat b_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\). The matrix is then

\[\mat k_{\mathrm{e}} = \int_{\mathrm{x}_\mathrm{a}}^{x_{\mathrm{b}}} \mat b_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,EA\,\mat b_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mathrm{d}x .\]

What does it add? This recipe also works for the element with three nodes and later for the beam, where we can no longer guess the columns by hand. In the book: (7.7).

To sum up. A column of the stiffness matrix is the two forces that hold one node moved by 1 and the other in place. The number \(EA/h\) is the spring, the minus is Newton, the zero sum is the ruler we carry across the room. In the next part we put elements together into a bar and fix it.

2 Assembly and supports: forces add at the node

For one element we already know the table of forces, the stiffness matrix (togostna matrika) \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\). How do we get the table of the whole bar from two such tables? Why can we not solve the system we get until the bar is fixed somewhere? And where is the force with which the support holds the bar hidden? We stay with the same hanging steel bar, 10 m long, the axis \(x\) points down, divided into two elements of 5 m.

First we write the table of the first element, in units of \(EA/h\). These are the numbers \(1\), \(-1\), \(-1\), \(1\) we found in the previous part. Along its rows and columns we write which nodes of the whole bar they are: the first element joins nodes \(0\) and \(1\). Below it we write the table of the second element, with the same numbers, since the two elements are equal. The second element joins nodes \(1\) and \(2\). The arrow says that both tables go into one, the matrix of the whole bar \(\mat K\). It has three rows and three columns, one for each node \(0\), \(1\), \(2\). The first table falls into rows and columns \(0\) and \(1\). The second falls into \(1\) and \(2\). Where do they meet? In the entry \((1, 1)\), and there we add the two numbers, \(1 + 1 = 2\).

That is the whole claim of this part. Node \(1\), which the two elements share, is held by both, so their forces on it add and the two tables fall one over the other. A bar fixed nowhere has no solution, because moving the whole bar costs no force. When we strike out the row and the column of the supported node, a solvable system is left, and the struck row gives us the support force. In the book: 7.2.

2.1 Why may we simply add the tables?

Why should the force on the shared node be exactly a sum? Adding matrices is only arithmetic, and we are after the physical reason. Let us look for it on the bar itself.

Let the axis \(x\) point down, as for the hanging bar. We draw the bar before the move, with nodes \(0\), \(1\) and \(2\), thin and dashed, because we are about to move it. Nodes \(0\) and \(2\) we hold with two walls, so they cannot move. Now we push node \(1\) down. What happens to the elements? The first element is stretched, we draw it blue. The second element is shortened, we draw it orange. The node has moved by \(d_1\), and that is how much the first element got longer and the second got shorter.

How hard must we hold node \(1\) in this place? Let us cut it out of the bar, with the blue end of the first element above it and the orange end of the second below it. The stretched first element pulls the node back up. To keep the node in place we must pull it down, with the force \(\frac{EA}{h}d_1\), because that is the force that stretches one element by \(d_1\). The shortened second element pushes the node back up. So for it too we must push the node down, with the same force \(\frac{EA}{h}d_1\). Both forces act on the same point in the same direction. Their sum is the force \(2\,\frac{EA}{h}d_1\).

Why may they be added? Because they are two forces on one point at rest. This is the equilibrium of a node at rest by Newton's laws, nothing else. Imagine holding two springs in one hand, one going up and one going down. The hand feels both. That is why the entry \((1, 1)\) in the first figure holds \(1 + 1 = 2\): each element adds its own one, because each on its own resists the move of node \(1\). The end nodes \(0\) and \(2\) belong to one element only, so they have just \(1\) on the diagonal. If you have only a minute for all this, remember the node cut free: every row of the matrix \(\mat K\) is the equilibrium of one node. Assembly is not a trick with matrices, it is Newton at every node. In the book: 7.13, point 1.

Assumption: the two elements are joined at the node. Both elements have the same displacement \(d_1\) at node \(1\), one single number. Why is that right? The bar is one piece, and we cut it only in our minds. When is it not right? When two pieces are joined with play, like a bolt in an oversized hole, and can move against each other. What would that do to the calculation? The elements would share no unknown, each would have its own, and nothing would overlap in the matrix. The matrix would fall apart into two separate pieces.

2.2 Where does each number go?

How do we know where in the large matrix each number goes? With two elements we see by heart where each table goes. With a hundred elements, or with a truss where nodes meet from all sides, we no longer do. We need a list of addresses.

We take a bar divided into four elements, so that the list is longer. We number the elements and write the numbers in circles. Every node of the bar gets its global number, from \(0\) to \(4\). But each element names its two nodes in its own way, with the local numbers \(1\) and \(2\), upper and lower. Now we write the connectivity table (tabela povezav). In the head are the columns \(e\), \(1\) and \(2\). The row of an element says which global number is its node \(1\) and which its node \(2\). The second element has the row \((1, 2)\).

How do we use the table? The number in row \(i\) and column \(j\) of an element's table is added into the matrix \(\mat K\) at the address given by the global numbers of its \(i\)-th and \(j\)-th node. That is all. It is exactly what we did in the first figure, where the rows of the connectivity table, \((0, 1)\) and \((1, 2)\), are written along the element tables. Behind this operation there is no theory, only counting: the table is a directory, like the list of house numbers a postman follows. Every inner node appears twice in the table, once as the lower node of the upper element and once as the upper node of the lower one. That is why it gets \(1\) twice on the diagonal. In the book: 7.10 and the algorithm in 7.2.2.

2.3 Why can the system \(\mat K\vek d = \vek f\) not be solved?

We have the matrix and the right side, a system of three equations in three unknowns \(d_0\), \(d_1\), \(d_2\). Let us try to solve it. It does not work. Why?

Again let the axis \(x\) point down. We draw the bar with nodes \(0\), \(1\), \(2\), this time with no wall at all. What happens if we move all three nodes down, all by the same distance \(c\)? The first element stays \(h\) long, so does the second. Nothing is stretched. How much force do we need for this? None. That is why the numbers in every row of the matrix \(\mat K\) add up to zero, for example \(1 - 1 + 0\) in the first row of the first figure. The matrix turns the move \((c, c, c)\) into zero forces.

What follows for the solution? If some \(\vek d\) solved the system, then \(\vek d\) with the move \(c\) added would solve it too, for every \(c\). The solution would not be one. Worse still: let us add all three equations. On the left we get zero, because every column adds up to zero. On the right we get the weight of all three nodes together, that is the weight of the bar \(qL\). Zero equals the weight of the bar? That holds for no \(\vek d\). The system has no solution.

Where is the mistake? Not in the arithmetic, in the model. A bar with no wall, on which only its weight acts, falls. It has no position in which it would rest, and a position of rest is exactly what we were looking for. At the bottom of this question is an experience everyone has: what is held by nothing falls. In the book: 7.3.1.

Assumption: there are enough supports. The system becomes solvable only once a support stops the move \(c\) that costs nothing. For a bar one fixed node is enough, any one. When is it not enough? For a truss, where part of the structure can turn without any bar being stretched. The result is a zero pivot in the elimination, which we show in the next part. In the book: 7.18.

2.4 Why may we strike out the row and column of the support?

We now hang the bar from the ceiling at node \(0\), so \(d_0 = 0\). How do we tell the system?

We write the unknowns \(d_0\), \(d_1\), \(d_2\) and below them the brackets of the matrix. Into them we copy the numbers of the first figure. The row and column of node \(0\) we write grey and frame, because we will decide about them separately. The other four numbers we write blue. Right of the equals sign is the right side, the weight hanging on each node: \(15.02\), \(30.05\) and \(15.02\) N. Why does the middle node have twice as much? Because it carries half the weight of the upper element and half the weight of the lower one. An end node carries half of one only.

Now we claim that we may strike out the grey row and the grey column. Why? First the column. Column \(0\) is multiplied by \(d_0\), and \(d_0\) is zero. So column \(0\) adds nothing to any equation. Then the row. Row \(0\) is the equilibrium of node \(0\). But node \(0\) also feels the force of the ceiling, which we do not know, so the right side there reads \(15.02 + r_0\). An equation with an extra unknown does not help us find the displacements, so we put it aside. We do not throw it away, we will need it.

What is left? The blue block \(\mat K_{\mathrm{ff}}\) with two equations for the free unknowns \(d_1\) and \(d_2\) (proste neznanke):

\[\frac{EA}{h} \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} d_1 \\ d_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 30.05 \\ 15.02 \end{pmatrix}\ \text{N} , \qquad \frac{EA}{h} = 3.079 \cdot 10^6\ \text{N/m} .\]

Let us add the two rows. On the left \(\frac{EA}{h}\,d_1\) is left, on the right \(45.07\) N. What does that mean? The first element carries everything that hangs below it, the weight of nodes \(1\) and \(2\), \(30.05 + 15.02\) N. The second row says that the second element carries only the weight of node \(2\), \(15.02\) N. In the book: 7.3.2, equations (7.16) and (7.17).

Assumption: the support does not move. The struck column vanished because \(d_0 = 0\). Why may we take that? The ceiling is far stiffer than the thin bar. When is it not right? When the support settles. Let us look at a bar whose support we move.

The axis \(x\) points down. The upper support holds the bar, and this time the bar is fixed at the bottom too. Now we move the lower support down, by \(\bar u = 0.1\) mm. Node \(0\) stays, nodes \(1\) and \(2\) go down. The column of node \(2\) is now multiplied by \(\bar u\) and is no longer zero. We move it to the right side, where it acts as an extra force on node \(1\), \(EA\,\bar u/h = 307.88\) N. That is about ten times the weight \(f_1 = 30.05\) N on the same node. So a tenth of a millimetre of settlement loads the bar more than all its weight. In the book: 7.3.3.

2.5 Where is the support force?

We put row \(0\) aside because it holds the unknown force of the ceiling. How large is that force?

The axis \(x\) points down. We draw the bar with nodes \(0\), \(1\), \(2\) and fix it to the ceiling at node \(0\). Each element has a weight, \(30.05\) N, acting at its middle. We split it in half between its two nodes and get the nodal forces, \(15.02\), \(30.05\) and \(15.02\) N, the right side of the system.

On the right we draw what the system of two equations gave us. The bar before loading we draw grey dashed, at the ceiling. After loading it is orange and longer. Node \(1\) has moved by \(14.64\) µm, node \(2\) by \(19.52\) µm. So the second element stretched only by \(4.88\) µm, the difference of the two displacements, because it carries only the weight of the lower node, \(N_2 = 15.02\) N. The two displacements are exact, the same as in chapter 6.

Now the row we put aside. Row \(0\) of the matrix is \(\frac{EA}{h}(1, -1, 0)\). Multiplied by the displacements, it says how hard we must hold node \(0\) from outside. The first element is stretched by \(14.64\) µm and pulls node \(0\) down with the force \(N_1 = 45.07\) N, so we must hold the node up: \(\frac{EA}{h}(d_0 - d_1) = -3.079 \cdot 10^6\ \text{N/m} \cdot 14.64\ \text{µm} = -45.07\) N. Two forces act on node \(0\) from outside, the weight on the node, \(15.02\) N down, and the force of the ceiling \(r_0\). Together they must give \(-45.07\) N:

\[15.02\ \text{N} + r_0 = -45.07\ \text{N} , \qquad r_0 = -60.10\ \text{N} .\]

The minus sign means against the axis \(x\), so the ceiling pulls the bar up with \(60.10\) N. That is exactly the weight of the whole bar, \(6.010\ \text{N/m} \cdot 10\ \text{m}\). Could it be otherwise? No. The bar is at rest, so the ceiling must carry all the bar weighs. That is the ground of this question. We call the support force the reaction (reakcija), because it is the support's response to what the bar brings to it. In the book: 7.3.4, equation (7.20).

2.6 Back up

Now let us go from the ground back to the first figure. Each element adds its table, the first, then the second, and where they share a node their forces add, \(1 + 1\). Since the bar is not fixed, the system has no solution. We strike out the row and column of the support, solve two equations and read the reaction from the row we put aside. Element tables, assembly, supports, solution, reactions: these are the steps of every finite element program. In the book: 7.10.1.

2.7 The view by energy

We can say the same with energy. Every stretched element stores energy, like a stretched spring. The first element stores \(U_1 = 329.93\) µJ, the second \(U_2 = 36.66\) µJ. Why does the first store nine times more? It stretched by \(14.64\) µm, the second only by \(4.88\) µm, three times less, and energy grows with the square of the elongation, \(3^2 = 9\). The energy of the whole bar is the sum, \(366.59\) µJ, and \(\tfrac12\,\vek d^\T\mat K\,\vek d\) gives exactly the same. What do we gain from this? The energy of every element is zero or more, so the sum is zero or more too, and \(\mat K\) never gives a negative energy. In the book: 7.14.

2.8 The view by pattern

Let us go back to the connectivity table of four elements. Two nodes meet in the same row only if they are neighbours. That is why the matrix \(\mat K\) has numbers only on the diagonal and right next to it:

\[\mat K = \frac{EA}{h} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ -1 & 2 & -1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 & 2 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 & 2 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} .\]

Only \(13\) of the \(25\) numbers are not zero. A zero stands wherever two nodes share no element. With a thousand elements almost all are zeros, and the computer will put that to good use. In the book: 7.8.

2.9 The view by integral

In chapter 6 we got the same matrix from integrals of the tent functions over the whole bar. Since every tent function lives only on two neighbouring elements, the integral falls apart into pieces by elements, and each piece is one element table. In the book: 7.13, point 3.

2.10 What does it cost to put the weight into the nodes?

We moved the weight into the nodes. Why may we? Half to each node keeps the total weight, \(15.02 + 30.05 + 15.02 = 60.10\) N. But what do we lose?

On the left is again our bar with the axis \(x\) down, hanging from the ceiling, with the nodal forces and the reaction. On the right we draw the axes, horizontally \(x\) from \(0\) to \(L\), vertically the axial force \(N\). The true axial force falls along a straight line from \(60.10\) N at the ceiling to zero at the lower end, because every cross-section carries the weight of the bar below it. A linear element, however, has one single force along the whole element, so we get two steps, \(45.07\) and \(15.02\) N, with the border at \(h\) and the end at \(L\). At the ceiling the step lies below the true line by \(qh/2\), that is \(15.02\) N, a quarter of the true value. That is the cost. The reaction is still exact: \(45.07 + 15.02 = 60.10\) N, because the missing part is brought exactly by the weight on node \(0\). How do we make the error smaller? The error \(qh/2\) grows with the element length \(h\), so with four elements it is half as large. An element with more nodes helps too. In the book: 7.3.4, equation (7.22), and 7.4.

3 A slanted bar feels only the displacement along itself

So far every node moved only along the bar, by one number. In a truss the bars point in different directions, and a node moves in the plane, by two numbers. How much of that displacement does a bar feel at all? And when does a truss not stand? We read both from one picture.

First we draw the axes \(x\) and \(y\). We call them global axes (globalne osi), since all bars of the truss share them. Then one bar, going from node \(i\) to node \(j\). The bar is tilted by the angle \(\theta\) to the \(x\) axis. We write its direction as the unit vector \(\vek n = (c, s)\), with \(c = \cos\theta\) and \(s = \sin\theta\). Along the bar we lay its own axis \(\bar x\). This is a local axis (lokalna os), since it belongs to this bar only.

Now we move node \(i\). We draw the displacement \(\vek u_{\mathrm{i}}\) in orange, it has two numbers \((u_{\mathrm{i}}, v_{\mathrm{i}})\). From the tip of the arrow we drop a perpendicular onto the bar. The displacement falls apart into the part along the bar \(\bar u_{\mathrm{i}}\) and the part across \(\bar w_{\mathrm{i}}\).

The claim of this part: only the part along the bar, \(\bar u_{\mathrm{i}} = \vek n\cdot\vek u_{\mathrm{i}} = c\,u_{\mathrm{i}} + s\,v_{\mathrm{i}}\), lengthens it, the part across only turns it. From this follow its stiffness matrix

\[\mat k_{\mathrm{e}} = \frac{EA}{L}\begin{bmatrix} \vek n\,\vek n^\T & -\vek n\,\vek n^\T \\ -\vek n\,\vek n^\T & \vek n\,\vek n^\T \end{bmatrix} ,\]

and the rule that a truss stands only if every movement of its nodes lengthens some bar. Let us see why, step by step.

3.1 Why does only the part along the bar count?

By how much does a bar get longer when we move its end? The part along is simple. If the end moves along the bar by \(\bar u_{\mathrm{i}}\), the bar gets longer or shorter by exactly that. But what about the part across? Let us look at a case where the whole displacement is across.

Take two bars on one line, each of length \(L\). We pin the left end to the wall at \(A\), the right one to the wall at \(B\). Between them is node \(C\). Now we push \(C\) down by \(v\), exactly across both bars. The bars tilt into a new position.

By how much did the left bar get longer? The new bar is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with legs \(L\) and \(v\), so by Pythagoras it is \(\sqrt{L^2 + v^2}\) long. With an arc about \(A\) we carry this length back onto the line of the bars. What sticks out past \(C\) is the elongation \(e\):

\[e = \sqrt{L^2 + v^2} - L = \frac{v^2}{\sqrt{L^2 + v^2} + L} \approx \frac{v^2}{2L} .\]

The second form comes from multiplying numerator and denominator by \(\sqrt{L^2 + v^2} + L\). For a small \(v\) the denominator is nearly \(2L\).

In the figure \(v = 0.4\,L\) and \(e = 0.077\,L\), so large that we can see it at all. And for a real displacement? Put \(v = L/1000\) into the same formula: \(e \approx (L/1000)^2/(2L) = L/2\,000\,000\). The displacement is a thousandth of the length, the elongation a two-millionth. For \(L = 4\) m and \(v = 1\) mm the elongation is \(0.000125\) mm.

This is the ground: Pythagoras, and the fact that the square of a small number is far smaller than the number itself. Back to the bar we started with. The part along lengthens it by its full amount, the part across only by the square of a small number. To first order only \(\bar u_{\mathrm{i}} = \vek n\cdot\vek u_{\mathrm{i}}\) counts. It is the shadow of the displacement on the direction of the bar, the dot product with the unit vector. That is why the angle of the bar enters the matrix only through \(c\) and \(s\). When both ends move, the difference counts, and the elongation is \(e = \vek n\cdot(\vek u_{\mathrm{j}} - \vek u_{\mathrm{i}})\). In the book: 7.5.1.

Here we made the assumption of small displacements, and we write the matrix on the shape before loading. Why it is right: displacements of structures are millimetres on metres, like \(v = 1\) mm at \(L = 4\) m. When it fails: when the quadratic elongation \(e\) is the only thing that lengthens the bar at all, as with these two bars on one line. What it does to the result: it drops the elongation \(e\) of the part across, \(0.000125\) mm against \(1\) mm of displacement at \(v = 1\) mm.

3.2 Where do \(c^2\), \(cs\) and \(s^2\) come from?

What does the hand feel that moves the end of a slanted bar horizontally? A column of the matrix is a table of forces: the forces with which we must hold the nodes so that one moves by \(1\) and the others stay at rest (part 01). Let us do this with a slanted bar, the bar \(BC\) of the wall truss.

First the axes \(x\) and \(y\). We pin the end \(B\) to the wall, with a pin that lets the bar turn about it. The bar goes from \(B\) to \(C\), it is \(5\) m long. Its direction is the unit vector \(\vek n = (0.8, -0.6)\), so \(c = 0.8\) and \(s = -0.6\). Along itself it is stiff like a spring of stiffness \(EA/L = 3.92\cdot 10^6\) N/m (part 01).

Now we move the end \(C\) by \(1\) in the \(x\) direction and hold it there. The bar now goes to \(C'\). How much did the bar feel? Only the part of the move along itself, \(\vek n\cdot(1, 0) = c = 0.8\). This is the first projection, pure geometry from the previous section.

The bar answers like a spring. It asks for a force along itself, \(\frac{EA}{L}\,c\). Why exactly along itself? The bar is pinned at both ends, the pins carry no moment, and it is loaded only at its ends. So only two forces act on it, and since the bar is at rest, Newton's laws make them equal, opposite and on one line. That line goes through both ends, so along the bar.

Now the second projection, this time of the force. The force lies on the bar, so it has the component in \(x\) \(\frac{EA}{L}\,c\cdot c\) and the component in \(y\) \(\frac{EA}{L}\,c\cdot s\). A component is the shadow of the force on an axis. In numbers:

\[\frac{EA}{L}\,c^2 = 3.92\cdot 10^6 \cdot 0.64 = 2.5088\cdot 10^6\ \text{N/m} ,\]
\[\frac{EA}{L}\,c\,s = 3.92\cdot 10^6 \cdot (-0.48) = -1.8816\cdot 10^6\ \text{N/m} .\]

We moved only in \(x\), but the force also has a part in \(y\), downwards. That is the off-diagonal entry \(cs\): the bar turns a sideways move into a force along itself. If you have only a minute, remember this: a bar can pull or push only along itself.

These two numbers are the first column of the block \(\frac{EA}{L}\,\vek n\,\vek n^\T\). A move by \(1\) in \(y\) gives in the same way \(\frac{EA}{L}\,s\,c\) and \(\frac{EA}{L}\,s^2\). Two projections in a row, one for the move and one for the force, so every entry has two factors from \(c\) and \(s\).

We stack the columns into a \(4 \times 4\) table, divided into four blocks. The rows and columns belong to the four displacements \(u_{\mathrm{i}}\), \(v_{\mathrm{i}}\), \(u_{\mathrm{j}}\), \(v_{\mathrm{j}}\), two for each node. In front of the table stands the factor \(EA/L\). Into each block we write \(c^2\), \(cs\) and \(s^2\), for the bar \(BC\) these are \(0.64\), \(-0.48\) and \(0.36\). Why four equal blocks with alternating signs? When we pull \(C\) out, the bar pulls \(B\) with the opposite force, since two opposite forces act on a bar at rest. So the block of the other node gets a minus.

What if we rename the ends? The labels \(i\) and \(j\) swap, \(\vek n\) becomes \(-\vek n\), but the product \(\vek n\,\vek n^\T\) stays the same. The matrix does not depend on which end we call first. In the book: 7.28 and (7.35), the proof goes through the energy of the bar.

Here is the second assumption, the truss model of 4.4: pins, forces only at the nodes, weight of the bars neglected. Why it is right: then only the forces at the ends act on the bar, and they lie along it, as we just saw. When it fails: a load on the bar between the nodes, or welded joints, then the bar also bends. What it does to the result: each bar has a single number, its axial force, and only \(\vek n\) appears in its table. How much this changes the result we say at the solution below. The third assumption, constant \(EA\) along the bar, means that one element per bar is exact (part 01).

3.3 Why is a truss then the same as the bar made of elements?

What else do we need to get from one bar to a truss? Nothing new. Once we know the table of one bar, the truss is only the assembly of part 02. Let us try it on the wall truss.

First the wall, which holds node \(1 = A\) and node \(2 = B\) with fixed pins. The bar \(AC\) is horizontal, \(\vek n = (1, 0)\), \(4\) m long, \(EA/L = 4.9\cdot 10^6\) N/m. The bar \(BC\) is our slanted bar. They meet at node \(3 = C\), where the load \(F = 10\) kN hangs.

Every node has two unknowns, six in all. Every bar brings its own \(4 \times 4\) table. We assemble the table of the structure as in part 02: each entry is added at the place of its bar's unknowns. Why do we add? Because at a node the forces of the bars add up. The two bars share only node \(C\), and bar \(AC\) has a nonzero entry in the block of \(C\) only in the \(x\) direction. So the contributions meet at a single place, \(4.9 + 2.5088 = 7.4088\).

The displacements of \(A\) and \(B\) are zero, so we cross out their rows and columns. What remains is the block of node \(C\):

\[\mat K_{\mathrm{ff}} = \begin{bmatrix} 7.4088 & -1.8816 \\ -1.8816 & 1.4112 \end{bmatrix}\cdot 10^6\ \text{N/m} .\]

This is the same matrix we got by differentiating the energy in 4.6.7, this time without a single thought about which bar gets longer how. In the book: 7.5.2.

What does the solution give? On the left again the wall with nodes \(A\) and \(B\), both bars and the load \(F\) at \(C\). Elimination gives two positive pivots and the solution. The truss sags: \(C\) moves by \(2.721\) mm to the left and by \(10.714\) mm down. With welded joints we would get the same two displacements to \(0.0013\) %, that is the cost of the pin model.

The force in each bar is again stiffness times elongation, \(N = \frac{EA}{L}\,\vek n\cdot(\vek u_{\mathrm{j}} - \vek u_{\mathrm{i}})\). Let us check it on the right. We cut the pin \(C\) out of the truss. Bar \(AC\) pushes it with \(13.33\) kN, since it is compressed. Bar \(BC\) pulls it towards \(B\) with \(16.67\) kN, since it is in tension. The load pulls it down with \(10\) kN. Is the pin at rest? In \(x\) we get \(13.33 - 0.8\cdot 16.67 \approx 0\), in \(y\) we get \(0.6\cdot 16.67 - 10 \approx 0\). Equilibrium holds, and these are the same results as in chapter 4.

3.4 When does a truss not stand?

Does every truss we put together from bars also stand? No. A truss does not stand when its nodes can move so that no bar gets longer to first order. Then no bar feels anything, as with the part across, no force resists, the energy is zero. Let us look for such movements of the wall truss without supports.

We look for movements with \(\vek n\cdot(\vek u_{\mathrm{j}} - \vek u_{\mathrm{i}}) = 0\) for both bars. First: we move all nodes by the same vector in \(x\). The differences of the displacements are zero, no bar gets longer. Second: the same in \(y\). Third: we draw the truss once more, \(B\) and \(C\) stay, and we move \(A\) across the bar \(AC\). This is a turn of bar \(AC\) about \(C\), the part across from the first section. Fourth: \(A\) and \(C\) stay, we move \(B\) across the bar \(BC\), a turn of bar \(BC\) about \(C\).

The matrix \(\mat K\) does not see these movements, it has four zero eigenvalues. The first two and the turn of both bars together about \(C\) are rigid movements of the whole. The turn of a single bar changes the angle between the bars and is not a rigid movement. The supports at \(A\) and \(B\) prevent all four. So the first failure is too few supports: the truss shifts or turns as a rigid body, and a pivot in the elimination is zero.

The second failure is a mechanism (mehanizem), a movement without elongation that is not a rigid movement. Back to the two bars on one line. Both are pinned at \(A\) and \(B\), so there are not too few supports. The bars have \(\vek n = (\pm 1, 0)\) and \(EA/L = 4.9\cdot 10^6\) N/m, so

\[\mat K_{\mathrm{ff}} = \begin{bmatrix} 9.8 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\cdot 10^6\ \text{N/m} ,\]

and the second pivot is zero. Why? The move of \(C\) downwards is across both bars, and a bar does not feel a move across itself, the first section once more.

Here the assumption of small displacements shows its price. Linear theory counts the elongation \(e\) as zero, so it thinks the bars carry nothing vertically. In reality they do carry through \(e\), but the force grows with the cube of the displacement, \(F \approx EA\,v^3/L^3\). At \(v = 1\) mm this is \(0.306\) mN, nothing against a load of \(10\) kN.

A quick test is counting: the forces in the bars and the reactions against two equations of equilibrium per node. The wall truss has \(2\) bars and \(4\) reactions, \(6 = 2\cdot 3\). But the two bars on one line also have \(6 = 2\cdot 3\), and their matrix is singular. Counting tells whether there are enough equations, not whether they are independent. Only the energy tells that, with a zero pivot in the elimination. In the book: 7.5.3, 7.32 and 7.33.

3.5 Back up

Now we build it all again from below. The bar feels the displacement of its end only through the part along itself. It answers like a spring \(EA/L\) with a force along itself, which we split into the component \(x\) and the component \(y\). That is the block \(\vek n\,\vek n^\T\), the other end gets the opposite force, hence the minus off the diagonal. We add the tables of the bars at the nodes, cross out the supported rows, solve, and get the displacement of \(C\) and the bar forces \(N = \frac{EA}{L}\,\vek n\cdot(\vek u_{\mathrm{j}} - \vek u_{\mathrm{i}})\). In space the same sentence holds, only \(\vek n\) has three components. In the book: 7.6.1.

3.6 The energy view: which movements are free

The energy of the bar is

\[U_{\mathrm{e}} = \frac{EA}{2L}\,\big(\vek n\cdot(\vek u_{\mathrm{j}} - \vek u_{\mathrm{i}})\big)^2 .\]

It is zero for every move across and for every move of the whole bar. This view gives the test for a mechanism: a movement with zero energy in all bars that the supports do not prevent.

3.7 The view by formula: two projections as a matrix

The matrix \(\mat T_{\mathrm{e}}\) takes the four numbers of the end displacements and returns two projections onto the bar, \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat T_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,\bar{\mat k}\,\mat T_{\mathrm{e}}\). In the book: 7.28.
