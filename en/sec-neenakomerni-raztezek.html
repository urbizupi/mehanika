Chapter 1 Strain

1.2 When a bar does not stretch the same everywhere

Let us hang a steel bar from the ceiling and let it hang. Apart from the ceiling that holds its top end, only its own weight acts on it. The top part of the bar has to carry the weight of the whole bar below it. The bottom end carries almost nothing. So we expect the top centimetres to stretch more than the bottom ones. If we measure the total elongation \(\Delta L\) and divide it by \(L\), we get some average, which does not tell us how the material is stretched at the top and how at the bottom. We want to know how the material is stretched close to each point of the bar, separately for every point.

The hatched lines are the ceiling, the bar hangs from it. Every cross section of the bar carries the weight of the part of the bar below it, so we draw this weight beside the cross section. The grey horizontal line near the top is a cross section. The curly bracket to the left below it shows the part of the bar that the cross section carries, that is, almost the whole bar. The blue horizontal line is its weight, about \(W\). It is longer because the weight is larger. The cross section in the middle carries less, the bracket is therefore shorter, and the blue line is half as long, \(W/2\). The grey triangle joins the ends of such lines: the weight falls evenly from the top to the bottom, because the part of the bar below the cross section gets shorter and shorter.

Figure 1.21 shows the difference. Before loading we draw marks on the bar at equal distances, like the marks on a ruler. If we pull the bar by a force at its lower end, and we neglect its weight (we imagine a bar of the same shape of the same steel, but without weight), every piece between two neighbouring marks is pulled by the same force, and all the gaps grow equally. Under its own weight the top gap grows the most, because the piece at the top carries the whole bar below it, and every gap lower down grows a little less. The lowest piece carries only its own weight and hardly gets longer. The two loaded bars in the figure have the same total elongation, so the same average \(\Delta L / L\), and yet the material in them is stretched very differently. Why they have the same total elongation is explained by the paragraphs after the figure.

Let us draw the bars from left to right. The hatched lines at the top are the ceiling from which all three bars hang. The left bar is before loading, divided into ten pieces. Orange is the state after loading. The middle bar is the same bar, pulled by a force at the end. The arrow points down, because we pull the bar down. This bar is imagined without weight, so that only the effect of the force at the end is seen. Every gap grows by the same amount. The right bar hangs under its own weight. The small orange arrows beside it point down and are equally long, because every piece between two marks has the same weight. The top gap is the largest, the bottom one the smallest. The grey dashed line shows where the lower end was before loading.

Beside each orange bar one piece is cut out on the right. The forces are drawn to scale. We chose the force at the end of the middle bar equal to the average pull in the right bar (the reason is in the paragraphs after the figure): it is half the weight \(W\) of the right bar, because the pull in it falls from \(W\) at the top to zero at the bottom. In the middle bar the filled piece is the third from the top, and the cut-out piece is the same piece with the forces on it. The arrows at the top and bottom are equally long, because nothing else acts on the piece. In the right bar the filled piece is the eighth from the top, and the cut-out piece is the same piece with the forces on it. The upper arrow is the pull of the part of the bar above the piece. This part carries the lower part of the bar and the piece itself, so the arrow is longer than the lower one. The small arrow in the piece is its weight. The difference of the upper and lower arrows is equal to this weight. This is the balance of forces on the piece: the sum of the forces on it is zero, the pull above is equal to the pull below, increased by the weight of the piece.

Why is the pull in the bar pulled at its end (the middle of the figure) the same everywhere, but not in the hanging bar (right)? Let us cut out one piece between two marks in thought, as drawn to the right of each loaded bar. The part of the bar above the piece pulls it up, the part below pulls it down. The piece is at rest, so the forces on it balance (EP (2.5.3)). In the bar pulled at its end nothing else acts on the piece, so the pull from above is exactly as large as the pull from below. This holds for every piece, and the lowest one is pulled by the force at the end, so every piece is pulled by that same force.

In the hanging bar the piece also has its own weight. The pull from above must carry both the pull from below and the weight of the piece, so it is larger by exactly this weight. Going up the bar, the pull grows by the weight of one piece at every mark, from zero at the bottom end to the weight of the whole bar at the top (BARBER (1.33)), as shown by the marks beside the right bar. Let \(W\) be the weight of the whole bar and \(w = W/10\) the weight of one of the ten pieces. The pull at the marks, from the bottom end up, is then

\[0,\ w,\ 2w,\ 3w,\ \dots,\ 10w = W .\]

So the difference lies in where the load acts. The force at the end acts at one place only, the weight acts on every piece of the material. Books call a force that acts on every bit of the material, like weight, a body force (prostorninska sila), and a force that acts on the surface of a body, like the pull at the end, a surface force (površinska sila) (SADD §3.1, PDF p. 63). We will write the balance of forces exactly in chapter 2.

The force at the end of the middle bar is chosen to equal the average of the pull in the hanging bar. The pull in the hanging bar falls evenly from \(W\) at the top to \(0\) at the bottom, so its average is

\[\frac{W + 0}{2} = \frac{W}{2} = F ,\]

half the weight \(W\) of the hanging bar (right). If a piece stretches in proportion to the force that pulls it, as we will see for steel, the total elongation is the sum of the elongations of the pieces, so proportional to the average force, and therefore both bars get longer by the same amount.

Let us write this as a calculation. A piece of length \(\Delta x = L/10\) is pulled by a larger pull at the top than at the bottom. Within the piece the pull changes evenly with depth (from the upper mark to the lower one by the weight \(w\) of one piece), so its value in the middle of the piece equals the average of the pull over the piece, and the piece gets longer as if this average pull pulled it. Let \(F_{\mathrm{i}}\) be the pull in the middle of the \(i\)-th piece, counted from the top. At the upper mark the pull is \((11 - i)\,w\), at the lower one \((10 - i)\,w\), so in the middle \(F_{\mathrm{i}} = (10{,}5 - i)\,w\). The piece gets longer by \(k\,F_{\mathrm{i}}\,\Delta x\), where \(k\) is a constant for this steel and this cross section (a doubled pull gives a doubled elongation). The elongations of the ten pieces add up, and since \(10\,\Delta x = L\):

\[\Delta L = \sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{10} k\,F_{\mathrm{i}}\,\Delta x = k\,\frac{F_1 + F_2 + \dots + F_{10}}{10}\,(10\,\Delta x) = k\,\bar F\,L ,\]

where \(\bar F\) is the average of the pulls. In the hanging bar

\[\bar F = \frac{1}{10} \sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{10} (10{,}5 - i)\,w = \frac{(105 - 55)\,w}{10} = 5\,w = \frac{W}{2}\]

exactly, because \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{10} i = 55\). In the middle bar the pull is \(F = W/2\) everywhere, so also \(\bar F = W/2\). In both loaded bars therefore \(\bar F = W/2\) and the same \(\Delta L\). If we took the pull at the upper or lower mark of the piece, we would get \(5{,}5\,w\) or \(4{,}5\,w\), so only an approximation, and the error would be exactly as much as the pull changes within the piece.

In the figure, the horizontal line is where the lower ends of both loaded bars are, and both measures \(\Delta L\) show that the lower end of each is below the end of the unloaded bar by the same \(\Delta L\).

How do we describe that? We follow the points of the bar (figure 1.22). We label a point by its distance \(x\) from the top end of the bar, measured on the bar before loading, and the axis \(x\) points down. The top of the bar is at \(x = 0\), the bottom end at \(x = L\). When the bar hangs, every point moves down a little. How far the point that was at distance \(x\) moves, we write as \(u(x)\) and call the displacement (pomik) of that point. So the displacement is the difference between the new and the old position of the point, the new position is \(x + u(x)\), and a displacement downward is positive. We always write \(x\) for the position before loading, other books often write it with a capital \(X\) and the new position with a small \(x\). The top of the bar is fixed to the ceiling, so it does not move: \(u(0) = 0\). Points lower down move more, because the elongations of all the parts of the bar above them add up.

The hatched lines at the top are the ceiling that holds the bar. The axis \(x\) points down, because we measure the distance from the ceiling downward. On it, \(0\) is at the ceiling, \(x\) and \(x + \Delta x\) are two chosen points, and \(L\) is the lower end of the bar before loading. The blackwhite bar on the left is the bar before loading. The grey piece in it is the piece between \(x\) and \(x + \Delta x\), the curly bracket shows its length \(\Delta x\). The orange bar on the right is the same bar after loading. It is longer, because it stretches. The orange piece in it is the same piece, the curly bracket shows its new length \(\Delta x + \Delta u\), where \(\Delta u\) is the difference of the two displacements shown by the lower arrows. The grey horizontal lines carry the positions of the points before loading to the bar after loading, and the blue arrow \(u(x)\), \(u(x + \Delta x)\) and \(u(L)\) are the displacements of these points. The lower end of the piece is lower, so \(u(x + \Delta x)\) is larger than \(u(x)\) and the piece is longer by \(\Delta u\) after loading.

Now pick a short piece of the bar between the points \(x\) and \(x + \Delta x\) (figure 1.22). Its initial length is \(\Delta x\). The top end of the piece moves by \(u(x)\), the bottom end by \(u(x + \Delta x)\). If the bottom end moves more than the top end, the piece has become longer, by the difference of the two displacements:

\[\Delta u = u(x + \Delta x) - u(x) .\]

The piece is a bar too, and the formula (1.1) holds for it at any length: its strain is the elongation \(\Delta u\) divided by the length \(\Delta x\). It has to be short because we look for how the material is stretched right next to the point \(x\), not an average over a longer part of the bar. As we said, the number \(\Delta L / L\) tells how the material is stretched next to the point \(x\) only if all parts of the bar stretch the same, otherwise it is only an average. In the whole hanging bar this does not hold, but in a short piece it nearly does. As with the pieces between the marks, the pull at the top end of the piece is larger than the pull at the bottom end by exactly the weight of the piece. Let \(W\) be the weight of the uniformly thick hanging bar, 10 m long, and at the same time the pull at the top. The weight of the piece is \(W\) times the fraction of the bar that the piece takes up:

\[\begin{aligned} \Delta x = 1\ \text{m}: \quad & \frac{W \cdot 1\ \text{m}}{10\ \text{m}} = \frac{W}{10},\\ \Delta x = 1\ \text{cm}: \quad & \frac{W \cdot 0{,}01\ \text{m}}{10\ \text{m}} = \frac{W}{1000}. \end{aligned}\]

So the weight of a piece 1 m long is a tenth of the pull at the top, and of a piece 1 cm long only a thousandth. Almost the same force therefore pulls every part of a short piece, and since steel, as we will see, stretches in proportion to the force, all its parts stretch almost the same as the material at its top end, right next to the point \(x\). The strain of the piece therefore tells almost exactly how the material is stretched right next to the point \(x\):

\[\varepsilon \approx \frac{u(x + \Delta x) - u(x)}{\Delta x} . \tag{1.2}\]

The sign \(\approx\) means "approximately equal". Even within the piece the strain changes a little from top to bottom, so this is still an average, only over a shorter part of the bar. The shorter the piece, the less the strain changes within it, and the better the number describes how the material is stretched right next to the point \(x\). The point itself has no length, so it cannot be stretched. Only the material near it can, and the shorter the piece we take, the closer to the point we look.

Let us try this with numbers. In chapters 2 and 3 we will compute that a steel bar of length \(L = 10\) m, equally thick everywhere and made of one steel, hanging under its own weight, stretches so that the displacement of the point at distance \(x\) from the ceiling is

\[u(x) = a\,(2Lx - x^2), \qquad a = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1} .\]

The constant \(a\) depends only on how heavy steel is and how strongly it resists stretching (BARBER (1.39) with the data for mild steel from BOWER, Table 3.1). The formula for \(u(x)\) comes from the fact that the pull in the bar falls evenly from the top to the bottom (as figure 1.20 shows) and that the displacement is the sum of the elongations of the pieces above the point. We do the derivation later, now we only compute with it. The top of the bar is at \(x = 0\), where \(u(0) = 0\), as it must be. The bottom end moves by \(u(10\ \text{m}) = a \cdot 100\ \text{m}^2 = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-5}\) m, two hundredths of a millimetre. A ten-metre steel bar stretches under its own weight by less than the thickness of a sheet of paper.

Let us draw the graph. The horizontal axis is the distance \(x\) from the ceiling in metres, the marks are \(0\) at the ceiling, \(5\) in the middle and \(10\) at the bottom end of the bar. The vertical axis is the displacement \(u\). It is enlarged, because the displacement is very small, at most \(1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-5}\) m, and the curve would otherwise look like a straight line. The blackwhite curve is the displacement \(u(x)\). It starts at \(0\), because the top does not move, and rises, more and more gently. The blue line touches the curve at the top, at \(x = 0\). The orange line touches it in the middle, at \(x = 5\) m. The grey vertical line joins the point of contact to the axis. Beside each line its slope is written, that is, the number that says by how much the displacement grows per metre along the axis \(x\). This is the number to which the strains of the pieces, which we compute below, will come close. Because the vertical axis is enlarged, the drawn lines are steeper than these numbers say.

Let us compute the strain of the piece (1.2) at the top of the bar (\(x = 0\)) and at the middle (\(x = 5\) m) for shorter and shorter pieces. We compute the first row, the piece at the top with \(\Delta x = 1\) m, as follows:

\[\frac{u(1\ \text{m}) - u(0)}{1\ \text{m}} = \frac{a\,(2 \cdot 10 \cdot 1 - 1^2)\ \text{m}^2 - 0}{1\ \text{m}} = 19\,a\ \text{m} = 3{,}709 \cdot 10^{-6} .\]

We compute the other rows in the same way:

length of the piece \(\Delta x\) at the top at the middle
1 m \(3{,}709 \cdot 10^{-6}\) \(1{,}757 \cdot 10^{-6}\)
0,1 m \(3{,}884 \cdot 10^{-6}\) \(1{,}932 \cdot 10^{-6}\)
0,01 m \(3{,}902 \cdot 10^{-6}\) \(1{,}950 \cdot 10^{-6}\)
0,001 m \(3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-6}\) \(1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6}\)

Let us draw this part by part. First, on the left is the whole bar. The hatched lines are the ceiling, they are the same above every part of the figure. The orange bar is the bar after loading. The distance \(L\) is its length before loading, \(L = 10\) m, drawn as 4 units. The grey dashed line shows the lower end before loading. The bar reaches below the line, because it has got longer. The filled piece at the top is the top metre of the bar. The grey frame around it, the grey line from it and the grey frame around the enlarged piece show that the next part of the figure displays it enlarged.

Each of the four right parts shows the top piece, shorter and shorter, but always enlarged so that it is drawn equally long before loading. Each next part enlarges the upper part of the piece in the previous one, as shown by the frame, the line and the enlarged frame in the second part, in the third and in the fourth. In each part there is the filled piece and the orange outline of the bar around it. The wavy edge at the bottom means that the bar continues below the piece and is cut off here. In each part there is also a grey dashed line, the lower end of the piece before loading. The distance \(\Delta x\) is the length of the piece before loading, the blue distance \(\Delta u\) is its elongation. Below each piece is its elongation divided by its length: for 1 m, for 0.1 m, for 0.01 m and for 0.001 m. So the enlarged pieces look almost the same: the quotients hardly change.

The numbers settle. At the top they approach about \(3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-6}\), at the middle about \(1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6}\), exactly half. The material at the top is stretched twice as much as at the middle, which agrees with our expectation that the top part carries more weight. The same is shown by the graph of the displacement \(u\) (figure 1.23): the curve is steeper at the top than at the middle, and the slopes are exactly the numbers from the table.

Why exactly half? Every cross section of the bar carries the weight of the part of the bar below it (figure 1.20). The cross section at the top carries the whole bar (it is drawn a little below the ceiling, so it carries almost all of it), the one in the middle only the lower half, so half the weight. The bar is equally thick everywhere, and in steel the elongation of a short piece is proportional to the force that pulls it, as long as we do not stretch it beyond the bound of about \(0{,}001\) from section 1.1: half the force gives half the elongation. We will learn this when we connect the force in a bar with its strain, and the formula for \(u(x)\) above is derived from it (BARBER (1.39)). The same reasoning predicts more. The weight carried by a cross section decreases evenly from the top to the bottom, in proportion to the length of the bar below the cross section, and at the bottom end it is zero. The strain should change in the same way. We will confirm this by calculation in section 1.4.

So it seems that every point has its own strain, the number that the strains of shorter and shorter pieces approach. But what exactly does "approach" mean? A table cannot be the answer. It shows only a few rows, and from them we do not know for certain how the numbers behave further on. And we cannot shrink the piece to nothing, because in the formula (1.2) we would get \(0/0\), a division by zero. We need an exact meaning of the words "the number that the ratio approaches as \(\Delta x\) gets smaller and smaller", and a way to compute that number without tables. This is a job for mathematics, and the next section does it.
