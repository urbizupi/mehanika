Chapter 11 The boundary value problem of linear elasticity

11.10 The Airy stress function (Airyjeva napetostna funkcija)

The plane problem of section 11.9 has three unknown stresses, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\). Two equilibrium equations (11.49) hold for them, and the third requirement is the compatibility of the strains, the equation \(S_1 = 0\) of lemma 11.31, which is of second order. Three unknowns and three equations. Can we gather them into one equation for one unknown function, and what does this give for the beams of chapter 5? There we obtained the stresses in a beam from the assumptions that cross-sections stay plane and that the fibres are in uniaxial stress, and the elastic solution we only stated and checked. In this section we derive it. Figure 11.88 shows the three stresses on an element that we will write with one function.

In figure 11.88 there is an element with three stresses, the frame around it shows its equilibrium, and the axes \(x\) and \(y\) give the directions of the stresses. The normal stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) pulls or pushes the left and right faces, the normal stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) the top and bottom, the shear stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) slides along the faces. Each normal stress is the sum of the part from \(\varphi\), a second derivative of the function, and the part \(V\), the potential of the body force \(\vek b\). On the right is the sum of the two normal stresses, on which the compatibility condition rests.

The path is this. First we look for a function that satisfies equilibrium by itself, and from compatibility we get an equation for it. Then we show that every field in equilibrium has such a function, say what the function tells about forces on the boundary, and that the same stress field solves the problem for every material. At the end we use it to derive three solutions for the beam of chapter 5. Everywhere \(R\) is a region in the plane, that is an open connected set (definition 1.136), the body is the prism over it as in section 11.9, and the material is steel with \(E = 196\) GPa and \(\nu = 0{,}3\).

11.10.1 From three stresses to one function

In chapter 4 we wrote from one function \(\varphi\) the stress field (4.83), which is in equilibrium without body force, and used it for virtual fields. Do we get the same way for true stresses? Without body force the same field would suffice. With body force the function must add a part that balances the equilibrium. In figure 11.88 this part is orange, and the blue part is the potential of the force.

For the forces we know this is simple. On figure 11.89 on the left is a body with the axis \(y\) upwards. Weight pulls every piece of it downwards, per unit volume with force \(\vek b = (0, -\rho g)\), the same everywhere. On the right is a ring rotating with angular velocity \(\omega\) about the centre \(O\). Its point at radius \(r\) moves on a circle, so it has an acceleration \(\omega^2 r\) towards the centre. In the system that rotates with the ring this acceleration is replaced by the centrifugal force \(\vek b = \rho\omega^2 (x, y)\) per unit volume, which points away from \(O\) and grows with the radius. Both forces have the form

\[\vek b = -\nabla V , \qquad b_{\mathrm{x}} = -\frac{\partial V}{\partial x} , \quad b_{\mathrm{y}} = -\frac{\partial V}{\partial y} , \tag{11.61}\]

where \(\nabla V\) is the gradient of the function \(V(x, y)\) (chapter 8), an arrow that points where \(V\) grows fastest. The force points in the opposite direction and is perpendicular to the lines on which \(V\) is constant. The function \(V\) is called the potential of the body force (potencial prostorninske sile). For weight \(V = \rho g y\), which is the potential energy of weight per unit volume: to lift a unit volume from height \(0\) to height \(y\) we do the work \(\rho g y\) against the weight \(\rho g\). For the centrifugal force \(V = -\rho\omega^2 (x^2 + y^2)/2\), so its lines of constant \(V\) are circles about \(O\).

Who first wrote the stresses with one function, and what did he overlook?

From history

In 1862 Airy presented to the Royal Society a paper on the bending of beams, published in 1863 (TIMO, PDF p. 197). He treated the rectangular beam as a plane problem, wrote the equilibrium equations and showed that both are satisfied if the stresses are derived from a function by second derivatives (TIMO, PDF p. 198). He took the function as a polynomial and chose its coefficients so that the boundary conditions are satisfied, but he did not take the compatibility equation into account, so his investigation is not complete. Even so, this was the first use of a stress function (TIMO, PDF p. 198). Maxwell corrected the mistake in his paper on reciprocal figures and diagrams of forces: he wrote the differential equation for the function and showed that without body forces it is the same for both plane problems and that the stresses do not depend on the elastic constants of the material (TIMO, PDF p. 292). Michell added when this holds. The stresses do not depend on the moduli of an isotropic plate if there are no body forces and the boundary is simply connected, and for a multiply connected boundary, for example a plate with holes, only if the resultant of forces on each boundary is zero (TIMO, PDF p. 292). The year of Michell's paper differs among the books: 1899 (TIMO, PDF p. 292, and LOVE, PDF p. 110) or 1900 (LOVE, PDF pp. 34 and 154), so we do not claim the year.

We asked whether the three stresses of the plane problem can be written with one function. Airy showed that this is possible for equilibrium, and Maxwell that the function must also satisfy an equation that makes the stresses belong to a displacement field. The next theorem states both requirements. Airy's part is the equilibrium of the element, Maxwell's the sum of the stresses.

Theorem 11.40 Airy stress function of the plane problem (Airyjeva napetostna funkcija ravninskega problema)

Let \(R\) be a region in the plane and let the state be plane strain (definition 11.32) or plane stress (definition 11.36) with a material \(E\), \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\). Let the body force be \(\vek b = -\nabla V\) with a function \(V\) that has continuous second partial derivatives, and let the function \(\varphi(x, y)\) have continuous partial derivatives up to fourth order. Let \(\nabla^4 = \nabla^2\nabla^2\) (definition 11.5), so \(\nabla^4\varphi = \partial^4\varphi/\partial x^4 + 2\,\partial^4\varphi/\partial x^2\partial y^2 + \partial^4\varphi/\partial y^4\). From the functions \(\varphi\) and \(V\) let us write the components

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \frac{\partial^2\varphi}{\partial y^2} + V , \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \frac{\partial^2\varphi}{\partial x^2} + V , \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = -\frac{\partial^2\varphi}{\partial x\,\partial y} . \tag{11.62}\]
\[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \varphi_{,\mathrm{yy}} + V , \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \varphi_{,\mathrm{xx}} + V , \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = -\varphi_{,\mathrm{xy}} . \tag{11.62}\]

Then:

  1. The components (11.62) satisfy the equilibrium equations (11.49). This is the equilibrium of the element.
  2. The components (11.62) satisfy the plane compatibility condition \(S_1 = 0\) (sum of the normal stresses) exactly when
    \[\begin{aligned} &\nabla^4\varphi = -\frac{1 - 2\nu}{1 - \nu}\,\nabla^2 V \\ &\quad \text{(plane strain)} , \\ &\nabla^4\varphi = -(1 - \nu)\,\nabla^2 V \\ &\quad \text{(plane stress)} . \end{aligned} \tag{11.63}\]
  3. Without body force, or if \(\nabla^2 V = 0\), we have \(S_1 = 0\) exactly when
    \[\nabla^4\varphi = 0 . \tag{11.64}\]
    This equation is called the biharmonic equation (biharmonična enačba), and its solutions biharmonic functions.
Proof

We must show that insertion gives equilibrium of the element, and compatibility we get from lemma 11.31, in which we only insert \(\sigma\) from (11.62).

  1. The first equilibrium equation, the sum of forces in direction \(x\), (11.49) with \(b_{\mathrm{x}} = -\partial V/\partial x\):
    \[\frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}}{\partial y} + b_{\mathrm{x}} = \frac{\partial^3\varphi}{\partial x\,\partial y^2} + \frac{\partial V}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial^3\varphi}{\partial y\,\partial x\,\partial y} - \frac{\partial V}{\partial x} = 0 ,\]
    because the two third derivatives of \(\varphi\) are equal by corollary 1.132. The derivatives of the potential cancel.
  2. The second equation, the sum of forces in direction \(y\):
    \[\frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}}{\partial y} + b_{\mathrm{y}} = -\frac{\partial^3\varphi}{\partial x\,\partial x\,\partial y} + \frac{\partial^3\varphi}{\partial y\,\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial V}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial V}{\partial y} = 0 ,\]
    for the same reason.
  3. The components (11.62) have continuous partial derivatives up to second order, because the second derivatives of \(\varphi\) or the function \(V\) are in them and \(\varphi\) is four times continuously differentiable, and \(\vek b = -\nabla V\) has continuous first derivatives. By steps 1 and 2 they satisfy equilibrium, so lemma 11.31 holds: \(E'\,S_1 = \nabla^2\Theta_2 + (1 + \nu')\,\mathrm{div}\,\vek b\) with \(\Theta_2 = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\). This is the sum of the normal stresses.
  4. The sum is \(\Theta_2 = \nabla^2\varphi + 2V\), so \(\nabla^2\Theta_2 = \nabla^4\varphi + 2\,\nabla^2 V\). The divergence of the force is \(\mathrm{div}\,\vek b = -\nabla^2 V\). Hence
    \[E'\,S_1 = \nabla^4\varphi + 2\,\nabla^2 V - (1 + \nu')\,\nabla^2 V = \nabla^4\varphi + (1 - \nu')\,\nabla^2 V .\]
  5. Since \(E' > 0\), \(S_1 = 0\) exactly when \(\nabla^4\varphi = -(1 - \nu')\,\nabla^2 V\). In plane strain \(\nu' = \nu^* = \nu/(1 - \nu)\) (theorem 11.33), so \(1 - \nu' = (1 - 2\nu)/(1 - \nu)\). In plane stress \(\nu' = \nu\) (theorem 11.37), so \(1 - \nu' = 1 - \nu\). This is (11.63). The term with \(V\) is the blue part of the sum.
  6. If \(V\) \(= 0\) or \(\nabla^2 V = 0\), the right side is zero, this is (11.64).

Source: SADD (7.5.1) to (7.5.6) (PDF pp. 158-159), (7.5.5) read from the crop of the theorem eq/SADD/7.5.5.png, BARBER (4.6) (PDF p. 63), CM §7.5.5 (PDF pp. 330-331). The proof with lemma 11.31 is not from the sources, SADD inserts (11.62) into the compatibility equations (7.1.7) and (7.2.7).

The theorem showed that one function and one equation of fourth order suffice. In plane strain and plane stress the equations differ only if \(\nabla^2 V \ne 0\), Airy had point 1 and overlooked point 2: his polynomial should also have satisfied equation (11.64). Point 1 is the equilibrium of the element, point 2 the sum of the stresses.

Let us test it on the hanging bar of chapter 1, this time as a plane strip hanging under its own weight \(\rho g\) per unit volume (axis \(x\) downwards, \(\vek b = \rho g\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), so \(V = -\rho g x\), which is linear, so \(\nabla^2 V = 0\)). From the bar in section 11.4 we know \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \rho g (L - x)\) and that the other components are zero. Which function gives this? The condition \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \partial^2\varphi/\partial x^2 + V = 0\) requires \(\partial^2\varphi/\partial x^2 = \rho g x\), so the term \(\rho g x^3/6\), and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \partial^2\varphi/\partial y^2 + V = \rho g (L - x)\) requires \(\partial^2\varphi/\partial y^2 = \rho g L\), so the term \(\rho g L y^2/2\). The function

\[\varphi = \rho g \left(\frac{x^3}{6} + \frac{L\,y^2}{2}\right)\]

is a polynomial of third degree, so \(\nabla^4\varphi = 0\), as (11.64) requires, and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = -\partial^2\varphi/\partial x\,\partial y = 0\). The stresses from (11.62) are \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \rho g L - \rho g x\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \rho g x - \rho g x = 0\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\), as for the bar. The middle graph draws \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) along the axis \(x\), pointing down, and the right one draws \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) with its zero line. On the middle graph of the figure are the part from the function, \(\partial^2\varphi/\partial y^2 = \rho g L\), which is the same along the whole length, the part \(V = -\rho g x\), which falls with depth, and their sum \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \rho g (L - x)\). At the top \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \rho g L = 0{,}7652\) MPa for \(L = 10\) m. The terms \(V\) in the stresses were necessary: the function \(\varphi\) alone would give \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \rho g x \ne 0\), and only the part \(V = -\rho g x\) cancels it, so that \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0\).

11.10.2 Does every field in equilibrium have a function? (ali ima vsako ravnovesno polje funkcijo)

Theorem 11.40 said what the function gives, but not whether we have one for a given solution at all. If all stresses in equilibrium have one, we can look for the solution among them, without loss. The answer is yes in a simply connected region (definition 1.142), and the proof is theorem 4.115 twice, and then once more. The procedure is shown in figure 11.91.

In figure 11.91 the first panel shows the prism over the region with a closed curve that can be shrunk to a point because the region has no hole. The second panel shows the field \(A\) from the first equilibrium equation, the third the field \(B\) from the second, the fourth the function \(\varphi\) whose slopes are \(B\) and \(A\). Two such functions differ by a plane.

Lemma 11.41 Existence of the Airy function (obstoj Airyjeve funkcije)

Let \(R\) be a connected and simply connected region of the plane and let \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) be functions with continuous first partial derivatives on \(R\) that satisfy the equilibrium equations (11.49) without body force. Then there is a function \(\varphi\) with continuous second partial derivatives on \(R\) such that \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \partial^2\varphi/\partial y^2\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \partial^2\varphi/\partial x^2\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = -\partial^2\varphi/\partial x\,\partial y\). Two such functions differ by \(\alpha + \beta x + \gamma y\) with constants \(\alpha\), \(\beta\), \(\gamma\). In figure 11.91 these are the function \(\varphi\) and the plane that may be added.

Proof

We must show that equilibrium gives twice a function whose first derivative with respect to \(x\) or \(y\) equals the stress components, and that these two derivatives again satisfy the condition for a function. Theorem 4.115 is written for a set in space, so we extend the plane region to a prism.

  1. Let \(D = R \times (0, 1)\). It is open and connected. It is simply connected, which we show by definition 1.142 in three moves. A closed curve in \(D\) is \(s \mapsto (\vek r(s), z(s))\) with continuous \(\vek r\) and \(z\) and equal end points. (a) The curve \(\vek r\) is a closed curve in \(R\), so \(R\) has a continuous contraction \(H_{\mathrm{R}}(s, t)\) with \(H_{\mathrm{R}}(s, 0) = \vek r(s)\) and \(H_{\mathrm{R}}(s, 1)\) the same for all \(s\). (b) For \(z\) take \(Z(s, t) = (1 - t)\,z(s) + t/2\), which is continuous, has \(Z(s, 0) = z(s)\) and \(Z(s, 1) = 1/2\) for all \(s\), and lies between \(z(s)\) and \(1/2\), hence in \((0, 1)\). (c) The map \((H_{\mathrm{R}}, Z)\) is continuous, lies in \(D\), has a constant value at \(t = 1\), and equal end points for every \(t\) because \(H_{\mathrm{R}}\) and \(Z\) have them. So \(D\) is not only connected but simply connected. The functions that we shall insert into the theorem do not depend on \(z\). In the figure this is the prism and the shrinking of the curve.
  2. The first equilibrium equation says \(\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/\partial x = \partial(-\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}})/\partial y\). This is the condition (4.75) for the field \(\vek w = (w_{\mathrm{x}}, w_{\mathrm{y}}, w_{\mathrm{z}}) = (-\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}, 0)\): \(\partial w_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial y = -\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}/\partial y = \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/\partial x = \partial w_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial x\), the other pairs contain a derivative with respect to \(z\) or \(w_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\). By theorem 4.115 there is \(A\) on \(D\) with \(\partial A/\partial x = -\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\), \(\partial A/\partial y = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and \(\partial A/\partial z = 0\). The last derivative is zero on the prism, so \(A\) does not depend on \(z\). This is the field \(A\).
  3. The second equation says \(\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}/\partial y = \partial(-\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}})/\partial x\). For the field \((\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}, -\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}, 0)\) the condition (4.75) is satisfied in the same way, so there is \(B(x, y)\) with \(\partial B/\partial x = \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) and \(\partial B/\partial y = -\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\). This is the field \(B\).
  4. We have \(\partial A/\partial x = -\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \partial B/\partial y\). This is the condition (4.75) for the field \((B, A, 0)\), so there is \(\varphi(x, y)\) with \(\partial\varphi/\partial x = B\) and \(\partial\varphi/\partial y = A\). This is the function \(\varphi\).
  5. Then \(\partial^2\varphi/\partial y^2 = \partial A/\partial y = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\partial^2\varphi/\partial x^2 = \partial B/\partial x = \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) and \(\partial^2\varphi/\partial x\,\partial y = \partial A/\partial x = -\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\). These derivatives are continuous, because the \(\sigma\) are continuous. The second derivatives are the curvatures of the surface \(\varphi\).
  6. If \(\varphi_1\) and \(\varphi_2\) are two such functions, the difference \(\psi\) has all three second derivatives zero. So \(\partial\psi/\partial x\) and \(\partial\psi/\partial y\) have zero derivatives with respect to both variables, and on a connected set they are constant, \(\beta\) and \(\gamma\). The function \(\psi - \beta x - \gamma y\) has zero gradient and is constant, \(\alpha\). The difference is a plane.

Source: BARBER §4.3 (PDF pp. 62-63) shows the same construction, with integration once with respect to \(x\) and once with respect to \(y\), without a condition on the region. The proof with theorem 4.115 and the condition of simple connectedness are not from the source.

The lemma says that for a simply connected body we lose nothing. For a region with a hole we do not know this: lemma 4.121 and the ring of chapter 4 showed that there the function may not be single-valued, and the displacement may have a jump. A solution with a hole (the tube) we already have, since we constructed it in section 11.7, and there we do not need the Airy function.

That simple connectivity in the lemma is really needed is shown by the annulus with inner radius \(40\) mm and outer radius \(60\) mm. Take the field that has \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = c/r^2\) in polar coordinates and the other components zero. On the outer edge the surroundings pull the annulus counterclockwise, on the inner edge in the opposite direction, and since \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}\) falls as \(1/r^2\), the arrows on the inner edge are \((60/40)^2 = 2{,}25\) times longer. So the annulus passes a moment from the shaft in the hole to the outer ring, like the hub of a wheel that the shaft turns. In Cartesian coordinates the field is

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -\frac{2c\,xy}{r^4} , \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \frac{2c\,xy}{r^4} , \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \frac{c\,(x^2 - y^2)}{r^4} .\]

Since \(r \ge 40\) mm, they have continuous first derivatives, and SymPy gives zero for the left side of both equilibrium equations without body force. The annulus is connected, so only simple connectivity fails. The function \(\varphi = c\,\theta\) with the angle \(\theta\) around the centre has \(\partial\varphi/\partial x = -c\,y/r^2\) and \(\partial\varphi/\partial y = c\,x/r^2\), hence \(\partial^2\varphi/\partial y^2 = -2c\,xy/r^4 = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\partial^2\varphi/\partial x^2 = 2c\,xy/r^4 = \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) and \(-\partial^2\varphi/\partial x\,\partial y = c\,(x^2 - y^2)/r^4 = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\). A small closed curve that does not encircle the hole brings \(\varphi\) back to its starting value, so there the function is unique. Locally this is an Airy function, but it is not unique: when we take the point \(P\) along the path around the hole from \(S\) back to \(S\), its angle \(\theta\) grows by \(2\pi\), and the graph of the function \(\varphi = c\,\theta\) shows that we return to the same point with a value that is larger by \(2\pi c\). No unique function \(\tilde\varphi\) gives these stresses. If we had one, the difference \(\psi = \tilde\varphi - c\,\theta\) would have all second derivatives zero on every part of the annulus where we choose a branch of the angle. The gradient of \(\psi\) would then be constant, \((\beta, \gamma)\), and \(\tilde\varphi - c\,\theta - \beta x - \gamma y\) would be constant along every path. After going around the hole \(\tilde\varphi\), \(x\) and \(y\) return to their earlier values, but \(c\,\theta\) does not, since the jump is \(2\pi c \ne 0\). The moment of the forces on the outer edge and on every circle \(r = \text{const.}\) is \(\int_0^{2\pi} r\,\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}\,r\,\mathrm{d}\theta = 2\pi c \ne 0\) per unit thickness.

11.10.3 Forces on the boundary from the function (sile na robu iz funkcije)

What does the function tell on the boundary of the region? There forces are prescribed, and the stresses are second derivatives of the function. Let us show that first derivatives suffice. On the boundary of the body \(R\) we choose an arc from the point \(P\) to the point \(Q\), on which forces of the exterior on the body act (figure 11.93). We walk along the arc in the direction in which the body is on the left. Then the outward normal \(\vek n\) is on the right of the unit tangent vector \(\vek t\).

Lemma 11.42 Boundary forces from the Airy function (sile na robu iz Airyjeve funkcije)

Let \(\varphi\) be a function with continuous second derivatives in a neighbourhood of an arc \(K\) of the boundary of the region \(R\), and let the stresses be (11.62) with \(V = 0\). Let the arc \(K\) be given by \(\big(x(s), y(s)\big)\) for \(s_{\mathrm{P}} \le s \le s_{\mathrm{Q}}\), where \(s\) is the arc length, \(x\) and \(y\) are continuously differentiable, the tangent vector is \(\vek t = (x', y')\), and let the body lie on the left of the direction of increasing \(s\), so the outward normal is \(\vek n = (y', -x')\). Then:

  1. The vector of force per unit length, \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n\), is \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\Big(\dfrac{\partial\varphi}{\partial y},\ -\dfrac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x}\Big)\), with derivatives along the arc. These are the forces on the arc.
  2. The resultant of forces \(\vek F\) on the arc from \(P\) to \(Q\) and its moment \(M_{\mathrm{O}}\) about the origin (the \(z\) component of the vector \(\int \overrightarrow{OX}\times\ten\sigma\vek n\,\mathrm{d}s\)) are
    \[\vek F = \Big(\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial y},\ -\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x}\Big)\Big|_{\mathrm{P}}^Q , \qquad M_{\mathrm{O}} = \Big(\varphi - x\,\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x} - y\,\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial y}\Big)\Big|_{\mathrm{P}}^Q , \tag{11.65}\]
    where \(\big|_{\mathrm{P}}^Q\) means the value at \(Q\) minus the value at \(P\).
  3. If the arc is free, \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \vek 0\), then \(\partial\varphi/\partial x\) and \(\partial\varphi/\partial y\) are constant along the arc, \(\beta\) and \(\gamma\), and \(\varphi = \alpha + \beta x + \gamma y\) on the arc with a constant \(\alpha\). Then \(\vek w(P)\) and \(\vek w(Q)\) are equal.
Proof

We must show that the components of the force are written as derivatives with respect to \(s\), from this we get the integrals by the fundamental theorem of calculus, and for the moment we use the rule for the derivative of a product.

  1. The components of force per unit length with normal \(\vek n = (y', -x')\) are \((\sigma\,\vek n)_{\mathrm{x}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,y' - \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,x' = \dfrac{\partial^2\varphi}{\partial y^2}\,y' + \dfrac{\partial^2\varphi}{\partial x\,\partial y}\,x'\) and \((\sigma\,\vek n)_{\mathrm{y}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,y' - \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\,x' = -\dfrac{\partial^2\varphi}{\partial x\,\partial y}\,y' - \dfrac{\partial^2\varphi}{\partial x^2}\,x'\).
  2. By the chain rule (lemma 4.43) the derivative of the function \(\partial\varphi/\partial y\) along the arc is \(\dfrac{\partial^2\varphi}{\partial x\,\partial y}\,x' + \dfrac{\partial^2\varphi}{\partial y^2}\,y'\). This is the first component from step 1. In the same way the derivative of the function \(-\partial\varphi/\partial x\) along the arc is \(-\dfrac{\partial^2\varphi}{\partial x^2}\,x' - \dfrac{\partial^2\varphi}{\partial x\,\partial y}\,y'\), the second component. This is point 1. Along the arc this is the derivative with respect to \(s\).
  3. The integral of a derivative with respect to \(s\) from \(s_{\mathrm{P}}\) to \(s_{\mathrm{Q}}\) is the difference of values (theorem 3.55), the value at \(Q\) minus the value at \(P\), which gives the force \(\vek F\) in (11.65).
  4. The moment of the force \(\ten\sigma\vek n\) about the origin is by step 2 \(x\,(\sigma\vek n)_{\mathrm{y}} - y\,(\sigma\vek n)_{\mathrm{x}} = -x\,\mathrm{d}\varphi_{\mathrm{x}}/\mathrm{d}s - y\,\mathrm{d}\varphi_{\mathrm{y}}/\mathrm{d}s\), where \(\varphi_{\mathrm{x}} = \partial\varphi/\partial x\), \(\varphi_{\mathrm{y}} = \partial\varphi/\partial y\). The derivative of the function \(x\varphi_{\mathrm{x}} + y\varphi_{\mathrm{y}} - \varphi\) along the arc is by the product rule and the chain rule \(\mathrm{d}\varphi/\mathrm{d}s = \varphi_{\mathrm{x}} x' + \varphi_{\mathrm{y}} y'\)
    \[x'\varphi_{\mathrm{x}} + x\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\varphi_{\mathrm{x}}}{\mathrm{d}s} + y'\varphi_{\mathrm{y}} + y\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\varphi_{\mathrm{y}}}{\mathrm{d}s} - \varphi_{\mathrm{x}} x' - \varphi_{\mathrm{y}} y' = x\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\varphi_{\mathrm{x}}}{\mathrm{d}s} + y\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\varphi_{\mathrm{y}}}{\mathrm{d}s} .\]
    The moment is therefore the derivative of the function \(-(x\varphi_{\mathrm{x}} + y\varphi_{\mathrm{y}} - \varphi)\), and the integral with respect to \(s\) gives (11.65).
  5. On a free arc, by step 2, the derivatives \(\partial\varphi/\partial y\) and \(-\partial\varphi/\partial x\) along the arc are zero, so constant, \(\gamma\) and \(-\beta\). The function \(\varphi - \beta x - \gamma y\) has along the arc the derivative \(\varphi_{\mathrm{x}} x' + \varphi_{\mathrm{y}} y' - \beta x' - \gamma y' = 0\), so it is constant. This is point 3. On a free arc there are no forces.

Source: SADD §7.5 (PDF p. 159) says that the condition for forces on the boundary is written with first derivatives of the function (section 11.5 of the book, which we have not read). The derivation is ours.

We tested the sign on a polynomial of fourth degree on a part of a circle, where the lemma gives the same number as direct integration of the forces (SymPy, script anf.py). The lemma says that the force and moment on an arc are differences of the values of the function and its derivatives at the end points. On the right of figure 11.93 are \(\vek w(P)\) and \(\vek w(Q)\), the values of the vector \(\vek w = (\partial\varphi/\partial y, -\partial\varphi/\partial x)\) at the end points of the arc, drawn from the same point. Their difference \(\vek F\) is the resultant of all forces on the arc. The values of the function between the end points do not change the resultant. On a free arc the function is just affine, and an affine function has zero stresses.

The lemma gives a second view of why the function alone satisfies equilibrium. Cut out of the body a piece bounded by a small closed curve and go around it from a point back to the same point. Then \(Q = P\), the difference in (11.65) is zero, and the forces on the cut piece have resultant \(\vek F = \vek 0\) and moment \(M_{\mathrm{O}} = 0\). Every cut piece is therefore in equilibrium, which is the equilibrium (11.49) stated with forces on a piece instead of with derivatives at a point. On the path around the hole of the annulus, however, \(\varphi = c\,\theta\), if we continue the angle \(\theta\) continuously along the path, does not return to the same value but jumps by \(2\pi c\). The derivatives \(\partial\varphi/\partial x = -c\,y/r^2\) and \(\partial\varphi/\partial y = c\,x/r^2\) do return, so \(\vek F = \vek 0\). The sum \(x\,\partial\varphi/\partial x + y\,\partial\varphi/\partial y\) is zero everywhere, and the lemma gives \(M_{\mathrm{O}} = 2\pi c\), the same moment as we got with the integral. A unique function would give \(\vek F = \vek 0\) and \(M_{\mathrm{O}} = 0\) on every closed curve, also around the hole. The annulus, however, passes the moment \(2\pi c\) from the inner edge to the outer one, so it has no unique function.

11.10.4 Independence of the constants (neodvisnost od konstant)

Equation (11.64) does not contain \(E\) and \(\nu\), and neither do the forces on the boundary by lemma 11.42. Are the stresses therefore really independent of the material? For the steel tube and the thin ring of section 11.9 the stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) were the same for different \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\), which points to just this.

What independence would mean is shown by figure 11.94. Take a plate with a notch of material \(E_1\), \(\nu_1\) and pull it at the ends with forces \(\bar{\vek t}\). The stress is not the same everywhere: in a piece far from the notch it is smaller than in a piece just below the notch, where the section is narrower and the same forces must pass through a smaller area. The plate elongates by the blue distance. Now take the same plate of material \(E_2\), \(\nu_2\), for example of aluminium with \(E_2 = 74\) GPa, and pull it with the same forces. It elongates more, at equal \(\nu\) by a distance \(196/74 = 2{,}65\) times larger, because the strain at the same stress is inversely proportional to the modulus. The corollary below says that the stresses in both pieces are the same as in the first plate.

Corollary 11.43 Independence of the stresses from the elastic constants (neodvisnost napetosti od elastičnih konstant)

Let \(R\) be a simply connected region, without body force, and let \(\ten\sigma\) be the stress field of a solution of the plane problem (in plane strain or plane stress) for a material \(E_1\), \(\nu_1\), with continuous second derivatives of the stresses and with force per unit length \(\bar{\vek t} = \ten\sigma\,\vek n\) on the lateral surface of the prism over \(R\). Let \(E_2\), \(\nu_2\) be an arbitrary material with \(-1 < \nu_2 < \tfrac12\). Then there is a plane strain state (definition 11.32) of the material \(E_2\), \(\nu_2\) on this prism, whose stresses are \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) of the field \(\ten\sigma\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = \nu_2\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}})\), which satisfies the equilibrium equations and has the same force \(\bar{\vek t}\) on the lateral surface. The same stress field in the plane therefore solves the problem for every material. In the figure these are the plate of the first and of the second material under the same forces.

Proof

We must show that the same stress field belongs to a displacement of the material \(E_2\), \(\nu_2\). From the stresses we find a function, from it the strains of the second material, and the displacement that belongs to them. The same stresses in the element belong to the second material.

  1. For the first solution let \(E_1'\), \(\nu_1'\) be the constants of its plane system (11.50) (\(E_1^*\), \(\nu_1^*\) in plane strain, \(E_1\), \(\nu_1\) in plane stress). For the second material let \(E_2' = E_2^*\), \(\nu_2' = \nu_2^*\) (theorem 11.33, point 3). Since \(-1 < \nu_2 < \tfrac12\), we have \(-\tfrac12 < \nu_2' < 1\) and \(E_2' > 0\). The constants differ for the first and the second material.
  2. By lemma 11.41 the field \(\ten\sigma\) has a function \(\varphi\) with stresses (11.62) and \(V = 0\). The second derivatives of the function \(\varphi\) are components of stress, which have continuous second derivatives, so \(\varphi\) has continuous derivatives up to fourth order. The first solution satisfies \(S_1 = 0\) (theorems 11.33, point 5, and 11.37, point 3, with \(\vek b = \vek 0\)), so \(\nabla^2\Theta_2 = 0\), and since \(\Theta_2 = \nabla^2\varphi\), we have \(\nabla^4\varphi = 0\). The function belongs to the same stress field in both.
  3. Hooke's law (11.50) with the constants \(E_2'\), \(\nu_2'\) gives from \(\ten\sigma\) the strains \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\), which by lemma 11.31 satisfy \(E_2'\,S_1 = \nabla^2\Theta_2 = \nabla^4\varphi = 0\). Together with \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\) they satisfy all six equations (1.76) (five are zero, as in step 5 of the proof of theorem 11.33). The prism \(D = R \times (0, 1)\) is simply connected (step 1 of the proof of lemma 11.41), so by theorem 4.120 there is a displacement \(\vek u\) with these strains.
  4. The displacement \(\vek u\) is plane up to a rigid motion. From \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) we have \(u_{\mathrm{z}} = w(x, y)\), from \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = 0\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\) we have \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial z = -\partial w/\partial x\) and \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial z = -\partial w/\partial y\). Since \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\) do not depend on \(z\), the derivatives \(\partial^2 w/\partial x^2\), \(\partial^2 w/\partial y^2\) and \(\partial^2 w/\partial x\,\partial y\) are zero, so \(w = a + bx + cy\) with constants. The field \((-bz, -cz, a + bx + cy)\) has zero strains and is a rigid motion. If we subtract it, there remains \((\tilde u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y), \tilde u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y), 0)\) with the same strains. The displacement of the second material is the elongation of the plate under the same forces.
  5. The field \(\tilde{\vek u}\) is a plane strain state of the material \(E_2\), \(\nu_2\). The strains from step 3 are (11.50) for the stresses \(\ten\sigma\), and the three-dimensional Hooke's law of the material \(E_2\), \(\nu_2\) gives from these strains stresses \(\ten\sigma'\), for which by theorem 11.33, point 3, the same relation (11.50) between strains and stresses in the plane holds. The relation is invertible: for the normal components the determinant is \((1 - \nu_2'^2)/E_2'^2 \ne 0\), and the shear factor \((1 + \nu_2')/E_2'\) is not zero, since \(-\tfrac12 < \nu_2' < 1\). So \(\ten\sigma' = \ten\sigma\) in the plane. By point 2 of the same theorem \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = \nu_2\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}})\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\).
  6. The equilibrium equations are satisfied by theorem 11.40, point 1, because the stresses do not depend on \(z\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\). On the lateral surface \(n_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\), so the force is \(\ten\sigma'\vek n = (\ten\sigma\,\vek n, 0) = \bar{\vek t}\), the same as for the first material.

Source: TIMO (PDF p. 292, read), SADD §7.5 (PDF p. 159), CM §7.5.5 (PDF p. 330). The sources claim that the stresses are independent of the constants and give no proof. The proof is ours and shows only that the same stress field solves the problem for every material. We do not prove uniqueness of the stresses of the plane problem.

The corollary showed that in a simply connected region without body forces the same stress field belongs to a solution for every material, and the displacements depend on the moduli. That this field is the only stress field of the problem we do not prove here. Theorem 11.4 holds for bodies of definition 11.1, in which the displacement is prescribed on whole pieces of the surface, whereas plane strain on a prism requires smooth walls at the ends (\(u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\)), that is different conditions by components. SADD and TIMO state the equality of stresses as a consequence of equation (11.64). For a multiply connected region Michell requires that the resultant of forces on the boundary of every hole is zero (TIMO, PDF p. 292). This holds for the tube, where the resultant of the pressure in the bore is zero by symmetry, and it agrees with the Lamé stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\), which do not contain \(E\) and \(\nu\). We only state this condition, we have no proof. For the cantilever, which we treat later in this section, we shall see the corollary in numbers: the stresses of the field do not contain \(\nu\), and the displacement of the free end depends on the material and the clamp. In the figure these are the same stress field and the different elongation.

11.10.5 Beams from polynomials (nosilci iz polinomov)

We know what the function tells and how to find a solution: we choose \(\varphi\) from polynomials that satisfy the biharmonic equation (11.64), and determine the constants from boundary conditions. We have already seen the method for the hanging bar as the semi-inverse method (section 11.4): part of the field is prescribed, the rest is determined. Here we prescribe the form of the function. A polynomial of second degree gives constant stresses. Third degree gives stresses linear in \(y\). Let us see whether this gives exact pure bending.

11.10.6 Pure bending (čisti upogib)

Let a rectangular beam \(-l \le x \le l\), \(-c \le y \le c\), of thickness \(b\), be loaded at the ends by a couple with moment \(M\) (the beam convention of chapter 5: \(M = -b\int y\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}y\)). The faces \(y = \pm c\) are free. In section 5.3.1 we said for pure bending that the elementary stress is exact, and referred to the elastic solution of SADD (example 8.2). Now we derive it ourselves. Figure 11.95 shows the beam, the couples at the ends and the free faces.

The upper part of figure 11.95 shows the beam with its axes, loaded by a couple \(M\) at both ends, and the free faces \(y = \pm c\). The lower part shows what the derivation will prove: the beam bends, the sections stay straight, the fibres are longer at the top and shorter at the bottom, and the unknown rigid motion is fixed by the supports.

Why \(\varphi = \gamma y^3\)? At the top of figure 11.96 is the graph of this function at constant \(x\), with the axes \(y\) and \(\varphi\) and the limits of the cross-section \(-c\) and \(c\). The stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \partial^2\varphi/\partial y^2\) is the second derivative of the graph. It is shown by a parabola that touches the graph with the same slope and the same second derivative. At \(y = c/2\) this parabola is curved upwards, the second derivative is positive. At \(y = -c/2\) the parabola is curved downwards, the second derivative is negative. At the bottom is the cross-section with the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 6\gamma y\) on it, linear in \(y\): above the axis tension, below it compression. Such a linear stress is the stress of pure bending of equation (5.25). Let us derive it.

Derivation 11.44 Pure bending of a rectangular beam from the Airy function (čisti upogib pravokotnega nosilca iz Airyjeve funkcije)

Hint

Derivation 11.46 Displacements of the cantilever and two idealized clamps (pomiki konzole in dva načina idealiziranega vpetja)

Goal: to write the displacement field \((u, v)\) of the cantilever from the stresses of derivation 11.45 and to show how the clamp determines the rigid motion and what the term \((2 + \nu)\) gives. Plane stress, \(G = E/(2(1 + \nu))\), \(h = 2c\). In figure 11.100 these are the two kinds of clamp.

1
\[\begin{gathered} u = \frac{F\,(Lx - x^2/2)\,y}{EI} + \frac{(2 + \nu)\,F\,y^3}{6EI} - \Big(k + \frac{Fc^2}{2GI}\Big)\,y + u_0 , \\ v = -\frac{\nu F\,(L - x)\,y^2}{2EI} - \frac{F\,(Lx^2/2 - x^3/6)}{EI} + k x + v_0 . \end{gathered}\]
Hint

The stresses are the same as in derivation 5.41 of chapter 5, so the same are Hooke's law (11.50) with \(E' = E\), \(\nu' = \nu\), the same strains \(\partial u/\partial x = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/E\), \(\partial v/\partial y = -\nu\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/E\), \(\partial u/\partial y + \partial v/\partial x = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}/G\) and the same separation of variables: the term \(y^2\) in \(f'(y)\) has the contributions of shear, \(1/G = 2(1 + \nu)/E\), and of the Poisson effect, \(-\nu/E\), which together give \((2 + \nu)/E\). There \(h^2/4 = c^2\), \((1 + \nu)Fh^2/(4EI) = Fc^2/(2GI)\), and the constants \(c_0\), \(c_1\) are here \(k\), \(u_0\). The field is therefore the same as there, what is new is that the stresses now come from a function with \(\nabla^4\varphi = 0\), so the strains are compatible. That the given \(u\), \(v\) satisfy the three strain equations we checked with SymPy (script anf.py). The constants \(u_0\), \(v_0\), \(k\) are a rigid translation and rotation, which the clamp determines. The axis bends into a curve.

2
\[\begin{gathered} \text{(i)}\ \ u = v = 0\ \text{and}\ \frac{\partial v}{\partial x} = 0\ \text{at}\ (0, 0):\ \ u_0 = v_0 = k = 0 . \\ \text{(ii)}\ \ u = v = 0\ \text{and}\ \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = 0\ \text{at}\ (0, 0):\ \ u_0 = v_0 = 0 ,\ \ k = -\frac{Fc^2}{2GI} . \end{gathered}\]
Hint

The point \((0, 0)\) is the middle of the clamp. In (i) the horizontal element of the axis does not rotate there, in (ii) the vertical element does not rotate. At \((0, 0)\) we have \(\partial u/\partial y = -(k + Fc^2/(2GI))\), because the first and second term in \(u\) have there a factor \(x\) or \(y^2\). These are the horizontal and the vertical element at the point \((0, 0)\).

3
\[\begin{gathered} \text{(i)}\ \ v(L, 0) = -\frac{F L^3}{3EI} = -7{,}9719\ \text{mm} , \\ \text{(ii)}\ \ v(L, 0) = -\frac{F L^3}{3EI} - \frac{(1 + \nu)\,F c^2 L}{EI} = -7{,}9844\ \text{mm} . \end{gathered}\]
Hint

At \(x = L\), \(y = 0\) we insert into \(v\). In (ii) \(kL = -Fc^2L/(2GI) = -(1 + \nu)Fc^2L/(EI)\). In (i) \(v(x, 0) = -Fx^2(3L - x)/(6EI)\), which is the deflection curve of the cantilever from theorem 5.57. These are the free ends of the axis.

Source: BARBER §9.1.1 (PDF p. 139), EP example 2.5.1 (pp. 78-80), SADD example 8.3 for the same procedure (PDF pp. 176-177). We checked the expressions for \(u\) and \(v\) and the numbers with SymPy (script anf.py).

The difference between (i) and (ii) is \(0{,}0124\) mm, the distance between the ends in figure 11.100. Its cause is one quantity, shown on the left of figure 11.101. The shear strain in the middle of the clamp is \(\gamma_0 = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}(0, 0)/G = -0{,}9375\ \text{MPa}/75\,385\ \text{MPa} = -1{,}2436\cdot 10^{-5}\), so the right angle between the horizontal and the vertical element there grows by \(|\gamma_0|\), since \(\gamma_0\) is negative. In (i) the vertical element turns, in (ii) the horizontal one and with it the whole axis, so the free end moves by \(L\) times this angle, \(0{,}01244\) mm. This is the difference between the rotation of the horizontal and the rotation of the vertical element at the same point. The Euler-Bernoulli model with plane cross-sections that stay perpendicular to the axis does not distinguish these two, since the shear strain in it is zero. The Timoshenko model of section 5.13.4 gives a shear part of \(0{,}00995\) mm (\(k = 5/6\)), so the deflection is \(7{,}9719 + 0{,}00995 = 7{,}9819\) mm (rounded), which is between the two. The three idealized clamps thus differ by \(0{,}16\ \%\) of the deflection, and the stresses are the same in all and do not contain \(\nu\) (corollary 11.43). The displacement depends on \(\nu\): it appears in \((1 + \nu)\) in (ii) and in the term \((2 + \nu)\). In (i) the clamp is also not a wall: the face \(x = 0\) moves by \(0{,}175\ \mu\)m in the direction \(x\) and \(1{,}435\ \mu\)m in the direction \(y\) at the edge \(y = c\) (SymPy), which is \(0{,}018\ \%\) of the deflection. A real wall would hold the whole face, while in (i) only the point \((0, 0)\) and the horizontal element at it stay still.

What does the term \((2 + \nu)F y^3/(6EI)\) mean? At a given \(x\) the displacement \(u\) has a term cubic in \(y\), so the points of the cross-section after loading no longer lie on a line (figure 11.102). The grey dashed line is the cross-section as it would be without this term, the orange curve is the true cross-section between the edges \(y = -c\) and \(y = c\). The axis \(x\) points to the right, the axis \(y\) upwards. At \(y = c = 20\) mm this term is \(w(c) = 0{,}0733\ \mu\)m, enlarged \(10^5\) times in the figure. The rotation of the cross-section at the free end moves the same edge by \(239\ \mu\)m (section 5.13.4), so the curvature of the cross-section is \(0{,}03\ \%\) of this displacement. The cross-section is therefore indeed almost plane, as chapter 5 assumed, but not exactly. We derived the term \((2 + \nu)\) in chapter 5 from the stresses of the beam (derivation 5.41), and here we know that these stresses come from a function that satisfies compatibility.

11.10.8 The simply supported beam (prostoležeči nosilec)

In chapter 5 (derivation 5.104) the simply supported beam with a uniform load had stresses from SADD (example 8.3), which we only checked. SADD does not justify the function \(\varphi\) ("a choice that has come from previous trial and error", SADD, PDF p. 173). Let us justify it.

Let the beam be \(-l \le x \le l\), \(-c \le y \le c\), with a pressure \(p\) (force per unit area) on the upper face, the lower face free, and at the ends the resultant \(N = 0\), \(M = 0\) and the shear force that equilibrium gives. As chapter 5 does, we compute with thickness 1, so \(p\) is a force per unit length. For \(q_0 = 1\) kN/m and thickness \(b = 20\) mm we have \(p = q_0/b = 50\) kPa and \(l = 1\) m, \(c = 20\) mm. In figure 11.103 these are the pressure, the supports and the ends.

In figure 11.103 there is the beam with a uniform pressure on the upper face, a free lower face, two supports that each take \(p\,l\), and ends where \(N\) and \(M\) are zero. The correction of the elementary stress at mid-span will come at the end.

Figure 11.104 shows what the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) across the depth must be. The profile starts at zero on the free lower face and ends at \(-p\) on the upper one, where the pressure \(p\) pushes on the beam. On both faces it stands vertical, because there is no shear stress there: the second equation (2.38) without body force and with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zy}} = 0\) is \(\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}/\partial x + \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}/\partial y = 0\), and on a face where \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\) at every \(x\) we have \(\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}/\partial x = 0\), so also \(\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}/\partial y = 0\). We split the profile into two parts. The mean of the values on the faces, \((0 - p)/2 = -p/2\), is the constant part. What remains is the odd part, \(-p/2\) at the top and \(p/2\) at the bottom, so it cancels in the sum of the values on the two faces. Step 4 of the derivation determines these two parts.

Derivation 11.47 Simply supported beam with a uniform load from the Airy function (prostoležeči nosilec z enakomerno obtežbo iz Airyjeve funkcije)

Goal: to find \(\varphi\) that satisfies the conditions on the faces \(y = \pm c\) and at the ends in the resultant, and to show that the stresses equal those of derivation 5.104. Plane stress, no body force, thickness 1, \(I = 2c^3/3\). In figure 11.103 these are the conditions on the faces and at the ends.

1
\[\varphi = A_{20}\,x^2 + A_{21}\,x^2 y + A_{03}\,y^3 + A_{23}\,x^2 y^3 + A_{05}\,y^5 .\]
Hint

Elementary theory gives \(M \propto l^2 - x^2\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} \propto (l^2 - x^2)\,y\), so \(\varphi\) must contain \(x^2 y^3\) (\(\partial^2/\partial y^2\) gives \(6x^2 y\)). This term gives \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} \propto y^3\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} \propto x y^2\). The stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) must be \(-p\) at \(y = c\) and zero at \(y = -c\), which with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = 2(A_{20} + A_{21} y + A_{23} y^3)\) requires three constants, hence \(A_{20} x^2\) and \(A_{21} x^2 y\). The term \(A_{03} y^3\) balances the moment at the ends. The load and supports are symmetric with respect to \(x = 0\), so only even powers of \(x\) appear. The term \(y^5\) follows in step 2 (SADD (8.1.25), PDF p. 173). The form follows from the elementary beam.

2
\[\nabla^4\varphi = 24\,A_{23}\,y + 120\,A_{05}\,y = 0 \ \Longrightarrow\ A_{05} = -\frac{A_{23}}{5} .\]
Hint

Equation (11.64): \(\nabla^4(x^2y^3) = 2\,\partial^4(x^2y^3)/\partial x^2\partial y^2 = 2\cdot 2\cdot 6y = 24y\) and \(\nabla^4 y^5 = 120y\), all other terms have the fourth derivative zero.

3
\[\begin{gathered} \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 2y\,(3A_{03} + 3A_{23}x^2 - 2A_{23}y^2) , \\ \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = 2\,(A_{20} + A_{21}y + A_{23}y^3) , \\ \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = -2x\,(A_{21} + 3A_{23}y^2) . \end{gathered}\]
Hint

Theorem 11.40, point 1, with \(\varphi\) from step 1 and \(A_{05}\) from step 2.

4
\[\begin{gathered} A_{21} + 3A_{23}c^2 = 0 , \quad 2\,(A_{20} + A_{21}c + A_{23}c^3) = -p , \\ 2\,(A_{20} - A_{21}c - A_{23}c^3) = 0 \\ \Longrightarrow\ A_{20} = -\frac{p}{4} , \quad A_{21} = -\frac{3p}{8c} , \quad A_{23} = \frac{p}{8c^3} . \end{gathered}\]
Hint

The faces \(y = \pm c\): \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\) is the same condition on both (the profile of \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) is vertical there), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}(c) = -p\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}(-c) = 0\). The sum of the last two gives \(A_{20} = -p/4\), so the mean \(2A_{20} = -p/2\), the difference \(A_{21}c + A_{23}c^3 = -p/4\), so half the odd part at the top, and with \(A_{21} = -3A_{23}c^2\) we have \(-2A_{23}c^3 = -p/4\). These are the loaded upper and the free lower face.

5
\[\int_{-\mathrm{c}}^{c} y\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(l, y)\,\mathrm{d}y = 0 \ \Longrightarrow\ A_{03} = A_{23}\Big(\frac{2c^2}{5} - l^2\Big) = \frac{p}{20c} - \frac{p\,l^2}{8c^3} .\]
Hint

The moment at the end \(x = \pm l\) is zero. With \(\int y^2\,\mathrm{d}y = 2c^3/3\) and \(\int y^4\,\mathrm{d}y = 2c^5/5\) we have \((3A_{03} + 3A_{23}l^2)\,2c^3/3 = 2A_{23}\cdot 2c^5/5\). The force \(N = \int\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}y\) is zero by itself, because \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) is odd in \(y\). At the ends the moment is zero.

6
\[\int_{-\mathrm{c}}^{c}\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}(l, y)\,\mathrm{d}y = -2l\int_{-\mathrm{c}}^{c}(A_{21} + 3A_{23}y^2)\,\mathrm{d}y = 8\,l\,c^3 A_{23} = p\,l .\]
Hint

We did not prescribe the shear force at the end, it follows from equilibrium: \(p\,l\) is half of the load \(2pl\), the reaction of the support. Since \(\varphi\) itself satisfies equilibrium, the sums of forces and moments of the whole beam are satisfied automatically (BARBER, PDF p. 76). The reactions are \(p\,l\) at the supports.

7
\[\begin{gathered} \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -\frac{M\,y}{I} - \frac{p}{I}\Big(\frac{y^3}{3} - \frac{c^2 y}{5}\Big) , \\ \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \frac{p\,x\,(c^2 - y^2)}{2I} , \quad \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = -\frac{p}{2I}\Big(c^2 y - \frac{y^3}{3}\Big) - \frac{p}{2} , \\ M = \frac{p\,(l^2 - x^2)}{2} . \end{gathered}\]
Hint

We insert the constants into step 3 and arrange with \(I = 2c^3/3\). The difference from equation (5.102) is zero in every component (SymPy). The difference from the elementary stress is the correction.

Source: SADD example 8.3, (8.1.24) to (8.1.30) (PDF pp. 173-175). SADD gives the constants without derivation, the justification of the form of the function and the calculation of the constants by steps are ours, all checked with SymPy (script anf.py).

The derivation showed how we find the function that SADD only stated, and that the same procedure, degree by degree, tells when a trial fails: the term \(x^2y^3\) without \(y^5\) would not be biharmonic. The solution differs from the elementary one by a term without force and moment, \(-(p/I)(y^3/3 - c^2y/5)\), which at the upper edge in the middle of the span is \(-p/5 = -0{,}0100\) MPa against \(-93{,}75\) MPa by elementary theory, since \(p = 50\) kPa. The shear force at the end is everywhere only in the resultant, so these are supports that give these stresses and not point supports. SADD (8.1.37) and (8.1.38) (PDF p. 177) give for the mid deflection the factor \(1 + (12/5)(4/5 + \nu/2)(c/l)^2 = 1{,}000912\) relative to the Euler-Bernoulli beam, which we do not derive here. The correction is small.

11.10.9 The clamp is felt only near the end (vpetje se čuti le blizu konca)

For three beams we obtained an exact solution from a polynomial, each with the numbers of chapter 5: pure bending with plane cross-sections, the cantilever with a curved cross-section and the term \((2 + \nu)\), and the simply supported beam with the difference \(-p/5\) from the elementary stress. All three are exact solutions of the plane system in which the ends are loaded with stresses that have the right resultant and moment, but not necessarily the right form. The answer to the question at the beginning of the section is therefore yes: one function suffices, we must solve \(\nabla^4\varphi = 0\) with boundary conditions, and the solution differs from the elementary one only in the terms that the assumptions of chapter 5 omit. Figures: pure bending, the cantilever and the simply supported beam.

What remains is the question that appeared at the clamp. The cantilever needs a parabolic shear stress at the ends, a real clamp gives something else. The three idealizations of the clamp gave the deflection of the free end \(7{,}9719\), \(7{,}9819\) and \(7{,}9844\) mm, the difference is \(0{,}16\ \%\). How far from the end does the clamp influence the stresses at all? This is answered by the next section, where we support Saint-Venant's principle, which in chapter 2 we only named, with numbers.
