Chapter 5 Beam theory (teorija nosilcev)

5.3 How a beam bends

Equilibrium gave us the internal forces \(N(x)\), \(V(x)\) and \(M(x)\) in every cross section, but not the shape that the beam takes. For the cantilever of section 5.1.3 we know that the moment at the clamp is \(-500\) N m, and we know nothing about how far the free end sinks. For that we must know where the points of the beam move. A loaded body has a displacement field (definition 1.23) with three unknown functions of three variables. For the bar we replaced it by one function \(u(x)\), because every point of a cross section moved the same way. In a beam the points of a cross section do not move the same way: in bending the fibres on one side get longer and on the other side shorter. We ask how many functions of one variable suffice to describe the displacements of all points of a beam.

We look for the answer in three steps. First we take a load for which symmetry alone gives the answer, pure bending. For other loads symmetry is not enough, so there we assume three properties of the cross sections and prove the form of the displacement field from them. At the end we say where these assumptions do not agree with the material that we know from chapter 3.

5.3.1 Pure bending

Let us start with a load for which every small piece of the middle part of the beam is loaded the same way. A simply supported beam (prostoležeči nosilec) of length \(L = 2\) m, made of a steel bar with a rectangular cross section 20 mm \(\times\) 40 mm, has at a distance \(a = 0{,}5\) m from each support one force \(F = 1\) kN downward (figure 5.17). We call this load four-point bending (štiritočkovni upogib), because the beam receives forces at four places: two reactions and two loads. The reactions are 1 kN each, upward.

We compute the internal forces with a cut, as we learned in section 5.2.2. We take the left part of the beam up to the cut at \(x\) and require that the sum of forces and of moments about the centre of the cut is zero. The force \((N, V, 0)\) and the moment \((0, 0, M)\) act on the left part from the right (the convention of section 5.2.1).

  1. At the left end acts the reaction \((0, 1000, 0)\) N at the point \((0, 0)\). For \(0 < x < a\) this is the only external force on the left part. The sum of forces in the direction \(y\) is \(1000 + V = 0\), so \(V = -1000\) N. The sum of forces in the direction \(x\) gives \(N = 0\).
  2. The moment of the reaction about the centre of the cut is the \(z\) component of the cross product \(\overrightarrow{r} \times \vek F\) with \(\overrightarrow{r} = (-x, 0, 0)\), so \((-x)(1000) - 0 = -1000\,x\) N m. The sum of moments is \(-1000\,x + M = 0\) and \(M = 1000\,x\) N m.
  3. For \(a < x < L - a\) the left part also carries the force \((0, -1000, 0)\) N at the point \((a, 0)\). Now \(V = -1000 + 1000 = 0\). The moment of this force about the cut is \((a - x)(-1000) = 1000\,(x - a)\) N m, so \(-1000\,x + 1000\,(x - a) + M = 0\) and \(M = 1000\,a = 500\) N m.

The middle part of the beam between the loads, 1 m long, therefore has \(N = 0\), \(V = 0\) and \(M = 500\) N m in every cross section, and the nonzero moment is the same everywhere. Such a part of a beam is in pure bending (čisti upogib). The result agrees with the relation \(M' = -V\) of corollary 5.11: for \(x < a\) we have \(V = -1000\) N and \(M' = 1000\) N, in the middle both are zero.

What happens to a cross section in pure bending? Let us recall the strip of section 1.18.3. The thin steel strip had the strains \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = y/R\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\) with radius \(R = 10\) m, and the displacement field

\[u_{\mathrm{x}} = \frac{x\,y}{R} , \qquad u_{\mathrm{y}} = -\frac{x^2}{2R} , \qquad u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0 .\]

In it all bands curve the same way and stack without gaps (figure 5.18). Its right end, at \(x = 0{,}2\) m, sinks by \(0{,}2^2/(2 \cdot 10)\) m \(= 2\) mm. We can notice something in it that we did not stress there. We call the segment of a cross section in the plane \(xy\) along which \(x\) and \(z\) are constant and which runs over the whole height of the cross section a normal line (prečnica), because before the load it is perpendicular to the axis. A point on the normal line at \(x\) that was at the height \(y\) moves in the direction \(x\) by \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = (x/R)\,y\). This displacement is proportional to the height \(y\). The normal line therefore does not curve, it stays straight and only tilts: the tilt is \(x/R\), because \(u_{\mathrm{x}}/y = x/R\). The cross section stays plane.

Is it so in every pure bending? The answer is yes, and symmetry gives it (figure 5.19). Take a piece of the beam with its middle at \(x_0\) and mirror it in the plane of the cross section at \(x_0\). The mirror image of the piece is the same piece. The load on its ends is also equal to its mirror image: in pure bending there is only a normal stress at both ends, which is equal at both ends at the same point of the cross section and at both ends points either away from the piece or toward it. If the cross section in the middle of the piece bulged to the right, its mirror image would bulge to the left. The mirror image of the deformed piece is again a solution for the same load, and the solution for a given load is unique up to a rigid displacement (lemma 4.133). The deformed piece is therefore equal to its mirror image up to a rigid displacement, so the cross section cannot bulge to either side and must stay in its plane. We state this precisely as follows.

Lemma 5.13 Plane sections in pure bending (ravni prerezi pri čistem upogibu)

Let a segment of a beam be the box \(x_{\mathrm{a}} \le x \le x_{\mathrm{b}}\), \(|y| \le h/2\), \(|z| \le b/2\) (a prismatic segment (prizmatičen odsek): the cross section is the same along the whole length), made of a homogeneous isotropic material with Hooke's law (theorem 3.11), whose strain energy density is positive for every small strain that is not zero (as in lemma 4.133). There are no body forces and the lateral surface of the segment, \(y = \pm h/2\) and \(z = \pm b/2\), is free, \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \vek 0\). Let the displacement field \(\vek u\) of the segment have small strains, be continuously differentiable with continuously differentiable stresses on an open set that contains the segment, and satisfy the equilibrium equations (2.31) at interior points. On every cross section let the stress vector be \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = (\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(y, z), 0, 0)\) with the same continuous function \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(y, z)\) (pure bending). Then for every \(x_0\) with \(x_{\mathrm{a}} < x_0 < x_{\mathrm{b}}\) and every piece \(|x - x_0| \le s\) of the segment there exist a field \(\vek w\) with \(w_{\mathrm{x}}(x_0, y, z) = 0\) and a small rigid displacement \(\vek r\) (lemma 4.72) such that on the piece \(\vek u = \vek w + \vek r\). The cross section at \(x_0\) therefore stays plane after the deformation, up to corrections of second order in the small displacements.

Proof

We must show that the mirror field has the same stresses on the same faces as the actual one, and use uniqueness. We take a piece \(K\) of the segment, \(|x - x_0| \le s\), which is a box, and the mirror \(S(x, y, z) = (2x_0 - x, y, z)\). Let \(s_{\mathrm{x}} = -1\) and \(s_{\mathrm{y}} = s_{\mathrm{z}} = 1\) be the signs of the reflection. The map \(S\) takes \(K\) to itself, the right cross face to the left, the left to the right and the lateral surface to itself, and an outward normal to an outward normal, \(\vek n(SP) = (s_{\mathrm{i}} n_{\mathrm{i}}(P))_{\mathrm{i}}\).

  1. The field \(\vek u\), restricted to \(K\), is a solution for the load \((\vek 0, \bar{\vek t})\), where \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) is the empty set (the piece is fixed nowhere) and \(\bar{\vek t} = \ten\sigma\,\vek n\) on the surface (definition 4.132): requirements 1 and 2 hold by assumption, requirement 3 by the definition of \(\bar{\vek t}\). On the right face (\(\vek n = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\)) we have \(\bar{\vek t} = (\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(y, z), 0, 0)\), on the left (\(\vek n = -\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\)) we have \(\bar{\vek t} = -\ten\sigma\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = (-\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(y, z), 0, 0)\) at the same \(y\) and \(z\), on the lateral surface \(\vek 0\). The load is continuous on every face.
  2. The mirror field is \(\tilde u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y, z) = -u_{\mathrm{x}}(2x_0 - x, y, z)\), \(\tilde u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y, z) = u_{\mathrm{y}}(2x_0 - x, y, z)\) and likewise \(\tilde u_{\mathrm{z}}\) with \(u_{\mathrm{z}}\). By the chain rule (lemma 4.43) the derivative with respect to \(x\) of each component \(f(2x_0 - x, y, z)\) equals \(-(\partial f/\partial x)(2x_0 - x, y, z)\), and the derivatives with respect to \(y\) and \(z\) do not change. Therefore \(\tilde\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}(P) = s_{\mathrm{i}} s_{\mathrm{j}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}(SP)\). For example \(\tilde\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \partial\tilde u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial x = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}(SP)\) and \(\tilde\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \tfrac12(\partial\tilde u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial y + \partial\tilde u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial x) = \tfrac12(-\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial y - \partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial x)(SP) = -\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}(SP)\).
  3. By Hooke's law (3.13) we have \(\tilde\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}} = \lambda\,(\mathrm{tr}\,\tilde{\ten\varepsilon})\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}} + 2\mu\,\tilde\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\). The trace is the same after the reflection, \(\mathrm{tr}\,\tilde{\ten\varepsilon}(P) = \mathrm{tr}\,\ten\varepsilon(SP)\), because the signs on the diagonal are \(s_{\mathrm{i}}^2 = 1\), and \(\delta_{\mathrm{ij}} = s_{\mathrm{i}} s_{\mathrm{j}}\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\). Therefore \(\tilde\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}(P) = s_{\mathrm{i}} s_{\mathrm{j}}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}(SP)\). The stresses \(\tilde\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\) are continuously differentiable because the \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\) are.
  4. The equilibrium equations also hold for \(\tilde{\ten\sigma}\). By the chain rule \(\partial\tilde\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}} = s_{\mathrm{i}} s_{\mathrm{j}}\,s_{\mathrm{j}}\,(\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}})(SP) = s_{\mathrm{i}}\,(\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}})(SP)\), because \(s_{\mathrm{j}}^2 = 1\) (the derivative with respect to \(x\) gives an extra sign \(s_{\mathrm{x}}\), with respect to \(y\) and \(z\) it does not). The sum over \(j\) is \(s_{\mathrm{i}}\) times the left side of equation (2.31) at the point \(SP\), so it is zero, because there are no body forces.
  5. The load of the mirror field is the same. By step 3 we have \((\tilde{\ten\sigma}\,\vek n)_{\mathrm{i}}(P) = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} s_{\mathrm{i}} s_{\mathrm{j}}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}(SP)\,n_{\mathrm{j}}(P) = s_{\mathrm{i}}\,(\ten\sigma\,\vek n)_{\mathrm{i}}(SP) = s_{\mathrm{i}}\,\bar t_{\mathrm{i}}(SP)\). On the right face \(SP\) is on the left and \(s_{\mathrm{i}}\,\bar t_{\mathrm{i}}(SP) = (\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(y, z), 0, 0) = \bar{\vek t}(P)\), on the left face likewise \((-\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(y, z), 0, 0)\), on the lateral surface \(\vek 0\). The field \(\tilde{\vek u}\) is therefore a solution for the same load \((\vek 0, \bar{\vek t})\) (requirement 1 of the definition holds, because \(S\) maps \(K\) to itself).
  6. By lemma 4.133, point 2, for the body \(K\) in one piece and the empty \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\), the difference \(\tilde{\vek u} - \vek u\) is a small rigid displacement.
  7. By lemma 4.134 with \(\alpha = \beta = \tfrac12\) the field \(\vek w = \tfrac12(\vek u + \tilde{\vek u})\) is a solution for the load \(\tfrac12(\vek 0, \bar{\vek t}) + \tfrac12(\vek 0, \bar{\vek t}) = (\vek 0, \bar{\vek t})\). The mirror field of \(\vek w\) is \(\tfrac12(\tilde{\vek u} + \tilde{\tilde{\vek u}}) = \tfrac12(\tilde{\vek u} + \vek u) = \vek w\), because reflecting twice leaves the field unchanged: \(\tilde{\tilde u}_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y, z) = -\tilde u_{\mathrm{x}}(2x_0 - x, y, z) = u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y, z)\), and likewise for the other two components.
  8. From \(\tilde w_{\mathrm{x}} = w_{\mathrm{x}}\) at the point \((x_0, y, z)\), where \(SP = P\), it follows that \(w_{\mathrm{x}} = -w_{\mathrm{x}}\), so \(w_{\mathrm{x}}(x_0, y, z) = 0\). The points of the cross section at \(x_0\) do not move in the direction \(x\) under the field \(\vek w\) and stay in the plane \(x = x_0\).
  9. The field \(\vek r = \vek u - \vek w = \tfrac12(\vek u - \tilde{\vek u})\) is half of the small rigid displacement of step 6, so it is a small rigid displacement \(\vek r(P) = \delta\vek a + \delta\boldsymbol\varphi \times \overrightarrow{OP}\). Under \(\vek u\) a point \(P\) of the cross section at \(x_0\) goes to the point \(P + \vek w(P) + \delta\vek a + \delta\boldsymbol\varphi \times \overrightarrow{OP}\). The points \(P + \vek w(P)\) lie in the plane \(x = x_0\), and the map \(Q \mapsto Q + \delta\vek a + \delta\boldsymbol\varphi \times \overrightarrow{OQ}\) is linear and so again maps a plane to a plane. The point \(P + \vek u(P)\) differs from this map by \(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi \times \vek w(P)\), which is of second order in the small displacements. The cross section after the deformation is therefore plane up to corrections of second order.

Source: The proof is not from a source. The same statement is confirmed by SADD Example 8.2 (PDF p. 171-172): for a beam with moments at the ends, which are satisfied only in the statically equivalent sense, the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{x}}\) is linear and all other components are zero, plane sections really stay plane (SADD (8.1.22), PDF p. 172), and the solution is not valid near the ends of the beam.

In four-point bending we assume the hypothesis of the lemma, that on every cross section there is only a normal stress with the same distribution, and we do not prove it: we know only the internal forces \(N = 0\), \(V = 0\) and \(M = 500\) N m, not the stresses. The assumption is supported by SADD Example 8.2, where exactly such a stress is the exact solution of the plane problem (PDF p. 171-172), valid far from the places where the load acts. The proof showed that a cross section cannot bulge in pure bending: the mirror field would bulge it to the other side, and the solution is unique up to a rigid displacement. The proof does not say how a cross section tilts and how the axis curves. We show this in the rest of the section. Symmetry helps only for a load where the stress is the same on all cross sections. For a beam that also has a shear force we cannot mirror cross sections into other cross sections, and symmetry is not enough.

5.3.2 Assumptions of the Euler-Bernoulli beam

From history

Mariotte had to design the pipelines supplying water to the Palace of Versailles, and this made him interested in the bending strength of beams (TIMO, PDF p. 29). Experimenting with wooden and glass rods, he found that Galileo's theory gives too large a breaking force (TIMO, PDF p. 29). In tension tests of various materials he saw that the elongations are proportional to the forces (TIMO, PDF p. 29). We have already met his correction of Galileo's calculation in section 1.1 (figure 1.8). Here we tell how he arrived at it and how he tested it. He started with a lever.

First he took a lever supported at the point \(C\). Three equal weights of 12 pounds hung on its left arm, at distances of 4, 2 and 1 foot from \(C\). They are balanced by a force \(F\) on the right arm, 12 feet from \(C\). From the balance of moments about \(C\), \(F = (12 \cdot 4 + 12 \cdot 2 + 12 \cdot 1)/12 = 7\) pounds (TIMO, PDF p. 29). If the force \(F\) is increased, the lever turns about \(C\). The displacements of the points where the weights hang are proportional to their distances from \(C\), but the forces stay equal, all three 12 pounds (TIMO, PDF p. 29). Mariotte then replaced the weights by three identical wires, each of strength 12 pounds. When the force in the farthest wire reaches 12 pounds, the forces in the other two are proportional to their elongations, so 6 and 3 pounds (TIMO, PDF p. 29). The breaking force is therefore \(F = (12 \cdot 4 + 6 \cdot 2 + 3 \cdot 1)/12 = 5{,}25\) pounds, not the 7 pounds of the rigid lever (TIMO, PDF p. 29-30). The force in each wire is thus proportional to its distance from the point of rotation, because it is proportional to the elongation, and the elongation to the distance.

He reasoned in the same way for a cantilever. At fracture the cross section turns about the point \(D\), and the forces in the longitudinal fibres are in the same proportion as their distances from \(D\) (TIMO, PDF p. 30). For a rectangular cross section of depth \(h\) and absolute strength \(S\) (the force the cross section carries in tension), the forces in the fibres grow from zero at \(D\) to the largest value at the edge. Their sum is therefore \(S/2\), and the moment about \(D\) is \((S/2)(2h/3) = S h/3\) (TIMO, PDF p. 30). The breaking force is thus two thirds of Galileo's value \(S h/2\) (TIMO, PDF p. 30-31). He tested it on a wooden bar. In tension the bar held \(S = 330\) pounds, and as a cantilever with an arm of \(l = 4\) inches it broke at \(L = 6\) pounds, so \(S : L = 55\). His formula gave 48, Galileo's 32 (TIMO, PDF p. 31). He blamed the difference on time, since the bar in tension might have held 300 pounds if the force had acted long enough (TIMO, PDF p. 31). A repetition with glass rods again showed that his formula predicts better than Galileo's (TIMO, PDF p. 31).

So the idea entered the thinking about bending that the cross section turns as a whole and that the fibres lengthen in proportion to their distance from the point of rotation.

Let us return to the question of the section: how the points of a beam move when it bends. The cross section that turns as a whole, according to Mariotte, is the first answer. Before we write it as an assumption, let us see what we already know about cross sections.

For the cantilever of section 5.1.3 the shear force is \(V = -500\) N in every cross section and the moment changes along the beam. There is no mirror symmetry in the cross section, and the lemma of the previous section says nothing. What do we take as a start? Let us see what is true in pure bending: the normal lines stayed straight (lemma 5.13), and in the strip they also kept their length and stayed perpendicular to the fibres. Let us assume that all this also holds for a beam with a shear force. These are the three assumptions of Euler and Bernoulli (EP, p. 73): after the deformation the normal lines of the beam remain

  • (a) straight,
  • (b) of the same length,
  • (c) perpendicular to the deformed axis of the beam.

CM (PDF p. 308) writes assumptions (b) and (c) with strains: that the normal line keeps its length means \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0\) (there is no elongation along the normal line), and that it stays perpendicular to the axis means \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\) (the right angle between a longitudinal fibre and the normal line does not change, section 1.17.3). The beam is loaded only in the plane \(xy\) and the cross section has this plane as a plane of symmetry, so we also assume \(u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\) and that the cross section does not deform in the direction \(z\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\). Each assumption has its reason and its cost, which we collect in a later section on the limits of beam theory. We look at the reasons on the cantilever of section 5.1.3.

  • Straightness (a) needs no reason of its own: it follows from (b) and (c), as we prove later in this section. In pure bending it is also proved (lemma 5.13).
  • Equal length (b) means that we neglect the transverse strains. By lemma 3.6 the transverse strain in a fibre under uniaxial stress is \(-\nu\) times the longitudinal one, so at the clamp of the cantilever at most \(0{,}3 \cdot 4{,}78 \cdot 10^{-4} = 1{,}43 \cdot 10^{-4}\). A normal line 40 mm long therefore changes on each half by less than \(20\ \text{mm} \cdot 1{,}43 \cdot 10^{-4} = 2{,}9\ \mu\text{m}\). What this neglect costs we say at the end of the section.
  • Perpendicularity (c) means \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\). For the strip of section 5.3.1 it holds. With a shear force the normal lines would tilt relative to the axis by the shear strain, which is small for a slender beam: for the cantilever of section 5.1.3 the largest shear stress, which we compute later in the chapter, is 0,9375 MPa (1 % of the largest longitudinal stress 93,75 MPa from section 5.2.1), the shear strain is \(\tau/G = 0{,}9375\ \text{MPa}/75{,}38\ \text{GPa} = 1{,}2 \cdot 10^{-5}\), which is 0,1 % of the slope 0,012 at the free end (we compute it later in the chapter) and 2,6 % of the largest longitudinal strain \(4{,}78 \cdot 10^{-4}\). A more precise estimate comes from a later section on the beam with shear deformation.

We write the assumptions as a definition.

Definition 5.14 Euler-Bernoulli beam model (Euler-Bernoullijev model nosilca)

Let a beam with the axis \(x\) and a rectangular cross section \(|y| \le h/2\), \(|z| \le b/2\) be loaded and supported in the plane \(xy\) (definition 5.1). The displacements of the beam in the Euler-Bernoulli beam model (Euler-Bernoullijev model nosilca) are a displacement field \((u_{\mathrm{x}}, u_{\mathrm{y}}, u_{\mathrm{z}})\) which is continuous on the whole beam, whose components have continuous partial derivatives up to second order at the interior points of every piece of the beam between two neighbouring point loads (or of the whole beam if there are none), and which at every interior point of the beam satisfies

\[u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0 , \qquad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = 0 , \qquad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0 , \qquad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0 , \qquad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0 . \tag{5.15}\]

The strains are the components of the small strain tensor (definition 1.115), computed by (1.63). The conditions \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\) are assumptions (b) and (c), and the conditions \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\) together with \(u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\) mean that the deformation is plane and the displacements \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) do not change with \(z\).

Source: EP (p. 73), CM (PDF p. 308). Writing assumption (a) as a consequence of the others is ours (the theorem on the displacement field of a beam later in this section). EP and CM do not write \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\) separately, because they choose the displacement field (EP (2.5.1), CM (7.3.22)) already in a form without dependence on \(z\).

5.3.3 Displacement field of a beam

The conditions (5.15) do not write assumption (a) about straight normal lines. Is it hidden in them? What displacement field follows from them? The answer is given by the same procedure as for the strip in section 1.18.3. There we concluded from \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0\) that all points of a normal line move the same way in the direction \(y\), and from \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\) that \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial y = -\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial x\). We use the same here. The second derivatives of the displacements are continuous only on each piece of the beam between two point loads, since the shear force jumps at a point force (lemma 5.12), while the displacement field is continuous across those points because the beam does not break. We therefore apply the theorem on each piece separately.

Theorem 5.15 Displacement field of a beam (polje pomikov nosilca)

Let \(B = \{(x, y, z) : x_1 < x < x_2, |y| < h/2, |z| < b/2\}\) be the set of interior points of the piece of the beam between neighbouring point loads at \(x = x_1\) and \(x = x_2\) (or of the whole beam, \(x_1 = 0\), \(x_2 = L\), if there are none) and let the displacement field \((u_{\mathrm{x}}, u_{\mathrm{y}}, u_{\mathrm{z}})\) satisfy at every point of \(B\) the conditions (5.15) of the Euler-Bernoulli model, with continuous partial derivatives of first and second order of the components \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\). Then there exist functions \(u(x)\) and \(v(x)\) on the interval \((x_1, x_2)\), with continuous derivatives up to second order, such that at every point of \(B\)

\[u_{\mathrm{x}} = u(x) - y\,v'(x) , \qquad u_{\mathrm{y}} = v(x) , \qquad u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0 . \tag{5.16}\]

The only nonzero component of the strain is

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = u'(x) - y\,v''(x) . \tag{5.17}\]
Proof

We must show that \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) does not depend on \(y\) and \(z\), that \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) grows linearly with \(y\) and that the coefficient of \(y\) is the derivative of the function \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\). We use corollary 1.85 on a function with zero derivative along segments inside \(B\), as in steps 1 to 3 of the proof for the strip in section 1.18.3.

  1. From \(u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = \tfrac12(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial z + \partial u_{\mathrm{z}} / \partial y) = 0\) it follows that \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial z = 0\) at every point of \(B\). By corollary 1.85 on the segment from \((x, y, 0)\) to \((x, y, z)\), which lies in \(B\), we have \(u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y, z) = u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y, 0)\). Likewise from \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = 0\) it follows that \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial z = 0\) and \(u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y, z) = u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y, 0)\). The displacements are functions of \(x\) and \(y\) only.
  2. From \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial y = 0\), by corollary 1.85 on the segment from \((x, 0)\) to \((x, y)\) it follows that \(u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y) = u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, 0) =: v(x)\). All points of a normal line move the same way in the direction \(y\).
  3. Therefore \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial x\) at the point \((x, y)\) is the limit of the difference quotients \((u_{\mathrm{y}}(x + t, y) - u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y))/t = (v(x + t) - v(x))/t\), which are equal to those at the point \((x, 0)\), as \(t \to 0\) (definition 1.5). So \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial x = v'(x)\) for every \(y\), and similarly \(\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial x^2 = v''(x)\). The derivatives \(v'\) and \(v''\) are continuous, because they are continuous derivatives of the component \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\).
  4. From \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\), by (1.63) it follows that \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial y = -\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial x = -v'(x)\).
  5. At constant \(x\) the derivative of the function \(y \mapsto u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y) + y\,v'(x)\) with respect to \(y\) equals \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial y + v'(x) = 0\) by step 4 (lemma 1.81 on the rules of differentiation). By corollary 1.85 on the segment from \((x, 0)\) to \((x, y)\) this function is constant, equal to its value at \(y = 0\), \(u(x) := u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, 0)\). So \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = u(x) - y\,v'(x)\). The function \(u\) has continuous derivatives up to second order as a restriction of the continuously differentiable component \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\).
  6. We compute the strains from (1.63): \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial x = u'(x) - y\,v''(x)\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\) by assumption, and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{z}} / \partial z = 0\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = 0\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\) by assumption.

Source: CM (7.3.22), (7.3.23) (PDF p. 308-309), EP (2.5.1), (2.5.2) (p. 73). The sources write the displacement field as an assumption and compute the strains from it. The proof that the field follows from \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\) is not from the sources, it uses steps 1 to 3 of section 1.18.3.

The proof showed that the straightness of a normal line follows from \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\): a point at the height \(y\) moves by \(-y\,v'(x)\) in the direction \(x\), so linearly with the height, and the normal line stays straight. This is assumption (a), which therefore did not need to be written separately. What this field means is shown by figure 5.21. The displacement of the axis is \((u(x), v(x))\). The displacement \(u(x)\) is the displacement of the bar of chapter 4: a point of the axis, \(y = 0\), moves by \(u(x)\) in the direction \(x\). The displacement \(v(x)\) is the deflection of the axis (poves osi), the displacement in the direction \(y\), which is the same for all points of a normal line.

So a point at the height \(y\) moves, besides the displacement of the axis, by \(-y\,v'(x)\) in the direction \(x\) (figure 5.22). What is \(v'(x)\)? It is the slope of the axis: by how much the axis rises per unit length, a ratio of two lengths, a number without a unit. For the cantilever of section 5.1.3 the slope at the free end is \(v' = -0{,}01196\) (we compute it later in the chapter). A point at the top of the cross section, at \(y = 20\) mm, therefore moves at the free end in the direction \(x\) by \(-0{,}020\ \text{m} \cdot (-0{,}01196) = 0{,}239\) mm. This is a horizontal displacement due to the slope, without length changes of the axis.

The normal line stays perpendicular to the axis and tilts by the same ratio as the slope of the axis. Let us show this with the small rotation tensor of section 1.18.3. For the field (5.16) we have by (1.54)

\[\omega_{\mathrm{xy}} = \tfrac12\Big(\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial x}\Big) = \tfrac12\,(-v' - v') = -v'(x) .\]

In a rigid rotation about the axis \(z\) by a small angle \(\varphi\) in radians (the ratio of the arc length to the radius), counterclockwise, the displacement of a point is \(\vek u = \varphi\,(-y, x, 0)\), and \(\omega_{\mathrm{xy}} = \tfrac12(-\varphi - \varphi) = -\varphi\). The material near the axis therefore rotates by \(\varphi = v'(x)\): the normal line rotates by the slope of the axis, so it stays perpendicular to it. For the cantilever of section 5.1.3 the normal line at the free end rotates clockwise, because \(v' < 0\).

The axis curves. How strongly? We already saw the answer for the strip in section 1.18.3: the second derivative \(\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial x^2\) tells how fast the slope changes along a fibre. For a beam this is \(v''(x)\) and we name this quantity in the definition.

Definition 5.16 Curvature of the axis (ukrivljenost osi)

Let \(v\) be a twice continuously differentiable function on every open interval between neighbouring point loads in \((0, L)\) (or on \((0, L)\) if there are none), the deflection of the axis of a beam. Let the slope be small, \(v'^2 \le 10^{-2}\). The curvature of the axis (ukrivljenost osi) is the function \(\kappa(x) = v''(x)\) with the unit \(\text{m}^{-1}\), which gives one number for every value of \(x\). The radius of curvature (polmer ukrivljenosti) is \(R(x) = 1/|\kappa(x)|\), where \(\kappa(x) \ne 0\).

Source: CM (7.3.22) (PDF p. 308) has the slope \(\mathrm{d}v/\mathrm{d}x\) in the displacement field. SADD (D.7), (D.8) (PDF p. 578) has the curvature \(1/\rho \approx d^2 v/dx^2\) for small slopes. The exact curvature of the graph of the function \(v\) is \(v''/(1 + v'^2)^{3/2}\) (SADD, PDF p. 578, the formula below (D.7)).

Sign: \(\kappa > 0\) where the slope increases and the axis curves upward (the shape of the letter U), \(\kappa < 0\) where the slope decreases. For the strip of section 1.18.3 we have \(v = -x^2/(2R)\) and \(\kappa = -1/R = -0{,}1\ \text{m}^{-1}\), radius of curvature 10 m, as with \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = y/R\). Figure 5.23 shows why we call this curvature: on a small piece of the axis the curvature \(\kappa\) means the change of the slope per unit length, and a circular arc of radius \(R\) has the same change of the slope, \(1/R\). The bound \(v'^2 \le 10^{-2}\) is chosen because at it the exact curvature differs from \(v''\) by the factor \((1 + v'^2)^{-3/2} \ge 0{,}985\), so by at most 1,5 %. For the cantilever of section 5.1.3 the largest slope is \(v' = -0{,}01196\), \(v'^2 = 1{,}43 \cdot 10^{-4}\), the factor there is \(1{,}0001430^{-3/2} = 0{,}99979\), the error 0,02 %. For the simply supported beam the largest slope at the support is \(0{,}01594\) (we compute it later in the chapter), \(v'^2 = 2{,}5 \cdot 10^{-4}\), the error 0,04 %.

The field (5.16) has three parts: \(u(x)\) is the bar of chapter 4, \(v(x)\) the deflection and \(-y\,v'(x)\) the tilt of the cross section. The strain (5.17) thus has two parts: \(u'(x)\) is the axial strain, the same for all \(y\), and \(-y\,v''(x) = -y\,\kappa(x)\) is the bending strain, which grows linearly with the height. For the strip of section 1.18.3 we have \(u = 0\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = -y\,v'' = y/R\), as in section 5.3.1. At positive curvature the upper fibres get shorter, because the axis curves upward and the inner side of a bend is shorter than the outer, and the upper fibres are on the inner side.

5.3.4 What we neglect here

Assumption (b), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0\), does not agree with the material. In a fibre pulled only by a longitudinal stress the transverse strain by lemma 3.6 is \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = -\nu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), which is not zero. Take the clamp of the cantilever of section 5.1.3, where the stress at the top of the cross section is \(+93{,}75\) MPa (section 5.2.1). The longitudinal strain there is \(93{,}75\ \text{MPa}/196\ \text{GPa} = 4{,}78 \cdot 10^{-4}\), and the transverse strain is \(-0{,}3 \cdot 4{,}78 \cdot 10^{-4} = -1{,}43 \cdot 10^{-4}\) in the direction \(y\) and the same in the direction \(z\). The width of the cross section, 20 mm, decreases at the top by \(2{,}87\ \mu\text{m}\), by \(1{,}43\ \mu\text{m}\) on each side, and at the bottom, where the longitudinal strain is opposite, it increases by the same. The rectangular cross section turns into a trapezoid (figure 5.24). The normal line also curves because of \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = -\nu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\): after integrating \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial y = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \nu\,\kappa\,y\) we get \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = v + \nu\,\kappa\,y^2/2\), its end points move by the same amount, and its middle is displaced relative to the end points by \(\nu\,|\kappa|\,h^2/8 = 0{,}3 \cdot 0{,}0239\ \text{m}^{-1} \cdot (0{,}04\ \text{m})^2/8 = 1{,}4\ \mu\text{m}\). The length of the normal line between its end points therefore does not change, but it stays straight only approximately. We call this anticlastic curvature (antiklastična ukrivljenost).

The model thus requires \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0\), and a material under uniaxial stress has \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = -\nu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\). We cannot have both at once. When we compute the stress from the strain we will keep \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0\), so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), and we use \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0\) only for the form of the displacement field. CM (PDF p. 309) admits this: in the relation \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) it takes \(\nu = 0\), but in the relation \(2G = E/(1 + \nu)\) it does not. We still use the model, because what it neglects is small: the transverse strain at the clamp of the cantilever is less than \(1{,}5 \cdot 10^{-4}\), and the longitudinal one is \(4{,}78 \cdot 10^{-4}\). How large the errors of the results are we collect in a later section on the limits of beam theory.

The second assumption, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\), is likewise only approximate when the shear force is \(V \ne 0\). Hooke's law connects the shear stress with the shear strain, and from \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\) it follows that \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 2G\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\), so also the shear force \(V = \int \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,dA = 0\). The shear force that equilibrium requires would have to be zero. EP (p. 74) calls this an inconsistency of Bernoulli's hypothesis, CM (PDF p. 309) a flaw. Therefore we do not compute the shear stresses from Hooke's law but from the shear force (a later section on shear stresses), and we allow the shear strain, which \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\) neglects, in the section on the beam with shear deformation. For the cantilever of section 5.1.3 the largest shear strain is \(\gamma = \tau_{\max}/G = 1{,}2 \cdot 10^{-5}\), and its contribution to the deflection of the free end is 0,125 % of the bending part, but for the same bar shortened to \(L = 80\) mm it is 19,5 % (we compute it in a later section on the beam with shear deformation).
