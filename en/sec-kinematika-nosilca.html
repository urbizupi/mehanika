Chapter 5 Beam theory (teorija nosilcev)

5.3 How a beam bends

Equilibrium gave us the internal forces \(N(x)\), \(V(x)\) and \(M(x)\) in every cross section, but not the shape that the beam takes. The cantilever of section 5.1.3 is clamped in the wall and loaded with a force at the free end. We know that the moment at the clamp is \(-500\) N m and that from straight it bends, but we know nothing about how far the free end drops. For that we must know where the points of the beam move. A loaded body has a displacement field (definition 1.23) with three unknown functions of three variables. For the bar we replaced it by one function \(u(x)\), because all points of a cross section moved the same way. In a beam the points of a cross section do not move the same way: in bending the cross section turns, the fibres on one side get longer, on the other shorter. We ask how many functions of one variable suffice to describe the displacements of all points of a beam.

We look for the answer in three steps. First we take a load for which symmetry alone gives the answer, pure bending. For other loads symmetry is not enough, so there we assume three properties of the cross sections and prove the form of the displacement field from them. At the end we say where these assumptions do not agree with the material that we know from chapter 3.

5.3.1 Pure bending

Let us start with a load for which every small piece of the middle part of the beam is loaded the same way. A simply supported beam (prostoležeči nosilec) of length \(L = 2\) m, made of a steel bar with a rectangular cross section 20 mm \(\times\) 40 mm, has at a distance \(a = 0{,}5\) m from each support one force \(F = 1\) kN downward (figure 5.44). We call this load four-point bending (štiritočkovni upogib), because the beam receives forces at four places: two reactions and two loads. The reactions are 1 kN each, upward. Why exactly that much? The beam and the loads are symmetric about the middle of the beam, so the two reactions are equal. The beam is at rest, so the sum of the vertical forces is zero and the reactions together carry both loads, \(2\) kN, each one half.

We compute the internal forces with a cut, as we learned in section 5.2.2. We take the left part of the beam up to the cut at \(x\) and require that the sum of forces and of moments about the centre of the cut is zero (figure 5.43). The force \((N, V, 0)\) and the moment \((0, 0, M)\) act on the left part from the right (the convention of section 5.2.1). We therefore draw the shear force \(V\) upward and the moment \(M\) counterclockwise, since the convention counts them positive so. If the calculation gives a negative number, the true force points opposite to the drawn one. There are no horizontal forces on the left part, so we do not draw the axial force \(N\).

  1. At the left end acts the reaction \((0, 1000, 0)\) N at the point \((0, 0)\). For \(0 < x < a\) this is the only external force on the left part (panel (1)). The sum of forces in the direction \(y\) is \(1000 + V = 0\), so \(V = -1000\) N: the shear force points downward, opposite to the drawn one. The sum of forces in the direction \(x\) gives \(N = 0\).
  2. The reaction lies at the distance \(x\) to the left of the cut. Its moment about the centre of the cut is the \(z\) component of the cross product \(\overrightarrow{r} \times \vek F\) with \(\overrightarrow{r} = (-x, 0, 0)\), so \((-x)(1000) - 0 = -1000\,x\) N m: on its own it would turn the left part clockwise. The sum of moments is \(-1000\,x + M = 0\) and \(M = 1000\,x\) N m, positive, so in the drawn direction.
  3. For \(a < x < L - a\) the left part (panel (2)) also carries the load \((0, -1000, 0)\) N at the point \((a, 0)\), at the distance \(a\) to the right of the reaction. Now \(V = -1000 + 1000 = 0\). The reaction still has the lever arm \(x\), the load has the lever arm \(x - a\). The moment of the load about the cut is \((a - x)(-1000) = 1000\,(x - a)\) N m, so \(-1000\,x + 1000\,(x - a) + M = 0\) and \(M = 1000\,a = 500\) N m, independent of \(x\), because moving the cut lengthens both lever arms by the same length and the two opposite moments change by the same amount.

The middle part of the beam between the loads, 1 m long, therefore has \(N = 0\), \(V = 0\) and \(M = 500\) N m in every cross section, and the nonzero moment is the same everywhere. Below the beam, at the same scale in \(x\), are drawn the axes of the shear force diagram and the axes of the moment diagram. Such a part of a beam is in pure bending (čisti upogib). The result agrees with the relation \(M' = -V\) of corollary 5.11: for \(x < a\) we have \(V = -1000\) N and \(M' = 1000\) N, in the middle both are zero.

What happens to a cross section in pure bending? Let us recall the strip of section 1.18.3. The thin steel strip had the strains \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = y/R\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\) with radius \(R = 10\) m, and the displacement field

\[u_{\mathrm{x}} = \frac{x\,y}{R} , \qquad u_{\mathrm{y}} = -\frac{x^2}{2R} , \qquad u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0 .\]

In it all bands curve the same way and stack without gaps (figure 5.45). The figure shows the strip before the load with its vertical normal lines and below it the same strip after the load with the axes \(x\) and \(y\). Its axis bends below its position before the load, which we draw grey dashed, and the right end at \(x = 0{,}2\) m sinks by \(0{,}2^2/(2 \cdot 10)\) m \(= 2\) mm. We can notice something in it that we did not stress there. We call the segment of a cross section in the plane \(xy\) along which \(x\) and \(z\) are constant and which runs over the whole height of the cross section a normal line (prečnica), because before the load it is perpendicular to the axis. A point on the normal line at \(x\) that was at the height \(y\) moves in the direction \(x\) by \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = (x/R)\,y\). This displacement is proportional to the height \(y\). The normal line therefore does not curve, it stays straight and only tilts: the tilt against the vertical is \(x/R\), because \(u_{\mathrm{x}}/y = x/R\). For the last normal line, \(x = 0{,}2\) m, this is \(0{,}2/10 = 0{,}02\), strongly enlarged in the figure. The cross section stays plane.

Is it so in every pure bending? For a homogeneous isotropic material and the same normal stress on every cross section the answer is yes, and symmetry gives it (figure 5.46). Take a piece of the beam with its middle at \(x_0\) and mirror it in the plane of the cross section at \(x_0\). The mirror image of the piece is the same piece. The load on its ends is also equal to its mirror image: in pure bending there is only a normal stress at both ends, which is equal at both ends at the same point of the cross section and at both ends points either away from the piece or toward it. If the cross section in the middle of the piece bulged to the right, its mirror image would bulge to the left. The mirror image of the deformed piece is again a solution for the same load, and the solution for a given load is unique up to a rigid displacement (lemma 4.134). Why only one? If the bulged cross section and its mirror image both carried the same load, their difference would be a change of shape carried by the load zero. But a body without load does not stay deformed: the deformation would store energy in it, and there is no force that would have put it in. So a spring from which we take off a weight returns to its original length, and with the same weight it stretches by the same amount every time. Lemma 4.134 shows this with the strain energy. What remains is only a motion of the piece as a whole, which stores no energy, so the solution holds only up to a rigid displacement. The deformed piece is therefore equal to its mirror image up to a rigid displacement, so the cross section cannot bulge to either side and stays plane, as a whole it can only move and tilt. We state this precisely as follows.

Lemma 5.13 Plane sections in pure bending (ravni prerezi pri čistem upogibu)

Let a segment of a beam be the box \(x_{\mathrm{a}} \le x \le x_{\mathrm{b}}\), \(|y| \le h/2\), \(|z| \le b/2\) (a prismatic segment (prizmatičen odsek): the cross section is the same along the whole length), made of a homogeneous isotropic material with Hooke's law (theorem 3.19), whose strain energy density is positive for every small strain that is not zero (as in lemma 4.134). There are no body forces and the lateral surface of the segment, \(y = \pm h/2\) and \(z = \pm b/2\), is free, \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \vek 0\). Let the displacement field \(\vek u\) of the segment have small strains, be continuously differentiable with continuously differentiable stresses on an open set that contains the segment, and satisfy the equilibrium equations (2.38) at interior points. On every cross section let the stress vector be \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = (\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(y, z), 0, 0)\) with the same continuous function \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(y, z)\) (pure bending). Then for every \(x_0\) with \(x_{\mathrm{a}} < x_0 < x_{\mathrm{b}}\) and every piece \(|x - x_0| \le s\) of the segment there exist a field \(\vek w\) with \(w_{\mathrm{x}}(x_0, y, z) = 0\) and a small rigid displacement \(\vek r\) (lemma 4.72) such that on the piece \(\vek u = \vek w + \vek r\). The cross section at \(x_0\) therefore stays plane after the deformation, up to corrections of second order in the small displacements.

Proof

We must show that the mirror field has the same stresses on the same faces as the actual one, and use uniqueness. We take a piece \(K\) of the segment, \(|x - x_0| \le s\), which is a box, and the mirror \(S(x, y, z) = (2x_0 - x, y, z)\). Let \(s_{\mathrm{x}} = -1\) and \(s_{\mathrm{y}} = s_{\mathrm{z}} = 1\) be the signs of the reflection. The map \(S\) takes \(K\) to itself, the right cross face to the left, the left to the right and the lateral surface to itself, and an outward normal to an outward normal, \(\vek n(SP) = (s_{\mathrm{i}} n_{\mathrm{i}}(P))_{\mathrm{i}}\).

  1. The field \(\vek u\), restricted to \(K\), is a solution for the load \((\vek 0, \bar{\vek t})\), where \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) is the empty set (the piece is fixed nowhere) and \(\bar{\vek t} = \ten\sigma\,\vek n\) on the surface (definition 4.133): requirements 1 and 2 hold by assumption, requirement 3 by the definition of \(\bar{\vek t}\). On the right face (\(\vek n = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\)) we have \(\bar{\vek t} = (\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(y, z), 0, 0)\), on the left (\(\vek n = -\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\)) we have \(\bar{\vek t} = -\ten\sigma\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = (-\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(y, z), 0, 0)\) at the same \(y\) and \(z\), on the lateral surface \(\vek 0\). The load is continuous on every face.
  2. The mirror field is \(\tilde u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y, z) = -u_{\mathrm{x}}(2x_0 - x, y, z)\), \(\tilde u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y, z) = u_{\mathrm{y}}(2x_0 - x, y, z)\) and likewise \(\tilde u_{\mathrm{z}}\) with \(u_{\mathrm{z}}\). The component \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) changes sign because the mirror reverses the direction \(x\): a bulge to the right becomes a bulge to the left. By the chain rule (lemma 4.43) the derivative with respect to \(x\) of each component \(f(2x_0 - x, y, z)\) equals \(-(\partial f/\partial x)(2x_0 - x, y, z)\), and the derivatives with respect to \(y\) and \(z\) do not change. Therefore \(\tilde\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}(P) = s_{\mathrm{i}} s_{\mathrm{j}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}(SP)\). For example \(\tilde\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \partial\tilde u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial x = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}(SP)\) and \(\tilde\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \tfrac12(\partial\tilde u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial y + \partial\tilde u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial x) = \tfrac12(-\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial y - \partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial x)(SP) = -\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}(SP)\).
  3. Let us compute the stresses of the mirror piece. By Hooke's law (3.28) we have \(\tilde\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}} = \lambda\,(\mathrm{tr}\,\tilde{\ten\varepsilon})\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}} + 2\mu\,\tilde\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\). The trace is the same after the reflection, \(\mathrm{tr}\,\tilde{\ten\varepsilon}(P) = \mathrm{tr}\,\ten\varepsilon(SP)\), because the signs on the diagonal are \(s_{\mathrm{i}}^2 = 1\), and \(\delta_{\mathrm{ij}} = s_{\mathrm{i}} s_{\mathrm{j}}\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\). Therefore \(\tilde\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}(P) = s_{\mathrm{i}} s_{\mathrm{j}}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}(SP)\). For \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) we have \(s_{\mathrm{x}} s_{\mathrm{x}} = 1\): the pull above and the push below are kept by the reflection. The stresses \(\tilde\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\) are continuously differentiable because the \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\) are.
  4. The equilibrium equations also hold for the mirror piece, that is for \(\tilde{\ten\sigma}\). By the chain rule \(\partial\tilde\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}} = s_{\mathrm{i}} s_{\mathrm{j}}\,s_{\mathrm{j}}\,(\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}})(SP) = s_{\mathrm{i}}\,(\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}})(SP)\), because \(s_{\mathrm{j}}^2 = 1\) (the derivative with respect to \(x\) gives an extra sign \(s_{\mathrm{x}}\), with respect to \(y\) and \(z\) it does not). The sum over \(j\) is \(s_{\mathrm{i}}\) times the left side of equation (2.38) at the point \(SP\), so it is zero, because there are no body forces.
  5. The load of the mirror field is the same. By step 3 we have \((\tilde{\ten\sigma}\,\vek n)_{\mathrm{i}}(P) = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} s_{\mathrm{i}} s_{\mathrm{j}}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}(SP)\,n_{\mathrm{j}}(P) = s_{\mathrm{i}}\,(\ten\sigma\,\vek n)_{\mathrm{i}}(SP) = s_{\mathrm{i}}\,\bar t_{\mathrm{i}}(SP)\). On the right face \(SP\) is on the left and \(s_{\mathrm{i}}\,\bar t_{\mathrm{i}}(SP) = (\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(y, z), 0, 0) = \bar{\vek t}(P)\), on the left face likewise \((-\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(y, z), 0, 0)\), on the lateral surface \(\vek 0\). The field \(\tilde{\vek u}\) is therefore a solution for the same load \((\vek 0, \bar{\vek t})\) (requirement 1 of the definition holds, because \(S\) maps \(K\) to itself).
  6. By lemma 4.134, point 2, for the body \(K\) in one piece and the empty \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\), the difference \(\tilde{\vek u} - \vek u\) is a small rigid displacement: under it the piece only moves and turns as a whole.
  1. By lemma 4.135 with \(\alpha = \beta = \tfrac12\) the field \(\vek w = \tfrac12(\vek u + \tilde{\vek u})\) is a solution for the load \(\tfrac12(\vek 0, \bar{\vek t}) + \tfrac12(\vek 0, \bar{\vek t}) = (\vek 0, \bar{\vek t})\). The mirror field of \(\vek w\) is \(\tfrac12(\tilde{\vek u} + \tilde{\tilde{\vek u}}) = \tfrac12(\tilde{\vek u} + \vek u) = \vek w\), because reflecting twice leaves the field unchanged: \(\tilde{\tilde u}_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y, z) = -\tilde u_{\mathrm{x}}(2x_0 - x, y, z) = u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y, z)\), and likewise for the other two components.
  2. From \(\tilde w_{\mathrm{x}} = w_{\mathrm{x}}\) at the point \((x_0, y, z)\), where \(SP = P\), it follows that \(w_{\mathrm{x}} = -w_{\mathrm{x}}\), so \(w_{\mathrm{x}}(x_0, y, z) = 0\). The points of the cross section at \(x_0\) in panel (a) do not move in the direction \(x\) under the field \(\vek w\) and stay in the plane \(x = x_0\). In the figure, on the \(x\) axis at \(x_0\): half the sum of the bulge to the right and the equally large bulge to the left is a plane cross section, the two bulges cancel.
  1. The field \(\vek r = \vek u - \vek w = \tfrac12(\vek u - \tilde{\vek u})\) is \(-\tfrac12\) times the small rigid displacement \(\tilde{\vek u} - \vek u\) of step 6, so again a small rigid displacement \(\vek r(P) = \delta\vek a + \delta\boldsymbol\varphi \times \overrightarrow{OP}\). In panel (b), under \(\vek u\) a point \(P\) of the cross section at \(x_0\) goes to the point \(P + \vek w(P) + \delta\vek a + \delta\boldsymbol\varphi \times \overrightarrow{OP}\). The points \(P + \vek w(P)\) lie in the plane \(x = x_0\), and the map \(Q \mapsto Q + \delta\vek a + \delta\boldsymbol\varphi \times \overrightarrow{OQ}\) is affine, its linear part \(\vek v \mapsto \vek v + \delta\boldsymbol\varphi\times\vek v\) has determinant \(1 + |\delta\boldsymbol\varphi|^2 > 0\) and is invertible, so it maps a plane to a plane. A singular map, as in panel (c), would squeeze the plane to a point, which a rigid displacement cannot do. The point \(P + \vek u(P)\) differs from the image of the point \(P + \vek w(P)\) under this map by \(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi \times \vek w(P)\), which is of second order in the small displacements. The cross section after the deformation is therefore plane up to corrections of second order.

Source: The proof is not from a source. The same statement is confirmed by SADD Example 8.2 (PDF p. 171-172): for a beam with moments at the ends, which are satisfied only in the statically equivalent sense, the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{x}}\) is linear and all other components are zero, plane sections really stay plane (SADD (8.1.22), PDF p. 172), and the solution is not necessarily valid near the ends of the beam, when the stress there is not linear.

In four-point bending we assume the hypothesis of the lemma, that on every cross section there is only a normal stress with the same distribution, and we do not prove it: we know only the internal forces \(N = 0\), \(V = 0\) and \(M = 500\) N m, not the stresses. The assumption is supported by SADD Example 8.2, where exactly such a stress is the exact solution of the plane problem (PDF p. 171-172), valid far from the places where the load acts. How far? The load presses on a small area and spreads over the cross section only at some distance from it, like the force of a bar pulled by a pin (Saint-Venant's principle, figure 2.16). For a strip a later chapter on the boundary value problem of elasticity computes that the deviation dies out within one to two depths of the cross section. With the depth 40 mm this is 40 to 80 mm from each load, so of the 1000 mm of the middle part at least 840 mm is in pure bending. The proof showed that a cross section cannot bulge in pure bending: the mirror field would bulge it to the other side, and the solution is unique up to a rigid displacement. The proof does not say how a cross section tilts and how the axis curves. We show this in the rest of the section. Symmetry helps only for a load where the stress is the same on all cross sections. For a beam that also has a shear force we cannot mirror cross sections into other cross sections, and symmetry is not enough.

5.3.2 Assumptions of the Euler-Bernoulli beam

From history

Mariotte had to design the pipelines supplying water to the Palace of Versailles, and this made him interested in the bending strength of beams (TIMO, PDF p. 29). Experimenting with wooden and glass rods, he found that Galileo's theory gives too large a breaking force (TIMO, PDF p. 29). In tension tests of various materials he saw that the elongations are proportional to the forces (TIMO, PDF p. 29). We have already met his correction of Galileo's calculation in section 1.1 (figure 1.16). Here we tell how he arrived at it and how he tested it. He started with a lever (figure 5.50).

First he took a lever supported at the point \(C\) (panel (1)). Three equal weights of 12 pounds hung on its left arm, at distances of 4, 2 and 1 foot from \(C\). They are balanced by a force \(F\) on the right arm, 12 feet from \(C\). From the balance of moments about \(C\) (TIMO, PDF p. 29)

\[12 \cdot F = 12 \cdot 4 + 12 \cdot 2 + 12 \cdot 1 , \qquad F = 7\ \text{pounds} .\]

If the force \(F\) is increased, the lever turns about \(C\). The displacements of the points where the weights hang are proportional to their distances from \(C\), but the forces stay equal, all three 12 pounds (TIMO, PDF p. 29).

Mariotte then replaced the weights by three identical wires, each of strength 12 pounds (panel (2)). When the lever turns from its horizontal position, the points where the wires are attached rise in proportion to their distance from \(C\), and the wires lengthen by as much. When the force in the farthest wire reaches 12 pounds, the forces in the other two are proportional to their elongations, so 6 and 3 pounds (TIMO, PDF p. 29). The breaking force is therefore (TIMO, PDF p. 29-30)

\[12 \cdot F = 12 \cdot 4 + 6 \cdot 2 + 3 \cdot 1 , \qquad F = 5{,}25\ \text{pounds} ,\]

not the 7 pounds of the rigid lever. The force in each wire is thus proportional to its distance from the point of rotation, because it is proportional to the elongation, and the elongation to the distance.

He reasoned in the same way for a cantilever. At fracture the cross section turns about the point \(D\), and the forces in the longitudinal fibres are in the same proportion as their distances from \(D\), like the forces in the wires (TIMO, PDF p. 30). What this means for the breaking moment is shown in figure 5.51.

We draw the cross section of depth \(h\) twice, both times with the point of rotation \(D\) on the lower edge. With Galileo (section 1.1) all fibres carry the same force, so their sum is the absolute strength \(S\), the force the cross section carries in tension, and it acts in the middle, \(h/2\) from \(D\). The moment about \(D\) is \(S h/2\). With Mariotte the forces in the fibres grow from zero at \(D\) to the largest value at the edge. Their sum is therefore \(S/2\), and the moment about \(D\) is \((S/2)(2h/3) = S h/3\) (TIMO, PDF p. 30). One half, because the average of forces that grow evenly from zero to the largest is half the largest. The lever arm \(2h/3\) is longer than \(h/2\), because the largest forces are also farthest from \(D\). The breaking moment \(S h/3\) is thus two thirds of Galileo's \(S h/2\), and the breaking force is in the same ratio (TIMO, PDF p. 30-31).

He tested his formula on wooden cylindrical bars \(1/4\) inch in diameter. In tension the bar held \(S = 330\) pounds, and as a cantilever with an arm of \(l = 4\) inches it broke at \(L = 6\) pounds (TIMO, PDF p. 31). On the number line of the ratio \(S : L\) this is \(S : L = 330/6 = 55\). The prediction follows from the balance of moments at fracture, \(L\,l = S h/3\), so \(S : L = 3l/h\) (TIMO, PDF p. 30). For the depth \(h\) Mariotte took the diameter, although he had derived the formula for a rectangular cross section (TIMO, PDF p. 31, footnote 2). His formula therefore gave \(3 \cdot 4/(1/4) = 48\), and Galileo's \(S : L = 2l/h\) gave \(2 \cdot 4/(1/4) = 32\) (TIMO, PDF p. 31), in the ratio \(48 : 32 = 3 : 2\), which is exactly the ratio of Galileo's and Mariotte's breaking moments. He blamed the difference on time: under a lasting force the bar in tension might have broken already at 300 pounds (TIMO, PDF p. 31), and the ratio \(S : L\) would be \(300/6 = 50\), closer to 48. A repetition with glass rods again showed that his formula predicts better than Galileo's (TIMO, PDF p. 31).

So the idea entered the thinking about bending that the cross section turns as a whole and that the fibres lengthen in proportion to their distance from the point of rotation, like the wires at the lever.

We are looking for how the points of a beam move when it bends. Mariotte's cross section, which turns as a whole, is the first answer. Before we write it as an assumption, let us see what we already know about cross sections.

For the cantilever of section 5.1.3 the shear force is \(V = -500\) N in every cross section and the moment changes along the beam. The lemma of the previous section rests on the mirror symmetry about the plane of a cross section, which is missing here: the size of the moment grows towards the clamp, so the mirror image of a piece of the cantilever about the plane of a cross section is not the same piece with the same load. The lemma therefore says nothing. What do we take as a start? Let us see what is true in pure bending: the normal lines stayed straight (lemma 5.13), and in the strip they also kept their length and stayed perpendicular to the fibres. Let us assume that all this also holds for a beam with a shear force. These are the three assumptions of Euler and Bernoulli (EP, p. 73): after the deformation the normal lines of the beam remain

  • (a) straight,
  • (b) of the same length,
  • (c) perpendicular to the deformed axis of the beam.

The figure shows the normal line before the load and after it next to the deformed axis, with which it makes a right angle. The wrong possibilities are crossed out: the curved normal line violates (a), the lengthened one, whose half has grown by \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\,h/2\), violates (b), and the normal line that is not perpendicular to the axis, but is tilted from the perpendicular by the angle \(\gamma\), violates (c).

CM (PDF p. 308) writes assumptions (b) and (c) with strains: that the normal line keeps its length means \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0\) (there is no elongation along the normal line, the elongation of a half is zero), and that it stays perpendicular to the axis means \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\) (the right angle between a longitudinal fibre and the normal line does not change, the angle \(\gamma = 2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\) is zero, section 1.17.3). We also assume that the points do not move in the direction \(z\), \(u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\), and that the right angles between the direction \(z\) and the directions \(x\) and \(y\) stay right, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\). For each assumption we say why it holds here, when it does not hold and how much it changes the result. We take the numbers for the cantilever of section 5.1.3, and we collect everything again in a later section on the limits of beam theory.

We read the costs of assumptions (b) and (c) in figure 5.53. On the horizontal axis is the slenderness \(L/h\), the length of the beam divided by the depth of the cross section, and on the vertical one the share by which the deflection differs from the deflection of the model. On both axes an equal step means equal multiplication, from 1 to 10 is as far as from 10 to 100. A share that falls with the square of the ratio \(h/L\) therefore becomes a hundred times smaller when \(L/h\) grows ten times, and it is a straight line that falls two steps down for one step to the right. The vertical grey line is our cantilever, \(L/h = 1000\ \text{mm}/40\ \text{mm} = 25\), and the horizontal one the limit of 1 %.

  • Straightness (a) needs no reason of its own: it follows from (b) and (c), as we prove later in this section. In pure bending it is also proved (lemma 5.13). Where (c) does not hold, the normal line is no longer straight: the shear force warps the cross section, because the shear strain is not the same over the depth, zero at the edges and largest in the middle (a later section on the accuracy of the shear formula). This error is part of the cost of assumption (c).
  • Equal length (b) means that we neglect the transverse strains, we take the elongation of half the normal line as zero. Why this is allowed: by lemma 3.12 the transverse strain in a fibre under uniaxial stress is \(-\nu\) times the longitudinal one, so at the clamp of the cantilever at most \(0{,}3 \cdot 4{,}78 \cdot 10^{-4} = 1{,}43 \cdot 10^{-4}\). A normal line 40 mm long therefore changes on each half by less than \(20\ \text{mm} \cdot 1{,}43 \cdot 10^{-4} = 2{,}9\ \mu\text{m}\). How much this changes the result is estimated by the exact plane solution of a simply supported beam under a uniform load (SADD (8.1.37), PDF p. 177). In it Poisson's ratio \(\nu\) appears only in a term that increases the deflection of the middle by the fraction \(\tfrac65\,\nu\,(h/L)^2\), so at \(\nu = 0{,}3\) by \(0{,}36\,(h/L)^2\), by 0,058 % at \(L/h = 25\) and by 0,014 % at \(L/h = 50\). This is an estimate of the size, not the exact cost of (b): when we change \(\nu\) at the same \(E\), the shear modulus \(G = E/(2(1 + \nu))\) of equation (3.22) changes too, so this term also holds part of the cost of assumption (c). When it does not hold: the fraction grows with the square of the ratio \(h/L\), and for a short deep beam with \(L/h = 2\) the same formula gives already 9 %. How the transverse strains show in the cross section we say at the end of the section.
  • Perpendicularity (c) means \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\), the angle \(\gamma\) is zero. For the strip of section 5.3.1 it holds. With a shear force the normal lines would tilt relative to the axis by the shear strain, which is small for a slender beam: for the cantilever of section 5.1.3 the largest shear stress, which we compute later in the chapter, is 0,9375 MPa (1 % of the largest longitudinal stress 93,75 MPa from section 5.2.1), the shear strain is \(\gamma = \tau/G = 0{,}9375\ \text{MPa}/75{,}38\ \text{GPa} = 1{,}2 \cdot 10^{-5}\), which is 0,1 % of the slope 0,012 at the free end (we compute it later in the chapter) and 2,6 % of the largest longitudinal strain \(4{,}78 \cdot 10^{-4}\). How much it changes the result: the shear strain increases the deflection of the free end by 0,125 % of the bending part. When it does not hold: the fraction grows with the square of the ratio \(h/L\), because at the same width the shear part of the deflection is proportional to \(L/h\) and the bending part to \((L/h)^3\): the shear stiffness of the cross section grows with the depth \(h\) and the bending stiffness with \(h^3\), while the deflection grows with the length \(L\) in shear and with \(L^3\) in bending. At \(L/h \approx 8{,}8\) it reaches 1 %, and for the same bar shortened to \(L = 80\) mm (\(L/h = 2\)) 19,5 % (a later section on the beam with shear deformation).
  • Plane deformation, \(u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\). Why it holds here: the loads and supports lie in the plane \(xy\), and the cross section is symmetric about the axis \(y\), so the mirror image of the beam about the plane \(xy\) is the same beam with the same load. Since the solution is unique (lemma 4.134), two points that lie as mirror images move as mirror images: the beam does not bend sideways, and the points of the plane \(xy\) do not leave it. When it does not hold: for a cross section without this symmetry, for example in the shape of the letter L, the beam under a vertical load also bends sideways, and we need the principal axes of the cross section (later in this chapter). How much it changes the result: for a symmetric cross section only the Poisson effect remains, which narrows or widens the cross section a little across its width, at the clamp of the cantilever by at most \(1{,}43\ \mu\text{m}\) on each side. It is the same effect as in (b) and has the same cost.

We write the assumptions as a definition.

Definition 5.14 Euler-Bernoulli beam model (Euler-Bernoullijev model nosilca)

Let a beam with the axis \(x\) and a rectangular cross section \(|y| \le h/2\), \(|z| \le b/2\) be loaded and supported in the plane \(xy\) (definition 5.1). The displacements of the beam in the Euler-Bernoulli beam model (Euler-Bernoullijev model nosilca) are a displacement field \((u_{\mathrm{x}}, u_{\mathrm{y}}, u_{\mathrm{z}})\) which is continuous on the whole beam, whose components have continuous partial derivatives up to second order at the interior points of every piece of the beam between two neighbouring point loads (or of the whole beam if there are none), and which at every interior point of the beam satisfies

\[u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0 , \qquad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = 0 , \qquad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0 , \qquad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0 , \qquad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0 . \tag{5.15}\]

The strains are the components of the small strain tensor (definition 1.121), computed by (1.71). The conditions \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\) are assumptions (b) and (c), and the conditions \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\) together with \(u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\) mean that the deformation is plane and the displacements \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) do not change with \(z\).

Source: EP (p. 73), CM (PDF p. 308). Writing assumption (a) as a consequence of the others is ours (the theorem on the displacement field of a beam later in this section). EP and CM do not write \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\) separately, because they choose the displacement field (EP (2.5.1), CM (7.3.22)) already in a form without dependence on \(z\).

5.3.3 Displacement field of a beam

The conditions (5.15) do not write assumption (a) about straight normal lines. Is it hidden in them? What displacement field follows from them? The answer is given by the same procedure as for the strip in section 1.18.3. There we concluded from \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0\) that all points of a normal line move the same way in the direction \(y\), and from \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\) that \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial y = -\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial x\). We use the same here. The second derivatives of the displacements are continuous only on each piece of the beam between two point loads, since at a point force the shear force jumps (lemma 5.12), and with it, as we will see later in the chapter, the third derivative of the deflection, which enters the second derivative of the horizontal displacement. The displacement field, however, is continuous across those points because the beam does not break. We therefore apply the theorem on each piece separately.

Let us draw in advance what the theorem will claim and how we will prove it (figure 5.54). On the left is the cross section with the axes \(z\) and \(y\). The conditions \(u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = 0\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\) say that the displacements \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) do not change along the blue segment in the direction \(z\), so a view from the side is enough. In the middle, seen from the side, are the normal line at \(x\) and the neighbouring normal line at \(x + t\), and on each a point on the axis and a point at the height \(y\). Because of \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0\) both points of the normal line at \(x\) rise by the same amount, \(v(x)\), and both points of the neighbouring normal line by \(v(x + t)\). On the right is the horizontal displacement of the normal line at \(x\). The normal line before the load is grey and dashed, and the point of the axis moves by \(u(x)\). If all its points moved the same way, the normal line would end on the second dashed line. Because of \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\), however, the horizontal displacement falls with the height: per unit height it decreases by \(v'(x)\), the derivative of the deflection, which we will call the slope of the axis. At the height \(y\) the point therefore lags by \(y\,v'(x)\) and its displacement is \(u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y) = u(x) - y\,v'(x)\). The normal line ends on the orange line, which is straight.

Theorem 5.15 Displacement field of a beam (polje pomikov nosilca)

Let \(B = \{(x, y, z) : x_1 < x < x_2, |y| < h/2, |z| < b/2\}\) be the set of interior points of the piece of the beam between neighbouring point loads at \(x = x_1\) and \(x = x_2\) (or of the whole beam, \(x_1 = 0\), \(x_2 = L\), if there are none) and let the displacement field \((u_{\mathrm{x}}, u_{\mathrm{y}}, u_{\mathrm{z}})\) satisfy at every point of \(B\) the conditions (5.15) of the Euler-Bernoulli model, with continuous partial derivatives of first and second order of the components \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\). Then there exist functions \(u(x)\) and \(v(x)\) on the interval \((x_1, x_2)\), with continuous derivatives up to second order, such that at every point of \(B\)

\[u_{\mathrm{x}} = u(x) - y\,v'(x) , \qquad u_{\mathrm{y}} = v(x) , \qquad u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0 . \tag{5.16}\]

The only nonzero component of the strain is

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = u'(x) - y\,v''(x) . \tag{5.17}\]
Proof

We must show that \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) does not depend on \(y\) and \(z\), that \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) grows linearly with \(y\) and that the coefficient of \(y\) is the derivative of the function \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\). We use corollary 1.86 on a function with zero derivative along segments inside \(B\), as in steps 1 to 3 of the proof for the strip in section 1.18.3.

  1. From \(u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = \tfrac12(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial z + \partial u_{\mathrm{z}} / \partial y) = 0\) it follows that \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial z = 0\) at every point of \(B\). By corollary 1.86 on the segment from \((x, y, 0)\) to \((x, y, z)\), which lies in \(B\), we have \(u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y, z) = u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y, 0)\). Likewise from \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = 0\) it follows that \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial z = 0\) and \(u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y, z) = u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y, 0)\). The displacements are functions of \(x\) and \(y\) only.
  2. From \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial y = 0\), by corollary 1.86 on the segment from \((x, 0)\) to \((x, y)\) it follows that \(u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y) = u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, 0) =: v(x)\). All points of a normal line move the same way in the direction \(y\).
  3. Therefore \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial x\) at the point \((x, y)\) is the limit of the difference quotients \((u_{\mathrm{y}}(x + t, y) - u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y))/t = (v(x + t) - v(x))/t\), which are equal to those at the point \((x, 0)\), as \(t \to 0\) (definition 1.5). So \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial x = v'(x)\) for every \(y\), and similarly \(\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial x^2 = v''(x)\). The derivatives \(v'\) and \(v''\) are continuous, because they are continuous derivatives of the component \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\).
  4. From \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\), by (1.71) it follows that \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial y = -\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial x = -v'(x)\): the horizontal displacement falls by \(v'(x)\) per unit height.
  5. At constant \(x\) the derivative of the function \(y \mapsto u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y) + y\,v'(x)\) with respect to \(y\) equals \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial y + v'(x) = 0\) by step 4 (lemma 1.82 on the rules of differentiation). By corollary 1.86 on the segment from \((x, 0)\) to \((x, y)\) this function is constant, equal to its value at \(y = 0\), \(u(x) := u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, 0)\). So \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = u(x) - y\,v'(x)\). The function \(u\) has continuous derivatives up to second order as a restriction of the continuously differentiable component \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\).
  6. We compute the strains from (1.71) and the displacement field: \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial x = u'(x) - y\,v''(x)\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\) by assumption, and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{z}} / \partial z = 0\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = 0\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\) by assumption.

Source: CM (7.3.22), (7.3.23) (PDF p. 308-309), EP (2.5.1), (2.5.2) (p. 73). The sources write the displacement field as an assumption and compute the strains from it. The proof that the field follows from \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\) is not from the sources, it uses steps 1 to 3 of section 1.18.3.

The proof showed that the straightness of a normal line follows from \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\): a point at the height \(y\) moves by \(-y\,v'(x)\) in the direction \(x\), so linearly with the height, and the normal line stays straight. This is assumption (a), which therefore did not need to be written separately. Each condition did one step. Without \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0\) step 2 falls away and the points of a normal line need not rise by the same amount, without \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\) step 4 falls away and the normal line may tilt differently from the axis and curve. When the two conditions are justified and what they cost, we say with numbers in section 5.3.4. What this field means is shown by figure 5.55. The cantilever is clamped on the left, the blackwhite grid is the state before the load and the orange one after it: the longitudinal fibres curve, the normal lines stay straight, the axes \(x\) and \(y\) show the directions. The displacement of the axis is \((u(x), v(x))\), at the free end the deflection \(v\) is drawn. The circle with the arrow leads to the enlarged free end on the right, which we will read together with the numbers. The displacement \(u(x)\) is the displacement of the bar of chapter 4: a point of the axis, \(y = 0\), moves by \(u(x)\) in the direction \(x\). The displacement \(v(x)\) is the deflection of the axis (poves osi), the displacement in the direction \(y\), which is the same for all points of a normal line.

So a point at the height \(y\) moves, besides the displacement of the axis, by \(-y\,v'(x)\) in the direction \(x\) (figure 5.56). On the left is the normal line before the load with the point \(P\) above the point of the axis. On the right is the normal line after the load, which has stayed perpendicular to the axis, the original position of the normal line is dashed, and the point \(P'\) has moved horizontally by \(y\,v'\). What is \(v'(x)\)? It is the slope of the axis: by how much the axis rises per unit length, a ratio of two lengths, a number without a unit. For the cantilever of section 5.1.3 the slope at the free end is \(v' = -0{,}01196\) (we compute it later in the chapter): the axis falls there by \(0{,}01196\) per unit length. A point at the top of the cross section, at \(y = 20\) mm, therefore moves at the free end in the direction \(x\) by \(-0{,}020\ \text{m} \cdot (-0{,}01196) = 0{,}239\) mm, measured from the normal line as it would be if it only moved down to the normal line as it is. A point at the bottom of the cross section moves back by the same amount. This is a horizontal displacement due to the slope, without length changes of the axis.

The normal line stays perpendicular to the axis. Why? Let us read the directions from figure 5.56. The axis rises by \(v'\) over the length 1, so its direction is \((1, v')\). The normal line shifts by \(-y\,v'\) at the height \(y\), so its direction is \((-y\,v', y)\), and after dividing by \(y\) it is \((-v', 1)\). The scalar product of the two directions is

\[1 \cdot (-v') + v' \cdot 1 = 0 ,\]

so the directions are perpendicular. The normal line tilts by the same ratio as the axis: the axis rises by \(v'\) per unit length, the normal line shifts back by \(v'\) per unit height. Here we left out the stretching of the axis. With it the direction of the axis is \((1 + u', v')\) and the scalar product is \(-u'\,v'\), a product of two small numbers, and zero for a cantilever without axial force, because \(u = 0\). The small rotation tensor of section 1.18.3 says the same. For the field (5.16) we have by (1.62)

\[\omega_{\mathrm{xy}} = \tfrac12\Big(\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial x}\Big) = \tfrac12\,(-v' - v') = -v'(x) .\]

In a rigid rotation about the axis \(z\) by a small angle \(\varphi\) in radians (the ratio of the arc length to the radius), counterclockwise, the displacement of a point is \(\vek u = \varphi\,(-y, x, 0)\), and \(\omega_{\mathrm{xy}} = \tfrac12(-\varphi - \varphi) = -\varphi\). The material near the axis therefore rotates by \(\varphi = v'(x)\). For the cantilever of section 5.1.3 the normal line at the free end rotates clockwise relative to the vertical position, because \(v' < 0\).

The axis curves, the slope changes along it. How strongly? We already saw the answer for the strip in section 1.18.3: the second derivative \(\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial x^2\) tells how fast the slope changes along a fibre. For a beam this is \(v''(x)\) and we name this quantity in the definition.

Definition 5.16 Curvature of the axis (ukrivljenost osi)

Let \(v\) be a twice continuously differentiable function on every open interval between neighbouring point loads in \((0, L)\) (or on \((0, L)\) if there are none), the deflection of the axis of a beam. Let the slope be small, \(v'^2 \le 10^{-2}\). The curvature of the axis (ukrivljenost osi) is the function \(\kappa(x) = v''(x)\) with the unit \(\text{m}^{-1}\), which gives one number for every value of \(x\). The radius of curvature (polmer ukrivljenosti) is \(R(x) = 1/|\kappa(x)|\), where \(\kappa(x) \ne 0\).

Source: CM (7.3.22) (PDF p. 308) has the slope \(\mathrm{d}v/\mathrm{d}x\) in the displacement field. SADD (D.7), (D.8) (PDF p. 578) has the curvature \(1/\rho \approx d^2 v/dx^2\) for small slopes. The exact curvature of the graph of the function \(v\) is \(v''/(1 + v'^2)^{3/2}\) (SADD, PDF p. 578, the formula below (D.7)).

Sign: \(\kappa > 0\) where the slope increases and the axis curves upward (the shape of the letter U), \(\kappa < 0\) where the slope decreases. For the strip of section 1.18.3 we have \(v = -x^2/(2R)\) and \(\kappa = -1/R = -0{,}1\ \text{m}^{-1}\), radius of curvature 10 m, as with \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = y/R\). Figure 5.57 shows why we call this curvature. At the top is the axis at the point \(x = 0\) and its osculating circle with its centre on the normal of the axis and radius \(R\). Below, next to the axes, is the slope \(v'\) as a function of \(x\), and the blue triangle shows its steepness \(\kappa\). On a small piece of the axis the curvature \(\kappa\) means the change of the slope per unit length, and a circular arc of radius \(R\) has the same change of the slope, \(1/R\). The bound \(v'^2 \le 10^{-2}\) is chosen because at it the exact curvature differs from \(v''\) by the factor \((1 + v'^2)^{-3/2} \ge 0{,}985\), so by at most 1,5 %. For the cantilever of section 5.1.3 the largest slope is \(v' = -0{,}01196\), \(v'^2 = 1{,}43 \cdot 10^{-4}\), the factor there is \(1{,}0001430^{-3/2} = 0{,}99979\), the error 0,02 %. For the simply supported beam the largest slope at the support is \(0{,}01594\) (we compute it later in the chapter), \(v'^2 = 2{,}5 \cdot 10^{-4}\), the error 0,04 %.

The field (5.16) has three parts: \(u(x)\) is the bar of chapter 4, \(v(x)\) the deflection and \(-y\,v'(x)\) the tilt of the cross section. The strain (5.17) thus has two parts: \(u'(x)\) is the axial strain, the same for all \(y\), and \(-y\,v''(x) = -y\,\kappa(x)\) is the bending strain, which grows linearly with the height. For the strip of section 1.18.3 we have \(u = 0\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = -y\,v'' = y/R\), as in section 5.3.1. At positive curvature the upper fibres get shorter, because the axis curves upward and the inner side of a bend is shorter than the outer, and the upper fibres are on the inner side. For the cantilever of figure 5.55 the curvature is negative and it is the other way round. The fibre at the top starts in the clamp, which does not move, and its end moves by \(0{,}239\) mm, so the fibre is longer by that much. The fibre at the bottom is shorter by \(0{,}239\) mm, and the axis keeps its length, because \(u = 0\).

5.3.4 What we neglect here

Assumption (b), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0\), does not agree with the material. In a fibre pulled only by a longitudinal stress the transverse strain by lemma 3.12 is \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = -\nu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), which is not zero: a bar that we stretch gets thinner. Take the clamp of the cantilever of section 5.1.3, where the stress at the top of the cross section is \(+93{,}75\) MPa (section 5.2.1). The longitudinal strain there is \(93{,}75\ \text{MPa}/196\ \text{GPa} = 4{,}78 \cdot 10^{-4}\), and the transverse strain is \(-0{,}3 \cdot 4{,}78 \cdot 10^{-4} = -1{,}43 \cdot 10^{-4}\) in the direction \(y\) and the same in the direction \(z\). What this does to the cross section is shown by figure 5.58. The axes \(z\) and \(y\) show the width and the height of the cross section, the blackwhite rectangle is the cross section before the load, the orange figure the cross section after it. The width, 20 mm, decreases at the top by \(2{,}87\ \mu\text{m}\), by \(1{,}43\ \mu\text{m}\) on each side, and at the bottom, where the longitudinal strain is opposite, it increases by the same on each side. The rectangular cross section takes the shape of a trapezoid.

The height changes too. Since \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = -\kappa\,y\), we have \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = -\nu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \nu\,\kappa\,y\) and the same \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\). From both, and from the fact that the cross section has no shear strain \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}\), the displacement \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = v + \nu\,\kappa\,(y^2 - z^2)/2\) follows (the computation is in a later section on the limits of beam theory). At the clamp \(\kappa = -0{,}0239\ \text{m}^{-1}\). On the normal line through the middle of the cross section, \(z = 0\), the upper end point moves down relative to its middle by \(\nu\,|\kappa|\,h^2/8 = 0{,}3 \cdot 0{,}0239\ \text{m}^{-1} \cdot (0{,}04\ \text{m})^2/8 = 1{,}4\ \mu\text{m}\), and the lower end point likewise down by \(1{,}4\ \mu\text{m}\), because the upper half of the normal line shortens and the lower half lengthens by the same amount. The end points move by the same amount, so the length of the normal line does not change, and the normal line stays straight, because these displacements are along it. What curves are the horizontal lines of the cross section: the middle of an edge is lower than its corners by \(\nu\,|\kappa|\,b^2/8 = 0{,}3 \cdot 0{,}0239\ \text{m}^{-1} \cdot (0{,}02\ \text{m})^2/8 = 0{,}36\ \mu\text{m}\). The lines across the width thus curve upward, like the letter U, while the axis of the beam curves downward at the same place. We call this anticlastic curvature (antiklastična ukrivljenost), opposite curvature: the upper surface of the beam is bent one way along the axis and the other way across the width, like a saddle.

The model thus requires \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0\), so the cross section stays rectangular, and a material under uniaxial stress has \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = -\nu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), so the cross section turns into the orange shape. We cannot have both at once. When we compute the stress from the strain we will keep \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\), so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), and we use \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0\) only for the form of the displacement field. CM (PDF p. 309) admits this: in the relation \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) it takes \(\nu = 0\), but in the relation \(2G = E/(1 + \nu)\) it does not.

Why this choice? The side surfaces of the beam are free, nothing presses on them, so the fibres are allowed to narrow, as the cross section narrows in the figure, and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) holds. The numbers say what the opposite choice would give. If we put \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) into Hooke's law (3.28), the stress would be \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = (\lambda + 2\mu)\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), and by (3.26)

\[\lambda + 2\mu = \frac{E\,(1 - \nu)}{(1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)} = 1{,}346\,E \quad \text{at } \nu = 0{,}3 .\]

A fibre that is not allowed to narrow at its sides resists stretching more. The beam would be 34,6 % stiffer and the deflection of the free end of the cantilever would be \(7{,}972\ \text{mm}/1{,}346 = 5{,}92\) mm instead of \(7{,}972\) mm (we compute this value later in the chapter). The choice \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), on the other hand, agrees with a more exact computation: for the simply supported beam with uniform load of section 5.1.3, which has \(L/h = 50\), the term with Poisson's ratio in the deflection by plane elasticity (SADD (8.1.37), PDF p. 177) contributes the share \(\tfrac{12}{5} \cdot \tfrac{\nu}{2} \cdot (h/L)^2 = 0{,}014\) % of the deflection. What the model neglects is small in the strains too: the transverse strain at the clamp of the cantilever is less than \(1{,}5 \cdot 10^{-4}\), and the longitudinal one is \(4{,}78 \cdot 10^{-4}\). The choice fails for a very wide beam, a strip of sheet metal with \(b\) much larger than \(h\). There the fibres cannot narrow freely in the direction \(z\), and with \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0\) Hooke's law (3.27) gives \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}/(1 - \nu^2) = 1{,}099\,E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), a stiffness larger by 9,9 % (chapter 18). The errors of all the assumptions we collect in a later section on the limits of beam theory.

The second assumption, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\), which forbids the normal line to tilt differently from the axis, is likewise only approximate when the shear force is \(V \ne 0\). Hooke's law connects the shear stress with the shear strain, and from \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\) it follows that \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 2G\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\), so also the shear force \(V = \int \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,dA = 0\). The shear force that equilibrium requires would have to be zero. EP (p. 74) calls this an inconsistency of Bernoulli's hypothesis, CM (PDF p. 309) a flaw. Therefore we do not compute the shear stresses from Hooke's law but from the shear force (a later section on shear stresses), and we allow the shear strain, which \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\) neglects, in the section on the beam with shear deformation. For the cantilever of section 5.1.3 the largest shear strain is \(\gamma = \tau_{\max}/G = 1{,}2 \cdot 10^{-5}\), and its contribution to the deflection of the free end is 0,125 % of the bending part, but for the same bar shortened to \(L = 80\) mm it is 19,5 % (we compute it in a later section on the beam with shear deformation).
