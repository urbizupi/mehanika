Chapter 5 Beam theory (teorija nosilcev)

Chapter 5Lecture: Beam theory (teorija nosilcev)

1 The cut: three numbers instead of all the stresses

Today we begin the theory of beams (teorija nosilcev). A beam is a body so slender that at every step one number per cross section is enough.

The road we will walk in four parts is this chain. We draw it for a beam on two supports, from \(0\) to \(L\). It starts at the load \(q\). Equilibrium turns it into the shear force \(V\). Equilibrium once more, then the material and the cross section turn the shear force into the moment \(M\) and the moment into the curvature \(v''\). Geometry turns the curvature into the deflection \(v\). Today we take the first step, from the load to the moment. That step is equilibrium.

Let us start with a question. First the axes: \(x\) to the right, \(y\) up. We take a bar and build one of its ends into a wall. At the free end we hang a weight that pulls the bar down with the force \(\vek F_{\mathrm{p}}\). A force is what the weight does to the bar: it has a size, here \(500\) N, a direction, down, and a point where it acts, the free end. The weight hangs one metre from the wall. How does the wall "feel" it at all? And why does the bar, when we overload it, break at the wall and not under the weight, where the force acts?

We get the answer with a single move. We cut the bar in thought at \(x\), and we measure the distances \(x\) and \(L - x\) from the wall. The right piece, the one with the weight, we draw on its own. Now only the cut face holds it. On that face we mark the centre of the cross section \(C\). The part at the wall must hold the cut-off part up with a force \(-V\) and turn it back with a moment \(-M\). The force at the end is the same as before, and it is at a distance \(L - x\) from the centre \(C\).

Here is today's claim in one sentence: the cut-off part is at rest, so the cut face must act on it with a force \(V\) that cancels the weight and with a moment \(M\) that cancels the weight times the lever arm, and the lever arm is longest at the wall. Three things in this claim need their why. Why may we cut? Why are three numbers enough? Why is the moment largest at the wall?

1.1 Why may we cut the beam in thought?

Look once more at the cut-off part. The whole beam is at rest. Is this piece at rest too? Of course, it is part of the beam, and what is at rest does not accelerate. For every piece that does not accelerate, Newton's law holds: the sum of all forces on it is zero and the sum of all moments on it is zero.

So what did the cut take away? Before, the left part pulled and pushed the right one across the cut face. When we remove it in thought, we replace what it did with forces on the face. This is the free-body diagram (diagram prostega telesa) of the stress chapter: we keep the body we watch and replace its surroundings by forces that act as they did. On our piece these are the force \(-V\) and the moment \(-M\).

What does the word moment mean? Think of a seesaw. A child sitting far from the pivot turns it more than one sitting close. The turning a force causes is the force times its distance from the pivot, the moment of the force (moment sile). The distance is called the lever arm (ročica). On our piece the force at the end has the lever arm \(L - x\) about the centre \(C\). This is the ground we stand on: a piece at rest has zero net force and zero net moment, and a moment is force times lever arm. In the book: 5.2.2.

1.2 Why only three numbers when there is a stress at every point of the face?

The cut face is not one point. We draw a piece of the beam with the cut face on the right, the axes as before. At every point of the face a different small force per unit area acts, the stress vector \(\vek t\), drawn at five points. At the top it pushes into the piece, at the bottom it pulls out of it. Infinitely many arrows. Why would three numbers be enough?

Because Newton cares only about the sum of the forces and the sum of the moments. The cut-off piece does not know which fibre carries how much. Back to the seesaw: two children on the same side turn it exactly as one child with their joint weight, seated at the right place. So we may replace all these arrows by one piece on which one force and one moment act at the centre \(C\). We split the force into a part along the axis, the axial force \(N\), and a part across the axis, the shear force \(V\) (prečna sila). We add the moment \(M\) about \(C\), the bending moment (upogibni moment). A force is the same whether it acts at the centre or at the edge, but it turns differently. That is why we also need the moment.

What did we lose? The distribution: which fibres are squeezed and which are stretched. It comes back in the third part of the lecture, when we get the stress in every fibre from \(M\). Today we leave it. In the book: 5.2.1, lemma 5.9.

Why three and not six? In space a force has three parts and a moment has three parts. Here the first assumption comes in. All loads lie in the plane of symmetry of the cross section, the plane \(xy\), which splits the section into two mirror halves. Why it holds for us: the weight hangs vertically through the axis, the section is a rectangle. When it does not hold: a force that passes beside the axis or acts at a slant also twists the beam and bends it sideways. What it does to the result: of the six numbers three remain, \(N\), \(V\) and \(M\), the other three are zero. Twisting is the subject of chapter 16.

One more thing, the sign, since it is where we err most easily. The axes are the same as before. On the left part the cut face looks in the direction \(+x\). There we count \(N\) positive toward \(+x\), \(V\) up and \(M\) counterclockwise. The right part feels the opposite, \(N\), \(V\) and \(M\) point the other way, because what the left part does to the right one, the right one returns to the left. What does a positive \(M\) mean? The piece bends like a smile, the bottom fibres stretch, the top fibres shorten. The cantilever does exactly the opposite, so its moment will be negative. In the book: 5.25.

1.3 Why is the moment largest at the wall?

What acts on the cut-off part? Back to it, and let us write Newton. Vertically only the weight, \(500\) N down, and the force of the cut \(-V\) act on it. For the piece not to move, \(-V\) must be \(500\) N up, so \(V = -500\) N. How much does this depend on \(x\)? Not at all. Wherever we cut, the shear force is the same.

Now the turning about the centre \(C\). The weight has the lever arm \(L - x\), so it turns the piece clockwise with the moment \(500\,(L - x)\). The cut face must turn it back with a moment \(-M\) of the same size. That gives

\[M(x) = -F\,(L - x) .\]

In the book: (5.6).

Let us see what this means with three cuts. We draw the cantilever (konzola), built into the wall, with the weight \(F = 500\) N at the end and the length \(L = 1\) m. We cut at three places. The first piece is short. The force of the cut is \(500\) N up, the lever arm is \(0{,}25\) m, so the moment of the cut is \(125\) N m. The second piece is twice as long. The force is again \(500\) N, the lever arm \(0{,}5\) m, the moment \(250\) N m. The third piece is the whole beam, the cut is at the wall and the cut face is the wall itself. The force is still \(500\) N, but the lever arm is a whole metre and the moment \(500\) N m.

Now we have the answer to the opening question. The force is the same in every section, but the moment grows with the lever arm and is largest at the wall. The cross section of the bar is the same everywhere, so the largest moment also means the largest stress in the fibres, as the third part shows. That is why the bar breaks at the wall. We know the ground of this step from a long wrench: with the same hand force a long wrench turns a bolt harder than a short one.

We will not keep cutting forever. We do all cuts at once with a diagram. At the top we draw the beam with the clamp and the weight. Below it the axis \(x\) and the axis \(V\). Since the shear force is the same everywhere, its diagram is a horizontal line at \(-500\) N. At the bottom the axis \(M\) and the line of the moment: \(-500\) N m at the wall, \(-250\) N m in the middle, zero at the end \(L\). Every point of the diagram is one cut of the previous figure.

1.4 Why is the slope of the moment diagram the shear force?

On the moment diagram we see a straight line. Over one metre it rises from \(-500\) N m to zero, so by \(500\) N m per metre. And the shear force is \(-500\) N. A coincidence? No.

We cut out a thin slice of length \(\Delta x\), with the load \(q\) on it. On the left face the left neighbour holds it with the force \(-V\), on the right face the right neighbour with the force \(V + \Delta V\). These two forces are nearly equal and opposite, but they are not on the same line. There is a gap \(\Delta x\) between them, so they turn the slice with a moment of about \(V\,\Delta x\). What cancels this turning? Only the difference of the moments on the two faces, \(-M\) on the left and \(M + \Delta M\) on the right. The load on the slice turns it too, but its lever arm is at most \(\Delta x\), so its moment is at most \(q\,\Delta x \cdot \Delta x\) and shrinks faster than \(V\,\Delta x\) as the slice shrinks. So \(\Delta M \approx -V\,\Delta x\), and as the slice shrinks, \(M' = -V\). The same with forces: the load on the slice, \(q\,\Delta x\), can only be cancelled by a difference of the shear forces, so \(V' = -q\). The axial forces on both faces balance in the same way, \(N' = -q_{\mathrm{x}}\). In the book: (5.13), proof in 5.2.3.

Let us check on the cantilever. \(V = -500\) N, so the slope of the moment must be \(+500\) N m per metre. On the diagram it is exactly that. Between the wall and the weight there is no load, \(q = 0\), so the shear force is constant. The ground is the same as before: Newton, only on a smaller piece. What happens under a point force, where \(q\) is not continuous, the book shows in 5.2.3 with 5.39: the shear force jumps there and the moment gets a kink.

1.5 Back up

Now we go from the ground back to the top. The cut-off piece is at rest, so the sum of forces on it is zero and the shear force cancels the weight, \(500\) N. The sum of moments is zero, so the moment of the cut cancels the weight times the lever arm. As the cut slides toward the wall, the lever arm grows from \(0{,}25\) m to one metre. The wall carries \(500\) N and \(500\) N m, and the section at the wall is the one that works hardest. It is the lever arm of the first figure, with the cut pushed to the wall.

1.6 By hand

Lay a ruler on the edge of a table so that part of it sticks out. Hold it on the table with one hand and press the free end with the other. The holding hand is the wall. It feels two things: the ruler pushes it down and tries to turn it. Push the ruler further out over the edge and press equally hard. The push stays the same, the turning is larger. That is the moment at the wall, which grows with the lever arm. What does this view add? A moment is not only a calculation. We feel it in the wrist.

1.7 Galileo's question

Galileo asked the same question, only for a bar on two supports. We draw the bar on one and the other support and put a force \(F\) on it, at the distance \(a\) from the left and \(b\) from the right support. Where does the force break the bar most easily? Galileo found that the effect of the force on breaking is largest under the force and proportional to the product \(a\,b\). Why? The left support carries the force \(F\,b/(a + b)\), and its lever arm to the point under the force is \(a\). Below the bar we draw the moment diagram: the moment grows from the supports toward the force and has its peak \(F\,a\,b/(a + b)\) under the force. For a given sum \(a + b\) the product is largest when the two distances are equal, so the peak is highest when the force acts in the middle. And since the effect is smaller near the supports, Galileo noticed that the section can be made smaller toward the supports to save material. What does this view add? The question of where the moment is largest leads straight to the shape of a beam. In the book: the history block in 5.2.

1.8 Assumptions

The second assumption is hidden in everything we did. We measured the lever arm on the bar as it was before loading. We draw the wall and the axis of the bar before loading, \(L = 1\) m long. Under the weight the bar bends. The free end drops by \(7{,}972\) mm and at the same time moves a little toward the wall. The lever arm of the weight is now shorter, \(L - 0{,}038\) mm.

Why may we neglect this? The drop of the end is \(0{,}8\) % of the length, while the lever arm shortens by only \(0{,}004\) % of the length. Moments on the bent bar differ from ours by about \(4\cdot 10^{-5}\) of their value. When we may not: for large deflections, and when an axial force also acts on the bent beam. A compressive force can bend it even more, that is buckling (uklon). A tensile one stiffens it, the stiffening of a tensioned beam. What it does to the result: the axial force and the deflection do not couple, the diagrams of \(V\) and \(M\) depend only on the load, not on how the beam bends. In the book: assumption 3 in 5.15.1.

The third assumption: the beam is statically determinate (statično določen). What does that mean? Let us count the unknowns. The cantilever is a beam clamped in a wall. The wall holds it with three unknown reactions, two forces and a couple, and the number below it is three. Equilibrium in the plane gives three equations, so the reactions and the diagrams follow from equilibrium alone. When not: the propped cantilever, clamped and also held by a roller support, has four unknowns and three equations. Too few supports are just as wrong: the beam on two roller supports has only two vertical reactions, and a horizontal force would move it along its axis. What it does to the result: with four unknowns we cannot get the diagrams without the deflection, which comes in the fourth part. The first assumption, the plane of symmetry, we met with the three numbers.

2 Plane sections stay plane: one number describes the strain

In the previous part a cut gave us the moment at the clamp of the cantilever, \(M(0) = -500\) N m. This moment bends the beam. Today we ask: which fibres get longer, which shorter, and by how much?

Let us take the cantilever of the previous part: a steel bar 1 m long with a cross section of 20 mm × 40 mm, loaded at its free end by a force of 500 N downward. A wall holds its left end. Next to the wall we cut out a short piece, as it was before the load. We draw it grey dashed, because this is the state before. The piece is \(h = 40\) mm high and \(\Delta x = 10\) mm long. Why so short? Because the moment changes along the cantilever, but over 10 mm only by 1 %, so we can treat the piece as if it had the same moment everywhere.

Now we load the piece. What happens to the cross sections at its ends? The wall holds the left cross section, the right one turns, and it turns as a whole flat card that does not curve. We draw them orange, because this is the state after. Between the cross sections are the fibres: the top one now runs along the outer side of the bend, the bottom one along the inner side. The middle fibre, the axis, keeps its length.

That is the whole claim of today's part, in one sentence: a cross section turns as a rigid flat card, so the fibre at the height \(y\) lengthens in proportion to \(y\),

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = -y\,v'' .\]

Let us draw it as a graph. Vertically the height \(y\) in the cross section, horizontally the strain \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\). The strain is a straight line through the origin: zero on the axis, the largest stretch at the top, the largest shortening at the bottom. One number, the curvature \(v''\), sets the slope of this line and with it the strain of every fibre of the cross section.

In this sentence we used four claims that we have not proved. The cross section stays plane. This holds also when the shear force is not zero. A point above the axis moves by the height times the slope. From this follows the strain \(-y\,v''\). For each one let us ask: why?

2.1 Why does a cross section stay plane?

Let us begin with the case where the answer is the most beautiful: a piece of a beam with the same moment at both ends and no shear force. Where does this happen in practice?

Let us draw a beam 2 m long and lay it on two supports. We hang on it two forces \(F = 1\) kN downward, each at the distance \(a = 0{,}5\) m from a support. The supports hold it with reactions of 1 kN upward each. Under the beam, at the same scale, we draw the axes of the shear force diagram. If we cut left of the first force, the left part carries only the reaction, and the shear force is \(-1\) kN. If we cut between the forces, the reaction and the force balance, and the shear force is zero. Now the axes of the moment diagram. The moment between the forces is constant, \(M = F\,a = 500\) N m. This middle metre of the beam is in pure bending (čisti upogib): the same moment everywhere, no shear force.

From the middle part we take a piece and put a mirror, the plane \(S\) at \(x_0\), through its middle. At both ends of the piece acts the same normal stress: at the top it pulls outward, at the bottom it pushes inward, on the left as on the right. The piece together with its load is its own mirror image. Now suppose that the cross section in the middle bulges to the right. What do we see in the mirror? The same piece, only with the cross section bulged to the left. The mirror image of a solution is again a solution for the same load. We would have two different solutions for one load. But one load has only one answer, up to a rigid displacement, that is lemma 4.134. So the cross section may bulge neither to the left nor to the right: it stays plane. The steps of the proof are in the book: 5.3.1, lemma 5.13.

The ground this stands on: a symmetric cause has a symmetric effect. If the piece bulged in one direction, something would have to choose that direction, but the mirror does not tell left from right.

2.2 Why do we assume it also when the shear force is not zero?

Our cantilever has the shear force \(-500\) N on every cross section, and the moment changes along it. There is no mirror any more, since the moment left of a cross section differs from the moment right of it. What now? Now we do not prove plane sections, we assume them. In pure bending we saw that the normal lines (prečnice), segments across the whole height of the cross section, stayed straight. We assume that they stay so here too.

Let us draw the axis after bending, tilted. Through the point of the axis went the normal line before the load, vertical. After the load we ask three things of it: (a) it stays straight, (b) it keeps its length, (c) it stays perpendicular to the axis. What do we forbid with this? A curved normal line, which breaks (a), a lengthened one, which breaks (b), and a tilted one that is not perpendicular to the axis, which breaks (c). These are the assumptions of Euler and Bernoulli, in the book 5.3.2.

These assumptions have no proof, so for each one we must say three things: why it is good here, when it does not hold and what it costs. Let us begin with (c), perpendicularity. In reality the shear force tilts the normal line a little, by the shear strain. Why may we neglect it? Let us see how much this tilt adds to the deflection of the end.

Along the axis of the deflection we draw, for three lengths of the same bar, a column that is the whole deflection of the end. Below each is the ratio \(L/h\). We draw the columns equally high, so that only the ratio of the parts shows. The blackwhite part is the deflection from bending, the blue one the addition from shear. For our cantilever, which is 25 times longer than it is high, the blue is only 0,125 % of the blackwhite. When does the assumption not hold? If we shorten the same bar to 80 mm, the shear part is already 19,5 %, and then we need the Timoshenko beam (5.13). What does it cost? Once we forbid the shear strain, Hooke's law gives us no shear stress, so we will have to get it from equilibrium (5.6).

Now (b), the same length. In reality it is wrong. Let us look at the cross section of the cantilever at the wall, with the axes \(z\) and \(y\), rectangular before the load. At the top the fibre is stretched, and like every stretched rubber band it gets thinner across. At the bottom it is compressed and gets thicker. The cross section becomes a trapezoid. By how much? For steel the transverse strain is \(-0{,}3\) times the longitudinal one (Poisson's ratio \(\nu = 0{,}3\)), \(0{,}3 \cdot 4{,}78\cdot 10^{-4} = 1{,}43\cdot 10^{-4}\), and the top narrows by 1,43 μm on each side. Why does this not worry us? The transverse strain \(1{,}43\cdot 10^{-4}\) is less than a third of the longitudinal \(4{,}78\cdot 10^{-4}\), and because of it the normal line curves by only 1,4 μm over a height of 40 mm. When would it worry us? When the shape of the cross section itself is what we want to know, not the deflection. What does it cost? The model requires \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0\), the material \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = -0{,}3\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), and we cannot have both. So we will compute the stress as if the fibre were only stretched, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), and use \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0\) only for the shape of the displacements. In the book: 5.3.4.

2.3 Why does a point above the axis move by the height times the slope?

So the normal line stays straight and perpendicular to the axis. By how much does a point on it move?

Let us draw the normal line before the load and on it the point of the axis. Above it we choose the point \(P\) at the height \(y\). On the right is the same normal line before, dashed. The axis bends and tilts. How much is it tilted? The slope \(v'\) tells us: over a length of 1 the axis rises by \(v'\). The normal line after the load stays perpendicular to the axis, so it tilts by the same angle. The point \(P\) moves to the point \(P'\), horizontally by \(y\,v'\). Why exactly the height times the slope? Because a turned stick behaves like the hand of a clock: a point twice as far from the axis of rotation travels a path twice as long. That is the ground this step stands on. In the book: 5.3.3, equation (5.16), \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = u - y\,v'\).

How much is this for our cantilever? At the free end the slope is \(v' = -0{,}01196\). The point at the top of the cross section, \(y = 20\) mm, moves there by \(0{,}020 \cdot 0{,}01196\) m \(= 0{,}239\) mm.

Here we assumed a small slope: the angle, its tangent and its sine are almost equal, and the curvature of the axis is simply \(v''\). Why is this good? The exact curvature differs from \(v''\) by the factor \((1 + v'^2)^{-3/2}\). For our cantilever \(v'^2 = 1{,}43\cdot 10^{-4}\), the error is 0,02 %. When does it not hold? When the slope is large, say for a thin lath that we bend into an arc. The book allows \(v'^2 \le 10^{-2}\), where the error is at most 1,5 % (definition 5.16).

2.4 Why is the strain \(-y\,v''\)?

Let us go back to the piece at the wall. Strain is the change of length divided by the original length. We learned this in the first chapter (1), and that is the ground. What happens to the lengths of the fibres?

The left cross section does not turn at the wall, because the wall holds it. The right one turns. To see by how much, we extend both cross sections downward. They meet at the point \(O\), and between them is the angle \(\Delta\varphi\). How large is this angle? The curvature \(v''\) tells by how much the slope of the axis changes per metre of length: curvature is the slope of the slope. At the wall of our cantilever \(v''(0) = -0{,}0239\) 1/m, and where this number comes from the next part shows. Over the length \(\Delta x = 10\) mm the slope changes by \(0{,}0239 \cdot 0{,}010 = 2{,}39\cdot 10^{-4}\), and that is the angle between the cross sections. Now we use the previous step: the top of the right cross section is at the height \(y = 20\) mm and moves by the height times the angle. So the top fibre lengthens by \(0{,}020\) m \(\cdot\ 2{,}39\cdot 10^{-4} = 4{,}78\) μm. The bottom one shortens by the same. Grey dashed shows where the bottom fibre would end if it kept its length. The axis is at the height zero, so it does not change.

We divide by the original length \(\Delta x\). The strain of the top fibre is \(4{,}78\ \mu\text{m} / 10\ \text{mm} = 4{,}78\cdot 10^{-4}\), which is \(y\,|v''|\). Since \(v'' < 0\) at the wall and the top is stretched, we write \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = -y\,v''\). The length \(\Delta x\) cancels in the division, so the strain does not depend on how long a piece we cut out. In the book: 5.15, equation (5.17).

2.5 Back up

Now we build the picture once more, from below. The cross sections are cards that stay flat and perpendicular to the axis. Neighbouring cards tilt toward each other, by \(v''\) per metre. So the fibres on one side are compressed, on the other stretched, in proportion to the distance from the axis. At the clamp of our cantilever \(v''(0) = M(0)/EI = -500/20\,906{,}7 = -0{,}0239\) 1/m. At the top, at \(y = 20\) mm, the strain is \(4{,}78\cdot 10^{-4}\), at the bottom just as large, but a shortening. That is a good half millimetre per metre. Where \(M/EI\) comes from is the question of the next part.

2.6 The arc and its centre

We can see the same without derivatives. Let us go back to the extended cross sections. The point \(O\) where they meet is the centre of the bend. Every fibre is an arc around \(O\). The length of an arc is the radius times the angle, and the angle \(\Delta\varphi\) is the same for all fibres. So the longer fibre is the one farther from \(O\). The axis is at the radius of curvature \(R = 41{,}8\) m from \(O\), the top fibre 20 mm more. The ratio of the two lengths is \(1 + 0{,}020/41{,}8\), the strain is \(y/R = 4{,}78\cdot 10^{-4}\). What this view adds: the curvature \(|v''|\) is nothing but \(1/R\), and the cantilever at the wall bends like a circle with a radius of 42 m.

2.7 By hand and eye

You can see the assumption at home. Take an eraser or a soft paperback and on its edge draw a rectangle with vertical lines, the normal lines. The axes \(x\) and \(y\) show the directions. Bend it. The fibres curve into arcs, the normal lines stay straight and only spread like a fan. What this view adds: the assumption is not made up, we see it with the naked eye.

2.8 How Mariotte came to it

The idea that a cross section turns as a whole is old. Mariotte reasoned about bending as about a lever: the cross section turns as a whole, and the fibres lengthen in proportion to their distance from the point of rotation. He tested his formula for the breaking force on a wooden bar, and it came closer to the measured value than Galileo's. The story with its numbers is in the book, in section 5.3.2. What this view adds: the picture of the turned cross section passed a test before it became an assumption.

3 \(M = EI\,v''\): why a board on edge is stiffer

Take a board and lay it flat across two chairs. When we sit on it, it sags visibly. Now stand the same board on its edge. When we step on it, it hardly moves. The material is the same, the area of the cross-section is the same. What changed?

We look for the answer on our cantilever. First we draw the axes \(x\) and \(y\), \(x\) along the beam, \(y\) up. We draw the beam, a steel bar \(20 \times 40\) mm standing on its edge. Its left end is clamped in a wall, and we push the free end with a force \(F = 500\) N downward. The bar is \(L = 1\) m long. Right at the wall we cut it in our mind. In the first part we found that the bending moment there is \(M(0) = -500\) N m, in the second that the fibres there are stretched in proportion to the height. Today we turn this into stress. We carry the heights of the section to the right and above each height we draw how hard that fibre pulls or pushes. We get a straight line with \(+93{,}75\) MPa at the top, tension, and \(-93{,}75\) MPa at the bottom, compression.

The claim of this part, right on this figure: the fibres are springs stretched in proportion to the height \(y\). So each pulls with a force in proportion to \(y\), and it has a lever arm about the axis, \(y\) again. The moment the section gives is therefore \(E\,v''\) times the sum over the section of the squared heights \(y^2\), each multiplied by the area of its strip:

\[M = EI\,v'' , \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -\frac{M\,y}{I} .\]

In the book: 5.35, equations (5.24) and (5.25).

This claim holds four things we do not believe yet. Why is the stress proportional to the stretch? Why is the zero of the stress exactly on the axis? Why the square of the height? And why exactly \(93{,}75\) MPa? Let us take them in turn.

3.1 Why is the stress in a fibre \(E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\)?

Recall the second part. We took a short piece of beam of length \(\Delta x\) and height \(h\), with the height \(y\) measured upward. We bent the piece. Both sections stayed straight and meet in the centre \(O\). So the top fibre is longer, the bottom one shorter, the middle one keeps its length. The strain is a straight line over the height, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = -y\,v''\).

How do we get a force from a stretch? We treat every fibre (vlakno) as the bar of the tensile test. There we pulled the bar along its length and its sides were free. We measured that the stress is proportional to the strain, \(\sigma = E\,\varepsilon\). If this holds for every fibre, the straight line of strain becomes a straight line of stress of the same shape, only multiplied by \(E\). In the book: 5.5.1, equation (5.21).

This is assumption 6: only \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) acts in a fibre, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) are zero. Why may we? The top and bottom faces of the beam are free or pressed only by the load. On the simply supported beam that is \(q_0/b = 50\) kPa, against \(93{,}75\) MPa in the fibres at most \(0{,}05\,\%\). When does it fail? Right at supports, under point forces and in very deep beams (in the book: 5.15.1). What does it do to the result? In front of the strain stands \(E\) alone, without Poisson's ratio \(\nu\). The price is that assumption 6 does not agree with assumption (b) of the second part, that the height of the section does not change. Both hold together only for \(\nu = 0\), so one of them is dropped. The error this brings into the deflection is, by the book, only \(0{,}014\,\%\) at \(L/h = 50\).

The ground of this question is a measured fact: a steel bar below its yield stress stretches in proportion to the force. This is assumption 7, a linear elastic, homogeneous and isotropic material. We have \(93{,}75\) MPa, which is \(43\,\%\) of the yield stress \(220\) MPa, so the stress line is indeed straight. Above the yield stress the proportionality no longer holds, so there the straight stress line fails. It also fails for a beam made of layers of different materials, where \(E\) is not the same everywhere.

3.2 Why does the neutral axis pass through the centroid?

So far we put the zero of the stress at mid-height. Why there? The beam of our cantilever has no axial force, nobody pulls it along its length. So the fibres above the zero must pull exactly as much as the fibres below it push. Let us see this on the section. We draw our section, the \(y\) axis and the \(z\) axis through the centroid (težišče). We pick a strip at height \(y\). Its stress is proportional to \(y\), its force to \(y\) times the strip's area. The mirror strip at height \(-y\) has the same area and the opposite force. The pair cancels, and so does every pair. The sum of the products of height and strip area, \(y\,\Delta A\), over the whole section is zero, in the limit of thin strips \(\int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\mathrm{d}A = 0\).

This is a seesaw. A seesaw is at rest when the sum of weight times distance on one side equals that on the other. The line about which the area of the section balances in this way is exactly the centroidal axis. Ground: the experience of a seesaw.

In a rectangle the centroid and the middle coincide. What about a section that is not symmetric? We draw a T-shaped section. The flange at the top has a lot of area, so the seesaw balances closer to the top. The neutral axis (nevtralna os) is \(27{,}14\) mm from the top and \(52{,}86\) mm from the bottom, not at mid-height. Under the same moment \(500\) N m the stress is again a straight line through the neutral axis. Since the bottom is farther away, the bottom has \(+17{,}5\) MPa, the top only \(-9{,}0\) MPa. The ratio \(17{,}5 : 9{,}0\) is the ratio of distances \(52{,}86 : 27{,}14 = 1{,}95\). In the book: 5.5.2, lemma 5.33.

This is assumption 8: the axis of the beam passes through the centroids of the sections. Why is it right? Only there do the axial force and the moment not affect each other. When not? With the axis elsewhere, an axial force would also bend the beam and a moment would also stretch it. What does it do to the result? For our rectangle nothing, since the centroid is in the middle. For the T it decides where the neutral axis is, and so which side carries the larger stress.

3.3 Why does \(y^2\) appear in the stiffness?

Why does a fibre twice as far from the axis give four times as much moment? This is the core of the proof, so let us draw it slowly. We draw a piece of beam from the side, with the fibres as thin lines and a cut on the right. Through the middle runs the axis, \(y = 0\). The section turns as a flat card: the top goes right, the bottom left. By how much does a fibre get longer? By the gap between the grey and the orange line at its height, and that gap is proportional to \(y\). The first \(y\).

Every fibre is a spring. A stretched fibre pulls, a shortened one pushes. We draw the fibre forces on the face: above the axis they point out of the piece, below it into the piece, long in proportion to the height. We pick one thin strip of fibres at height \(y\), of area \(\Delta A\). Its force \(\Delta F\) is stress times area, so it is proportional to \(y\,\Delta A\).

And what does this force do to turning? A moment is force times lever arm. The lever arm about the axis is \(y\) again. The second \(y\). So the strip's share of the moment, \(\Delta M\), is proportional to \(y^2\,\Delta A\). We add the shares of all strips and get the moment \(M\) that the section carries. As the strips get thinner, the sum of \(y^2\,\Delta A\) becomes an integral, the second moment of area \(I\) (vztrajnostni moment), and that is all:

\[M = E\,v'' \int_{\mathrm{A}} y^2\,\mathrm{d}A = EI\,v'' .\]

Ground: a moment is force times lever arm, and forces add. In the book: 5.5.3, step 4 of the proof of lemma 5.33.

On our cantilever the moment at the clamp is negative. The arrows pull at the top, push at the bottom, and all turn the face clockwise. That is exactly \(M(0) = -500\) N m from the first part.

What does the square mean in numbers? We cut the section into strips of height \(\Delta y = 5\) mm. The strip at height \(y_{\mathrm{k}}\) gives \(y_{\mathrm{k}}^2\,b\,\Delta y\). We draw the shares as bars. Strips at the edge give long bars, strips at the axis almost nothing. The outer half of the height gives \(87{,}5\,\%\) of the second moment of area, the inner half only \(12{,}5\,\%\). For a rectangle the sum is \(I = bh^3/12\). In the book: 5.4.3, equation (5.18).

3.4 Why exactly \(93{,}75\) MPa at the clamp?

Where does exactly this number come from? Back to the stress at the clamp. From the first part we have \(M(0) = -500\) N m, and the section has \(I = 20 \cdot 40^3/12 = 106\,667\) mm⁴ \(= 1{,}0667\cdot 10^{-7}\) m⁴. The top is at \(y = 0{,}020\) m:

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -\frac{M\,y}{I} = \frac{500 \cdot 0{,}020}{1{,}0667\cdot 10^{-7}}\ \text{Pa} = 93{,}75\ \text{MPa} .\]

This is tension at the top, and compression of the same size at the bottom. Why does an overloaded beam break exactly at the wall and exactly at the surface? At the wall the moment is largest, at the surface \(y\) is largest.

3.5 Back up: the answer to the board

Now we put it all together. The section is a flat card, so the stretch is proportional to \(y\). A fibre is a spring, so the force is proportional to \(y\). The zero is at the centroid, since there is no axial force. The lever arm is \(y\) once more, so \(M = EI\,v''\). Inverted: the curvature is \(v'' = M/(EI)\), the stress \(-M\,y/I\).

Now we answer the board. We draw the section on edge, \(40\) mm high and \(20\) mm wide. We shade the outer halves of the height blue, that is where most of the second moment of area is. We get \(I = 106\,667\) mm⁴. Now we lay the same bar flat: it is only \(20\) mm high and \(40\) mm wide. Its outer halves are now only \(5\) to \(10\) mm from the \(z\) axis. We get \(I = 40 \cdot 20^3/12 = 26\,667\) mm⁴, four times less. The width enters \(I\) once, the height to the third power.

What does this mean for the same moment \(-500\) N m? On edge the curvature is \(-0{,}0239\) 1/m, flat it is four times larger, \(-0{,}0957\) 1/m. The stress on edge is \(\pm 93{,}75\) MPa, flat \(\pm 187{,}5\) MPa, twice as large, since \(I\) is four times smaller and the surface \(y\) only half. The board on edge is four times stiffer.

3.6 Where to put the material

If the outer strips give almost all of \(I\), why put material in the middle at all? We draw a rectangle \(20 \times 154\) mm with area \(3080\) mm². Under \(500\) N m it has a stress of \(6{,}32\) MPa at the edge. Now we arrange the same area as an I section, two flanges far from the axis and a thin web. At the edge it has only \(2{,}38\) MPa. Its \(I = 2{,}098\cdot 10^{7}\) mm⁴ is \(3{,}45\) times larger. That is why steel beams look the way they do. What does this view add? The square of the height is not only a calculation, it tells us where to put the material.

3.7 Stiffness as a number

Why do we call \(EI\) a stiffness? From \(M = EI\,v''\) we get \(EI = M/v''\), the moment for a curvature of \(1\) per metre, that is for an arc of radius \(1\) m. For our bar \(EI = 196\ \text{GPa} \cdot 1{,}0667\cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^4 = 20\,906{,}7\) N m², the bending stiffness (upogibna togost). For the curvature on edge we need only \(500\) N m, because the arc is very flat, with a radius of \(41{,}8\) m. What does this view add? The unit N m² and a feeling for the size. Doubling the height makes \(EI\) eight times larger, the material only twice. In the book: 5.36.

3.8 Algebraically: what the axis through the centroid buys

If \(y\) is measured from an arbitrary line, we put the stress \(E\,(u' - y\,v'')\) into \(N\) and \(M\) and get

\[N = E\,(A\,u' - S\,v'') , \qquad M = E\,(I_0\,v'' - S\,u') ,\]

where \(S = \int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\mathrm{d}A\) (equation (5.22)). The term with \(S\) couples stretching and bending. On the seesaw of strips we saw that \(S = 0\) at the centroid. Then \(N = EA\,u'\) and \(M = EI\,v''\) remain, two separate equations. What does this view add? It says exactly which term vanishes when the axis goes through the centroid.

3.9 Historically: where is the line without stretch

Where is the line in which the fibres neither lengthen nor shorten? Mariotte, computing the breaking force, took the same turning point as Galileo, at the edge of the section. Navier first put this line on the edge of the concave side. Coulomb wrote three equations of statics, among them that the sum of horizontal forces over the section is zero. Navier later concluded with these equations that the line passes through the centroid. That is exactly our seesaw of strips: the horizontal forces add to zero, so the zero lies at the centroid. What does this view add? The right place was not self-evident to find. In the book: the history block in 5.5.2.

3.10 Assumptions and their price

Three assumptions we stated where they entered: uniaxial stress in a fibre (6) at the stress from the strain, the linear elastic material (7) at the straight stress line and the axis through the centroids (8) at the neutral axis. One is left, assumption 2: the section is symmetric and the load lies in the plane of symmetry. Where did it enter? At the fibre arrows, which all lie in the plane of the drawing. Why is it right here? The rectangle is symmetric and the force acts in its plane of symmetry. When not? An unsymmetric section also bends sideways. What does it do to the result? Then we need the principal axes of the section. In the book: 5.4.4.

4 From load to deflection: four integrations and the ends of the beam

Let us go back to our cantilever. By how much does its free end drop under a force of 500 N? And why does a cantilever twice as long drop eight times as much?

First we set up the axes. The \(x\) axis runs along the beam, the \(y\) axis points up. We draw the beam as it is before loading, as a straight blackwhite line. We clamp its left end in a wall, drawn hatched. The wall lets the end neither move nor turn. The beam is \(L = 1\) m long. On the free end we hang a force \(F = 500\) N downwards. The beam bends and its axis becomes the orange curve. We call this curve the deflection curve (upogibnica), because it is the shape the axis bends into. We look for two numbers. The first is by how much the end drops: 7,972 mm. The second is how steeply the axis is inclined there: a slope of 0,01196.

The claim of this segment in one sentence: the moment at each section sets the curvature there, \(v'' = M/(EI)\), curvature is the change of slope and slope is the change of height, so adding up twice from the wall, where height and slope are zero, gives the whole curve and the end drop \(F L^3/(3EI)\).

The claim uses three ideas we have to justify. Why a chain from load to deflection at all? Why is curvature the second derivative? And why does the wall decide where we start adding? We take them in turn.

4.1 Why is the deflection curve the solution of \(EI\,v'''' = q\)?

What connects the load, which we know, with the deflection, which we seek? In the three segments before this one we built three links. Now we put them one under the other.

Take a beam on two supports, drawn as an axis 2 m long. A load \(q\) presses on it evenly, 1 kN on every metre. The first step is the equilibrium of a piece of the beam: the load changes the shear force, \(V' = -q\), so the shear force grows evenly along the length. The second step is equilibrium again: the shear force changes the moment, \(M' = -V\). The third step is the material and the section: the moment gives the curvature, \(v'' = M/(EI)\). From the curvature to the deflection there are two more steps.

How many derivatives lie between the load and the deflection? Let us count the arrows. Two from \(q\) to \(M\), two from \(v''\) to \(v\). Four in all. If we combine the relations we get \(M'' = q\), and with \(M = EI\,v''\)

\[EI\,v'''' = q .\]

The equation is no new physics. It is only the chain, written with one unknown function. In the book: 5.7.1 and (5.33).

Here we used an assumption, so let us say it out loud: \(EI\) is the same along the whole length, because the section and the material do not change along the axis. That is why we could take it out of the derivative. In a beam that tapers towards the end, \(EI\) stays inside the derivative, \((EI\,v'')'' = q\). For our flat steel strip the assumption holds exactly.

4.2 Why is curvature the second derivative?

What is a slope? A slope is how much the height rises per unit of length. That is the meaning of the word, nothing more.

Let us draw a curved axis and choose a point on it. The circle that fits the axis most closely at this point we draw in grey. Its centre lies on the perpendicular to the axis, at the distance of the radius \(R\). Now under the axis we set up new axes and draw how the slope changes along \(x\). We get a straight line. So the slope is not the same everywhere, it grows. By how much it grows per unit of length is shown by the blue triangle. This number is the curvature. Slope is the change of height, curvature is the change of slope. We used the meaning of slope twice, so the curvature is the second derivative \(v''\).

And a straight axis? Its slope is the same everywhere, the line of the slope is horizontal and there is no triangle. The curvature is zero, as it must be.

Here the assumption of a small slope enters. The exact curvature is \(v''/(1 + v'^2)^{3/2}\). Why may we drop the denominator? For our cantilever the largest slope is 0,01196, its square \(1{,}4\cdot 10^{-4}\), so the exact curvature differs from \(v''\) by a factor \(1 + 2{,}1\cdot 10^{-4}\). When does this fail? For a fishing rod or a thin strip bent by hand, where the slope is no longer small compared with 1. What does the assumption give us? The equation stays linear, and we will need that when we add loads. In the book: 5.3.3 and 5.16.

4.3 Why do we add up from the wall?

In every cross section we know the curvature. How do we get the shape of the whole axis from it? Now we have everything to solve the cantilever by hand. We draw three graphs one under the other, all with the same \(x\) axis.

Over all three rows we draw the length of the beam \(L\). In the top row we set up the axes for the curvature. We know the moment of the cantilever from the first segment, \(M(x) = -F\,(L - x)\). At the wall it is \(-500\) N m, at the free end zero. The curvature is the moment divided by \(EI\), so it is a straight line that touches the axis at the free end. At the wall the curvature is largest in size, \(v''(0) = -500/20\,906{,}7 = -0{,}02392\) 1/m.

What does the curvature tell us about the slope? On each piece of the axis it tells by how much the slope changes over that piece. We add the pieces from the wall. The sum is the area under the line. The area of a triangle is half the base times the height:

\[\tfrac12 \cdot 1\ \text{m} \cdot 0{,}02392\ \tfrac{1}{\text{m}} = 0{,}01196 .\]

That is how much the slope changes from the wall to the end. But where does it start? The curvature does not say. The wall tells us. In the middle row we set up the axes for the slope. At the wall the slope is zero, because the clamped end does not turn. The curve of the slope therefore starts at zero and falls by exactly this area, to the slope of the end \(v'(L) = -0{,}01196\). Near the wall it is steep, because the curvature is large there. At the end it is horizontal, because the curvature is zero there.

We repeat the same once more. The slope on each piece tells by how much the height changes. We add from the wall. Where do we start? Again the wall tells us. In the bottom row we set up the axes for the deflection. The end at the wall is at height zero. Adding up gives the deflection curve, which starts horizontally and falls more and more. The area between the curve of the slope and the axis is the drop of the end, \(v(L) = -F L^3/(3EI) = -7{,}972\) mm. This region is not a triangle, so we do not read its area off so simply. In the book the steps with the constants: 5.9.2.

4.4 Why do we need end conditions, and why four?

Could we do without the wall? Let us see.

Let us draw again the axis of the beam before loading and below it the deflection curve, in orange. This time we do not draw the wall. Now we move the deflection curve down by a distance \(c_2\). Has the curvature changed anywhere? No, the curve is bent the same everywhere. We take it once more and turn it so that it starts with a slope \(c_1\). Again the curvature is the same everywhere. So both grey curves satisfy the same equation as the true one. They differ by \(c_1 x + c_2\), which is a shift and a small turn of the whole beam as a rigid body.

This is an everyday experience. Knowing how fast we walk does not tell us where we started. Every adding up forgets the start. Four integrations, four forgotten numbers. We get them back with four conditions, two at each end. Now we draw the wall too. Only the wall picks the orange curve: height zero and slope zero at \(x = 0\).

Which two numbers does a single end give? Let us ask what the support physically prevents. A clamp prevents moving and turning, so \(v = 0\) and \(v' = 0\). The force and the couple with which it holds are unknown. A pin support prevents moving but not turning. So \(v = 0\), and because it does not resist the turning of the end, the moment there is zero, \(M = 0\). A free end is held by nothing, so there we know what acts on it: the shear force and the moment. At each end we prescribe one member of each pair (displacement or force, turn or moment), never both. For our cantilever the four conditions are

\[v(0) = 0 , \quad v'(0) = 0 , \quad V(L) = -500\ \text{N} , \quad M(L) = 0 .\]

The conditions at the free end gave the moment in the top row of L5.30, which is why the line of the curvature ends at zero there. The conditions at the wall gave the start of the slope and the start of the deflection. In the book: 5.7.2.

The wall is idealized. Why is that acceptable? A real clamp turns a little. We have drawn this already: the curve that starts with a slope at the wall is turned as a whole. A turn \(\varphi\) at the wall is multiplied by the length at the end and adds \(\varphi L\). When does it count? For short stiff beams in soft supports. What is the effect? A turn of only \(8\cdot 10^{-5}\) rad, that is 0,0046 degrees, adds 1 % to the drop of our cantilever. A compliant clamp therefore always increases the drop. In the book: 5.15.2.

4.5 Why \(L^3\)?

Why does a cantilever twice as long drop eight times as much and not twice? We double the length and look at L5.30.

First, the curvature at the wall doubles, because the force gets a lever arm twice as long. Second, the triangle of the area now has twice the height and twice the base, so the slope of the end is four times larger. Third, this four times larger slope is added over a path twice as long, so the drop of the end is eight times larger. Three factors \(L\), one from the lever arm and two from the two additions:

\[v'(L) = -\frac{F L^2}{2EI} , \qquad v(L) = -\frac{F L^3}{3EI} .\]

The same works the other way. Double the height of the section and \(I = b h^3/12\) grows eight times. The curvature at the wall falls to one eighth, and with it all three rows and the drop too.

4.6 Back up: the drop in a few minutes

Let us now put it all together, as we would tell it in a few minutes. We look at the cantilever. At the wall the height and the slope are zero. The moment is largest in size there, \(-500\) N m, so the curvature is largest in size there too, \(-0{,}02392\) 1/m. Towards the force the moment falls to zero and the axis bends less and less. The curvature, added from the wall, gives the slope of the end 0,01196. The slope, added from the wall, gives the drop of the end 7,972 mm, that is 0,8 % of the length. A beam twice as long, a drop eight times larger.

4.7 View from the top: the whole theory in one figure

Let us go back once more to the chain. Each of the four segments of this chapter is one arrow on it. The first segment is the equilibrium from load to moment. The second and third are the material and the section from moment to curvature. This segment is the geometry from curvature to deflection. A beam is a body so thin that one number per section is enough at every stage. What does this view add? A map: in every beam problem we know which arrow we are on.

4.8 View by hand: a test with a weight

Would we really measure 7,972 mm? Let us set up a test.

We clamp the bar 1 m long in a rigid table. On the end we hang a weight of mass 51 kg, which gives 500 N. Over the end we place a dial gauge, which measures the deflection of the end. The model predicts 7,97 mm. Near the wall, but at least one section height away, at the distance \(x_0\), we also glue two strain gauges, which measure the stretch of the top and the bottom fibre. We load in steps of 100 N. The readings must lie on a straight line through the origin, because the equation is linear. This view adds that the result is not only a calculation. We check it with a weight and a gauge.

4.9 View by comparison: own weight and adding loads

At the top is our cantilever with the force at the end and a drop of 7,972 mm. At the bottom is the same cantilever, loaded only by its own weight, 61,21 N per metre. The drop is only 0,366 mm, \(q_0 L^4/(8EI)\), that is 4,6 % of the drop under the force. Why so little? The strip weighs 61,21 N in all, about eight times less than the force. And it does not hang at the end: every part of it is nearer the wall than the end, has a shorter lever arm and gives a smaller moment. Because the equation is linear, the drops of the two loads simply add, \(7{,}972 + 0{,}366 = 8{,}338\) mm. So a table of a few basic cases covers many beams. In the book: 5.9.5.

4.10 Assumptions and what they cost

We have already stated three assumptions where they entered: constant \(EI\), a small slope and an ideal wall. One more remains.

We set up the axes: horizontally the slenderness \(L/h\), vertically the drop divided by the length. We got the curvature from the moment alone. We left out the shear sliding of the sections. Why may we? At our slenderness \(L/h = 25\) the shear gives only 0,125 % of the drop. When not? Left of \(L/h = 8{,}8\) the shear part exceeds 1 %, at \(L/h = 2\) it is already 19,5 %, and there we need a model that includes shear. At the top is the limit of a small slope, where the slope exceeds 0,1, for our cantilever a drop of 67 mm. Slanting across is the yield limit. Our cantilever is far from both blue limits and reaches 43 % of the yield limit. So the model gives it a drop we may trust. In the book: 5.15.1.
