Chapter 5 Beam theory (teorija nosilcev)

5.11 Mathematics: calculus of variations

Three times we have already found equilibrium by looking for the smallest energy. For the bar with a load the energy was a function of one number, the extension. For the truss of two bars it was a function of two numbers, the displacements of the node. For the bar and the body the input was a whole function or field, and the output one number, the total potential energy (definition 4.89). For the bar with a load and the truss the result was an algebraic equation for one or two numbers, for the bar under distributed load and the body an equation in the interior and conditions on the boundary. Is there one rule behind this that we can prove once for all such problems, the beam included?

Such a rule is given by the calculus of variations (variacijski račun), whose fundamental lemma we proved in chapter 4 (lemma 4.77). In this section we develop it in full for an integral along the length in which a function \(v\) and its first two derivatives appear. The beam has such an integral, and the general form also covers the bar, the beam on an elastic foundation and the beam with an axial force. The route is as follows. First we say what we look for: a rule that assigns one number to a whole function. Then we look at how this number changes when we move the function a little, and from this we derive the equation that the function with the smallest value must satisfy. We find out which conditions at the ends follow by themselves and which must be prescribed. At the end we show when a stationary point really gives the smallest value, and apply this to the beam. We test everything on the cantilever of section 5.1.3: length \(L = 1\) m, bending stiffness \(EI = 20\,906{,}7\) N m\(^2\), clamped at \(x = 0\), a force of \(500\) N downward at the free end.

We prove the theorems with tools of the earlier chapters: the derivative of an integral with respect to a parameter (lemma 4.19), integration by parts (corollary 3.56), the fundamental lemma with smooth bumps (lemma 4.123) and the derivative at an interior extremum (lemma 1.83). Besides these we use only the chain rule, the mean value theorem and the properties of the integral.

From history

Problems in which we seek a curve with a largest or smallest property had appeared before Euler. In 1686 Newton reduced the problem of the solid of revolution of least resistance to a differential equation (CAJORI, PDF p. 250). In 1696 Jacob Bernoulli proposed the well-known problem of figures with equal perimeter and in 1701 published his own solution (CAJORI, PDF p. 237). Johann Bernoulli found the curve of swiftest descent (CAJORI, PDF p. 238), a problem that he solved in 1697 himself and that his brother Jacob solved the same year (CAJORI, PDF p. 250). L'Hospital also solved it and studied the body of least resistance (BALL, PDF p. 398). In these problems we do not seek a number but a curve: every path from \(A\) to \(B\) gives its own time of descent, and we seek the path with the shortest. Johann and Jacob Bernoulli quarrelled bitterly over the problem of figures with equal perimeter (CAJORI, PDF p. 238).

Euler took up these problems as Johann's pupil. The problems of figures with equal perimeter, of the curve of swiftest descent in a resisting medium and of the shortest lines on a surface led him to the calculus of variations (BALL, PDF p. 424, CAJORI, PDF p. 250). He first wrote the idea down in a paper of 1741 and extended it in 1744 (BALL, PDF p. 424). His method was essentially geometric (CAJORI, PDF p. 250). In the book of 1744 he wrote that nothing in the universe happens without some largest or smallest value appearing, and that every phenomenon can be explained by direct causes or by seeking a largest or smallest value (TIMO, PDF p. 37, 39). He showed this on the catenary. A chain hung at two points can be treated by the equilibrium of the forces on a small piece of the chain, or by energy: of all curves of the given length the true one is that for which the potential energy of the weight is smallest, that is, where the centre of gravity of the chain is lowest. The problem becomes the search for the extreme value of the integral \(\int w y\,\mathrm{d}s\) for a given length \(s\) of the curve, where \(w\) is the weight per unit length (TIMO, PDF p. 39-40). Both ways gave the same differential equation (TIMO, PDF p. 40). For the elastic bar, Daniel Bernoulli told him that all the force in a curved elastic bar can be expressed by one expression and that this expression is smallest in the elastic curve. For a uniform cross section and a bar that is straight in its natural position this is \(\int \mathrm{d}s/R^2\), where \(R\) is the radius of curvature (TIMO, PDF p. 40). For small slopes \(1/R = |v''|\) (definition 5.16) and the length of the arc is almost the length of the axis, so this expression, multiplied by \(\tfrac12\,EI\), is the bending energy that we compute for the true deflection of the cantilever in section 5.11.1. With the calculus of variations Euler obtained from this the same equation that Jacob Bernoulli had already derived from equilibrium (TIMO, PDF p. 40).

At nineteen Lagrange communicated to Euler a general method for such problems (CAJORI, PDF p. 267). Euler held back the publication of his own research for some time so that the young Lagrange could complete his and claim the invention (CAJORI, PDF p. 267). Euler had assumed the limits of the integrals along the \(x\) axis to be fixed, that is, fixed end points of the sought curve, and compared it only with curves with the same end points. Lagrange removed this restriction and let the end point move and all coordinates of the curve vary at the same time, while the end point \(A\) in panel (b) stays in place. Euler's method lacked an analytic foundation, and Lagrange supplied it by separating the principles from geometric considerations (CAJORI, PDF p. 267). The complete development of the new calculus was first carried out by Lagrange in 1759 (BALL, PDF p. 424). In 1766 Euler introduced the name that this section bears (CAJORI, PDF p. 267).

Let us return to the question of the section: for the bar and the body the smallest energy led to an equation in the interior and to conditions on the boundary (section 4.6.6), while for the beam we obtained the equation and the conditions at the ends from virtual displacements (theorem 5.66). Lagrange left the end points of the curve free, and from this idea we shall get the conditions at the ends of the beam.

5.11.1 Functional

Let us look at the cantilever. Every curve that leaves the origin horizontally at the clamp \(x = 0\) along the \(x\) axis is a possible shape of bending, but only one is in equilibrium. The energy of each of them is a single datum about the whole curve. Take two curves with the same displacement of the end, \(v(L) = -7{,}972\) mm: the true deflection and the parabola \(v(x) = -7{,}972\ \text{mm}\cdot x^2/L^2\). The energies differ because the curves bend differently. The bending energy of a beam is the integral of the density \(\tfrac12\,EI\,v''^2\) along the length: it is the curvature \(v''\) of the curve that strains the beam, and so the energy depends on the whole curve. We derive it exactly later in the chapter and take it as given here, and for the true deflection it agrees with the Clapeyron value of section 5.9. For the true deflection the bending energy is \(\int_0^L \tfrac12\,EI\,v''^2\,\mathrm{d}x = 1{,}993\) J, for the parabola, which has the constant curvature \(v'' = -0{,}01594\) 1/m, it is \(2{,}657\) J. The potential energy of the force is the same for both, \(-F_{\mathrm{y}}\,v(L) = 500\ \text{N}\cdot(-7{,}972\ \text{mm}) = -3{,}986\) J, since they have the same end at \(x = L\) (the force \(F_{\mathrm{y}} = -500\) N points down, and the deflection is also down). The total potential energy is thus \(-1{,}993\) J for the true deflection and \(-1{,}329\) J for the parabola (figure 5.166). The number depends on the whole curve and not only on its end.

Why does the parabola need more energy for the same displacement of the end? Below in the figure we draw the curvature of both curves over the same axis \(x\). The true deflection is curved most at the clamp, \(v''(0) = -0{,}0239\) 1/m, less and less towards the end, and zero at \(x = L\), because the curvature is proportional to the moment, \(M = EI\,v''\) (theorem 5.35), and the moment \(-F\,(L - x)\) is largest at the clamp. The parabola is curved equally everywhere. Now think about what curvature does to the end. A piece of length \(h\) at \(x\) bends and turns the whole beam to its right by the angle \(v''(x)\,h\), so the end moves by this angle times the distance \(L - x\) to the end. Curvature at the clamp moves the end with a long lever arm, curvature near the end hardly moves it, while the energy grows with the square of the curvature wherever it is. The true deflection bends where this moves the end most, the parabola bends equally also near the end, where it hardly moves the end, and so it uses more energy for the same displacement of the end. That the true deflection has the smallest total potential energy of all admissible curves we prove in section 5.11.6.

Definition 5.67 Functional (funkcional)

Let \(\mathcal A\) be a set of functions that take a number \(x\) from the interval \([0, L]\) as input and give a real number as output. A functional (funkcional) on \(\mathcal A\) is a rule \(\Pi\) that assigns one real number \(\Pi(v)\) to every function \(v\) in \(\mathcal A\). The input of a functional is thus a whole function, for example the true deflection of the cantilever, the output a number, for it \(\Pi = -1{,}993\) J.

Source: EP §3.5.2, equation (3.5.14) (p. 138), CM §7.6.2 (PDF p. 343).

Functionals are the total potential energy of the bar (equation (4.63)) and of the beam, and every integral of the form

\[\begin{aligned} \Pi(v) &= \int_0^L F\big(x,\,v(x),\,v'(x),\,v''(x)\big)\,\mathrm{d}x \\ &\quad - f_0\,v(0) - \mu_0\,v'(0) - f_{\mathrm{L}}\,v(L) - \mu_{\mathrm{L}}\,v'(L) , \end{aligned} \tag{5.68}\]

where \(F(x, v, p, r)\) is a given function of four numbers, continuous on \([0, L] \times \mathbb R^3\) (the integrand, in which the letter \(v\) stands for the number \(v(x)\), \(p\) for the number \(v'(x)\) and \(r\) for the number \(v''(x)\)). Continuity of a function of four numbers is defined as for a function of a point in space (definition 1.68 and the text below it), except that the distance is computed from four coordinates. Lemma 4.35 on the continuity of a composite function holds for it too, with the same proof and one coordinate more. Hence \(x \mapsto F\big(x, v(x), v'(x), v''(x)\big)\) is continuous whenever \(v\), \(v'\) and \(v''\) are continuous, and its integral exists (theorem 3.53). The numbers \(f_0\) and \(f_{\mathrm{L}}\) are forces at the ends pointing in the direction of increasing \(v\), and the numbers \(\mu_0\) and \(\mu_{\mathrm{L}}\) are moments of couples at the ends that turn the cross section in the direction of increasing \(v'\). The potential energy of a force is \(-f\,v\), as for the load in section 4.6.3. The potential energy of a couple is \(-\mu\,v'\), because the couple does the work \(\mu\,v'\) in the displacement \(v\) (lemma 5.62 with \(v\) in place of \(\delta v\)). For a beam with a force \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\) and a couple \(M_0\) at the end \(x = L\) we therefore have \(f_{\mathrm{L}} = F_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\mu_{\mathrm{L}} = M_0\). For the cantilever of the introduction \(f_{\mathrm{L}} = F_{\mathrm{y}} = -500\) N and the other three values, \(f_0\), \(\mu_0\) and \(\mu_{\mathrm{L}}\), are zero.

Which functions are admissible? Admissible functions (dopustne funkcije) are functions \(v\) with continuous first two derivatives on \([0, L]\) (one-sided at the end points) and with prescribed values of \(v\) or \(v'\) at the ends where the supports require them. Two derivatives are required by the energy, which contains \(v''\). Among the admissible functions are the polynomials that satisfy the essential conditions (for the cantilever \(a\,x^2 + b\,x^3\), such as the parabola \(\tilde v\), but not \(c\,x\), which is not horizontal at the clamp), which we will take as trial fields, and the true deflection curves of the beams of section 5.1.3. What the supports prescribe we will see in section 5.11.4.

5.11.2 First variation

How does the functional change when we move the function \(v\)? For the truss we took the displacement \(\alpha\,\vek v\) and looked at a function of the number \(\alpha\) (section 4.6.6). We do the same here. Let \(v\) be an admissible function and \(\eta\) a function that differs from it by an admissible displacement: \(v + \alpha\,\eta\) must be admissible for every \(\alpha\). This means that \(\eta\) has continuous first two derivatives on \([0, L]\), that \(\eta = 0\) at an end where the displacement \(v\) is prescribed, and \(\eta' = 0\) at an end where the rotation \(v'\) is prescribed. Such an \(\eta\) is called an admissible variation (dopustna variacija). The curves \(v + \alpha\,\eta\) for \(\alpha = \pm 4\) mm of figure 5.168 are admissible, because they leave the origin horizontally at the clamp. For the beam it corresponds to the virtual displacement across the axis of definition 5.61: a displacement the supports allow. Books write \(\delta v = \alpha\,\eta\) and call it the variation of the function (EP (3.5.3), p. 135), and the letter \(\delta\) means an admissible change of the function, as for the virtual displacement. Here \(\alpha\) is any real number. EP, p. 135, requires a small \(\alpha\), but the first variation as a derivative with respect to \(\alpha\) does not need smallness.

Definition 5.68 First variation (prva variacija)

Let \(\Pi\) be the functional (5.68), \(v\) an admissible function and \(\eta\) an admissible variation. The function of the number \(\alpha\) (the parabola on the right of the figure)

\[g(\alpha) = \Pi(v + \alpha\,\eta)\]

is defined for every real \(\alpha\). If \(g\) is differentiable at \(\alpha = 0\), the first variation (prva variacija) of the functional \(\Pi\) at \(v\) in the direction \(\eta\) is the number, the slope of the tangent to the graph of \(g\) at \(\alpha = 0\)

\[\delta\Pi(v, \eta) = g'(0) = \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\alpha}\,\Pi(v + \alpha\,\eta)\Big|_{\alpha = 0} .\]

The function \(v\) is a stationary point (stacionarna točka) of the functional if \(\delta\Pi(v, \eta) = 0\) for every admissible variation \(\eta\).

Source: EP (3.5.8), (3.5.31), (3.5.32) (pp. 136, 143). The same as the derivative in the direction \(\vek v\) in section 4.6.6.

For the cantilever take \(\eta(x) = x^2/L^2\). This function is zero at \(x = 0\) and has a zero derivative there, as the conditions of the clamp require. If \(v\) is the true deflection, then \(\Pi(v + \alpha\,\eta) = -1{,}993\ \text{J} + 2\,EI\,\alpha^2/L^3\) (computed with SymPy), a parabola in \(\alpha\) with its lowest point at \(\alpha = 0\) and a horizontal tangent there (figure 5.168). In the left panel the deflections are drawn along the \(x\) axis: the deflections \(\alpha = 4\) mm and \(\alpha = -4\) mm lie above and below the true one, their energies are the blue dots on the parabola, and on the \(\alpha\) and \(\Pi\) axes the value \(-1{,}993\) J is read off. At \(\alpha = \pm 4\) mm the energy is \(-1{,}324\) J, \(0{,}669\) J above the lowest value. There is no linear term, because the first variation at the true deflection is zero. Why the function \(g\) is a polynomial of second degree in \(\alpha\) we will see in section 5.11.6.

5.11.3 Euler-Lagrange equation

For the bar we concluded from \(\delta\Pi = 0\) for all virtual displacements that \(N' + q = 0\) inside. The conclusion used the fundamental lemma, which we proved with bumps \(p\) that have only a continuous first derivative, while their second derivative jumps at the end points (lemma 4.77). Such bumps are not admissible variations for the beam, because the energy contains the second derivative. Why do we require the second derivative to be continuous? Because \(EI\,v''\) is the moment, and the moment does not jump without a point couple (lemma 5.12). We need a version with bumps that also have a continuous second derivative. Smooth bumps \(q_{\alpha\beta}\) with continuous derivatives up to the third order we have already prepared (lemma 4.123, point 1). The next lemma states this conclusion as an independent claim, so that we can use it several more times.

Lemma 5.69 Fundamental lemma with two continuous derivatives (osnovna lema z dvema zveznima odvodoma)

Let \(R\) be a function continuous on the closed interval \([a, b]\) with \(a < b\), and let

\[\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b R(x)\,\eta(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\]

for every function \(\eta\) that has continuous first two derivatives on \([a, b]\) (one-sided at the end points) and satisfies \(\eta(a) = \eta'(a) = \eta(b) = \eta'(b) = 0\). Then \(R(x) = 0\) for every \(x \in [a, b]\).

Proof

The conclusion is the same as in steps 6 and 7 of the proof of theorem 5.66, with the function \(R\) in place of \(q - s\) and the interval \([a, b]\) in place of \([0, L]\).

  1. For numbers \(a < \alpha < \beta < b\) the bump \(q_{\alpha\beta}\) of lemma 4.123 has by point 1 continuous derivatives up to the third order and is zero outside \((\alpha, \beta)\), so at the end points \(a\) and \(b\) it is zero together with its derivative. The lemma allows it as \(\eta\), hence \(\int_\alpha^\beta R\,q_{\alpha\beta}\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\) for every such pair.
  2. This is the same hypothesis as in step 6 of that proof, so by step 7, \(R = 0\) on \((a, b)\). The argument of step 7 is a contradiction drawn in figure 4.210, where \(R\) plays the role of the function \(g\): if \(R\) were positive at the point \(c\), it would be, on an interval around \(c\), larger than half that value, and the integral of its product with a bump on that interval would be positive, not zero. Here, instead of the drawn bump, we take the smooth bump \(q_{\alpha\beta}\), which is positive on the same interval and zero outside it, so the argument stays the same.
  3. The function \(R\) is continuous on \([a, b]\) and by step 2 zero on \((a, b)\). Suppose \(R(a) = c \ne 0\). By continuity at \(a\) with \(\varepsilon = |c|/2\) there is \(\delta > 0\) such that \(|R(x) - c| < |c|/2\) for every \(x \in [a, b]\) with \(x < a + \delta\), hence \(R(x) \ne 0\). But the points of this piece to the right of \(a\) lie in \((a, b)\), where \(R = 0\). So \(R(a) = 0\), and likewise, by the mirror image, \(R(b) = 0\).

Source: EP lemma 3.1 (pp. 140-141) for functions of one variable, without a proof with bumps. The proof is not from a source.

Now the theorem. Let us write down what we will need (figure 5.170). Denote by \(F_{\mathrm{v}}\), \(F_{\mathrm{p}}\) and \(F_{\mathrm{r}}\) the partial derivatives of the function \(F(x, v, p, r)\) with respect to the second, third and fourth variable (see definition 1.26, Partial derivative): we differentiate with respect to one variable and hold the other three fixed. All three functions \(\hat F\) are drawn over the same \(x\)-axis. For a given function \(v\) take the point \(x\) with its slope \(v'(x)\) and curvature \(v''(x)\). Then \(\hat F_{\mathrm{v}}(x) = F_{\mathrm{v}}\big(x, v(x), v'(x), v''(x)\big)\) is a function of the number \(x\), and likewise \(\hat F_{\mathrm{p}}\) and \(\hat F_{\mathrm{r}}\). The derivative \(\mathrm{d}\hat F_{\mathrm{p}}/\mathrm{d}x\) is the derivative of this composite function with respect to \(x\), not the partial derivative with respect to the first variable.

In the proof we will choose the variations separately for each end. Four such variations, and two more for a point inside the beam, which we will need at the end of the section, are drawn in figure 5.171, all above the axis \(x\) from \(0\) to \(L\).

Theorem 5.70 Euler-Lagrange equation (Euler-Lagrangeova enačba)

Let \(F(x, v, p, r)\) be a function that is continuous on \([0, L] \times \mathbb R^3\) and has continuous partial derivatives \(F_{\mathrm{v}}\), \(F_{\mathrm{p}}\) and \(F_{\mathrm{r}}\) there. Let \(\Pi\) be the functional (5.68) and \(v\) an admissible function for which \(\hat F_{\mathrm{p}}\) is continuously differentiable on \([0, L]\) and \(\hat F_{\mathrm{r}}\) is twice continuously differentiable on \([0, L]\). Then \(v\) is a stationary point of the functional \(\Pi\) if and only if it satisfies

\[\hat F_{\mathrm{v}} - \frac{\mathrm{d}\hat F_{\mathrm{p}}}{\mathrm{d}x} + \frac{\mathrm{d}^2 \hat F_{\mathrm{r}}}{\mathrm{d}x^2} = 0 \quad \text{on } [0, L] , \tag{5.69}\]

and at each end the natural conditions:

  • at \(x = L\): \(\hat F_{\mathrm{p}} - \hat F_{\mathrm{r}}{}' = f_{\mathrm{L}}\) when the displacement \(v(L)\) is not prescribed, and \(\hat F_{\mathrm{r}} = \mu_{\mathrm{L}}\) when the rotation \(v'(L)\) is not prescribed,
  • at \(x = 0\): \(\hat F_{\mathrm{p}} - \hat F_{\mathrm{r}}{}' = -f_0\) when the displacement \(v(0)\) is not prescribed, and \(\hat F_{\mathrm{r}} = -\mu_0\) when the rotation \(v'(0)\) is not prescribed.
Proof

We must show that the first variation is the sum of an integral and terms at the ends. Then with special variations we extract the equation and each natural condition.

1. First variation.

  1. Let \(\eta\) be an admissible variation and \(c < d\) arbitrary numbers. For the cantilever the functions \(v + \alpha\,\eta\) are the deflections above and below the true one in figure 5.168. For every \(x \in [0, L]\) and every real \(\alpha\) let \(f(x, \alpha) = F\big(x, v + \alpha\,\eta, v' + \alpha\,\eta', v'' + \alpha\,\eta''\big)\), where \(v\), \(\eta\) and their derivatives are taken at \(x\). At fixed \(x\), \(f\) is a composite function: the map \(\alpha \mapsto \big(v + \alpha\,\eta, v' + \alpha\,\eta', v'' + \alpha\,\eta''\big)\) with derivative \((\eta, \eta', \eta'')\), followed by the function \(F\) of the three variables \((v, p, r)\), which has continuous partial derivatives on \(\mathbb R^3\). By the chain rule (lemma 4.43) with \(D = \mathbb R\) therefore
    \[\frac{\partial f}{\partial \alpha}(x, \alpha) = F_{\mathrm{v}}\,\eta + F_{\mathrm{p}}\,\eta' + F_{\mathrm{r}}\,\eta'' ,\]
    where the derivatives of \(F\) are taken at the point \(\big(x, v + \alpha\,\eta, v' + \alpha\,\eta', v'' + \alpha\,\eta''\big)\). The functions \(f\) and \(\partial f/\partial\alpha\) are continuous on the rectangle \([0, L] \times [c, d]\) as composites of continuous functions (lemma 4.35 with four coordinates and corollary 1.65), since \(F\), \(F_{\mathrm{v}}\), \(F_{\mathrm{p}}\), \(F_{\mathrm{r}}\), \(v\), \(\eta\) and the first two derivatives of \(v\) and \(\eta\) are continuous.
  2. By lemma 4.19, point 1, the function \(\alpha \mapsto \int_0^L f(x, \alpha)\,\mathrm{d}x\) is differentiable on \((c, d)\) and its derivative is the integral of the derivative. The numbers \(c < d\) are arbitrary, so this holds for every real \(\alpha\). The terms at the ends in \(\Pi(v + \alpha\,\eta)\) are linear in \(\alpha\). At \(\alpha = 0\) the first variation, which is the slope of the curve \(\Pi(v + \alpha\,\eta)\) at \(\alpha = 0\), is therefore
    \[\begin{aligned} \delta\Pi(v, \eta) &= \int_0^L \big(\hat F_{\mathrm{v}}\,\eta + \hat F_{\mathrm{p}}\,\eta' + \hat F_{\mathrm{r}}\,\eta''\big)\,\mathrm{d}x \\ &\quad - f_0\,\eta(0) - \mu_0\,\eta'(0) - f_{\mathrm{L}}\,\eta(L) - \mu_{\mathrm{L}}\,\eta'(L) . \end{aligned}\]
  3. Integration by parts (corollary 3.56), three times. First with \(\hat F_{\mathrm{p}}\) and \(\eta\):
    \[\int_0^L \hat F_{\mathrm{p}}\,\eta'\,\mathrm{d}x = \big[\hat F_{\mathrm{p}}\,\eta\big]_0^L - \int_0^L \hat F_{\mathrm{p}}{}'\,\eta\,\mathrm{d}x .\]
    Second with \(\hat F_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(\eta'\):
    \[\int_0^L \hat F_{\mathrm{r}}\,\eta''\,\mathrm{d}x = \big[\hat F_{\mathrm{r}}\,\eta'\big]_0^L - \int_0^L \hat F_{\mathrm{r}}{}'\,\eta'\,\mathrm{d}x .\]
    Third with \(\hat F_{\mathrm{r}}{}'\) and \(\eta\):
    \[\int_0^L \hat F_{\mathrm{r}}{}'\,\eta'\,\mathrm{d}x = \big[\hat F_{\mathrm{r}}{}'\,\eta\big]_0^L - \int_0^L \hat F_{\mathrm{r}}{}''\,\eta\,\mathrm{d}x .\]
  4. We combine:
    \[\begin{aligned} \delta\Pi(v, \eta) &= \int_0^L \Big(\hat F_{\mathrm{v}} - \hat F_{\mathrm{p}}{}' + \hat F_{\mathrm{r}}{}''\Big)\,\eta\,\mathrm{d}x \\ &\quad + \Big[\big(\hat F_{\mathrm{p}} - \hat F_{\mathrm{r}}{}'\big)\,\eta + \hat F_{\mathrm{r}}\,\eta'\Big]_0^L \\ &\quad - f_0\,\eta(0) - \mu_0\,\eta'(0) - f_{\mathrm{L}}\,\eta(L) - \mu_{\mathrm{L}}\,\eta'(L) . \end{aligned} \tag{5.70}\]

2. The equation.

  1. If \(\delta\Pi(v, \eta) = 0\) for all admissible variations, this holds also for those that are zero at both end points together with their derivative. Such variations are always admissible by choice. The terms at the ends in equation (5.70) are then zero, and \(\int_0^L R\,\eta\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\) with \(R = \hat F_{\mathrm{v}} - \hat F_{\mathrm{p}}{}' + \hat F_{\mathrm{r}}{}''\). The function \(R\) is continuous because \(\hat F_{\mathrm{v}}\) (lemma 4.35 with four coordinates), \(\hat F_{\mathrm{p}}{}'\) and \(\hat F_{\mathrm{r}}{}''\) are continuous. By lemma 5.69, \(R = 0\) on \([0, L]\), which is equation (5.69).

3. Natural conditions.

  1. When the equation holds, only the terms at the ends remain in equation (5.70). At \(x = L\) what remains is \(\big(\hat F_{\mathrm{p}} - \hat F_{\mathrm{r}}{}' - f_{\mathrm{L}}\big)(L)\,\eta(L) + \big(\hat F_{\mathrm{r}}(L) - \mu_{\mathrm{L}}\big)\,\eta'(L)\), at \(x = 0\) it is \(-\big(\hat F_{\mathrm{p}} - \hat F_{\mathrm{r}}{}' + f_0\big)(0)\,\eta(0) - \big(\hat F_{\mathrm{r}}(0) + \mu_0\big)\,\eta'(0)\).
  2. Let \(v(L)\) be free, that is, let \(\eta(L)\) not be prescribed to be zero. Take \(\eta_1(x) = (x/L)^2\,(3 - 2x/L)\). It has continuous derivatives of all orders, because it is a polynomial, and \(\eta_1(0) = \eta_1'(0) = 0\), \(\eta_1(L) = 1\), \(\eta_1'(L) = 0\). At \(x = 0\) it is zero together with its derivative (the graph touches the axis there), and at \(x = L\) it has a zero derivative, so it is admissible whatever is prescribed at \(x = 0\) and whether \(v'(L)\) is prescribed or not. Of the four numbers \(\eta_1(0)\), \(\eta_1'(0)\), \(\eta_1(L)\), \(\eta_1'(L)\) only \(\eta_1(L) = 1\) is nonzero, so of the terms at the ends a single one remains. The first variation is then \(\big(\hat F_{\mathrm{p}} - \hat F_{\mathrm{r}}{}' - f_{\mathrm{L}}\big)(L)\), which must be zero. At \(x = 0\) we proceed in the same way with \(\eta_3(x) = (1 - x/L)^2\,(1 + 2x/L)\), which has \(\eta_3(0) = 1\), \(\eta_3'(0) = 0\), \(\eta_3(L) = \eta_3'(L) = 0\).
  3. Let \(v'(L)\) be free. Take \(\eta_2(x) = x^2\,(x - L)/L^2\), which has \(\eta_2(0) = \eta_2'(0) = \eta_2(L) = 0\) and \(\eta_2'(L) = 1\): the graph passes through \(L\) at 45 degrees. The first variation is \(\hat F_{\mathrm{r}}(L) - \mu_{\mathrm{L}}\), which must be zero. At \(x = 0\) we take \(\eta_4(x) = x\,(x - L)^2/L^2\) with \(\eta_4(0) = 0\), \(\eta_4'(0) = 1\) and zeros at \(L\), and we get \(\hat F_{\mathrm{r}}(0) + \mu_0 = 0\).
  4. Conversely: if the equation and all natural conditions hold, the integral and the term at each end in equation (5.70) are zero. At an end each of the two summands is zero, either because the coefficient is zero by the natural condition, or because \(\eta\) (or \(\eta'\)) is zero there by the definition of an admissible variation. Hence \(\delta\Pi(v, \eta) = 0\) for every admissible variation.

Source: EP (3.5.39), (3.5.40) (p. 144) for \(F(x, u, u')\) and EP p. 146 for higher order, without proof. Differentiation under the integral is lemma 4.19, which in content is LEBL2 theorem 9.1.1 (PDF pp. 65-66). The step-by-step proof for \(F(x, v, v', v'')\) is not from a source.

We proved the theorem with three tools: differentiation under the integral, integration by parts and the lemma on the bump. Inside, the variation is an arbitrary function, so the lemma with a bump makes its coefficient vanish. At the ends the coefficients of the values of \(\eta\) and \(\eta'\) that the supports leave free remain, and they give the natural conditions, each with one of the variations \(\eta_1\) to \(\eta_4\).

The theorem requires \(\hat F_{\mathrm{r}}\) to be twice continuously differentiable, because the integration by parts in step 3 differentiates \(\hat F_{\mathrm{r}}\) twice. For the beam \(\hat F_{\mathrm{r}} = EI\,v'' = M\). For the cantilever with a force at the end this is the straight line \(M = 500\ \text{N}\,(x - L)\), which has continuous derivatives of all orders, so the assumption holds. It fails when a point force acts at an interior point: there the shear force \(V = -M'\) jumps, for a force of \(1\) kN at the middle of a simply supported beam from \(-500\) N to \(+500\) N, and \(M'\) is not continuous. Then equation (5.69) holds only on each piece separately, and at the point of the force we get in addition a condition for the jump, as we show at the end of the section.

Let us test the theorem on three functionals, first on the examples of figure 5.172.

  1. On the left, part (a): the bar with the axial load \(q_{\mathrm{x}}\) (written \(q\) in chapter 4). The displacement \(u\) along the axis plays the role of the function \(v\), and \(F = \tfrac12\,EA\,p^2 - q_{\mathrm{x}}\,v\) does not contain \(r\), so \(\hat F_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\). Then \(F_{\mathrm{v}} = -q_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(\hat F_{\mathrm{p}} = EA\,u' = N\), and equation (5.69) is \(-q_{\mathrm{x}} - N' = 0\), that is \(N' + q_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\), the equilibrium of the bar of section 4.4.4. The natural condition at \(x = L\) is \(\hat F_{\mathrm{p}} = f_{\mathrm{L}}\), that is \(N(L) = f_{\mathrm{L}}\), where \(f_{\mathrm{L}}\) is the axial force acting on the end of the bar. A bar carries no couples at its ends, \(\mu_0 = \mu_{\mathrm{L}} = 0\), so the condition for the rotation, \(\hat F_{\mathrm{r}} = \mu_{\mathrm{L}}\), holds by itself because \(\hat F_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\). The theorem thus covers the bar of chapter 4, with a more demanding set of admissible functions that also have a continuous second derivative.
  2. The beam. The function \(F = \tfrac12\,EI\,r^2 - q\,v\) has \(F_{\mathrm{v}} = -q\), \(F_{\mathrm{p}} = 0\) and \(F_{\mathrm{r}} = EI\,r\), so \(\hat F_{\mathrm{r}} = EI\,v'' = M\). Equation (5.69) is \(-q + (EI\,v'')'' = 0\), that is \((EI\,v'')'' = q\), which for constant stiffness gives \(EI\,v'''' = q\) with \(q\) positive up. The natural condition for the displacement at \(x = L\) is \(-\hat F_{\mathrm{r}}{}' = f_{\mathrm{L}}\). Since the shear force is \(V = -M' = -(EI\,v'')'\) (D1, section 5.2), this is \(V(L) = f_{\mathrm{L}} = F_{\mathrm{y}}\), and the natural condition for the rotation is \(M(L) = EI\,v''(L) = \mu_{\mathrm{L}} = M_0\). Both are exactly the conditions of a free end of section 5.7.2, now derived from the energy. At \(x = 0\) the conditions give \(V(0) = -f_0\) and \(M(0) = -\mu_0\): the load at the left end is opposite to the internal force, because the internal force is the force of the part at larger \(x\) on the part at smaller \(x\).
  3. On the right, part (b): the beam on an elastic foundation with an axial force. The function \(F = \tfrac12\,EI\,r^2 + \tfrac12\,c\,p^2 + \tfrac12\,k\,v^2 - q\,v\) with constant numbers \(c\) and \(k\) gives \(-q + k\,v - (c\,v')' + (EI\,v'')'' = 0\), that is \(EI\,v'''' - c\,v'' + k\,v = q\) for constant \(EI\) (we checked the computation with SymPy). In chapter 25 we will see that the term \(\tfrac12\,k\,v^2\) describes an elastic foundation with stiffness \(k\) (force per unit length per unit deflection), and in chapter 8 that the term \(\tfrac12\,c\,p^2\) describes, for small slopes, a constant tensile axial force \(c\) (lemma 8.149). Here the example serves as a test that the theorem is not limited to our cases.

The cantilever of the introduction (figure 5.140): \(q = 0\), \(F_{\mathrm{y}} = -500\) N at the free end, \(M_0 = 0\). The equation is \(v'''' = 0\). The essential conditions at the clamp \(x = 0\) are \(v(0) = v'(0) = 0\), the natural ones at \(x = L\) are \(V(L) = -EI\,v'''(L) = -500\) N and \(M(L) = EI\,v''(L) = 0\). Since \(v'''' = 0\), \(v'''\) is constant (corollary 1.86), and the first condition gives \(v''' = 500\ \text{N}/EI\). Integrating from \(L\) toward \(x\) and using the second condition \(v''(L) = 0\) we get \(v''(x) = 500\ \text{N}\,(x - L)/EI\), then by integrating twice and using the essential conditions

\[v(x) = \frac{500\ \text{N}}{EI}\Big(\frac{x^3}{6} - \frac{L\,x^2}{2}\Big) = -\frac{500\ \text{N}\,x^2\,(3L - x)}{6\,EI} ,\]

and the displacement of the end is \(v(L) = -500\ \text{N}\cdot (1\ \text{m})^3/(3 \cdot 20\,906{,}7\ \text{N m}^2) = -7{,}972\) mm. This is the deflection that equilibrium gave in section 5.9, now from the functional.

A force at an interior point. If a force \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\) acts at a point \(x_0\) inside the beam, we add the term \(-F_{\mathrm{y}}\,v(x_0)\) to the functional. For the true deflection curve the shear force jumps at \(x_0\), so \(\hat F_{\mathrm{r}} = M\) is not twice continuously differentiable on \([0, L]\), and we apply the theorem on the pieces \([0, x_0]\) and \([x_0, L]\). We carry out steps 1 to 4 of the proof on each piece and add the integrals (lemma 3.54, point 2). The argument of the proof of lemma 5.69 with bumps \(q_{\alpha\beta}\) lying inside one piece gives the equation on each piece. The terms at the ends of the pieces add up at \(x_0\) to \(\big[V(x_0^-) - V(x_0^+) - F_{\mathrm{y}}\big]\,\eta(x_0) + \big[M(x_0^-) - M(x_0^+)\big]\,\eta'(x_0)\), where for the beam we wrote \(\hat F_{\mathrm{p}} - \hat F_{\mathrm{r}}{}' = V\) and \(\hat F_{\mathrm{r}} = M\). The second bracket is zero, because \(EI\) is constant and \(v''\) is continuous. The variation \(\eta(x) = x^2\,(L - x)^2\) is zero at both ends together with its derivative and has \(\eta(x_0) > 0\), so the first bracket is zero too: \(V(x_0^+) - V(x_0^-) = -F_{\mathrm{y}}\), as in lemma 5.12. A couple with moment \(M_0\) at an interior point adds the term \(-M_0\,v'(x_0)\) and requires \(v''\) to jump at \(x_0\), so there we allow functions that have only a continuous first derivative. The same computation, completed with the variation \(x^2\,(L - x)^2\,(x - x_0)\), which has the value zero and a nonzero derivative at \(x_0\), gives the jump of the moment \(M(x_0^+) - M(x_0^-) = -M_0\).

Lemma 5.71 A minimum is a stationary point (najmanjša vrednost je stacionarna točka)

Let the hypotheses on \(F\) of theorem 5.70 hold. If \(\Pi\) has its smallest value among admissible functions at an admissible function \(v\), that is \(\Pi(w) \ge \Pi(v)\) for every admissible \(w\), then \(v\) is a stationary point of the functional \(\Pi\).

Proof

We must show that the first variation is zero in every admissible direction. We get it from the derivative at an interior extremum.

  1. Let \(\eta\) be an admissible variation. The function \(g(\alpha) = \Pi(v + \alpha\,\eta)\) is differentiable at every real \(\alpha\) by steps 1 and 2 of the proof of theorem 5.70.
  2. The function \(v + \alpha\,\eta\) is admissible for every \(\alpha\), so \(g(\alpha) \ge g(0)\). The function \(g\) therefore has its smallest value on \((-1, 1)\) at the interior point \(0\).
  3. By lemma 1.83, \(g'(0) = \delta\Pi(v, \eta) = 0\), and the tangent to the graph of \(g\) is horizontal there.

Source: EP (3.5.32) (p. 143), in the same sense. The step-by-step proof is not from a source.

The proof used that \(v + \alpha\,\eta\) is admissible for positive and for negative \(\alpha\), so \(0\) is an interior point, like the bottom of the parabola in figure 5.168. For the beam this is so because \(\eta\) is zero wherever the supports prescribe a displacement or a rotation, so \(v + \alpha\,\eta\) satisfies the supports for every \(\alpha\). It would not be so with a support that only carries the beam but does not hold it down. There only displacements to one side are admissible, for example \(\alpha \ge 0\), and the smallest value can be at the end point \(\alpha = 0\) with a slope that is not zero: the function \(g(\alpha) = \alpha\) has its smallest value on \(\alpha \ge 0\) at \(\alpha = 0\), but its derivative there is \(1\).

The first part of the route is thus complete: the smallest value requires stationarity, and stationarity requires, when \(v\) has the smoothness of theorem 5.70, equation (5.69) and the natural conditions. The question remains whether a stationary point really gives the smallest value, and that is the subject of section 5.11.5. First, though, let us look at what the conditions at the ends mean.

5.11.4 Essential and natural boundary conditions

In the proof of the theorem we got two terms at each end: the coefficient of \(\eta\) and the coefficient of \(\eta'\). Each is either multiplied by a variation that is zero because of the support (at the clamp of the cantilever in figure 5.173, \(\eta(0)\) and \(\eta'(0)\) are zero), or the variation is free there and the coefficient must be zero, otherwise we would choose a variation such as \(\eta_1\), for which the first variation is not zero. This is a general rule (EP p. 147). Where a support prescribes the displacement \(v\) or the rotation \(v'\), the condition is essential (bistven), because it is already contained in the set of admissible functions, and the coefficient of the variation gives nothing. Where the displacement or the rotation is not prescribed, the coefficient must be zero. This condition is called natural (naraven), because the first variation itself produces it.

For the beam there are two pairs at each end (figure 5.173, the cantilever above, clamped into the wall at \(x = 0\) and the simply supported beam below): the displacement \(v\) with the shear force \(V\), and the rotation \(v'\) with the moment \(M\). The theorem gives exactly one condition for each pair. If \(v(L)\) is prescribed, as at the left and right support below, the condition is essential, \(\eta(L) = 0\), and the force \(V(L)\) is not prescribed but is a reaction of the support, determined by the solution. If \(v(L)\) is not prescribed, as at the free end of the cantilever with the force \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\) above, the condition is natural, \(V(L) = f_{\mathrm{L}}\). Both members of a pair cannot be prescribed (EP pp. 146-147): the force in a support that holds \(v(L)\) is no longer ours to choose. The support gives as much force as is needed for the end to stay in place. For a uniform load \(q_0 = 1\) kN/m on a simply supported beam with \(L = 2\) m this is the reaction \(q_0 L/2 = 1\) kN (figure 5.31), and if we prescribed another value, the beam would not be in equilibrium. The equation of fourth order has four free constants (section 5.8), and four conditions, one from each pair at each end, determine them when the supports exclude rigid motion (the condition of corollary 5.75), otherwise see the end of section 5.11.6.

At \(x = 0\) the natural conditions are \(V(0) = -f_0\) and \(M(0) = -\mu_0\). The cantilever has essential conditions at \(x = 0\) and natural ones at \(x = L\) (where the couple \(M_0\) also acts), while the simply supported beam of section 5.1.3 has the essential \(v(0) = v(L) = 0\) (the left and right support) and the natural \(M(0) = 0\) and \(M(L) = 0\). This is exactly the condition the functional produces by itself.

Essential conditions behave differently from natural ones in an approximate computation: the trial field must be chosen so that it satisfies the essential conditions, but not the natural ones, because those are not part of the set of admissible functions. The true minimum satisfies them by itself. We will see an example in section 5.11.6.

5.11.5 Second variation and the smallest value

A stationary point can be a lowest point, a highest point or a saddle (section 4.6.6). How do we tell them apart? For a function of one number we look at the second derivative (figure 5.175, top). On the axes \(\alpha\) and \(g\) three functions are drawn, all with a horizontal tangent at \(\alpha = 0\). Where the second derivative is positive, the slope grows, the graph bends upward and \(0\) is the lowest point. Where it is negative, \(0\) is the highest point. Where it is zero, the second derivative does not decide: a graph like \(\alpha^3\) has a horizontal tangent, but is neither a bottom nor a top. At a saddle of the functional, \(g\) is a bottom in some directions \(\eta\) and a top in others. Here we look at the second derivative of the function \(g(\alpha) = \Pi(v + \alpha\,\eta)\).

Definition 5.72 Second variation (druga variacija)

Let \(\Pi\) be the functional (5.68), \(v\) an admissible function, \(\eta\) an admissible variation and \(g(\alpha) = \Pi(v + \alpha\,\eta)\). If \(g\) has a second derivative at \(\alpha = 0\), the second variation (druga variacija) of the functional \(\Pi\) at \(v\) in the direction \(\eta\) is the number

\[\delta^2\Pi(v, \eta) = g''(0) = \frac{\mathrm{d}^2}{\mathrm{d}\alpha^2}\,\Pi(v + \alpha\,\eta)\Big|_{\alpha = 0} .\]

Source: EP (3.5.33) (p. 143), where the second variation is defined with an extra factor \(\alpha^2/2\), which does not change its sign.

We compute the second derivative of \(g\), which tells how its graph bends, like the first, by differentiating under the integral. Denote by \(F_{\mathrm{ij}}\) the second partial derivative of \(F\) with respect to the \(i\)-th and the \(j\)-th of the variables \((v, p, r)\), for \(i, j = 1, 2, 3\), and write \((\eta_1, \eta_2, \eta_3) = (\eta, \eta', \eta'')\).

Lemma 5.73 Second variation as an integral (druga variacija kot integral)

Let \(F(x, v, p, r)\) be a function that is continuous on \([0, L] \times \mathbb R^3\) and has continuous partial derivatives of the first and second order with respect to the variables \(v\), \(p\) and \(r\) there. Let \(\Pi\) be the functional (5.68), \(v\) an admissible function and \(\eta\) an admissible variation. Then \(g(\alpha) = \Pi(v + \alpha\,\eta)\) is twice differentiable at every real \(\alpha\), and

\[g''(\alpha) = \int_0^L \sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{3}\sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^{3} F_{\mathrm{ij}}\big(x, v + \alpha\,\eta, v' + \alpha\,\eta', v'' + \alpha\,\eta''\big)\,\eta_{\mathrm{i}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{d}x , \tag{5.71}\]

where \(v\), \(\eta\) and their derivatives are taken at \(x\). At \(\alpha = 0\) this is \(\delta^2\Pi(v, \eta)\).

Proof

We must show that we may differentiate the slope \(g'\) of the graph under the integral once more.

  1. Write \(F_1 = F_{\mathrm{v}}\), \(F_2 = F_{\mathrm{p}}\) and \(F_3 = F_{\mathrm{r}}\). By steps 1 and 2 of the proof of theorem 5.70, the slope \(g'(\alpha)\) of the graph for every real \(\alpha\) is
    \[\begin{aligned} g'(\alpha) &= \int_0^L h(x, \alpha)\,\mathrm{d}x \\ &\quad - f_0\,\eta(0) - \mu_0\,\eta'(0) - f_{\mathrm{L}}\,\eta(L) - \mu_{\mathrm{L}}\,\eta'(L) , \\ h(x, \alpha) &= \sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{3} F_{\mathrm{i}}\big(x, v + \alpha\,\eta, v' + \alpha\,\eta', v'' + \alpha\,\eta''\big)\,\eta_{\mathrm{i}} . \end{aligned}\]
  2. At fixed \(x\) each \(F_{\mathrm{i}}\) is a function of the three variables \((v, p, r)\) with continuous partial derivatives \(F_{\mathrm{i}1}\), \(F_{\mathrm{i}2}\), \(F_{\mathrm{i}3}\). By the chain rule (lemma 4.43), used as in step 1 of the proof of the theorem, the change of the slope is \(\partial h/\partial\alpha = \sum_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j}} F_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{i}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{j}}\), with the derivatives \(F_{\mathrm{ij}}\) at the same point.
  3. Let \(c < d\) be arbitrary numbers on the \(\alpha\) axis. The functions \(h\) and \(\partial h/\partial\alpha\) are continuous on the rectangle \([0, L] \times [c, d]\), as in step 1 of the proof of the theorem (lemma 4.35 with four coordinates and corollary 1.65).
  4. By lemma 4.19, point 1, the integral in \(g'\) is differentiable on the interval \((c, d)\) with derivative \(\int_0^L \partial h/\partial\alpha\,\mathrm{d}x\). The terms at the ends in \(g'\) do not depend on \(\alpha\), so their derivative is zero. The numbers \(c < d\) are arbitrary, so equation (5.71) holds for every real \(\alpha\).

Source: EP (3.5.33) (p. 143) defines the second variation without this formula. The proof is not from a source.

Let us check the formula on the cantilever of figure 5.168, where \(\eta = x^2/L^2\). For the beam the only nonzero second derivative is \(F_{33} = F_{\mathrm{rr}} = EI\), and \(\eta_3 = \eta'' = 2/L^2\), so

\[\delta^2\Pi(v, \eta) = \int_0^L EI\,\Big(\frac{2}{L^2}\Big)^2\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{4\,EI}{L^3} = 83\,626{,}8\ \text{N/m} .\]

In the figure the parabola is \(\Pi(v + \alpha\,\eta) = -1{,}993\ \text{J} + 2\,EI\,\alpha^2/L^3\), and its second derivative with respect to \(\alpha\) is \(4\,EI/L^3\), the same number (computed with SymPy). The second variation is thus what we see in the figure as the bending of the parabola: the larger it is, the faster the energy grows when we move away from the true deflection. In millimetres \(83\,626{,}8\) N/m \(= 0{,}0836\) J/mm², and half of it at \(\alpha = 1\) mm, \(0{,}0418\) J, is the rise above the lowest value that we computed for this parabola.

When is \(g''\) nowhere negative? It suffices that the double sum in equation (5.71) is not negative when any three numbers are put in place of \((\eta_1, \eta_2, \eta_3)\). A function \(F\) with continuous second derivatives with respect to \((v, p, r)\) is convex in \((v, p, r)\) (konveksna po \((v, p, r)\)) if for every \(x \in [0, L]\) and every triple \((v, p, r)\)

\[\sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{3}\sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^{3} F_{\mathrm{ij}}(x, v, p, r)\,a_{\mathrm{i}}\,a_{\mathrm{j}} \ge 0 \quad \text{for any three real numbers } a_1, a_2, a_3 .\]

For a function of one number this means \(F'' \ge 0\), so the slope of the graph does not decrease. The general definition of a convex function requires that the graph between two of its points does not lie above the segment joining them. For functions with continuous second derivatives it is equivalent to our condition (CHK §3.2, theorem 3.2, PDF pp. 46-48). For the beam \(F = \tfrac12\,EI\,r^2 - q\,v\), the only nonzero second derivative is \(F_{\mathrm{rr}} = EI\), and the sum is \(EI\,a_3^2 \ge 0\). For the cantilever \(q = 0\) and \(F = \tfrac12\,EI\,r^2\). At the clamp the curvature is \(v''(0) = M(0)/EI = -0{,}02392\) 1/m and the energy density is \(5{,}979\) J/m. Halfway between \(r = 0\) and \(r = v''(0)\) the graph is at \(1{,}495\) J/m and the segment at \(2{,}989\) J/m, \(1{,}495\) J/m higher. The graph lies below the segment, as convexity requires.

Theorem 5.74 Stationary point of a convex functional is a minimum (stacionarna točka konveksnega funkcionala je najmanjša)

Let \(F\) be a function with the hypotheses of lemma 5.73 that is convex in \((v, p, r)\), and \(\Pi\) the functional (5.68). If the admissible function \(v\) is a stationary point of the functional \(\Pi\), then \(\Pi(\tilde v) \ge \Pi(v)\) for every admissible function \(\tilde v\).

Proof

We must show that the function \(g(\alpha) = \Pi\big(v + \alpha\,(\tilde v - v)\big)\) does not decrease on \([0, 1]\). Its derivative at \(0\) is zero, and its second derivative is not negative. For the cantilever \(g\) is drawn in figure 5.175 bottom right.

  1. Let \(\eta = \tilde v - v\). The functions \(v\) and \(\tilde v\) have continuous first two derivatives and equal values where the supports prescribe them, so \(\eta\) is an admissible variation. We have \(g(0) = \Pi(v)\) and \(g(1) = \Pi(\tilde v)\).
  2. By lemma 5.73, \(g\) is twice differentiable at every real \(\alpha\) and \(g''(\alpha)\) is the integral (5.71). Since \(v\) is a stationary point, \(g'(0) = \delta\Pi(v, \eta) = 0\), and the tangent at \(0\) is horizontal.
  3. The integrand in (5.71) is continuous and by convexity, with \(a_{\mathrm{i}} = \eta_{\mathrm{i}}(x)\), it is negative at no \(x\). By monotonicity of the integral (lemma 3.54, point 4), \(g''(\alpha) \ge 0\) for every \(\alpha\): the graph bends downward nowhere.
  4. Let \(0 < s < 1\). The function \(g'\) is differentiable, hence continuous (lemma 1.80). By the mean value theorem 1.85 for \(g'\) on \([0, s]\) there is \(\xi \in (0, s)\) with \(g'(s) - g'(0) = g''(\xi)\,s \ge 0\). Since \(g'(0) = 0\), \(g'(s) \ge 0\).
  5. The function \(g\) is differentiable, hence continuous on \([0, 1]\). By the same theorem for \(g\) on \([0, 1]\) there is \(s \in (0, 1)\) with \(g(1) - g(0) = g'(s)\): the tangent at \(s\) is parallel to the segment from \(g(0)\) to \(g(1)\). By step 4 this is not negative. This is \(\Pi(\tilde v) - \Pi(v) \ge 0\).

Source: The proof is not from a source. EP theorem 6.2 (pp. 283-284) gives the minimum of a quadratic functional with a positive operator, which is a special case.

The theorem showed that for a convex integrand stationarity suffices. For the cantilever, where \(\tilde v\) is the parabola with the same displacement of the end, \(g(\alpha) = -1{,}993\ \text{J} + 0{,}664\ \text{J}\,\alpha^2\) (computed with SymPy). Its derivative \(g'(\alpha) = 1{,}329\ \text{J}\,\alpha\) is zero at \(0\), the tangent at \(s = 1/2\) has the slope \(0{,}664\) J, equal to the slope of the segment, and the difference \(g(1) - g(0) = 0{,}664\) J is by how much the energy of the parabola exceeds that of the true deflection. EP, p. 145, solves the Euler equation (3.5.40) and calls the solution the true minimizer of the functional without mentioning convexity: without it the solution is only a stationary point, which can also be a maximum or a saddle, and convexity is what makes it a minimum. An example with the opposite sign is at the end of section 5.11.6. For the beam \(F\) is a polynomial of second degree in \(r\), so we compute the difference of two energies exactly, and this gives us more: the smallest value is attained only once.

5.11.6 Minimum energy of a beam

For the beam \(F = \tfrac12\,EI\,r^2 - q\,v\), and the functional is the sum of a quadratic part and linear parts. Therefore \(\Pi(v + \alpha\,\eta)\) is a polynomial of second degree in \(\alpha\), as for the truss (equation (4.61)): we expand the square \((v'' + \alpha\,\eta'')^2\), and the terms with \(q\,v\) and the ends are linear. The difference of two energies can then be computed exactly, and the argument does not need the mean value theorem.

What we claim is shown in figure 5.177 on the cantilever of section 5.11.1. At the top the cantilever is clamped into the wall at \(x = 0\), and the \(x\) axis runs along it. We draw the true deflection \(v\) in blackwhite and some other admissible function \(\tilde v\) in blue, here the parabola that we compute below. The difference \(\eta = \tilde v - v\) is measured in the figure at the free end. At the bottom the horizontal axis carries the number \(\alpha\) and the vertical axis the energy \(\Pi(v + \alpha\,\eta)\): at \(\alpha = 0\) we are at \(v\), at \(\alpha = 1\) at \(\tilde v\). The curve is a parabola with its lowest point at \(\alpha = 0\), where the energy is \(-1{,}993\) J. There the tangent is horizontal. At \(\alpha = 1\) the energy is \(-1{,}495\) J, \(0{,}498\) J more than at the bottom. The corollary says that this is so for every admissible \(\tilde v\), and how large the rise is.

Corollary 5.75 Minimum total potential energy of a beam (najmanjša potencialna energija nosilca)

Let \(EI > 0\) be constant and \(q\) a continuous function on \([0, L]\) (a prismatic beam), and let

\[\Pi(v) = \int_0^L \tfrac12\,EI\,v''^2\,\mathrm{d}x - \int_0^L q\,v\,\mathrm{d}x - f_0\,v(0) - \mu_0\,v'(0) - f_{\mathrm{L}}\,v(L) - \mu_{\mathrm{L}}\,v'(L)\]

on the set of admissible functions, which have continuous first two derivatives and satisfy the essential conditions of the supports. Let the supports be such that the only admissible variation with \(\eta'' = 0\) is the zero function. Let the admissible function \(v\) be such that \(M = EI\,v''\) is twice continuously differentiable, \(M'' = q\) on \([0, L]\), and the natural conditions of theorem 5.70 hold. Then for every admissible function \(\tilde v\)

\[\Pi(\tilde v) - \Pi(v) = \int_0^L \tfrac12\,EI\,\big(\tilde v'' - v''\big)^2\,\mathrm{d}x \ge 0 ,\]

with equality only for \(\tilde v = v\).

Proof

We must show the same splitting as for the bar and then that the rise above the bottom, the integral of the square, is zero only for the zero function.

  1. Let \(\eta = \tilde v - v\). It is an admissible variation as in step 1 of the proof of theorem 5.74.
  2. We expand the square as the area of a square with the side \(v'' + \eta''\): \(\tfrac12\,EI\,(v'' + \eta'')^2 = \tfrac12\,EI\,v''^2 + EI\,v''\,\eta'' + \tfrac12\,EI\,\eta''^2\). We split the linear terms \(-q\,\tilde v\) and the terms at the ends into parts for \(v\) and for \(\eta\).
  3. We integrate (linearity of the integral, lemma 3.54, point 1). We get three parts: the energy at \(v\), the square bracket, which is linear in \(\eta\) and fixes the slope at the bottom, and the quadratic term, which is the rise above the bottom:
    \[\Pi(\tilde v) = \Pi(v) + \Big[\int_0^L \big(EI\,v''\,\eta'' - q\,\eta\big)\mathrm{d}x - f_0\,\eta(0) - \mu_0\,\eta'(0) - f_{\mathrm{L}}\,\eta(L) - \mu_{\mathrm{L}}\,\eta'(L)\Big] + \int_0^L \tfrac12\,EI\,\eta''^2\,\mathrm{d}x .\]
  4. The expression in square brackets is \(\delta\Pi(v, \eta)\), since for \(F = \tfrac12\,EI\,r^2 - q\,v\) the integrand of the first variation is \(F_{\mathrm{v}}\,\eta + F_{\mathrm{r}}\,\eta'' = -q\,\eta + EI\,v''\,\eta''\). The hypotheses of theorem 5.70 hold: \(F_{\mathrm{v}} = -q\), \(F_{\mathrm{p}} = 0\) and \(F_{\mathrm{r}} = EI\,r\) are continuous, \(\hat F_{\mathrm{p}} = 0\), \(\hat F_{\mathrm{r}} = M\) is twice continuously differentiable, and equation (5.69) is \(M'' - q = 0\). Since \(v\) satisfies the equation and the natural conditions, the expression is zero by the converse direction of the theorem. So the tangent at the bottom is horizontal.
  5. The integrand \(\tfrac12\,EI\,\eta''^2\) is nowhere negative, so by monotonicity (lemma 3.54, point 4) the integral is not negative: the rise above the bottom is never a descent. So \(\Pi(\tilde v)\) is at least \(\Pi(v)\). This is the equation of the corollary.
  6. Let the integral be zero. As in steps 6 and 7 of the proof of corollary 4.93, the continuous nonnegative function \(\tfrac12\,EI\,\eta''^2\) with zero integral is zero everywhere, so \(\eta'' = 0\). By the hypothesis on the supports \(\eta = 0\), that is \(\tilde v = v\), the bottom itself.

Source: EP (3.3.3), (3.3.4) (p. 124) for the total potential energy of a beam, EP §4.3.1 (pp. 179-183) for the principle. The step-by-step proof is not from the sources.

Every hypothesis of the corollary enters in one step. The condition \(EI > 0\) enters in step 5: because of it the quadratic term is not negative and the parabola of the energy opens upward. It holds for every real material and cross section. What happens with the opposite sign we show at the end of the section on \(-\Pi\). That \(EI\) is constant only shortens the writing: for a continuous \(EI(x) > 0\) all steps go the same way with \(EI(x)\) under the integral, because theorem 5.70 allows \(F\) to depend on \(x\). That the energy is quadratic in \(v\) follows from definition 5.16, which for a small slope replaces the curvature of the axis by \(v''\). For our cantilever the largest slope is \(F\,L^2/(2\,EI) = 0{,}012\) at the free end, and the exact curvature \(v''/(1 + v'^2)^{3/2}\) differs from \(v''\) by at most \(0{,}02\) %. For deflections comparable to the length this no longer holds and the energy is not quadratic. The last hypothesis is the condition on the supports.

The condition on the supports excludes a rigid displacement (figure 5.179). A function with \(\eta'' = 0\) on \([0, L]\) is linear, \(\eta = a + b\,x\): by corollary 1.86 \(\eta' = b\) is constant, and by the same corollary \(\eta - b\,x = a\) is constant too. In part (a) we move a beam without supports from its position before the displacement by \(a\) and turn it with the slope \(b\), and it stays straight. For the cantilever in part (b) the essential conditions \(\eta(0) = \eta'(0) = 0\) give \(a = b = 0\): the wall holds the value and the slope, a line with slope \(b \neq 0\) would have the wrong slope at the wall, so only \(\eta = 0\) remains. For the simply supported beam in part (c) the conditions \(\eta(0) = \eta(L) = 0\) at the supports do the same: a line through the left support misses the right support by \(b\,L\), so again \(\eta = 0\). For a beam without supports, however, the rigid displacement \(a + b\,x\) is an admissible variation. Since its \(\eta'' = 0\), the energy changes along it only by the work of the loads with the opposite sign, and this work is \(a\,\big(\int_0^L q\,\mathrm{d}x + f_0 + f_{\mathrm{L}}\big) + b\,\big(\int_0^L x\,q\,\mathrm{d}x + L\,f_{\mathrm{L}} + \mu_0 + \mu_{\mathrm{L}}\big)\). The brackets are the sum of the forces and the sum of the moments about the point \(x = 0\). If the loads are in equilibrium, both are zero, all functions \(v + a + b\,x\) have the same energy, and the solution is not unique.

From the corollary it follows that the true deflection of the cantilever has the energy \(-1{,}993\) J and that the energy of every other admissible deflection is larger. Figure 5.180 shows this for all parabolas \(\tilde v = a\,x^2\) at once: the horizontal axis carries the number \(a\) in units of \(10^{-3}\) m\(^{-1}\), the vertical axis the energy in joules, the blackwhite curve is the energy of the parabola with the given \(a\) and the blue dashed line the energy of the true deflection. The blackwhite curve is everywhere above the dashed line. In section 5.11.1 we saw this on an example: the parabola with the same end has \(-1{,}329\) J. The difference is given exactly by the integral \(\int_0^L \tfrac12\,EI\,(\tilde v'' - v'')^2\,\mathrm{d}x\), which for this parabola is \(0{,}664\) J, exactly the difference between \(-1{,}329\) J and \(-1{,}993\) J.

Let us test the corollary on a trial field. For the cantilever take the trial function \(\tilde v(x) = a\,x^2\) with an unknown number \(a\) (m\(^{-1}\)). It satisfies the essential conditions \(\tilde v(0) = \tilde v'(0) = 0\), but not the natural ones. Its energy is \(\Pi(a\,x^2) = 2\,EI\,L\,a^2 + 500\ \text{N}\cdot L^2\,a\), where the first term, the bending energy, comes from \(\tfrac12\,EI\,(2a)^2\,L\) and the second, the term of the force, from \(-F_{\mathrm{y}}\,a\,L^2\) with \(F_{\mathrm{y}} = -500\) N. This is a parabola in \(a\), and its lowest point is at \(4\,EI\,L\,a + 500\ \text{N}\cdot L^2 = 0\), so \(a = -500\ \text{N}\cdot L/(4\,EI) = -5{,}979 \cdot 10^{-3}\ \text{m}^{-1}\) and \(\tilde v(L) = a\,L^2 = -5{,}979\) mm. This is \(3/4\) of the true displacement of the end, \(-7{,}972\) mm (true deflection). The energy is \(-1{,}495\) J, which is \(3/4\) of the true \(-1{,}993\) J, and by the corollary it is larger than the true one by \(\int_0^L \tfrac12\,EI\,(\tilde v'' - v'')^2\,\mathrm{d}x = 0{,}498\) J (figure 5.181). In the figure the cantilever is clamped into the wall at \(x = 0\), the deflections above are drawn along the \(x\) axis, and the curvatures below along a second \(x\) axis.

Why does the energy of the parabolas have a bottom at all? Let us look at its two parts in figure 5.180. The bending energy grows with the square of \(a\), while the term of the force falls only linearly, because the force does work proportional to the displacement. For a small deflection the straight line falls faster than the parabola rises, and the sum falls: the beam bends further. For a large deflection the square wins and the sum rises. The bottom is where the two slopes are equal and opposite. There the bending energy is \(+1{,}495\) J and the term of the force \(-2{,}989\) J, twice as much: as it descends, the force gives up twice the energy that the beam stores. This view adds a cause for the smallest energy that the splitting in the proof does not show: equilibrium is the position in which bending further no longer pays.

Why exactly \(3/4\)? The true curvature \(v'' = 500\ \text{N}\,(x - L)/EI\) is linear, largest in absolute value at the clamp and zero at the free end, like the moment \(M = EI\,v''\) (section 5.2). The parabola has the constant curvature \(2a = -500\ \text{N}\cdot L/(2\,EI)\), which is exactly the mean of the true curvature over the length, so the two grey areas between them are equal. It was the same for the hanging bar, where the strain of the best straight line was the mean of the true one (figure 4.288). The displacement of the end, however, does not average the curvature evenly. By integration by parts (corollary 3.56 with the functions \(L - x\) and \(v'\)), the fundamental theorem of calculus 3.55 and \(v(0) = v'(0) = 0\),

\[v(L) = \int_0^L (L - x)\,v''(x)\,\mathrm{d}x ,\]

so the curvature at \(x\) counts with the lever arm \(L - x\), which is largest at the clamp, exactly where the parabola has too little curvature. For the constant curvature \(2a\) the integral gives \(a\,L^2 = -500\ \text{N}\cdot L^3/(4\,EI)\), for the true one \(-500\ \text{N}\cdot L^3/(3\,EI)\), and the ratio is \(3/4\). The energy is also \(3/4\) of the true one. The lowest value of a parabola \(A\,a^2 + B\,a\) is \(\tfrac12\,B\,a\) at its lowest point, and here \(B\,a = -F_{\mathrm{y}}\,\tilde v(L)\), so \(\Pi = -\tfrac12\,F_{\mathrm{y}}\,\tilde v(L)\). The same holds for the true deflection \(v\), which is the lowest point of the family \(t\,v\), so the ratio of the energies equals the ratio of the displacements of the end.

Extending the trial function to \(a\,x^2 + b\,x^3\), the curvature \(2a + 6b\,x\) is linear like the true one, and minimizing over \(a\) and \(b\) gives \(a = -500\ \text{N}\cdot L/(2\,EI)\) and \(b = 500\ \text{N}/(6\,EI)\) (SymPy), which is exactly the true deflection. The true deflection lies in this family and by the corollary has the smallest energy among all admissible functions, so the minimization finds it exactly. This idea, looking for the smallest \(\Pi\) among functions with finitely many parameters, is the idea of computing with trial functions, which we develop in chapter 6.

What convexity does in theorem 5.74 is shown by an example where it is missing. Let us reverse the sign: \(-\Pi\) has the integrand \(-F = -\tfrac12\,EI\,r^2 + q\,v\) and \(-F_{\mathrm{rr}} = -EI < 0\), so it is not convex. The first variation is \(\delta(-\Pi) = -\delta\Pi\), so it has the same Euler equation and the true deflection \(v\) of the cantilever is still a stationary point. But it is not the smallest. In figure 5.182 the horizontal axis carries the number \(t\) in m\(^{-1}\) and the vertical axis the energy in joules. The blue curve is \(\Pi(v + t\,x^2)\) with the bottom \(-1{,}993\) J, the blackwhite one is its mirror image \(-\Pi(v + t\,x^2)\) with the top \(+1{,}993\) J.

  1. Let \(\varphi\) be an admissible variation, \(\varphi \neq 0\). The decomposition in corollary 5.75 for \(\tilde v = v + t\,\varphi\) gives \(\Pi(v + t\,\varphi) = \Pi(v) + t^2 \int_0^L \tfrac12\,EI\,\varphi''^2\,\mathrm{d}x\), so \(-\Pi(v + t\,\varphi) = -\Pi(v) - t^2 \int_0^L \tfrac12\,EI\,\varphi''^2\,\mathrm{d}x\). The integral is positive, because \(\varphi'' \neq 0\) (by the assumption about the supports), so \(-\Pi\) is smaller than at \(v\) for every \(t \neq 0\). The true deflection is the largest value, not the smallest.
  2. Take \(\varphi = x^2\), which meets the essential conditions \(\varphi(0) = \varphi'(0) = 0\). Since \(\varphi'' = 2\), the integral is \(\tfrac12\,EI\,2^2\,L = 2\,EI\,L = 41\,813\) J m\(^2\) for \(EI = 20\,906{,}7\) N m\(^2\) and \(L = 1\) m. This same term was in the energy \(\Pi(a\,x^2)\) above. At \(t = 0\) we have \(-\Pi = +1{,}993\) J.
  3. At \(t = 0{,}01\) m\(^{-1}\) we have \(-\Pi(v + t\,x^2) = 1{,}993 - 41\,813 \cdot 0{,}01^2 = -2{,}188\) J, and at \(t = 0{,}1\) m\(^{-1}\) it is \(-416{,}1\) J, far below the edge of the figure. As \(t \to \infty\) we have \(-\Pi \to -\infty\), so \(-\Pi\) has no smallest value. The theorem must not be applied to \(-\Pi\), because \(-F\) is not convex, and its conclusion indeed fails here.

Even among the parabolas \(a\,x^2\) alone (without \(v\)), \(-\Pi(a\,x^2) = -2\,EI\,L\,a^2 - 500\ \text{N}\cdot L^2\,a\) has no lower bound: as \(a \to \pm\infty\) it tends to \(-\infty\). The highest value of this family is \(+1{,}495\) J at \(a = -5{,}979 \cdot 10^{-3}\) m\(^{-1}\), the mirror image of the bottom in figure 5.180, smaller than \(+1{,}993\) J at the true deflection.

5.11.7 Where next

This section treated a functional of one function of one variable with derivatives up to the second order and a finite set of conditions at two ends. Extensions come where we first need them. Functionals of several functions and constraints with Lagrange multipliers (Lagrangeevi množitelji) will be in chapter 8, where we derive Lagrange's equations from Hamilton's principle. There we will also use the second variation to judge stability, for example in buckling, where convexity fails. The Euler-Lagrange equations for functionals of fields of two and three variables (we already had the functional of a body in section 4.6.6), where the divergence theorem takes the place of integration by parts, follow in chapters 18 and 11. Direct methods, in which the functional is minimized among functions with finitely many parameters, as we did with the parabola, are the subject of chapter 6. The penalty method (kazenska metoda) belongs to chapter 12.
