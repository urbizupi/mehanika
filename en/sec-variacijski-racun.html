Chapter 5 Beam theory (teorija nosilcev)

5.11 Mathematics: calculus of variations

Three times we have already found equilibrium by looking for the smallest energy. For the bar with a load the energy was a function of one number, the extension. For the truss of two bars it was a function of two numbers, the displacements of the node. For the bar and the body the input was a whole function or field, and the output one number, the total potential energy (definition 4.89). Each time equilibrium came out as an equation inside the body and conditions at the ends or on the boundary. Is there one rule behind this that we can prove once for all such problems, the beam included?

Such a rule is given by the calculus of variations (variacijski račun), whose fundamental lemma we proved in chapter 4 (lemma 4.77). In this section we develop it in full for an integral along the length in which a function \(v\) and its first two derivatives appear. The beam has such an integral, and the general form also covers the bar, the beam on an elastic foundation and the beam with an axial force. The route is as follows. First we say what we look for: a rule that assigns one number to a whole function. Then we look at how this number changes when we move the function a little, and from this we derive the equation that the function with the smallest value must satisfy. We find out which conditions at the ends follow by themselves and which must be prescribed. At the end we show when a stationary point really gives the smallest value, and apply this to the beam. We test everything on the cantilever of section 5.1.3: a steel flat bar \(20 \times 40\) mm standing on edge, \(E = 196\) GPa, length \(L = 1\) m, attached at \(x = 0\), with the free end loaded by a force of \(500\) N downward. Its bending stiffness is \(EI = 20\,906{,}7\) N m\(^2\).

We prove the theorems with tools of the earlier chapters: the derivative of an integral with respect to a parameter (lemma 4.19), integration by parts (corollary 3.41), the fundamental lemma with smooth bumps (lemma 4.122) and the derivative at an interior extremum (lemma 1.82). Besides these we use only the chain rule, the mean value theorem and the properties of the integral.

From history

Problems in which we seek a curve with a largest or smallest property had appeared before Euler. In 1686 Newton reduced the problem of the solid of revolution of least resistance to a differential equation (CAJORI, PDF p. 250). In 1696 Jacob Bernoulli proposed the well-known problem of figures with equal perimeter and in 1701 published his own solution (CAJORI, PDF p. 237). Johann Bernoulli found the curve of swiftest descent (CAJORI, PDF p. 238), a problem that he solved in 1697 himself and that his brother Jacob solved the same year (CAJORI, PDF p. 250). L'Hospital also solved it and studied the body of least resistance (BALL, PDF p. 398). In these problems we do not seek a number but a curve. Johann and Jacob Bernoulli quarrelled bitterly over the problem of figures with equal perimeter (CAJORI, PDF p. 238).

Euler took up these problems as Johann's pupil. The problems of figures with equal perimeter, of the curve of swiftest descent in a resisting medium and of the shortest lines on a surface led him to the calculus of variations (BALL, PDF p. 424, CAJORI, PDF p. 250). He first wrote the idea down in a paper of 1741 and extended it in 1744 (BALL, PDF p. 424). His method was essentially geometric (CAJORI, PDF p. 250). In the book of 1744 he wrote that nothing in the universe happens without some largest or smallest value appearing, and that every phenomenon can be explained by direct causes or by seeking a largest or smallest value (TIMO, PDF p. 37, 39). He showed this on the catenary. A chain hung at two points can be treated by the equilibrium of the forces on a small piece of the chain, or by energy: of all curves of the given length the true one is that for which the potential energy of the weight is smallest, that is, where the centre of gravity of the chain is lowest. The problem becomes the search for the extreme value of the integral \(\int w y\,\mathrm{d}s\) for a given length \(s\) of the curve, where \(w\) is the weight per unit length (TIMO, PDF p. 39-40). Both ways gave the same differential equation (TIMO, PDF p. 40). For the elastic bar, Daniel Bernoulli told him that all the force in a curved elastic bar can be expressed by one expression and that this expression is smallest in the elastic curve. For a uniform cross section and a bar that is straight in its natural position this is \(\int \mathrm{d}s/R^2\), where \(R\) is the radius of curvature (TIMO, PDF p. 40). With the calculus of variations Euler obtained from this the same equation that Jacob Bernoulli had already derived from equilibrium (TIMO, PDF p. 40).

At nineteen Lagrange communicated to Euler a general method for such problems (CAJORI, PDF p. 267). Euler held back the publication of his own research for some time so that the young Lagrange could complete his and claim the invention (CAJORI, PDF p. 267). Euler had assumed the limits of the integrals to be fixed, that is, fixed end points of the sought curve. Lagrange removed this restriction and allowed all coordinates of the curve to vary at the same time. Euler's method lacked an analytic foundation, and Lagrange supplied it by separating the principles from geometric considerations (CAJORI, PDF p. 267). The complete development of the new calculus was first carried out by Lagrange in 1759 (BALL, PDF p. 424). In 1766 Euler introduced the name that this section bears (CAJORI, PDF p. 267).

Let us return to the question of the section: three times in a row, for the bar, the body and the beam, the smallest energy led to an equation in the interior and to conditions at the ends. Lagrange left the end points of the curve free, and from this idea we shall get the conditions at the ends of the beam.

5.11.1 Functional

Let us look at the cantilever. Every curve that leaves the origin horizontally at the clamp \(x = 0\) is a possible shape of bending, but only one is in equilibrium. The energy of each of them is a single datum about the whole curve. Take two curves with the same displacement of the end, \(v(L) = -7{,}972\) mm: the true deflection and the parabola \(v(x) = -7{,}972\ \text{mm}\cdot x^2/L^2\). The energies differ because the curves bend differently. For the true deflection the bending energy is \(\int_0^L \tfrac12\,EI\,v''^2\,\mathrm{d}x = 1{,}993\) J, for the parabola, which has the constant curvature \(v'' = -0{,}01594\) 1/m, it is \(2{,}657\) J. The potential energy of the force is the same for both, \(-F_{\mathrm{y}}\,v(L) = 500\ \text{N}\cdot(-7{,}972\ \text{mm}) = -3{,}986\) J, since they have the same end (the force \(F_{\mathrm{y}} = -500\) N points down, and the deflection is also down). The total potential energy is thus \(-1{,}993\) J for the true deflection and \(-1{,}329\) J for the parabola (figure 5.62). The number depends on the whole curve and not only on its end.

Definition 5.66 Functional (funkcional)

Let \(\mathcal A\) be a set of functions that take a number \(x\) from the interval \([0, L]\) as input and give a real number as output. A functional (funkcional) on \(\mathcal A\) is a rule \(\Pi\) that assigns one real number \(\Pi(v)\) to every function \(v\) in \(\mathcal A\). The input of a functional is thus a whole function, the output a number.

Source: EP §3.5.2, equation (3.5.14) (p. 138), CM §7.6.2 (PDF p. 343).

Functionals are the total potential energy of the bar (equation (4.62)) and of the beam, and every integral of the form

\[\begin{aligned} \Pi(v) &= \int_0^L F\big(x,\,v(x),\,v'(x),\,v''(x)\big)\,\mathrm{d}x \\ &\quad - f_0\,v(0) - \mu_0\,v'(0) - f_{\mathrm{L}}\,v(L) - \mu_{\mathrm{L}}\,v'(L) , \end{aligned} \tag{5.63}\]

where \(F(x, v, p, r)\) is a given function of four numbers, continuous on \([0, L] \times \mathbb R^3\) (the integrand, in which the letter \(v\) stands for the number \(v(x)\), \(p\) for the number \(v'(x)\) and \(r\) for the number \(v''(x)\)). Continuity of a function of four numbers is defined as for a function of a point in space (definition 1.67 and the text below it), except that the distance is computed from four coordinates. Lemma 4.35 on the continuity of a composite function holds for it too, with the same proof and one coordinate more. Hence \(x \mapsto F\big(x, v(x), v'(x), v''(x)\big)\) is continuous whenever \(v\), \(v'\) and \(v''\) are continuous, and its integral exists (theorem 3.38). The numbers \(f_0\) and \(f_{\mathrm{L}}\) are forces at the ends pointing in the direction of increasing \(v\), and the numbers \(\mu_0\) and \(\mu_{\mathrm{L}}\) are moments of couples at the ends that turn the cross section in the direction of increasing \(v'\). The potential energy of a force is \(-f\,v\), as for the load in section 4.6.3. The potential energy of a couple is \(-\mu\,v'\), because the couple does the work \(\mu\,v'\) in the displacement \(v\) (lemma 5.61 with \(v\) in place of \(\delta v\)). For a beam with a force \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\) and a couple \(M_0\) at the end \(x = L\) we therefore have \(f_{\mathrm{L}} = F_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\mu_{\mathrm{L}} = M_0\). For the cantilever of the introduction \(f_{\mathrm{L}} = F_{\mathrm{y}} = -500\) N and the other four values are zero.

Which functions are admissible? Admissible functions (dopustne funkcije) are functions \(v\) with continuous first two derivatives on \([0, L]\) (one-sided at the end points) and with prescribed values of \(v\) or \(v'\) at the ends where the supports require them. Two derivatives are required by the energy, which contains \(v''\). Among the admissible functions are the polynomials we will take as trial fields and the true deflection curves of the beams of section 5.1.3. What the supports prescribe we will see in section 5.11.4.

5.11.2 First variation

How does the functional change when we move the function \(v\)? For the truss we took the displacement \(\alpha\,\vek v\) and looked at a function of the number \(\alpha\) (section 4.6.6). We do the same here. Let \(v\) be an admissible function and \(\eta\) a function that differs from it by an admissible displacement: \(v + \alpha\,\eta\) must be admissible for every \(\alpha\). This means that \(\eta\) has continuous first two derivatives on \([0, L]\), that \(\eta = 0\) at an end where the displacement \(v\) is prescribed, and \(\eta' = 0\) at an end where the rotation \(v'\) is prescribed. Such an \(\eta\) is called an admissible variation (dopustna variacija). For the beam it corresponds to the virtual displacement across the axis of definition 5.60: a displacement the supports allow. Books write \(\delta v = \alpha\,\eta\) and call it the variation of the function (EP (3.5.3), p. 135), and the letter \(\delta\) means a small admissible change of the function, as for the virtual displacement.

Definition 5.67 First variation (prva variacija)

Let \(\Pi\) be the functional (5.63), \(v\) an admissible function and \(\eta\) an admissible variation. The function of the number \(\alpha\)

\[g(\alpha) = \Pi(v + \alpha\,\eta)\]

is defined for every real \(\alpha\). If \(g\) is differentiable at \(\alpha = 0\), the first variation (prva variacija) of the functional \(\Pi\) at \(v\) in the direction \(\eta\) is the number

\[\delta\Pi(v, \eta) = g'(0) = \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\alpha}\,\Pi(v + \alpha\,\eta)\Big|_{\alpha = 0} .\]

The function \(v\) is a stationary point (stacionarna točka) of the functional if \(\delta\Pi(v, \eta) = 0\) for every admissible variation \(\eta\).

Source: EP (3.5.8), (3.5.31), (3.5.32) (pp. 136, 143). The same as the derivative in the direction \(\vek v\) in section 4.6.6.

For the cantilever take \(\eta(x) = x^2/L^2\). This function is zero at \(x = 0\) and has a zero derivative there, as the conditions of the clamp require. If \(v\) is the true deflection, then \(\Pi(v + \alpha\,\eta) = -1{,}993\ \text{J} + 2\,EI\,\alpha^2/L^3\) (computed with SymPy), a parabola in \(\alpha\) with its lowest point at \(\alpha = 0\) and a horizontal tangent there (figure 5.63). At \(\alpha = 1\) mm the distance from the lowest value is \(0{,}0418\) J. There is no linear term, because the first variation at the true deflection is zero. Why the function \(g\) is a polynomial of second degree in \(\alpha\) we will see in section 5.11.6.

5.11.3 Euler-Lagrange equation

For the bar we concluded from \(\delta\Pi = 0\) for all virtual displacements that \(N' + q = 0\) inside. The conclusion used the fundamental lemma, which we proved with bumps that have only a continuous first derivative, while their second derivative jumps at the end points (lemma 4.77). Such bumps are not admissible variations for the beam, because the energy contains the second derivative. We need a version with bumps that also have a continuous second derivative. Smooth bumps \(q_{\alpha\beta}\) with continuous derivatives up to the third order we have already prepared (lemma 4.122, point 1). The next lemma states as an independent claim the same conclusion that we already drew in steps 6 and 7 of the proof of theorem 5.65, so that we can use it several more times.

Lemma 5.68 Fundamental lemma with two continuous derivatives (osnovna lema z dvema zveznima odvodoma)

Let \(R\) be a function continuous on the closed interval \([a, b]\) with \(a < b\), and let

\[\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b R(x)\,\eta(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\]

for every function \(\eta\) that has continuous first two derivatives on \([a, b]\) (one-sided at the end points) and satisfies \(\eta(a) = \eta'(a) = \eta(b) = \eta'(b) = 0\). Then \(R(x) = 0\) for every \(x \in [a, b]\).

Proof

The conclusion is the same as in steps 6 and 7 of the proof of theorem 5.65, with the function \(R\) in place of \(q - s\) and the interval \([a, b]\) in place of \([0, L]\).

  1. For numbers \(a < \alpha < \beta < b\) the bump \(q_{\alpha\beta}\) of lemma 4.122 has by point 1 continuous derivatives up to the third order and is zero outside \((\alpha, \beta)\), so at the end points \(a\) and \(b\) it is zero together with its derivative. The lemma allows it as \(\eta\), hence \(\int_\alpha^\beta R\,q_{\alpha\beta}\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\) for every such pair.
  2. This is the same hypothesis as in step 6 of that proof, so by step 7 (a contradiction with a bump on an interval where \(R\) has a constant sign and is larger in absolute value than half its value at a point where it is not zero) \(R = 0\) on \((a, b)\).
  3. The function \(R\) is continuous on \([a, b]\), so it is also zero at the end points \(a\) and \(b\) as a limit of zeros.

Source: EP lemma 3.1 (pp. 140-141) for functions of one variable, without a proof with bumps. The proof is not from a source.

Now the theorem. Let us write down what we will need. Denote by \(F_{\mathrm{v}}\), \(F_{\mathrm{p}}\) and \(F_{\mathrm{r}}\) the partial derivatives of the function \(F(x, v, p, r)\) with respect to the second, third and fourth variable (see definition 1.25, Partial derivative): we differentiate with respect to one variable and hold the other three fixed. For a given function \(v\), \(\hat F_{\mathrm{v}}(x) = F_{\mathrm{v}}\big(x, v(x), v'(x), v''(x)\big)\) is a function of the number \(x\), and likewise \(\hat F_{\mathrm{p}}\) and \(\hat F_{\mathrm{r}}\). The derivative \(\mathrm{d}\hat F_{\mathrm{p}}/\mathrm{d}x\) is the derivative of this composite function with respect to \(x\), not the partial derivative with respect to the first variable.

Theorem 5.69 Euler-Lagrange equation (Euler-Lagrangeova enačba)

Let \(F(x, v, p, r)\) be a function that is continuous on \([0, L] \times \mathbb R^3\) and has continuous partial derivatives \(F_{\mathrm{v}}\), \(F_{\mathrm{p}}\) and \(F_{\mathrm{r}}\) there. Let \(\Pi\) be the functional (5.63) and \(v\) an admissible function for which \(\hat F_{\mathrm{p}}\) is continuously differentiable on \([0, L]\) and \(\hat F_{\mathrm{r}}\) is twice continuously differentiable on \([0, L]\). Then \(v\) is a stationary point of the functional \(\Pi\) if and only if it satisfies

\[\hat F_{\mathrm{v}} - \frac{\mathrm{d}\hat F_{\mathrm{p}}}{\mathrm{d}x} + \frac{\mathrm{d}^2 \hat F_{\mathrm{r}}}{\mathrm{d}x^2} = 0 \quad \text{on } [0, L] , \tag{5.64}\]

and at each end the natural conditions:

  • at \(x = L\): \(\hat F_{\mathrm{p}} - \hat F_{\mathrm{r}}{}' = f_{\mathrm{L}}\) when the displacement \(v(L)\) is not prescribed, and \(\hat F_{\mathrm{r}} = \mu_{\mathrm{L}}\) when the rotation \(v'(L)\) is not prescribed,
  • at \(x = 0\): \(\hat F_{\mathrm{p}} - \hat F_{\mathrm{r}}{}' = -f_0\) when the displacement \(v(0)\) is not prescribed, and \(\hat F_{\mathrm{r}} = -\mu_0\) when the rotation \(v'(0)\) is not prescribed.
Proof

We must show that the first variation is the sum of an integral and terms at the ends. Then with special variations we extract the equation and each natural condition.

1. First variation.

  1. Let \(\eta\) be an admissible variation and \(c < d\) arbitrary numbers. For every \(x \in [0, L]\) and every real \(\alpha\) let \(f(x, \alpha) = F\big(x, v + \alpha\,\eta, v' + \alpha\,\eta', v'' + \alpha\,\eta''\big)\), where \(v\), \(\eta\) and their derivatives are taken at \(x\). At fixed \(x\), \(f\) is a composite function: the map \(\alpha \mapsto \big(v + \alpha\,\eta, v' + \alpha\,\eta', v'' + \alpha\,\eta''\big)\) with derivative \((\eta, \eta', \eta'')\), followed by the function \(F\) of the three variables \((v, p, r)\), which has continuous partial derivatives on \(\mathbb R^3\). By the chain rule (lemma 4.43) with \(D = \mathbb R\) therefore
    \[\frac{\partial f}{\partial \alpha}(x, \alpha) = F_{\mathrm{v}}\,\eta + F_{\mathrm{p}}\,\eta' + F_{\mathrm{r}}\,\eta'' ,\]
    where the derivatives of \(F\) are taken at the point \(\big(x, v + \alpha\,\eta, v' + \alpha\,\eta', v'' + \alpha\,\eta''\big)\). The functions \(f\) and \(\partial f/\partial\alpha\) are continuous on the rectangle \([0, L] \times [c, d]\) as composites of continuous functions (lemma 4.35 with four coordinates and corollary 1.64), since \(F\), \(F_{\mathrm{v}}\), \(F_{\mathrm{p}}\), \(F_{\mathrm{r}}\), \(v\), \(\eta\) and the first two derivatives of \(v\) and \(\eta\) are continuous.
  2. By lemma 4.19, point 1, the function \(\alpha \mapsto \int_0^L f(x, \alpha)\,\mathrm{d}x\) is differentiable on \((c, d)\) and its derivative is the integral of the derivative. The numbers \(c < d\) are arbitrary, so this holds for every real \(\alpha\). The terms at the ends in \(\Pi(v + \alpha\,\eta)\) are linear in \(\alpha\). At \(\alpha = 0\) therefore
    \[\begin{aligned} \delta\Pi(v, \eta) &= \int_0^L \big(\hat F_{\mathrm{v}}\,\eta + \hat F_{\mathrm{p}}\,\eta' + \hat F_{\mathrm{r}}\,\eta''\big)\,\mathrm{d}x \\ &\quad - f_0\,\eta(0) - \mu_0\,\eta'(0) - f_{\mathrm{L}}\,\eta(L) - \mu_{\mathrm{L}}\,\eta'(L) . \end{aligned}\]
  3. Integration by parts (corollary 3.41) with the functions \(\hat F_{\mathrm{p}}\) and \(\eta\), which are continuously differentiable, gives \(\int_0^L \hat F_{\mathrm{p}}\,\eta'\,\mathrm{d}x = \big[\hat F_{\mathrm{p}}\,\eta\big]_0^L - \int_0^L \hat F_{\mathrm{p}}{}'\,\eta\,\mathrm{d}x\). With \(\hat F_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(\eta'\) (both continuously differentiable), \(\int_0^L \hat F_{\mathrm{r}}\,\eta''\,\mathrm{d}x = \big[\hat F_{\mathrm{r}}\,\eta'\big]_0^L - \int_0^L \hat F_{\mathrm{r}}{}'\,\eta'\,\mathrm{d}x\), and once more with \(\hat F_{\mathrm{r}}{}'\) and \(\eta\), \(\int_0^L \hat F_{\mathrm{r}}{}'\,\eta'\,\mathrm{d}x = \big[\hat F_{\mathrm{r}}{}'\,\eta\big]_0^L - \int_0^L \hat F_{\mathrm{r}}{}''\,\eta\,\mathrm{d}x\).
  4. We combine:
    \[\begin{aligned} \delta\Pi(v, \eta) &= \int_0^L \Big(\hat F_{\mathrm{v}} - \hat F_{\mathrm{p}}{}' + \hat F_{\mathrm{r}}{}''\Big)\,\eta\,\mathrm{d}x \\ &\quad + \Big[\big(\hat F_{\mathrm{p}} - \hat F_{\mathrm{r}}{}'\big)\,\eta + \hat F_{\mathrm{r}}\,\eta'\Big]_0^L \\ &\quad - f_0\,\eta(0) - \mu_0\,\eta'(0) - f_{\mathrm{L}}\,\eta(L) - \mu_{\mathrm{L}}\,\eta'(L) . \end{aligned} \tag{5.65}\]

2. The equation.

  1. If \(\delta\Pi(v, \eta) = 0\) for all admissible variations, this holds also for those that are zero at both end points together with their derivative. Such variations are always admissible by choice. The terms at the ends in equation (5.65) are then zero, and \(\int_0^L R\,\eta\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\) with \(R = \hat F_{\mathrm{v}} - \hat F_{\mathrm{p}}{}' + \hat F_{\mathrm{r}}{}''\). The function \(R\) is continuous because \(\hat F_{\mathrm{v}}\) (lemma 4.35 with four coordinates), \(\hat F_{\mathrm{p}}{}'\) and \(\hat F_{\mathrm{r}}{}''\) are continuous. By lemma 5.68, \(R = 0\) on \([0, L]\), which is equation (5.64).

3. Natural conditions.

  1. When the equation holds, only the terms at the ends remain in equation (5.65). At \(x = L\) what remains is \(\big(\hat F_{\mathrm{p}} - \hat F_{\mathrm{r}}{}' - f_{\mathrm{L}}\big)(L)\,\eta(L) + \big(\hat F_{\mathrm{r}}(L) - \mu_{\mathrm{L}}\big)\,\eta'(L)\), at \(x = 0\) it is \(-\big(\hat F_{\mathrm{p}} - \hat F_{\mathrm{r}}{}' + f_0\big)(0)\,\eta(0) - \big(\hat F_{\mathrm{r}}(0) + \mu_0\big)\,\eta'(0)\).
  2. Let \(v(L)\) be free, that is, let \(\eta(L)\) not be prescribed to be zero. Take \(\eta_1(x) = (x/L)^2\,(3 - 2x/L)\). It has continuous derivatives of all orders, because it is a polynomial, and \(\eta_1(0) = \eta_1'(0) = 0\), \(\eta_1(L) = 1\), \(\eta_1'(L) = 0\). At \(x = 0\) it is zero together with its derivative, and at \(x = L\) it has a zero derivative, so it is admissible whatever is prescribed at \(x = 0\) and whether \(v'(L)\) is prescribed or not. The first variation is then \(\big(\hat F_{\mathrm{p}} - \hat F_{\mathrm{r}}{}' - f_{\mathrm{L}}\big)(L)\), which must be zero. At \(x = 0\) we proceed in the same way with \(\eta_3(x) = (1 - x/L)^2\,(1 + 2x/L)\), which has \(\eta_3(0) = 1\), \(\eta_3'(0) = 0\), \(\eta_3(L) = \eta_3'(L) = 0\).
  3. Let \(v'(L)\) be free. Take \(\eta_2(x) = x^2\,(x - L)/L^2\), which has \(\eta_2(0) = \eta_2'(0) = \eta_2(L) = 0\) and \(\eta_2'(L) = 1\). The first variation is \(\hat F_{\mathrm{r}}(L) - \mu_{\mathrm{L}}\), which must be zero. At \(x = 0\) we take \(\eta_4(x) = x\,(x - L)^2/L^2\) with \(\eta_4(0) = 0\), \(\eta_4'(0) = 1\) and zeros at \(L\), and we get \(\hat F_{\mathrm{r}}(0) + \mu_0 = 0\).
  4. Conversely: if the equation and all natural conditions hold, the integral and the term at each end in equation (5.65) are zero. At an end each of the two summands is zero, either because the coefficient is zero by the natural condition, or because \(\eta\) (or \(\eta'\)) is zero there by the definition of an admissible variation. Hence \(\delta\Pi(v, \eta) = 0\) for every admissible variation.

Source: EP (3.5.39), (3.5.40) (p. 144) for \(F(x, u, u')\) and EP p. 146 for higher order, without proof. Differentiation under the integral is lemma 4.19, which in content is LEBL2 theorem 9.1.1 (PDF pp. 65-66). The step-by-step proof for \(F(x, v, v', v'')\) is not from a source.

We proved the theorem with three tools: differentiation under the integral, integration by parts and the lemma on the bump. Inside, the variation is an arbitrary function, so the lemma makes its coefficient vanish. At the ends the coefficients of the values of \(\eta\) and \(\eta'\) that the supports leave free remain, and they give the natural conditions.

Let us test the theorem on three functionals.

  1. The bar with the axial load \(q_{\mathrm{x}}\) (written \(q\) in chapter 4). The displacement \(u\) along the axis plays the role of the function \(v\), and \(F = \tfrac12\,EA\,p^2 - q_{\mathrm{x}}\,v\) does not contain \(r\), so \(\hat F_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\). Then \(F_{\mathrm{v}} = -q_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(\hat F_{\mathrm{p}} = EA\,u' = N\), and equation (5.64) is \(-q_{\mathrm{x}} - N' = 0\), that is \(N' + q_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\), the equilibrium of the bar of section 4.4.4. The natural condition at \(x = L\) is \(\hat F_{\mathrm{p}} = f_{\mathrm{L}}\), that is \(N(L) = f_{\mathrm{L}}\), where \(f_{\mathrm{L}}\) is the axial force acting on the end of the bar. The condition for the rotation holds by itself for the bar, because \(\hat F_{\mathrm{r}} = 0 = \mu_{\mathrm{L}}\). The theorem thus covers the bar of chapter 4, with a more demanding set of admissible functions that also have a continuous second derivative.
  2. The beam. The function \(F = \tfrac12\,EI\,r^2 - q\,v\) has \(F_{\mathrm{v}} = -q\), \(F_{\mathrm{p}} = 0\) and \(F_{\mathrm{r}} = EI\,r\), so \(\hat F_{\mathrm{r}} = EI\,v'' = M\). Equation (5.64) is \(-q + (EI\,v'')'' = 0\), that is \((EI\,v'')'' = q\), which for constant stiffness gives \(EI\,v'''' = q\) with \(q\) positive up. The natural condition for the displacement at \(x = L\) is \(-\hat F_{\mathrm{r}}{}' = f_{\mathrm{L}}\). Since the shear force is \(V = -M' = -(EI\,v'')'\) (D1, section 5.2), this is \(V(L) = f_{\mathrm{L}} = F_{\mathrm{y}}\), and the natural condition for the rotation is \(M(L) = EI\,v''(L) = \mu_{\mathrm{L}} = M_0\). Both are exactly the conditions of a free end of section 5.7.2, now derived from the energy. At \(x = 0\) the conditions give \(V(0) = -f_0\) and \(M(0) = -\mu_0\): the load at the left end is opposite to the internal force, because the internal force is the force of the part at larger \(x\) on the part at smaller \(x\).
  3. The beam on an elastic foundation with an axial force. The function \(F = \tfrac12\,EI\,r^2 + \tfrac12\,c\,p^2 + \tfrac12\,k\,v^2 - q\,v\) with constant numbers \(c\) and \(k\) gives \(-q + k\,v - (c\,v')' + (EI\,v'')'' = 0\), that is \(EI\,v'''' - c\,v'' + k\,v = q\) for constant \(EI\) (we checked the computation with SymPy). In chapter 25 we will see that the term \(\tfrac12\,k\,v^2\) describes an elastic foundation with stiffness \(k\) (force per unit length per unit deflection), and in chapter 8 that the term \(\tfrac12\,c\,p^2\) describes a constant tensile axial force \(c\). Here the example serves as a test that the theorem is not limited to our cases.

The cantilever of the introduction: \(q = 0\), \(F_{\mathrm{y}} = -500\) N, \(M_0 = 0\). The equation is \(v'''' = 0\). The essential conditions at \(x = 0\) are \(v(0) = v'(0) = 0\), the natural ones at \(x = L\) are \(V(L) = -EI\,v'''(L) = -500\) N and \(M(L) = EI\,v''(L) = 0\). Since \(v'''' = 0\), \(v'''\) is constant (corollary 1.85), and the first condition gives \(v''' = 500\ \text{N}/EI\). Integrating from \(L\) toward \(x\) and using the second condition \(v''(L) = 0\) we get \(v''(x) = 500\ \text{N}\,(x - L)/EI\), then by integrating twice and using the essential conditions

\[v(x) = \frac{500\ \text{N}}{EI}\Big(\frac{x^3}{6} - \frac{L\,x^2}{2}\Big) = -\frac{500\ \text{N}\,x^2\,(3L - x)}{6\,EI} ,\]

and the displacement of the end is \(v(L) = -500\ \text{N}\cdot (1\ \text{m})^3/(3 \cdot 20\,906{,}7\ \text{N m}^2) = -7{,}972\) mm. This is the deflection that equilibrium gave in section 5.9, now from the functional.

A force at an interior point. If a force \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\) acts at a point \(x_0\) inside the beam, we add the term \(-F_{\mathrm{y}}\,v(x_0)\) to the functional. For the true deflection curve the shear force jumps at \(x_0\), so \(\hat F_{\mathrm{r}} = M\) is not twice continuously differentiable on \([0, L]\), and we apply the theorem on the pieces \([0, x_0]\) and \([x_0, L]\). We carry out steps 1 to 4 of the proof on each piece and add the integrals (lemma 3.39, point 2). The argument of the proof of lemma 5.68 with bumps \(q_{\alpha\beta}\) lying inside one piece gives the equation on each piece. The terms at the ends of the pieces add up at \(x_0\) to \(\big[V(x_0^-) - V(x_0^+) - F_{\mathrm{y}}\big]\,\eta(x_0) + \big[M(x_0^-) - M(x_0^+)\big]\,\eta'(x_0)\), where for the beam we wrote \(\hat F_{\mathrm{p}} - \hat F_{\mathrm{r}}{}' = V\) and \(\hat F_{\mathrm{r}} = M\). The second bracket is zero, because \(EI\) is constant and \(v''\) is continuous. The variation \(\eta(x) = x^2\,(L - x)^2\) is zero at both ends together with its derivative and has \(\eta(x_0) > 0\), so the first bracket is zero too: \(V(x_0^+) - V(x_0^-) = -F_{\mathrm{y}}\), as in lemma 5.12. A couple with moment \(M_0\) at an interior point adds the term \(-M_0\,v'(x_0)\) and requires \(v''\) to jump at \(x_0\), so there we allow functions that have only a continuous first derivative. The same computation, completed with the variation \(x^2\,(L - x)^2\,(x - x_0)\), which has the value zero and a nonzero derivative at \(x_0\), gives the jump of the moment \(M(x_0^+) - M(x_0^-) = -M_0\).

Lemma 5.70 A minimum is a stationary point (najmanjša vrednost je stacionarna točka)

Let the hypotheses on \(F\) of theorem 5.69 hold. If \(\Pi\) has its smallest value among admissible functions at an admissible function \(v\), that is \(\Pi(w) \ge \Pi(v)\) for every admissible \(w\), then \(v\) is a stationary point of the functional \(\Pi\).

Proof

We must show that the first variation is zero in every admissible direction. We get it from the derivative at an interior extremum.

  1. Let \(\eta\) be an admissible variation. The function \(g(\alpha) = \Pi(v + \alpha\,\eta)\) is differentiable at every real \(\alpha\) by steps 1 and 2 of the proof of theorem 5.69.
  2. The function \(v + \alpha\,\eta\) is admissible for every \(\alpha\), so \(g(\alpha) \ge g(0)\). The function \(g\) therefore has its smallest value on \((-1, 1)\) at the interior point \(0\).
  3. By lemma 1.82, \(g'(0) = \delta\Pi(v, \eta) = 0\).

Source: EP (3.5.32) (p. 143), in the same sense. The step-by-step proof is not from a source.

The first part of the route is thus complete: the smallest value requires stationarity, and stationarity requires equation (5.64) and the natural conditions. The question remains whether a stationary point really gives the smallest value, and that is the subject of section 5.11.5. First, though, let us look at what the conditions at the ends mean.

5.11.4 Essential and natural boundary conditions

In the proof of the theorem we got two terms at each end: the coefficient of \(\eta\) and the coefficient of \(\eta'\). Each is either multiplied by a variation that is zero because of the support, or the variation is free there and the coefficient must be zero, otherwise we would choose a variation for which the first variation is not zero. This is a general rule (EP p. 147). Where a support prescribes the displacement \(v\) or the rotation \(v'\), the condition is essential (bistven), because it is already contained in the set of admissible functions, and the coefficient of the variation gives nothing. Where the displacement or the rotation is not prescribed, the coefficient must be zero. This condition is called natural (naraven), because the first variation itself produces it.

For the beam there are two pairs at each end (figure 5.64): the displacement \(v\) with the shear force \(V\), and the rotation \(v'\) with the moment \(M\). The theorem gives exactly one condition for each pair. If \(v(L)\) is prescribed, the condition is essential, \(\eta(L) = 0\), and the force \(V(L)\) is not prescribed but is a reaction of the support, determined by the solution. If \(v(L)\) is not prescribed, the condition is natural, \(V(L) = f_{\mathrm{L}}\). Both members of a pair cannot be prescribed (EP pp. 146-147): the force in a support that holds \(v(L)\) is no longer ours to choose. The equation of fourth order has four free constants (section 5.8), and four conditions, one from each pair at each end, determine them.

Overview of the conditions at the end \(x = L\) for the usual supports, all with our sign conventions:

end essential conditions natural conditions
clamp \(v(L) = 0\), \(v'(L) = 0\) none (the reactions \(V\) and \(M\) are results)
pin support \(v(L) = 0\) \(M(L) = \mu_{\mathrm{L}}\), for \(\mu_{\mathrm{L}} = 0\) hence \(EI\,v''(L) = 0\)
free end none \(V(L) = f_{\mathrm{L}}\) and \(M(L) = \mu_{\mathrm{L}}\)
sliding clamp \(v'(L) = 0\) \(V(L) = f_{\mathrm{L}}\)

At \(x = 0\) the natural conditions are \(V(0) = -f_0\) and \(M(0) = -\mu_0\). The cantilever has essential conditions at \(x = 0\) and natural ones at \(x = L\), while the simply supported beam of section 5.1.3 has the essential \(v(0) = v(L) = 0\) and the natural \(M(0) = M(L) = 0\). For the load \(q = -q_0\) with \(q_0 = 1\) kN/m and \(L = 2\) m the solution of the equation \(EI\,v'''' = -q_0\) with these conditions is a deflection with mid-span displacement \(-5\,q_0\,L^4/(384\,EI) = -9{,}965\) mm, and the moment \(M = \tfrac12\,q_0\,x\,(L - x)\) is indeed zero at \(x = 0\) and \(x = L\). This is exactly the condition the functional produces by itself.

Essential conditions behave differently from natural ones in an approximate computation: the trial field must be chosen so that it satisfies the essential conditions, but not the natural ones, because those are not part of the set of admissible functions. The true minimum satisfies them by itself. We will see an example in section 5.11.6.

5.11.5 Second variation and the smallest value

A stationary point can be a lowest point, a highest point or a saddle (section 4.6.6). How do we tell them apart? For a function of one number we look at the second derivative. Here we look at the second derivative of the function \(g(\alpha) = \Pi(v + \alpha\,\eta)\).

Definition 5.71 Second variation (druga variacija)

Let \(\Pi\) be the functional (5.63), \(v\) an admissible function, \(\eta\) an admissible variation and \(g(\alpha) = \Pi(v + \alpha\,\eta)\). If \(g\) has a second derivative at \(\alpha = 0\), the second variation (druga variacija) of the functional \(\Pi\) at \(v\) in the direction \(\eta\) is the number

\[\delta^2\Pi(v, \eta) = g''(0) = \frac{\mathrm{d}^2}{\mathrm{d}\alpha^2}\,\Pi(v + \alpha\,\eta)\Big|_{\alpha = 0} .\]

Source: EP (3.5.33) (p. 143), where the second variation is defined with an extra factor \(\alpha^2/2\), which does not change its sign.

We compute the second derivative of \(g\) like the first, by differentiating under the integral. Denote by \(F_{\mathrm{ij}}\) the second partial derivative of \(F\) with respect to the \(i\)-th and the \(j\)-th of the variables \((v, p, r)\), for \(i, j = 1, 2, 3\), and write \((\eta_1, \eta_2, \eta_3) = (\eta, \eta', \eta'')\).

Lemma 5.72 Second variation as an integral (druga variacija kot integral)

Let \(F(x, v, p, r)\) be a function that is continuous on \([0, L] \times \mathbb R^3\) and has continuous partial derivatives of the first and second order with respect to the variables \(v\), \(p\) and \(r\) there. Let \(\Pi\) be the functional (5.63), \(v\) an admissible function and \(\eta\) an admissible variation. Then \(g(\alpha) = \Pi(v + \alpha\,\eta)\) is twice differentiable at every real \(\alpha\), and

\[g''(\alpha) = \int_0^L \sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{3}\sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^{3} F_{\mathrm{ij}}\big(x, v + \alpha\,\eta, v' + \alpha\,\eta', v'' + \alpha\,\eta''\big)\,\eta_{\mathrm{i}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{d}x , \tag{5.66}\]

where \(v\), \(\eta\) and their derivatives are taken at \(x\). At \(\alpha = 0\) this is \(\delta^2\Pi(v, \eta)\).

Proof

We must show that we may differentiate \(g'\) under the integral once more.

  1. Write \(F_1 = F_{\mathrm{v}}\), \(F_2 = F_{\mathrm{p}}\) and \(F_3 = F_{\mathrm{r}}\). By steps 1 and 2 of the proof of theorem 5.69, for every real \(\alpha\)
    \[\begin{aligned} g'(\alpha) &= \int_0^L h(x, \alpha)\,\mathrm{d}x \\ &\quad - f_0\,\eta(0) - \mu_0\,\eta'(0) - f_{\mathrm{L}}\,\eta(L) - \mu_{\mathrm{L}}\,\eta'(L) , \\ h(x, \alpha) &= \sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{3} F_{\mathrm{i}}\big(x, v + \alpha\,\eta, v' + \alpha\,\eta', v'' + \alpha\,\eta''\big)\,\eta_{\mathrm{i}} . \end{aligned}\]
  2. At fixed \(x\) each \(F_{\mathrm{i}}\) is a function of the three variables \((v, p, r)\) with continuous partial derivatives \(F_{\mathrm{i}1}\), \(F_{\mathrm{i}2}\), \(F_{\mathrm{i}3}\). By the chain rule (lemma 4.43), used as in step 1 of the proof of the theorem, \(\partial h/\partial\alpha = \sum_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j}} F_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{i}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{j}}\), with the derivatives \(F_{\mathrm{ij}}\) at the same point.
  3. Let \(c < d\) be arbitrary numbers. The functions \(h\) and \(\partial h/\partial\alpha\) are continuous on the rectangle \([0, L] \times [c, d]\), as in step 1 of the proof of the theorem (lemma 4.35 with four coordinates and corollary 1.64).
  4. By lemma 4.19, point 1, the integral in \(g'\) is differentiable on \((c, d)\) with derivative \(\int_0^L \partial h/\partial\alpha\,\mathrm{d}x\). The terms at the ends in \(g'\) do not depend on \(\alpha\), so their derivative is zero. The numbers \(c < d\) are arbitrary, so equation (5.66) holds for every real \(\alpha\).

Source: EP (3.5.33) (p. 143) defines the second variation without this formula. The proof is not from a source.

When is \(g''\) nowhere negative? It suffices that the double sum in equation (5.66) is not negative when any three numbers are put in place of \((\eta_1, \eta_2, \eta_3)\). A function \(F\) with continuous second derivatives with respect to \((v, p, r)\) is convex in \((v, p, r)\) (konveksna po \((v, p, r)\)) if for every \(x \in [0, L]\) and every triple \((v, p, r)\)

\[\sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{3}\sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^{3} F_{\mathrm{ij}}(x, v, p, r)\,a_{\mathrm{i}}\,a_{\mathrm{j}} \ge 0 \quad \text{for any three real numbers } a_1, a_2, a_3 .\]

For a function of one number this means \(F'' \ge 0\), so the slope of the graph does not decrease. The general definition of a convex function requires that the graph between two of its points does not lie above the segment joining them. For functions with continuous second derivatives it is equivalent to our condition (CHK §3.2, theorem 3.2, PDF pp. 46-48). For the beam \(F = \tfrac12\,EI\,r^2 - q\,v\), the only nonzero second derivative is \(F_{\mathrm{rr}} = EI\), and the sum is \(EI\,a_3^2 \ge 0\).

Theorem 5.73 Stationary point of a convex functional is a minimum (stacionarna točka konveksnega funkcionala je najmanjša)

Let \(F\) be a function with the hypotheses of lemma 5.72 that is convex in \((v, p, r)\), and \(\Pi\) the functional (5.63). If the admissible function \(v\) is a stationary point of the functional \(\Pi\), then \(\Pi(\tilde v) \ge \Pi(v)\) for every admissible function \(\tilde v\).

Proof

We must show that the function \(g(\alpha) = \Pi\big(v + \alpha\,(\tilde v - v)\big)\) does not decrease on \([0, 1]\). Its derivative at \(0\) is zero, and its second derivative is not negative.

  1. Let \(\eta = \tilde v - v\). The functions \(v\) and \(\tilde v\) have continuous first two derivatives and equal values where the supports prescribe them, so \(\eta\) is an admissible variation. We have \(g(0) = \Pi(v)\) and \(g(1) = \Pi(\tilde v)\).
  2. By lemma 5.72, \(g\) is twice differentiable at every real \(\alpha\) and \(g''(\alpha)\) is the integral (5.66). Since \(v\) is a stationary point, \(g'(0) = \delta\Pi(v, \eta) = 0\).
  3. The integrand in (5.66) is continuous and by convexity, with \(a_{\mathrm{i}} = \eta_{\mathrm{i}}(x)\), it is negative at no \(x\). By monotonicity of the integral (lemma 3.39, point 4), \(g''(\alpha) \ge 0\) for every \(\alpha\).
  4. Let \(0 < s < 1\). The function \(g'\) is differentiable, hence continuous (lemma 1.79). By the mean value theorem 1.84 for \(g'\) on \([0, s]\) there is \(\xi \in (0, s)\) with \(g'(s) - g'(0) = g''(\xi)\,s \ge 0\). Since \(g'(0) = 0\), \(g'(s) \ge 0\).
  5. The function \(g\) is differentiable, hence continuous on \([0, 1]\). By the same theorem for \(g\) on \([0, 1]\) there is \(s \in (0, 1)\) with \(g(1) - g(0) = g'(s)\), which by step 4 is not negative. This is \(\Pi(\tilde v) \ge \Pi(v)\).

Source: The proof is not from a source. EP theorem 6.2 (pp. 283-284) gives the minimum of a quadratic functional with a positive operator, which is a special case.

The theorem showed that for a convex integrand stationarity suffices. EP, p. 145, solves the Euler equation (3.5.40) and calls the solution the true minimizer of the functional without mentioning convexity: without it the solution is only a stationary point, which can also be a maximum or a saddle, and convexity is what makes it a minimum (rule 42). For the beam \(F\) is a polynomial of second degree in \(r\), so we compute the difference of two energies exactly, and this gives us more: the smallest value is attained only once.

5.11.6 Minimum energy of a beam

For the beam \(F = \tfrac12\,EI\,r^2 - q\,v\), and the functional is the sum of a quadratic part and linear parts. Therefore \(\Pi(v + \alpha\,\eta)\) is a polynomial of second degree in \(\alpha\), as for the truss (equation (4.60)): we expand the square \((v'' + \alpha\,\eta'')^2\), and the terms with \(q\,v\) and the ends are linear. The difference of two energies can then be computed exactly, and the argument does not need the mean value theorem.

Corollary 5.74 Minimum total potential energy of a beam (najmanjša potencialna energija nosilca)

Let \(EI > 0\) be constant and \(q\) a continuous function on \([0, L]\) (a prismatic beam), and let

\[\Pi(v) = \int_0^L \tfrac12\,EI\,v''^2\,\mathrm{d}x - \int_0^L q\,v\,\mathrm{d}x - f_0\,v(0) - \mu_0\,v'(0) - f_{\mathrm{L}}\,v(L) - \mu_{\mathrm{L}}\,v'(L)\]

on the set of admissible functions, which have continuous first two derivatives and satisfy the essential conditions of the supports. Let the supports be such that the only admissible variation with \(\eta'' = 0\) is the zero function. Let the admissible function \(v\) be such that \(M = EI\,v''\) is twice continuously differentiable, \(M'' = q\) on \([0, L]\), and the natural conditions of theorem 5.69 hold. Then for every admissible function \(\tilde v\)

\[\Pi(\tilde v) - \Pi(v) = \int_0^L \tfrac12\,EI\,\big(\tilde v'' - v''\big)^2\,\mathrm{d}x \ge 0 ,\]

with equality only for \(\tilde v = v\).

Proof

We must show the same splitting as for the bar and then that the integral of the square is zero only for the zero function.

  1. Let \(\eta = \tilde v - v\). It is an admissible variation as in step 1 of the proof of theorem 5.73.
  2. We expand the square: \(\tfrac12\,EI\,(v'' + \eta'')^2 = \tfrac12\,EI\,v''^2 + EI\,v''\,\eta'' + \tfrac12\,EI\,\eta''^2\). We split the linear terms \(-q\,\tilde v\) and the terms at the ends into parts for \(v\) and for \(\eta\).
  3. We integrate (linearity of the integral, lemma 3.39, point 1):
    \[\Pi(\tilde v) = \Pi(v) + \Big[\int_0^L \big(EI\,v''\,\eta'' - q\,\eta\big)\mathrm{d}x - f_0\,\eta(0) - \mu_0\,\eta'(0) - f_{\mathrm{L}}\,\eta(L) - \mu_{\mathrm{L}}\,\eta'(L)\Big] + \int_0^L \tfrac12\,EI\,\eta''^2\,\mathrm{d}x .\]
  4. The expression in square brackets is \(\delta\Pi(v, \eta)\), since for \(F = \tfrac12\,EI\,r^2 - q\,v\) the integrand of the first variation is \(F_{\mathrm{v}}\,\eta + F_{\mathrm{r}}\,\eta'' = -q\,\eta + EI\,v''\,\eta''\). The hypotheses of theorem 5.69 hold: \(F_{\mathrm{v}} = -q\), \(F_{\mathrm{p}} = 0\) and \(F_{\mathrm{r}} = EI\,r\) are continuous, \(\hat F_{\mathrm{p}} = 0\), \(\hat F_{\mathrm{r}} = M\) is twice continuously differentiable, and equation (5.64) is \(M'' - q = 0\). Since \(v\) satisfies the equation and the natural conditions, the expression is zero by the converse direction of the theorem.
  5. The integrand \(\tfrac12\,EI\,\eta''^2\) is nowhere negative, so by monotonicity (lemma 3.39, point 4) the integral is not negative. This is the equation of the corollary.
  6. Let the integral be zero. As in steps 6 and 7 of the proof of corollary 4.93, the continuous nonnegative function \(\tfrac12\,EI\,\eta''^2\) with zero integral is zero everywhere, so \(\eta'' = 0\). By the hypothesis on the supports \(\eta = 0\), that is \(\tilde v = v\).

Source: EP (3.3.3), (3.3.4) (p. 124) for the total potential energy of a beam, EP §4.3.1 (pp. 179-183) for the principle. The step-by-step proof is not from the sources.

The condition on the supports excludes a rigid displacement. A function with \(\eta'' = 0\) on \([0, L]\) is linear, \(\eta = a + b\,x\): by corollary 1.85 \(\eta' = b\) is constant, and by the same corollary \(\eta - b\,x = a\) is constant too. For the cantilever the essential conditions \(\eta(0) = \eta'(0) = 0\) give \(a = b = 0\), and for the simply supported beam the conditions \(\eta(0) = \eta(L) = 0\) do the same. For a beam without supports, however, the rigid displacement \(a + b\,x\) is an admissible variation. Since its \(\eta'' = 0\), the energy changes along it only by the work of the loads with the opposite sign, and this work is \(a\,\big(\int_0^L q\,\mathrm{d}x + f_0 + f_{\mathrm{L}}\big) + b\,\big(\int_0^L x\,q\,\mathrm{d}x + L\,f_{\mathrm{L}} + \mu_0 + \mu_{\mathrm{L}}\big)\). The brackets are the sum of the forces and the sum of the moments about the point \(x = 0\). If the loads are in equilibrium, both are zero, all functions \(v + a + b\,x\) have the same energy, and the solution is not unique.

From the corollary it follows that the true deflection of the cantilever has the energy \(-1{,}993\) J and that the energy of every other admissible deflection is larger. In section 5.11.1 we saw this on an example: the parabola with the same end has \(-1{,}329\) J. The difference is given exactly by the integral \(\int_0^L \tfrac12\,EI\,(\tilde v'' - v'')^2\,\mathrm{d}x\), which for this parabola is \(0{,}664\) J, exactly the difference between \(-1{,}329\) J and \(-1{,}993\) J.

Let us test the corollary on a trial field. For the cantilever take the trial function \(\tilde v(x) = a\,x^2\) with an unknown number \(a\) (m\(^{-1}\)). It satisfies the essential conditions \(\tilde v(0) = \tilde v'(0) = 0\), but not the natural ones. Its energy is \(\Pi(a\,x^2) = 2\,EI\,L\,a^2 + 500\ \text{N}\cdot L^2\,a\), where the first term comes from \(\tfrac12\,EI\,(2a)^2\,L\) and the second from \(-F_{\mathrm{y}}\,a\,L^2\) with \(F_{\mathrm{y}} = -500\) N. This is a parabola in \(a\), and its lowest point is at \(4\,EI\,L\,a + 500\ \text{N}\cdot L^2 = 0\), so \(a = -500\ \text{N}\cdot L/(4\,EI) = -5{,}979 \cdot 10^{-3}\ \text{m}^{-1}\) and \(\tilde v(L) = a\,L^2 = -5{,}979\) mm. This is \(3/4\) of the true displacement of the end, \(-7{,}972\) mm. The energy is \(-1{,}495\) J, which is \(3/4\) of the true \(-1{,}993\) J, and by the corollary it is larger than the true one by \(\int_0^L \tfrac12\,EI\,(\tilde v'' - v'')^2\,\mathrm{d}x = 0{,}498\) J (figure 5.65).

Why exactly \(3/4\)? The true curvature \(v'' = 500\ \text{N}\,(x - L)/EI\) is linear, largest in absolute value at the clamp and zero at the free end, like the moment \(M = EI\,v''\) (section 5.2). The parabola has the constant curvature \(2a = -500\ \text{N}\cdot L/(2\,EI)\), which is exactly the mean of the true curvature over the length. It was the same for the hanging bar, where the strain of the best straight line was the mean of the true one (figure 4.80). The displacement of the end, however, does not average the curvature evenly. By integration by parts (corollary 3.41 with the functions \(L - x\) and \(v'\)), the fundamental theorem of calculus 3.40 and \(v(0) = v'(0) = 0\),

\[v(L) = \int_0^L (L - x)\,v''(x)\,\mathrm{d}x ,\]

so the curvature at \(x\) counts with the lever arm \(L - x\), which is largest at the clamp, exactly where the parabola has too little curvature. For the constant curvature \(2a\) the integral gives \(a\,L^2 = -500\ \text{N}\cdot L^3/(4\,EI)\), for the true one \(-500\ \text{N}\cdot L^3/(3\,EI)\), and the ratio is \(3/4\). The energy is also \(3/4\) of the true one. The lowest value of a parabola \(A\,a^2 + B\,a\) is \(\tfrac12\,B\,a\) at its lowest point, and here \(B\,a = -F_{\mathrm{y}}\,\tilde v(L)\), so \(\Pi = -\tfrac12\,F_{\mathrm{y}}\,\tilde v(L)\). The same holds for the true deflection \(v\), which is the lowest point of the family \(t\,v\), so the ratio of the energies equals the ratio of the displacements of the end.

Extending the trial function to \(a\,x^2 + b\,x^3\), the curvature \(2a + 6b\,x\) is linear like the true one, and minimizing over \(a\) and \(b\) gives \(a = -500\ \text{N}\cdot L/(2\,EI)\) and \(b = 500\ \text{N}/(6\,EI)\) (SymPy), which is exactly the true deflection. The true deflection lies in this family and by the corollary has the smallest energy among all admissible functions, so the minimization finds it exactly. This idea, looking for the smallest \(\Pi\) among functions with finitely many parameters, is the idea of computing with trial functions, which we develop in chapter 6.

5.11.7 Where next

This section treated a functional of one function of one variable with derivatives up to the second order and a finite set of conditions at two ends. Extensions come where we first need them. Functionals of several functions and constraints with Lagrange multipliers (Lagrangeevi množitelji) will be in chapter 8, where we derive Lagrange's equations from Hamilton's principle. There we will also use the second variation to judge stability, for example in buckling, where convexity fails. Functionals of fields of two and three variables, where the divergence theorem takes the place of integration by parts, follow in chapters 18 and 11. Direct methods, in which the functional is minimized among functions with finitely many parameters, as we did with the parabola, are the subject of chapter 6. The penalty method (kazenska metoda) belongs to chapter 12.
