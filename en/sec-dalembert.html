Chapter 8 Analytical dynamics (analitična dinamika)

8.7 d'Alembert's principle (d'Alembertovo načelo)

The pendulum of example A is a weight of mass \(m = 1\) kg on a weightless rigid bar of length \(l = 1\) m, turning about a pin \(O\), and in the plane \(xy\) the weight force acts on it. The angle \(\theta\) is measured from the vertical downward. In sections 8.1 to 8.6 we computed the equilibrium of such a system with virtual work, without the force in the pin. When the pendulum swings, however, the forces on it are not in equilibrium. At \(\theta = 30^\circ\) the component of the weight along the tangent to the circle is \(m g \sin\theta = 1 \cdot 9{,}81 \cdot 0{,}5 = 4{,}905\) N, and the force of the bar on the weight points along the bar, so perpendicular to the tangent. The resultant of the forces on the weight has a nonzero component along the tangent, so the weight is not in equilibrium and it accelerates. Can we still use the statics of sections 8.1 to 8.5 for the motion?

The answer is yes, and the idea is simple. Newton's law for a weight of mass \(m\) and acceleration \(\vek a\) is \(\vek F = m\,\vek a\), where \(\vek F\) is the resultant of the forces. Write it as \(\vek F + (-m\,\vek a) = \vek 0\). The weight then behaves as if it were in equilibrium under the forces \(\vek F\) and an additional force \(-m\,\vek a\), which we call the inertial force (vztrajnostna sila). It is not a real force caused by some other body, but the expression \(m\,\vek a\) moved to the other side of the equation. For the pendulum, at the point \(\theta = 30^\circ\) and with \(\dot\theta = 2\) rad/s, the acceleration has, by the computation in section 8.7.5, the tangential component \(a_{\mathrm{t}} = -4{,}905\) m/s\(^2\), so the inertial force has the tangential component \(+4{,}905\) N and cancels the tangential component of the weight, which points the opposite way. A body that is not just a weight will have such forces in every small piece. So we first write the equation that the motion of a body satisfies, then carry the principle of virtual displacements from chapter 4 over to motion, and for a system of rigid bodies we obtain an equation in which the constraint forces do not appear. At the end we derive from these equations two more equations for a rigid body, which we need in examples B and C, and test everything on the pendulum.

8.7.1 Velocity, acceleration and the equation of motion (hitrost, pospešek in gibalna enačba)

So far we looked at a body at one instant. Now the position of the points of the body changes with time \(t\). We label a point of the body by its position \(\vek X\) before loading, as in chapter 1. At the instant \(t\) this point is at the place \(\vek x(\vek X, t) = \vek X + \vek u(\vek X, t)\), where \(\vek u\) is the displacement. The velocity and acceleration of the point are the rate of change of its position and the rate of change of this velocity, and we take them for the same point of matter, so at constant \(\vek X\).

Definition 8.67 Velocity and acceleration of a material point (hitrost in pospešek točke telesa)

Let \(\vek x(\vek X, t)\) be the position vector of the point of a body with position \(\vek X\) before loading at the instant \(t\), twice continuously differentiable in \(t\) at every constant \(\vek X\). The velocity (hitrost) of the point is the vector \(\vek v = \partial\vek x/\partial t\) and the acceleration (pospešek) the vector \(\vek a = \partial^2\vek x/\partial t^2\), both at constant \(\vek X\), so componentwise the derivative with respect to time.

Source: GR (1.1) (PDF p. 32), BOWER §2.4.4.1 (PDF p. 86, \(\mathrm{d}v_{\mathrm{i}}/\mathrm{d}t\) in the equation of motion). The notation with \(\vek X\) and \(\vek u\) is not from the sources.

For the pendulum \(\vek x(t) = l\,(\sin\theta, -\cos\theta)\), and by the chain rule (lemma 8.29, item 3) with the derivatives of sine and cosine (theorem 8.12), which hold for an angle in radians (definition 8.7), \(\vek v = l\,\dot\theta\,(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\) and

\[\vek a = l\,\ddot\theta\,(\cos\theta, \sin\theta) + l\,\dot\theta^2\,(-\sin\theta, \cos\theta) . \tag{8.55}\]

The first vector is the unit vector of the tangent \(\vek e_{\mathrm{t}} = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\), the second is opposite to the unit vector \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}} = (\sin\theta, -\cos\theta)\) from \(O\) toward the weight. The acceleration therefore has a tangential part \(l\,\ddot\theta\), which changes the speed of the point, and a normal part \(-l\,\dot\theta^2\), which points toward \(O\) and bends the path.

Which law connects the acceleration with the forces on a body? For the point of the weight it is Newton's law. For a body it is the equation that chapter 2 has already written in words: in the equilibrium equations (theorem 2.28) the density times the acceleration appears on the right-hand side (section 2.7). We accept it as a law of nature, as we accepted equilibrium, and give it a name.

Definition 8.68 Equation of motion of a body (gibalna enačba telesa)

Let the body have at a point \(P\) the density \(\rho\), the body force density \(\vek b\) (definition 2.26), the stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\) and the acceleration \(\vek a\) (definition 8.67). At every interior point of the body at every instant

\[\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}} + b_{\mathrm{j}} = \rho\,a_{\mathrm{j}} , \qquad j = x, y, z . \tag{8.56}\]

Source: BOWER §2.3.1 (PDF p. 76) and §2.4.4.1 (PDF p. 86), CM (5.3.12) (PDF p. 228). This is a law, not a theorem, so we do not prove it. BOWER takes \(\vek b\) per unit mass, here it is, as in definition 2.26, per unit volume.

The left-hand side is a force per unit volume, the right-hand side a mass per unit volume times the acceleration, so this is Newton's law for a piece of matter. For \(\vek a = \vek 0\) it is the equilibrium equation (2.31).

8.7.2 The principle for a body (načelo za telo)

The principle of virtual displacements (theorem 4.82) is the equilibrium equations multiplied by a virtual displacement and integrated over the body. We do the same computation with the equation of motion. A virtual displacement here compares positions at the same instant: time stands still during the virtual displacement, because the forces and accelerations at \(t\) decide whether the motion is the true one. At the instant \(t\) the body occupies a set of points \(V\), which we treat as in chapter 4 (small displacements, section 4.5.4), and the other conditions are the same as there.

Theorem 8.69 d'Alembert's principle for a body (d'Alembertovo načelo za telo)

Let the body occupy at the instant \(t\) a set of points \(V\) that is a body made of boxes (definition 4.11), with two parts of its surface \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) and \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\), a vector \(\bar{\vek t}\) and a body force density \(\vek b\) as in theorem 4.82. Let the density \(\rho\) be continuous on \(V\) and the acceleration \(\vek a\) (definition 8.67) continuous on \(V\). Assume:

  1. the stress components are continuously differentiable on an open set that contains the body together with its surface,
  2. at every interior point of the body the equation of motion (8.56) holds,
  3. at every point of the body the stress is symmetric,
  4. at every point of \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\), \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \bar{\vek t}\), where \(\vek n\) is the outward unit normal.

Then for every kinematically admissible virtual displacement \(\delta\vek u\) with virtual strain \(\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) (definition 4.79)

\[\int_{\mathrm{V}} \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\mathrm{d}V = \int_{\mathrm{V}} (\vek b - \rho\,\vek a)\cdot\delta\vek u\,\mathrm{d}V + \int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{t}} \bar{\vek t}\cdot\delta\vek u\,\mathrm{d}A . \tag{8.57}\]
Proof

We must show that in equality (4.50) the volume integral on the right turns into the work of the body force and of the inertial force \(-\rho\,\vek a\), and the surface integral into the work of the prescribed force. The steps are the steps of the proof of theorem 4.82, except that the equation of motion holds in place of the equilibrium equation.

  1. Assumptions 1 and 3 and the continuity of the field \(\delta\vek u\) are the assumptions of lemma 4.81, so (4.50) holds.
  2. With indices the equation of motion (8.56) reads \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}} = \rho\,a_{\mathrm{j}} - b_{\mathrm{j}}\). At every interior point we therefore have
    \[\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \Big(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}}\Big)\,\delta u_{\mathrm{j}} = -\sum_{\mathrm{j}} (b_{\mathrm{j}} - \rho\,a_{\mathrm{j}})\,\delta u_{\mathrm{j}} = -(\vek b - \rho\,\vek a)\cdot\delta\vek u .\]
  3. The equality also holds at the points of the surface, as in step 3 of the proof of theorem 4.82. There the continuity of both sides was needed, and since \(\rho\) and \(\vek a\) are continuous like \(\vek b\), the difference \(\vek b - \rho\,\vek a\) is continuous too.
  4. The functions under the volume integral in (4.50) and \(-(\vek b - \rho\,\vek a)\cdot\delta\vek u\) are therefore equal at every point of the body, so their integrals are equal.
  5. The surface integral is split into \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) and \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) as in step 5 of the same proof: on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) we have \(\delta\vek u = \vek 0\), on \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) we have \(\vek t(\vek n) = \bar{\vek t}\) by assumption 4.
  6. We insert steps 4 and 5 into (4.50). The minus before the volume integral and the minus from step 4 give a plus, and we obtain (8.57).

Source: BOWER §2.4.4.1 (PDF p. 86): takes the equality with \(\rho\,\mathrm{d}v_{\mathrm{i}}/\mathrm{d}t\) and \(\rho\,b_{\mathrm{i}}\), sketches the proof with the divergence theorem. GR (1.1) (PDF p. 32) and (1.5) (PDF p. 33) for a particle. The proof with the steps of theorem 4.82 is not from the sources.

The theorem says the same as theorem 4.82, except that the body force is replaced by \(\vek b - \rho\,\vek a\). Motion is therefore equilibrium under an added body force \(-\rho\,\vek a\) per unit volume, the inertial force, and everything we proved about statics holds for it, including the meaning of virtual work (figure 8.22).

We test it on the bar from chapter 3: a hanging steel bar of length \(L = 10\) m and diameter 10 mm (\(A = 78{,}54\) mm\(^2\)) and density \(\rho = 7800\) kg/m\(^3\) has mass \(m = \rho A L = 6{,}126\) kg and weight \(m g = 60{,}10\) N. Its top end is attached to a crane that lifts the bar vertically upward with an acceleration \(a_0 = 0{,}5\,g = 4{,}905\) m/s\(^2\). Let the axis \(x\) point vertically downward from the top end, as for the hanging bar. The weight has density \(b = \rho g\) in the direction \(+x\), the acceleration is \(-a_0\) in the direction \(x\). We idealize the bar: we neglect the oscillations of the bar after the lifting begins (chapter 13 will treat them), so all points have the same acceleration. In equation (8.57) we have \(b - \rho a = \rho g - \rho(-a_0) = \rho\,(g + a_0)\) in the direction \(+x\): the bar is loaded as if gravity were larger, \(g + a_0 = 14{,}715\) m/s\(^2\). The virtual work equation is therefore the equation of the hanging bar with \(g\) replaced by \(g + a_0\), and it has the same solution. The axial force in the top cross-section is thus \(\rho (g + a_0) A L = m\,(g + a_0) = 90{,}15\) N, the stress there \(\rho (g + a_0) L = 1{,}148\) MPa instead of \(0{,}765\) MPa for the bar at rest, and the ratio is \(1{,}5 = (g + a_0)/g\). The same follows from Newton's law for the whole bar: the force of the crane \(F\) satisfies \(F - m g = m\,a_0\), so \(F = m\,(g + a_0) = 90{,}15\) N. If the crane let go of the bar and it fell freely, the acceleration would be \(a = g\) downward, \(b - \rho a = 0\), and there would be no stress in the bar.

8.7.3 A system of rigid bodies (sistem togih teles)

For a system of rigid bodies joined by pins, guides and other constraints (section 8.1), we are not interested in the internal state of the bodies but in the motion and the forces of the constraints. By the definition of a rigid body (definition 1.11) the distances between points do not change, so there is no internal virtual work.

Corollary 8.70 d'Alembert's principle for a system of rigid bodies (d'Alembertovo načelo za sistem togih teles)

Let a system of rigid bodies \(1, \dots, k\) have ideal constraints (definition 8.2), and at the instant \(t\) let body \(j\) occupy a set of points \(V_{\mathrm{j}}\) that is a body made of boxes (definition 4.11), with continuous density \(\rho_{\mathrm{j}}\), continuous acceleration \(\vek a\) and body force density \(\vek b\). Besides the body force let applied forces (uporabljene sile) \(\vek F_{\mathrm{j}1}, \dots\) at points \(A_{\mathrm{j}1}, \dots\) and constraint forces act on body \(j\). At the time \(t\) let the bodies satisfy the equation of motion (definition 8.68) and assumptions 1, 3 of theorem 8.69. Let \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{j}} = \delta\vek a_{\mathrm{j}} + \delta\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{j}} \times \vek r\) be a rigid virtual displacement of body \(j\) (\(\vek r = \overrightarrow{O_{\mathrm{j}} P}\), lemma 4.72), composed of displacements that respect the constraints (definition 8.1). Then

\[\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \Big[ \int_{\mathrm{V}_\mathrm{j}} (\vek b - \rho_{\mathrm{j}}\,\vek a)\cdot\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{d}V + \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \vek F_{\mathrm{ji}}\cdot\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{ji}} \Big] = 0 , \tag{8.58}\]

where \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{ji}}\) is the virtual displacement of the point \(A_{\mathrm{ji}}\).

Proof

We must show that for each body the internal virtual work drops out and that the virtual work of the constraint forces cancels in the sum. We take the constraint forces as forces on the body, as in theorem 4.73, where the forces of the supports are among the forces.

  1. For body \(j\) we take theorem 8.69 without a part of the surface with prescribed displacement, \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) empty. All forces on the surface, applied ones and constraint forces, are prescribed forces on \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\), and point forces are an idealization of a force on a very small surface, whose work is \(\vek F\cdot\delta\vek u\) at the point of application (definition 4.71). The position \(V_{\mathrm{j}}\) at the instant \(t\) is the position relative to which the body is without deformation, so no assumption of small displacements is used.
  2. The rigid virtual displacement \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{j}}\) is continuously differentiable. Its derivative is a constant antisymmetric matrix \(W_{\mathrm{ik}}\), because \(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi \times \vek r = \mat W\,\vek r\) (section 4.4.3, lemma 4.72), so the virtual strain is \(\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ik}} = \tfrac12(W_{\mathrm{ik}} + W_{\mathrm{ki}}) = 0\) at every point and the left-hand side of (8.57) is zero.
  3. For body \(j\) the sum of the virtual works of the body force, the inertial force, the applied forces and the constraint forces is therefore zero.
  4. We sum over the bodies. The displacements \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{j}}\) respect the constraints, so the total virtual work of the constraint forces is zero by the definition of an ideal constraint (definition 8.2). What remains is (8.58).

Source: GR §1.2.1 (PDF p. 35-36, "effective forces" for a system of particles), SHABANA (3.65), (3.66) (PDF p. 120). For a rigid body SHABANA shows that the virtual work of the internal forces is zero (PDF p. 120, (3.62)). The proof after theorem 8.69 is not from the sources.

Equation (8.58) is the equation of theorem 8.3 on the virtual work of a system, in which \(-\rho\,\vek a\) per piece of matter is added to the applied forces. The constraint forces do not appear in it, so we do not compute them. This is both the price and the advantage: if we were interested only in the equations of motion, they dropped out, and if we need the constraint forces, we will get them in section 8.9 with Lagrange multipliers, as in section 8.5.

8.7.4 The equations of motion of a rigid body (gibalni enačbi togega telesa)

For examples B and C we need what follows from (8.58) for one rigid body. First we name the quantities that appear in the result.

Definition 8.71 Mass, centre of mass and moment of inertia of a body (masa, težišče in vztrajnostni moment telesa)

A body of density \(\rho\) (a function of the point, in kg/m\(^3\)), occupying a set \(V\), has mass (masa) \(m = \int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\mathrm{d}V\). The centre of mass (težišče telesa) is the point \(G\) with position vector \(\vek x_{\mathrm{G}} = \tfrac1m \int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\vek x\,\mathrm{d}V\), so the average of the positions of the points weighted by the density. For it, \(\int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\vek r\,\mathrm{d}V = \vek 0\) for \(\vek r = \vek x - \vek x_{\mathrm{G}}\), because by the linearity of the integral (lemma 4.10, point 1) \(\int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,(\vek x - \vek x_{\mathrm{G}})\,\mathrm{d}V = \int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\vek x\,\mathrm{d}V - \vek x_{\mathrm{G}}\int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\mathrm{d}V = m\,\vek x_{\mathrm{G}} - m\,\vek x_{\mathrm{G}}\). The moment of inertia of the body about an axis (vztrajnostni moment telesa glede na os) through a point \(Q\) in the direction of a unit vector \(\vek n\) is the number \(J = \int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\ell^2\,\mathrm{d}V\), where \(\ell\) is the distance of the point from the axis. For an axis in the direction \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) we write it \(J_{\mathrm{Q}}\).

Source: SHABANA (3.231) (PDF p. 159) for the general form with the inertia tensor, here only for an axis. The centre of mass of a body is a variant of the centroid of a figure from definition 5.21, in which the area is replaced by mass. The moment of inertia about an axis is not the second moment of area \(I_{\mathrm{z}}\) of a cross-section from section 5.4.3, which chapter 5 named similarly. \(J\) has the unit kg m\(^2\).

For a thin bar of mass 30 kg and length 2 m, lying along the axis \(x\) with its middle at \(G\), the density per length is \(15\) kg/m, and about the axis through \(G\) perpendicular to the bar \(J_{\mathrm{G}} = \int_{-1}^{1} 15\,x^2\,\mathrm{d}x = 15 \cdot \tfrac23 = 10\) kg m\(^2\), as \(m L^2/12 = 30 \cdot 4/12\) (section 8.8). A small weight of mass 10 kg at a distance of 1 m from the axis has \(J \approx 10 \cdot 1^2 = 10\) kg m\(^2\), because all its points are almost at the distance \(1\) m. The smaller the weight, the more exactly this holds.

The theorem needs one more thing, a description of the motion of a rigid body. We label a point of the body by the vector \(\vek r_0\) from the centre of mass \(G\) to it in the position at the time \(0\), so that \(\int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\vek r_0\,\mathrm{d}V = \vek 0\) (definition 8.71). For a rigid body the distances between points do not change, so later the whole vector from \(G\) to the point turns with the same vector \(\vek r_0\), \(\vek r = \mat Q(t)\,\vek r_0\) with an orthogonal matrix \(\mat Q(t)\). That the motion of every rigid body has this form follows from section 8.4.1: by (8.24) the difference of the positions of the point and of the point \(G\) at time \(t\) equals \(\mat Q(t)\) times the difference of their positions at time \(0\), that is \(\vek r_0\). The theorem therefore states the form among its assumptions. The mass of a piece of a rigid body does not change, so we compute the integral over the body over the points \(\vek r_0\) with the density \(\rho(\vek r_0)\) and take it as independent of time.

Theorem 8.72 Newton-Euler equations of a rigid body (gibalni enačbi togega telesa)

Let a rigid body of mass \(m\) and centre of mass \(G\) (definition 8.71) at the instant \(t\) be as in corollary 8.70, but without constraints: we count the constraint forces, if it has any, among the forces on it. Let its motion be \(\vek x(t) = \vek x_{\mathrm{G}}(t) + \mat Q(t)\,\vek r_0\) for every point \(\vek r_0\) of the body, with an orthogonal matrix \(\mat Q(t)\) whose elements are twice continuously differentiable, and with \(\vek x_{\mathrm{G}}(t)\) twice continuously differentiable. Let \(\vek F\) be the sum of all forces on it, body forces and point forces, and \(\vek M_{\mathrm{O}}\) the sum of their moments about a chosen point \(O\) with position vector \(\vek O\). Then

\[\vek F = \int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\vek a\,\mathrm{d}V = m\,\vek a_{\mathrm{G}} , \qquad \vek M_{\mathrm{O}} = \int_{\mathrm{V}} (\vek x - \vek O)\times\rho\,\vek a\,\mathrm{d}V , \tag{8.59}\]

where \(\vek a_{\mathrm{G}} = \mathrm{d}^2\vek x_{\mathrm{G}}/\mathrm{d}t^2\) is the acceleration of the centre of mass.

Proof

We must show that from the principle for one rigid body, in which \(\delta\vek a\) and \(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi\) are arbitrary, we obtain both equations, and that we then compute the acceleration from the description of the motion, without differentiating under the integral.

  1. In the principle (8.58) we take one body without constraints. The virtual displacement of the point with position vector \(\vek x\) is \(\delta\vek u = \delta\vek a + \delta\boldsymbol\varphi\times\vek r\) for \(\vek r = \vek x - \vek O\).
  2. By theorem 4.73, equation (4.44), the virtual work of the point forces is \(\delta\vek a\cdot\sum_{\mathrm{i}}\vek F_{\mathrm{i}} + \delta\boldsymbol\varphi\cdot\sum_{\mathrm{i}}\vek r_{\mathrm{i}}\times\vek F_{\mathrm{i}}\). The same rearrangement of the scalar triple product holds at every point for the body force, \(\vek b\cdot\delta\vek u = \delta\vek a\cdot\vek b + \delta\boldsymbol\varphi\cdot(\vek r\times\vek b)\) (step 2 of the proof of the same theorem). Since \(\delta\vek a\) and \(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi\) do not depend on the point, we take them out by the linearity of the integral (lemma 4.10, point 1), and the virtual work of all forces is \(\delta\vek a\cdot\vek F + \delta\boldsymbol\varphi\cdot\vek M_{\mathrm{O}}\) with \(\vek F = \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\vek F_{\mathrm{i}} + \int_{\mathrm{V}} \vek b\,\mathrm{d}V\) and \(\vek M_{\mathrm{O}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\vek r_{\mathrm{i}}\times\vek F_{\mathrm{i}} + \int_{\mathrm{V}} \vek r\times\vek b\,\mathrm{d}V\).
  3. In the same way the work of the inertial force is \(-\int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\vek a\cdot\delta\vek u\,\mathrm{d}V = -\delta\vek a\cdot\int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\vek a\,\mathrm{d}V - \delta\boldsymbol\varphi\cdot\int_{\mathrm{V}} \vek r\times\rho\,\vek a\,\mathrm{d}V\).
  4. By (8.58) the sum of the works is zero for all \(\delta\vek a\) and \(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi\): \(\delta\vek a\cdot(\vek F - \int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\vek a\,\mathrm{d}V) + \delta\boldsymbol\varphi\cdot(\vek M_{\mathrm{O}} - \int_{\mathrm{V}} \vek r\times\rho\,\vek a\,\mathrm{d}V) = 0\).
  5. We choose \(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi = \vek 0\) and \(\delta\vek a = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}, \vek e_{\mathrm{y}}, \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), then \(\delta\vek a = \vek 0\) and \(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}, \vek e_{\mathrm{y}}, \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), as in steps 5 and 6 of the proof of theorem 4.73. Both brackets are zero vectors, so \(\vek F = \int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\vek a\,\mathrm{d}V\) and the second part of equation (8.59).
  6. The acceleration of a point is the second derivative of its position at constant \(\vek r_0\), componentwise: \(\vek a = \vek a_{\mathrm{G}} + \ddot{\mat Q}(t)\,\vek r_0\). The matrix \(\ddot{\mat Q}\) and the vector \(\vek a_{\mathrm{G}}\) do not depend on the point, so we take them out by the linearity of the integral: \(\int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\vek a\,\mathrm{d}V = \vek a_{\mathrm{G}}\int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\mathrm{d}V + \ddot{\mat Q}\int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\vek r_0\,\mathrm{d}V = m\,\vek a_{\mathrm{G}} + \vek 0\), because \(\int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\vek r_0\,\mathrm{d}V = \vek 0\) by the definition of the centre of mass.

Source: SHABANA (3.269), (3.270) (PDF p. 165), who derives them with the Lagrange equation. The proof from theorem 8.69 is not from the sources.

The resultant of the forces is the mass times the acceleration of the centre of mass, and the moment of the forces about a point \(O\) is the moment of the inertial forces about it. For a body turning in a plane the computation is short, and it gives what we need.

Let a rigid body move in the plane \(xy\), so with the axis of rotation in the direction \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), and let its points move only in \(x\) and \(y\). The components \(x\) and \(y\) of the vector \(\vek r = \mat Q\,\vek r_0\) from theorem 8.72 are then \(\vek r = \mat R(\varphi)\,\vek r_0\) with a rotation by the angle \(\varphi(t)\) and a constant \(\vek r_0\), where \(\mat R(\varphi) = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\varphi & -\sin\varphi \\ \sin\varphi & \cos\varphi \end{bmatrix}\) (the transpose of the rotation matrix \(\mat Q\) of definition 2.37 for axes rotated by \(\varphi\)). This is plane motion (ravninsko gibanje), and that every motion of a rigid body in the plane has this form is stated by lemma 8.40. The component \(z\) of the moment \(\vek r\times\vek a\) is \(r_{\mathrm{x}} a_{\mathrm{y}} - r_{\mathrm{y}} a_{\mathrm{x}}\) and depends only on the components \(x\) and \(y\), so a computation in the plane suffices. Let \(\mat J = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\), so that \(\mat J\,\vek r = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\times\vek r\). By the derivatives of sine and cosine (theorem 8.12), \(\mathrm{d}\mat R/\mathrm{d}\varphi = \mat J\,\mat R\) and so \(\dot{\vek r} = \dot\varphi\,\mat J\,\vek r\). One more derivative with \(\mat J\,\mat J = -\mat I\) gives

\[\vek a = \vek a_{\mathrm{G}} + \ddot\varphi\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\times\vek r - \dot\varphi^2\,\vek r . \tag{8.60}\]

The tangential part \(\ddot\varphi\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\times\vek r\) and the normal part \(-\dot\varphi^2\,\vek r\) are the same as for the pendulum in (8.55).

Corollary 8.73 Plane motion of a rigid body (ravninsko gibanje togega telesa)

Let a rigid body (definition 1.11) at the instant \(t\) move in the plane \(xy\) with the angle \(\varphi\), as in theorem 8.72 with \(\mat Q = \mat R(\varphi)\), so with the acceleration from equation (8.60), and let \(M_{\mathrm{G}}\) be the component \(z\) of the moment of all forces on it about the centre of mass \(G\). Then \(J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\ddot\varphi = M_{\mathrm{G}}\), where \(J_{\mathrm{G}}\) is the moment of inertia about the axis through \(G\) in the direction \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) (definition 8.71). If the body has a point \(O\) at rest (a pin), \(J_{\mathrm{O}}\,\ddot\varphi = M_{\mathrm{O}}\), where \(M_{\mathrm{O}}\) is the moment of the forces about \(O\).

Proof

We must show that in (8.59), for the component \(z\) of the moment, only the term with \(\ddot\varphi\) remains.

  1. We take \(O = G\) in (8.59) and \(\vek r = \vek x - \vek x_{\mathrm{G}}\). By (8.60) the component \(z\) of the vector \(\vek r\times\vek a\) is a sum of three terms. For \(\vek a_{\mathrm{G}}\) the \(z\)-component of \(\vek r \times \vek a_{\mathrm{G}}\) is \(r_{\mathrm{x}} a_{\mathrm{G},\mathrm{y}} - r_{\mathrm{y}} a_{\mathrm{G},\mathrm{x}}\), and after integration with \(\rho\) it gives the component \(z\) of the vector \((\int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\vek r\,\mathrm{d}V)\times\vek a_{\mathrm{G}} = \vek 0\), because the centre of mass is at \(G\).
  2. For \(\ddot\varphi\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\times\vek r = \ddot\varphi\,(-r_{\mathrm{y}}, r_{\mathrm{x}}, 0)\) the \(z\)-component is \(r_{\mathrm{x}}\,\ddot\varphi\,r_{\mathrm{x}} - r_{\mathrm{y}}\,(-\ddot\varphi\,r_{\mathrm{y}}) = \ddot\varphi\,(r_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + r_{\mathrm{y}}^2) = \ddot\varphi\,\ell^2\), where \(\ell\) is the distance from the axis through \(G\).
  3. For \(-\dot\varphi^2\,\vek r\) the \(z\)-component is \(-\dot\varphi^2\,(r_{\mathrm{x}} r_{\mathrm{y}} - r_{\mathrm{y}} r_{\mathrm{x}}) = 0\).
  4. The integral with \(\rho\) gives \(M_{\mathrm{G}} = \ddot\varphi \int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\ell^2\,\mathrm{d}V = J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\ddot\varphi\).
  5. Let \(O\) be a point of the body at rest. Let \(\vek s_0\) be the vector from \(O\) to a point of the body in the position at the time \(0\). The vector from \(O\) to the point turns with the same matrix as the vector from \(G\) (the difference of two vectors \(\mat R\,\vek r_0\) is \(\mat R\) times the difference), so \(\vek x = \vek O + \mat R(\varphi)\,\vek s_0\) with constant \(\vek s_0\), and \(\vek a_{\mathrm{O}} = \vek 0\). For \(\vek r = \vek x - \vek O\) equation (8.60) holds with \(\vek a_{\mathrm{G}}\) replaced by \(\vek a_{\mathrm{O}} = \vek 0\), so the term from step 1 is absent, and steps 2 to 4 give \(M_{\mathrm{O}} = J_{\mathrm{O}}\,\ddot\varphi\). The pin is one of the constraint forces, its moment about \(O\) is zero because its arm is zero, so \(M_{\mathrm{O}}\) is the moment of the remaining forces.

Source: SHABANA (3.269), (3.270) (PDF p. 165) are the spatial equations, the plane case is not written in them and is our specialization. The proof from theorem 8.72 is not from the sources.

We test it on example B. A rigid weightless bar \(OA\) of length \(L = 1\) m is turned about the pin \(O\) at the bottom, at the top \(A\) it has a point weight of mass \(m = 10\) kg, whose weight is included in the load \(P\) and which contributes only inertia (the model is ours), a torsion spring of stiffness \(k_{\mathrm{r}} = 2000\) N m is unstretched at \(\theta = 0\), and the vertical load \(P\) acts downward at \(A\). The angle \(\theta\) is measured from the vertical upward counterclockwise, so \(A = L\,(-\sin\theta, \cos\theta)\). The moment of the spring in the direction of increasing \(\theta\) is \(-k_{\mathrm{r}}\,\theta\), the moment of the load \(\vek r\times\vek F\) for \(\vek r = L\,(-\sin\theta, \cos\theta)\) and \(\vek F = (0, -P)\) is \(P L \sin\theta\), which increases the angle. The moment \(M_{\mathrm{O}} = P L \sin\theta - k_{\mathrm{r}}\,\theta\) is also \(-\partial V/\partial\theta\) for \(V = \tfrac12 k_{\mathrm{r}}\,\theta^2 + P L \cos\theta\) from section 8.5. The moment of inertia is \(J_{\mathrm{O}} = m L^2 = 10\) kg m\(^2\) and \(\ddot\theta = (P L \sin\theta - k_{\mathrm{r}}\,\theta)/(m L^2)\) by corollary 8.73. At \(\theta = 0{,}1\) rad, \(\ddot\theta = -20{,}0\) rad/s\(^2\) for \(P = 0\) and \(\ddot\theta = -10{,}017\) rad/s\(^2\) for \(P = 1000\) N \(= \tfrac12 P_{\mathrm{cr}}\) (\(M_{\mathrm{O}} = -200\) N m and \(-100{,}17\) N m). The linear equation \(m L^2\,\ddot\theta = -(k_{\mathrm{r}} - P L)\,\theta\) gives \(-20{,}0\) and \(-10{,}0\) rad/s\(^2\), so it agrees to \(0{,}17\,\%\), because \(\sin 0{,}1 = 0{,}0998\) and not \(0{,}1\) (lemma 8.19). The angular frequency of the linear motion \(\sqrt{(k_{\mathrm{r}} - P L)/(m L^2)}\) is \(14{,}142\) at \(P = 0\) and \(10{,}000\) rad/s at \(P = 1000\) N, and for \(P \to P_{\mathrm{cr}} = k_{\mathrm{r}}/L\) it falls toward zero. Why it falls we can already see: at \(P = P_{\mathrm{cr}}\) the moment of the spring at small \(\theta\) is cancelled by the moment of the load, and nothing returns the bar to the position \(\theta = 0\).

We also test example C, which has two degrees of freedom. A rigid bar of length 2 m and mass 30 kg (\(J_{\mathrm{G}} = 10\) kg m\(^2\)) is held under its ends by vertical springs \(k_1 = 20\) kN/m on the left and \(k_2 = 30\) kN/m on the right, and a guide excludes horizontal displacement (an idealization). The coordinates are \(y\), the vertical displacement of the centre of mass upward, and \(\varphi\), the rotation counterclockwise, both measured from the position where the springs are unstretched. The displacements of the ends are \(y_1 = y - \sin\varphi\) on the left and \(y_2 = y + \sin\varphi\) on the right, because the ends are at the horizontal distance \(\pm 1\) m from \(G\). By theorem 8.72 and corollary 8.73

\[\begin{aligned} m\,\ddot y &= -k_1\,y_1 - k_2\,y_2 - m g , \\ J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\ddot\varphi &= -\cos\varphi\,(-k_1\,y_1) \\ &\quad + \cos\varphi\,(-k_2\,y_2) , \end{aligned} \tag{8.61}\]

since the vertical force on the left end is \(-k_1 y_1\) at the arm \(-\cos\varphi\) and on the right end \(-k_2 y_2\) at the arm \(+\cos\varphi\) (the moment of a vertical force \(F\) at the arm \(x\) is \(x F\)). From rest at \(y = \varphi = 0\), \(\ddot y = -g = -9{,}81\) m/s\(^2\) and \(\ddot\varphi = 0\): the unstretched springs hold nothing back, and the bar falls like a free body. At \(y = 0\) and \(\varphi = 0{,}001\) rad, \(y_1 = -0{,}001\) m and \(y_2 = 0{,}001\) m, the spring forces are \(+20\) N at the left end and \(-30\) N at the right end, so \(\ddot y = (20 - 30 - 294{,}3)/30 = -10{,}143\) m/s\(^2\) and the moment about \(G\) is \((-1)\cdot 20 + 1\cdot(-30) = -50\) N m, so \(\ddot\varphi = -5{,}000\) rad/s\(^2\). The same moment is given by the linearization with the stiffness matrix \(\mat K\) of example C, \(-(\mat K \vek q)_\varphi = -10\,000\,y - 50\,000\,\varphi = -50\) N m. The factor 30 at \(\ddot y\) and 10 at \(\ddot\varphi\) will be the matrix \(\mat M = \mathrm{diag}(30, 10)\) in sections 8.9 and 8.13.

From history

The idea of virtual displacements was written down in a letter to Varignon by Johann Bernoulli (TIMO, PDF p. 35), who in it used in 1717 the expression virtual velocities (EP, PDF p. 2). We met the same idea on the lever in chapter 4. The Traité de dynamique of d'Alembert appeared in 1743 (BALL, PDF p. 403, TIMO, PDF p. 43), and in it d'Alembert wrote down the principle that bears his name: the internal forces of inertia, that is, the forces that resist acceleration, are equal and opposite to the forces that produce the acceleration (BALL, PDF p. 403). This is the expression \(\vek F - m\,\vek a = \vek 0\) from the beginning of the section, and with it, according to d'Alembert, the equations of motion of any rigid system can be obtained (BALL, PDF p. 403). Lagrange in his book Mécanique analytique introduced generalized coordinates and generalized forces with d'Alembert's principle and the principle of virtual displacements (TIMO, PDF p. 45). EP says of him that with d'Alembert's principle he converted dynamics to statics and then with the principle of virtual displacements derived the equations of motion with the energies (EP, PDF p. 3). Why this was a step forward is told by BALL. Instead of following the motion of each part of the system separately, as d'Alembert and Euler had done, he showed that it is enough to determine the position of the system with as many variables as it has degrees of freedom, since the kinetic and potential energy are expressed with them and the equations of motion follow by differentiation (BALL, PDF p. 435). There is not a single figure in the book, because the methods do not require geometrical or mechanical considerations, but only algebraic operations in a prescribed order (TIMO, PDF p. 45). It appeared in 1788 (BALL, PDF p. 436, EP, PDF p. 3, GR, PDF p. 43). The principle that we will meet in section 8.10 is according to GR the time-integrated form of the principle of virtual work (GR, PDF p. 41). Hamilton published it in 1834 (GR, PDF p. 41, EP, PDF p. 3).

Let us return to what we looked at before the story: the principle for a system of rigid bodies (8.58) is statics with the added forces \(-\rho\,\vek a\), and the equations of motion of a rigid body (8.59) give from it for plane motion \(m\,\vek a_{\mathrm{G}} = \vek F\) and \(J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\ddot\varphi = M_{\mathrm{G}}\). We test the principle once more on the pendulum with a weight, where in the end we must show that the force of the bar drops out.

8.7.5 Example: the pendulum (zgled: nihalo)

The point of the weight \(m = 1\) kg on a rigid bar of length \(l = 1\) m, the pin \(O\) at the origin, \(\theta\) from the vertical downward (example A). The weight is a point body, so the inertial force is \(-m\,\vek a\) and there is no integral. The configuration is \(\vek x = l\,(\sin\theta, -\cos\theta)\), there is one degree of freedom, and the compatible virtual displacement for the constraint \(|\vek x| = l\) is a displacement along the circle, \(\delta\vek x = l\,\delta\theta\,(\cos\theta, \sin\theta) = l\,\delta\theta\,\vek e_{\mathrm{t}}\) (figure 8.23). The forces on the weight: the weight \(\vek W = (0, -m g)\) and the force of the bar \(-N\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\), where \(N\) is the force in the bar (a bar in tension has \(N > 0\)). The acceleration is (8.55). By equation (8.58):

  1. The force of the bar, \(-N\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\), is perpendicular to \(\delta\vek x\): \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{t}} = \sin\theta\cos\theta - \cos\theta\sin\theta = 0\). Its virtual work is zero, which is the property of an ideal constraint.
  2. The work of the weight is \(\vek W\cdot\delta\vek x = -m g\,l\,\sin\theta\,\delta\theta\).
  3. The work of the inertial force is \(-m\,\vek a\cdot\delta\vek x = -m\,l\,\delta\theta\,(\vek a\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{t}})\). The normal part of the acceleration, \(-l\,\dot\theta^2\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\), is perpendicular to \(\vek e_{\mathrm{t}}\) and drops out. What remains is \(\vek a\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{t}} = l\,\ddot\theta\), so the work \(-m\,l^2\,\ddot\theta\,\delta\theta\).
  4. The sum is zero for every \(\delta\theta\): \((-m g\,l\sin\theta - m\,l^2\,\ddot\theta)\,\delta\theta = 0\). Since \(\delta\theta\) is arbitrary,
    \[\ddot\theta + \frac gl\,\sin\theta = 0 . \tag{8.62}\]

This is the equation of example A. It agrees with corollary 8.73: \(J_{\mathrm{O}} = m l^2\) and the moment of the weight about \(O\) in the direction of increasing \(\theta\) is \(-m g\,l\,\sin\theta\). Numbers: at \(\theta = 30^\circ\) (\(0{,}5236\) rad) and \(\dot\theta = 2\) rad/s, \(\ddot\theta = -9{,}81 \cdot 0{,}5 = -4{,}905\) rad/s\(^2\), the acceleration has the components \(\vek a = (-6{,}248, 1{,}012)\) m/s\(^2\) (from (8.55), SymPy), \(\vek a\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{t}} = -4{,}905\) m/s\(^2\) and \(\vek a\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{r}} = -4{,}000\) m/s\(^2\), the work of the weight \(-4{,}905\,\delta\theta\) J and the work of the inertial force \(+4{,}905\,\delta\theta\) J cancel. When released from \(\theta = 90^\circ\) from rest, \(\ddot\theta = -g/l = -9{,}81\) rad/s\(^2\).

Equation (8.62) does not contain the force of the bar. Where did it go? The normal component of Newton's law, the \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\)-component of the equation \(m\,\vek a = \vek W - N\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\), is \(-m\,l\,\dot\theta^2 = m g\cos\theta - N\), so \(N = m\,(g\cos\theta + l\,\dot\theta^2)\), and at \(\theta = 30^\circ\) and \(\dot\theta = 2\) rad/s, \(N = 8{,}496 + 4 = 12{,}50\) N. This component is perpendicular to all admissible virtual displacements, so virtual work does not see it. This is the same as in statics: we do not compute the constraint force, because the equilibrium equation does not contain it along the admissible displacements, and we will get it with a Lagrange multiplier when we write the constraint as an equation (section 8.9).

For example D, along this route we would need the accelerations of two bars and of the slider, each separately. We therefore write the equation of motion of example D only in section 8.9, where we obtain it from the energy of motion in the coordinate \(\theta\).
