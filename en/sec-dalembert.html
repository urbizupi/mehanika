Chapter 8 Analytical dynamics (analitična dinamika)

8.7 d'Alembert's principle (d'Alembertovo načelo)

The pendulum of example A is a weight of mass \(m = 1\) kg on a weightless rigid bar of length \(l = 1\) m, turning about a pin \(O\), and in the plane \(xy\) the weight force acts on it. In sections 8.1 to 8.6 we computed the equilibrium of such a system with virtual work, without the force in the pin. When the pendulum swings, however, the forces on it are not in equilibrium. Let us look at them at one instant of the motion (figure 8.123).

The weight hangs on a bar of length \(l\), which turns about the pin \(O\), so it moves along a circle. The angle \(\theta\) is measured from the vertical downward, and we look at the instant when \(\theta = 30^\circ\). Two forces act on the weight. The weight force \(m\vek g\) points downward and has the magnitude \(1 \cdot 9{,}81 = 9{,}81\) N. Its component in the direction of the tangent to the circle is \(m g \sin\theta = 9{,}81 \cdot 0{,}5 = 4{,}905\) N. The force of the bar \(\vek N\) on the weight points along the bar, so perpendicular to the tangent, and therefore cannot cancel the tangential component of the weight force, however large it is. The sum of the two forces \(\vek F\) therefore has a nonzero component along the tangent: the weight is not in equilibrium and it accelerates. Can we still use the statics of sections 8.1 to 8.5 for the motion?

The answer is yes, and the idea is simple. Newton's law for a weight of mass \(m\) and acceleration \(\vek a\) is \(\vek F = m\,\vek a\), where \(\vek F\) is the resultant of the forces, in figure 8.123 the orange arrow. Write it as \(\vek F + (-m\,\vek a) = \vek 0\). The weight then behaves as if it were in equilibrium under the forces \(\vek F\) and an additional force \(-m\,\vek a\), which we call the inertial force (vztrajnostna sila). It is not a real force caused by some other body, but the expression \(m\,\vek a\) moved to the other side of the equation. It is named after inertia: a body with mass resists a change of its velocity, and the expression is the larger, the larger the mass and the faster the velocity changes.

We know the inertial force from a bus that brakes (figure 8.124). The bus drives to the right with the velocity \(\vek v\) and brakes with a deceleration of \(2\) m/s\(^2\), so its acceleration \(\vek a\) points backward, to the left. A passenger of mass \(m = 70\) kg stands and does not hold on. For him to slow down together with the bus, a backward force must act on him, by Newton's law \(m\,a = 70 \cdot 2 = 140\) N. Only a body that touches him can give such a force, and that is the floor, which brakes the soles with the force \(\vek T\). This force acts on the feet, low below the centre of mass. It brakes the feet, while the upper part of the body continues with almost unchanged velocity, so the passenger leans forward. To the passenger it seems that something pulls him forward, although nobody pushes him. This is the inertial force \(-m\,\vek a\), of size \(140\) N, which we draw at the centre of mass blue and dashed, because no body causes it. With it the horizontal forces on the passenger are in equilibrium: the force of the floor backward and the inertial force forward are equally large.

Back to the pendulum. On the right of figure 8.123 we lay the forces one after the other. We start with the weight force, put the force of the bar at its end, and at the end of that the inertial force \(-m\,\vek a\), which we draw blue and dashed, because no body causes it. The last arrow ends where the first began: the triangle is closed, the sum of the three vectors is zero, and this is the picture of equilibrium. The numbers confirm it. By the computation in section 8.7.6 the acceleration at this instant, with \(\dot\theta = 2\) rad/s, has the tangential component \(a_{\mathrm{t}} = -4{,}905\) m/s\(^2\). The inertial force therefore has the tangential component \(+4{,}905\) N and cancels the tangential component of the weight force, which points the opposite way.

A body that is not just a weight will have such forces in every small piece. So we first write the equation that the motion of a body satisfies, then carry the principle of virtual displacements from chapter 4 over to motion, and for a system of rigid bodies we obtain an equation in which the constraint forces do not appear. From these equations we derive two more equations for a rigid body, which we need in examples B and C, and the form of the principle with which we compute systems of rigid bodies. At the end we test everything on the pendulum and on a cylinder that rolls on a block.

8.7.1 Velocity, acceleration and the equation of motion (hitrost, pospešek in gibalna enačba)

So far we looked at a body at one instant. Now the position of the points of the body changes with time \(t\) (figure 8.125).

The figure shows the body at the instant \(t\). Choose in it one point of matter and label it by its position \(\vek X\) before loading, as in chapter 1. At the instant \(t\) this point is at the place \(\vek x(\vek X, t) = \vek X + \vek u(\vek X, t)\), where \(\vek u\) is the displacement. As time runs, the point travels along its path, which we draw grey and dashed. In a short time \(\Delta t\) it moves along the path to a new place, by about \(\vek v\,\Delta t\), where \(\vek v\) is its velocity. The velocity and acceleration of the point are the rate of change of its position and the rate of change of this velocity, and we take them for the same point of matter, so at constant \(\vek X\).

How good is the approximation \(\vek v\,\Delta t\)? Let us look at it on the weight of the pendulum of figure 8.123, which at \(\theta = 30^\circ\) has the velocity \(l\,\dot\theta = 1 \cdot 2 = 2\) m/s along the tangent (figure 8.126). Each frame shows the initial position of the weight and a part of the circle along which it moves. The blackwhite arrow \(\vek v\,\Delta t\) points along the tangent, the orange arrow \(\Delta\vek x\) to the position where the weight really is after the time \(\Delta t\) (we computed it with the pendulum equation of section 8.7.6). The gap between the tips is the error of the approximation. The frames are magnified so that the blackwhite arrow has the same length in all of them. In the middle and the right frame the gap is too small to see, so the circle at the tips shows it magnified ten and a hundred times more. For \(\Delta t = 0{,}1\) s, \(|\vek v\,\Delta t| = 200\) mm and the gap is \(31{,}7\) mm, for \(\Delta t = 0{,}01\) s \(20\) mm and \(0{,}32\) mm, for \(\Delta t = 0{,}001\) s \(2\) mm and \(0{,}0032\) mm. The gap is almost exactly \(\tfrac12\,|\vek a|\,\Delta t^2\) with \(|\vek a| = 6{,}33\) m/s\(^2\), which for \(m = 1\) kg the orange arrow \(m\,\vek a\) in figure 8.123 shows. This is the distance the acceleration adds in the time \(\Delta t\), as for a body that accelerates from rest. So with a ten times shorter \(\Delta t\) the gap shrinks a hundred times, the arrow \(\vek v\,\Delta t\) only ten times, and their ratio falls from \(16\) % to \(1{,}6\) % and \(0{,}16\) %. The displacement divided by \(\Delta t\) therefore approaches the velocity \(\vek v\), and the definition below takes this limit as the velocity.

Definition 8.77 Velocity and acceleration of a material point (hitrost in pospešek točke telesa)

Let \(\vek x(\vek X, t)\) be the position vector of the point of a body with position \(\vek X\) before loading at the instant \(t\), twice continuously differentiable in \(t\) at every constant \(\vek X\). The velocity (hitrost) of the point, which travels along its path, is the vector \(\vek v = \partial\vek x/\partial t\) and the acceleration (pospešek) the vector \(\vek a = \partial^2\vek x/\partial t^2\), both at constant \(\vek X\), so componentwise the derivative with respect to time.

Source: GR (1.1) (PDF p. 32), BOWER §2.4.4.1 (PDF p. 86, \(\mathrm{d}v_{\mathrm{i}}/\mathrm{d}t\) in the equation of motion). The notation with \(\vek X\) and \(\vek u\) is not from the sources.

For the pendulum \(\vek x(t) = l\,(\sin\theta, -\cos\theta)\), and by the chain rule (lemma 8.30, item 3) with the derivatives of sine and cosine (theorem 8.13), which hold for an angle in radians (definition 8.8), \(\vek v = l\,\dot\theta\,(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\) and

\[\vek a = l\,\ddot\theta\,(\cos\theta, \sin\theta) + l\,\dot\theta^2\,(-\sin\theta, \cos\theta) . \tag{8.73}\]

The first vector is the unit vector of the tangent \(\vek e_{\mathrm{t}} = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\), the second is opposite to the unit vector \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}} = (\sin\theta, -\cos\theta)\) from \(O\) toward the weight. The acceleration therefore has a tangential part \(l\,\ddot\theta\), which changes the speed of the point, and a normal part \(-l\,\dot\theta^2\), which points toward \(O\) and bends the path. In figure 8.123 their sum is the orange arrow, since for \(m = 1\) kg the vector \(m\,\vek a\) has the same numbers as \(\vek a\). At \(\theta = 30^\circ\) and \(\dot\theta = 2\) rad/s the tangential part is \(-4{,}905\) m/s\(^2\), toward smaller \(\theta\), and the normal part \(-l\,\dot\theta^2 = -4{,}000\) m/s\(^2\), toward \(O\), so the arrow points to the left and slightly upward.

Which law connects the acceleration with the forces on a body? For the point of the weight it is Newton's law. For a body it is the equation for every piece of matter, which chapter 2 has already written in words: in the equilibrium equations (theorem 2.31) the density times the acceleration appears on the right-hand side (section 2.7). We accept it as a law of nature, as we accepted equilibrium, and give it a name.

Definition 8.78 Equation of motion of a body (gibalna enačba telesa)

Let the body have at a point \(P\) the density \(\rho\), the body force density \(\vek b\) (definition 2.28), the stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\) and the acceleration \(\vek a\) (definition 8.77). At every interior point of the body where the stress components have partial derivatives and the density and the acceleration are continuous near it, at every instant

\[\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}} + b_{\mathrm{j}} = \rho\,a_{\mathrm{j}} , \qquad j = x, y, z . \tag{8.74}\]

Source: BOWER §2.3.1 (PDF p. 76) and §2.4.4.1 (PDF p. 86), CM (5.3.12) (PDF p. 228). This is a law, not a theorem, so we do not prove it. BOWER takes \(\vek b\) per unit mass, here it is, as in definition 2.28, per unit volume.

The left-hand side is a force per unit volume, the right-hand side a mass per unit volume times the acceleration, so this is Newton's law for a piece of matter. Why is the sum of the derivatives of the stresses a force per unit volume? At theorem 2.31 we looked at a small cube of side \(h\) about a point. The forces of the stresses on opposite faces differ by the change of the stress across the cube times the face area \(h^2\). This difference, divided by the volume of the cube \(h^3\), is the change of the stress divided by the side \(h\), and in the limit the derivative. To it we add the body force \(\vek b\). How large is the right-hand side? For steel with \(\rho = 7800\) kg/m\(^3\) and the acceleration \(4{,}905\) m/s\(^2\), \(\rho\,a = 38\,259\) N/m\(^3\), so \(0{,}038\) N on every cubic centimetre. This is half of what this centimetre weighs, \(\rho\,g = 0{,}077\) N, because the acceleration is half of \(g\). For \(\vek a = \vek 0\) the equation of motion is the equilibrium equation (2.38).

8.7.2 The principle for a body (načelo za telo)

We obtained the principle of virtual displacements (theorem 4.82) by multiplying the equilibrium equations by a virtual displacement and integrating over the body. We do the same computation with the equation of motion. A virtual displacement here compares positions at the same instant: time stands still during the virtual displacement. In figure 8.125 the true displacement of the piece in the time \(\Delta t\) goes along the path, and the motion decides it. The virtual displacement \(\delta\vek u\), in contrast, is imagined: we choose its direction ourselves, so in the figure it does not lie on the path. Why do we stop time? The equation of motion (8.74) ties the forces and the acceleration at the same instant, and with the virtual displacement we only test it from all directions. If time ran during the displacement, the forces and accelerations would change, and we would compare two different equations.

At the instant \(t\) the body occupies a set of points \(V\), which we treat as in chapter 4 (small displacements, section 4.5.4), and the other conditions are the same as there. The assumption of small displacements means that we compute the integrals over the position of the body as if it did not stretch under the load. It is right as long as the strains are small against \(1\): for the steel bar below the largest strain is \(5{,}9 \cdot 10^{-6}\), and the error of the integrals is of this order. For a rubber band that stretches to twice its length the assumption does not hold, and we would have to integrate over the current position. For the systems of rigid bodies in section 8.7.3 we will not need it.

Theorem 8.79 d'Alembert's principle for a body (d'Alembertovo načelo za telo)

Let the body occupy at the instant \(t\) a set of points \(V\) that is a body made of boxes (definition 4.11), with two parts of its surface \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) and \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\), a vector \(\bar{\vek t}\) and a body force density \(\vek b\) as in theorem 4.82. Let the density \(\rho\) be continuous on \(V\) and the acceleration \(\vek a\) (definition 8.77) continuous on \(V\). Assume:

  1. the stress components are continuously differentiable on an open set that contains the body together with its surface,
  2. at every interior point of the body the equation of motion (8.74) holds,
  3. at every point of the body the stress is symmetric,
  4. at every point of the part \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\), \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \bar{\vek t}\), where \(\vek n\) is the outward unit normal.

Then for every kinematically admissible virtual displacement \(\delta\vek u\) with virtual strain \(\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) (definition 4.79)

\[\int_{\mathrm{V}} \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\mathrm{d}V = \int_{\mathrm{V}} (\vek b - \rho\,\vek a)\cdot\delta\vek u\,\mathrm{d}V + \int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{t}} \bar{\vek t}\cdot\delta\vek u\,\mathrm{d}A . \tag{8.75}\]
Proof

We must show that in equality (4.51) the volume integral on the right turns into the work of the body force and of the inertial force \(-\rho\,\vek a\), and the surface integral into the work of the prescribed force. The steps are the steps of the proof of theorem 4.82, except that the equation of motion holds in place of the equilibrium equation.

  1. Assumptions 1 and 3 and the continuous differentiability of the field \(\delta\vek u\) (definition 4.79) are the assumptions of lemma 4.81, so (4.51) holds for the body.
  2. With indices the equation of motion (8.74) reads \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}} = \rho\,a_{\mathrm{j}} - b_{\mathrm{j}}\). At every interior point, in figure 8.125 in the piece, we therefore have
    \[\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \Big(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}}\Big)\,\delta u_{\mathrm{j}} = -\sum_{\mathrm{j}} (b_{\mathrm{j}} - \rho\,a_{\mathrm{j}})\,\delta u_{\mathrm{j}} = -(\vek b - \rho\,\vek a)\cdot\delta\vek u .\]
  3. The equality also holds at the points of the surface, as in step 3 of the proof of theorem 4.82. Every point \(Q\) of the surface is the limit of interior points \(Q_{\mathrm{n}}\), at which the equality holds by step 2. Both sides are continuous, so their values at \(Q\) are limits of equal sequences and are equal. There the continuity of both sides was needed, and since \(\rho\) and \(\vek a\) are continuous like \(\vek b\), the difference \(\vek b - \rho\,\vek a\) is continuous too.
  4. The functions under the volume integral in (4.51) and \(-(\vek b - \rho\,\vek a)\cdot\delta\vek u\) are therefore equal at every point of the body, so their integrals are equal. In figure 8.125 the second function is the sum of the body force and the inertial force of the piece, multiplied by its virtual displacement.
  5. The surface integral is split into \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) and \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) as in step 5 of the same proof: on the part \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) we have \(\delta\vek u = \vek 0\), on the part \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) we have \(\vek t(\vek n) = \bar{\vek t}\) by assumption 4, which is the prescribed force in the figure.
  6. We insert steps 4 and 5 into (4.51). The minus before the volume integral and the minus from step 4 give a plus, and we obtain (8.75).

Source: BOWER §2.4.4.1 (PDF p. 86): takes the equality with \(\rho\,\mathrm{d}v_{\mathrm{i}}/\mathrm{d}t\) and \(\rho\,b_{\mathrm{i}}\), sketches the proof with the divergence theorem. GR (1.1) (PDF p. 32) and (1.5) (PDF p. 33) for a particle. The proof with the steps of theorem 4.82 is not from the sources.

The theorem says the same as theorem 4.82, except that the body force is replaced by \(\vek b - \rho\,\vek a\). Motion is therefore equilibrium under an added body force \(-\rho\,\vek a\) per unit volume, the inertial force. At each instant separately, everything we derived about statics from virtual work therefore holds, including the meaning of virtual work (figure 8.125). What we derived from the potential energy does not hold, for example the principle of minimum total potential energy (theorem 4.91). The inertial force depends not on the position but on the acceleration, which we are still looking for, so it has no potential energy.

The body in the figure has the hatched part of the surface \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\), on which the displacement is prescribed, and the part \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\), on which the prescribed force \(\bar{\vek t}\) acts. In a small piece act the body force \(\vek b\) and next to it the inertial force \(-\rho\,\vek a\), opposite to the acceleration of the piece. Equation (8.75) takes the dot product of their sum with the virtual displacement \(\delta\vek u\) of the piece and integrates it over the body.

We test it on the bar from chapter 3, which we hang on a crane (figure 8.127).

A hanging steel bar of length \(L = 10\) m, diameter 10 mm (\(A = 78{,}54\) mm\(^2\)) and density \(\rho = 7800\) kg/m\(^3\) has mass \(m = \rho A L = 6{,}126\) kg and weight \(m g = 60{,}10\) N. Its top end is attached to a crane. Let the axis \(x\) point vertically downward from the top end, as for the hanging bar. While the crane is at rest (left), every piece is pulled downward by the weight of density \(\rho g\), the crane holds the top end with the force \(60{,}10\) N, and the stress in the top cross-section is \(\rho g L = 0{,}765\) MPa.

Now let the crane lift the bar vertically upward with the acceleration \(a_0 = 0{,}5\,g = 4{,}905\) m/s\(^2\) (right). We idealize the bar: we assume that all its points have the same acceleration. This is right when the crane has been accelerating uniformly for some time and the bar has settled. It does not hold just after a jerky start of the lifting, when the bar oscillates lengthwise and the force in the top cross-section oscillates about the value we are going to compute (chapter 13 will treat such oscillations). Without damping the top force then reaches up to \(m\,(g + 2a_0) = 120{,}2\) N (figure 8.128 below), a third more than \(90{,}15\) N. The weight has density \(b = \rho g\) in the direction \(+x\), the acceleration is \(-a_0\) in the direction \(x\), so in equation (8.75) we have

\[b - \rho a = \rho g - \rho(-a_0) = \rho\,(g + a_0) , \qquad g + a_0 = 14{,}715\ \mathrm{m/s^2} .\]

The inertial force \(\rho a_0\) on every piece points downward, against the acceleration, and adds to the weight: the bar is loaded as if gravity were larger. The virtual work equation is therefore the equation of the hanging bar with \(g\) replaced by \(g + a_0\), and it has the same solution. The axial force in the top cross-section is thus \(\rho (g + a_0) A L = m\,(g + a_0) = 90{,}15\) N, the stress there \(\rho (g + a_0) L = 1{,}148\) MPa instead of \(0{,}765\) MPa for the bar at rest. The ratio is \(1{,}5 = (g + a_0)/g\), because the load of every piece grew by this factor. The same follows from Newton's law for the whole bar: the force of the crane \(F\) satisfies \(F - m g = m\,a_0\), so \(F = m\,(g + a_0) = 90{,}15\) N. If the crane let go of the bar and it fell freely, the acceleration would be \(a = g\) downward, \(b - \rho a = 0\), and there would be no stress in the bar once the lengthwise oscillation set off by the release had died out.

How large is the error of the idealization that all points of the bar have the same acceleration? Figure 8.128 shows it. On the horizontal axis is the time \(t\) from the start of lifting in milliseconds, on the vertical one the force \(F\) with which the crane holds the top end. The grey dashed line at \(90{,}15\) N is the force of the idealized bar, the lower dashed line at \(60{,}10\) N the force while the crane is at rest. In the upper graph the crane switches on the acceleration \(a_0\) at once at \(t = 0\). The lower part of the bar is then still at rest while the upper part already accelerates, so the bar stretches and contracts lengthwise, and the force at the top rises to \(120{,}2\) N and falls back to \(60{,}10\) N, again every \(7{,}98\) ms. This is the period of the lengthwise oscillation of the bar, which chapter 13 will derive. The force oscillates about the value of the idealized bar, by \(30{,}05\) N up and down, which is a third of \(90{,}15\) N. The added force \(m\,a_0 = 30{,}05\) N thus doubles at the peak. The same happens with a weight on a spring: if we let it go at the unstretched spring, the spring stretches at most twice as far as when we lower the weight slowly, because the weight still has a velocity at the equilibrium position. In the lower graph the crane raises the acceleration uniformly over \(20\) ms. The force then follows the straight line from \(60{,}10\) to \(90{,}15\) N within \(1{,}5\) N, and after \(20\) ms it oscillates only by \(3{,}0\) N about \(90{,}15\) N, which is \(3{,}3\) %. The idealization is thus good when the acceleration of the crane changes over a time several times longer than the period \(7{,}98\) ms, and bad at a jerk. Damping in the steel and in the attachment calms the oscillation in time, so after a long enough time it is right after a jerk too.

8.7.3 A system of rigid bodies (sistem togih teles)

For a system of rigid bodies joined by pins, guides and other constraints (section 8.1), we are not interested in the internal state of the bodies but in the motion and the forces of the constraints. By the definition of a rigid body (definition 1.11) the distances between points do not change. The virtual displacement of a rigid body is therefore rigid too, a translation and a rotation, in which no piece stretches or changes its right angles. Stresses do work only on stretches and on changes of the right angles of pieces (the left-hand side of equation (8.75)), so there is no internal virtual work. The rigid body is an idealization. It is right when the stretches of the bodies are much smaller than their displacements: the steel bar on the crane stretches under the weight and the inertial force by less than \(0{,}03\) mm, while the crane moves it from rest by \(\tfrac12 a_0 t^2 = 2{,}45\) m in one second. It does not hold for soft parts, such as a spring, which we therefore treat as a force between bodies, nor for bodies that oscillate noticeably. Such is the bar just after a jerky start of the lifting: the force at its top oscillates by \(30{,}05\) N about the \(90{,}15\) N that the rigid bar gives, so by a third. In the principle this idealization erases the left-hand side, the integral of the stresses.

Corollary 8.80 d'Alembert's principle for a system of rigid bodies (d'Alembertovo načelo za sistem togih teles)

Let a system of rigid bodies \(1, \dots, k\) have ideal constraints (definition 8.3, for example a hinge), and at the instant \(t\) let body \(j\) occupy a set of points \(V_{\mathrm{j}}\) that is a body made of boxes (definition 4.11), with continuous density \(\rho_{\mathrm{j}}\), continuous acceleration \(\vek a\) and body force density \(\vek b\). Besides the body force let applied forces (uporabljene sile) \(\vek F_{\mathrm{j}1}, \dots\) at points \(A_{\mathrm{j}1}, \dots\) and constraint forces act on body \(j\). At the time \(t\) let the bodies satisfy the equation of motion (definition 8.78) and assumptions 1, 3 and 4 of theorem 8.79, with \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) empty, \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) the whole surface of the body and \(\bar{\vek t}\) the applied and constraint forces per unit area, and let \(\vek b\) be continuous. A point force is the limit of such a force on ever smaller patches. Let \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{j}} = \delta\vek a_{\mathrm{j}} + \delta\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{j}} \times \vek r\) be a rigid virtual displacement of body \(j\) (\(\vek r = \overrightarrow{O_{\mathrm{j}} P}\), lemma 4.72), composed of displacements that respect the constraints (definition 8.1). Then

\[\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \Big[ \int_{\mathrm{V}_\mathrm{j}} (\vek b - \rho_{\mathrm{j}}\,\vek a)\cdot\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{d}V + \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \vek F_{\mathrm{ji}}\cdot\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{ji}} \Big] = 0 , \tag{8.76}\]

where \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{ji}}\) is the virtual displacement of the point \(A_{\mathrm{ji}}\).

Proof

We must show that for each body the internal virtual work drops out and that the virtual work of the constraint forces cancels in the sum. We take the constraint forces, for example \(\vek F_1\) and \(\vek F_2\) in a hinge, as forces on the body, as in theorem 4.73, where the forces of the supports are among the forces.

  1. For body \(j\) we take theorem 8.79 without a part of the surface with prescribed displacement, \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) empty. All forces on the surface, applied ones and constraint forces, are prescribed forces on the part \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\), and point forces are an idealization of a force on a very small surface, whose work is \(\vek F\cdot\delta\vek u\) at the point of application (definition 4.71). The position \(V_{\mathrm{j}}\) at the instant \(t\) is the position relative to which the body is without deformation, so no assumption of small displacements is used.
  2. The rigid virtual displacement \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{j}}\), a translation and a rotation, is continuously differentiable. Its derivative is a constant antisymmetric matrix \(W_{\mathrm{ik}}\), because \(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi \times \vek r = \mat W\,\vek r\) (section 4.4.3, lemma 4.72), so the virtual strain is \(\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ik}} = \tfrac12(W_{\mathrm{ik}} + W_{\mathrm{ki}}) = 0\) at every point and the left-hand side of (8.75) is zero. In figure 4.235 the rotated box keeps the lengths of its edges and its right angles, unlike the stretched and the sheared one, on which the stresses would do work.
  3. For body \(j\) the sum of the virtual works of the body force, the inertial force, the applied forces and the constraint forces is therefore zero.
  4. We sum over the bodies. The displacements \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{j}}\) respect the constraints, so the total virtual work of the constraint forces is zero by the definition of an ideal constraint (definition 8.3). So it is at a hinge, where the force \(\vek F_1\) and the force \(\vek F_2 = -\vek F_1\) work on the same displacement. What remains is (8.76).

Source: GR §1.2.1 (PDF p. 35-36, "effective forces" for a system of particles), SHABANA (3.65), (3.66) (PDF p. 120). For a rigid body SHABANA shows that the virtual work of the internal forces is zero (PDF p. 120, (3.62)). The proof after theorem 8.79 is not from the sources.

Equation (8.76) is the equation of theorem 8.4 on the virtual work of a system, in which the inertial force \(-\rho\,\vek a\) on every piece of matter joins the applied forces. The constraint forces do not appear in it, so we do not compute them. This is both the price and the advantage: if we were interested only in the equations of motion, they dropped out, and if we need the constraint forces, we will get them in section 8.9 with Lagrange multipliers, as in section 8.5, or by cutting one body free and writing Newton's law for it (section 8.7.7). The integral over the body is awkward for computing. In section 8.7.5 we replace it, for a rigid body in the plane, by one force at the centre of mass and one moment.

8.7.4 The equations of motion of a rigid body (gibalni enačbi togega telesa)

For examples B and C we need what follows from (8.76) for one rigid body. First we name the quantities that appear in the result.

Definition 8.81 Mass, centre of mass and moment of inertia of a body (masa, težišče in vztrajnostni moment telesa)

A body of density \(\rho\) (a function of the point, in kg/m\(^3\)), occupying a set \(V\), has mass (masa) \(m = \int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\mathrm{d}V\). The centre of mass (težišče telesa) is the point \(G\) with position vector \(\vek x_{\mathrm{G}} = \tfrac1m \int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\vek x\,\mathrm{d}V\), so the average of the positions of the points weighted by the density. For it, \(\int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\vek r\,\mathrm{d}V = \vek 0\) for \(\vek r = \vek x - \vek x_{\mathrm{G}}\), because by the linearity of the integral (lemma 4.10, point 1) \(\int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,(\vek x - \vek x_{\mathrm{G}})\,\mathrm{d}V = \int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\vek x\,\mathrm{d}V - \vek x_{\mathrm{G}}\int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\mathrm{d}V = m\,\vek x_{\mathrm{G}} - m\,\vek x_{\mathrm{G}}\). The moment of inertia of the body about an axis (vztrajnostni moment telesa glede na os) through a point \(Q\) in the direction of a unit vector \(\vek n\) is the number \(J = \int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\ell^2\,\mathrm{d}V\), where \(\ell\) is the distance of the point from the axis. For an axis in the direction \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) we write it \(J_{\mathrm{Q}}\).

Source: SHABANA (3.231) (PDF p. 159) for the general form with the inertia tensor, here only for an axis. The centre of mass of a body is a variant of the centroid of a figure from definition 5.21, in which the area is replaced by mass. The moment of inertia about an axis is not the second moment of area \(I_{\mathrm{z}}\) of a cross-section from section 5.4.3, which chapter 5 named similarly. \(J\) has the unit kg m\(^2\).

For a thin bar of mass 30 kg and length 2 m (the bar of example C in figure 8.132), lying along the axis \(x\) with its middle at \(G\), the density per length is \(15\) kg/m, and about the axis through \(G\) perpendicular to the bar \(J_{\mathrm{G}} = \int_{-1}^{1} 15\,x^2\,\mathrm{d}x = 15 \cdot \tfrac23 = 10\) kg m\(^2\), as \(m L^2/12 = 30 \cdot 4/12\) (section 8.8). A small weight of mass 10 kg at a distance of 1 m from the axis (the weight of example B) has \(J \approx 10 \cdot 1^2 = 10\) kg m\(^2\), because all its points are almost at the distance \(1\) m. The smaller the weight, the more exactly this holds. How exactly is told by the computation in the proof of Steiner's theorem (theorem 5.28, there with area, here with mass): \(J = 10 \cdot 1^2 + J_{\mathrm{u}}\), where \(J_{\mathrm{u}}\) is the moment of inertia of the weight about the parallel axis through its centre of mass. For a weight whose points are at most 5 cm from its centre of mass, \(J_{\mathrm{u}} \le 10 \cdot 0{,}05^2 = 0{,}025\) kg m\(^2\), so \(J = 10\) kg m\(^2\) is too small by at most \(0{,}25\,\%\).

Why does the square of the distance appear in \(J\)? A piece at the distance \(\ell\) from the axis has, at the angular acceleration \(\ddot\varphi\), the acceleration \(\ell\,\ddot\varphi\) along the circle. Its inertial force is therefore proportional to \(\ell\), and its moment about the axis has in addition the arm \(\ell\). Mass that is twice as far from the axis therefore resists turning four times as much. The computation is step 2 of the proof of corollary 8.83.

The theorem needs one more thing, a description of the motion of a rigid body (figure 8.129, left).

In the body we mark the centre of mass \(G\) and one point. We label the point by the vector \(\vek r_0\) from the centre of mass to it in the position at the time \(0\), so that \(\int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\vek r_0\,\mathrm{d}V = \vek 0\) (definition 8.81). For a rigid body the distances between points do not change, so later the whole vector \(\vek r\) from \(G\) to the point turns, \(\vek r = \mat Q(t)\,\vek r_0\) with an orthogonal matrix \(\mat Q(t)\). That the motion of every rigid body has this form follows from section 8.4.1: by (8.26) the difference of the positions of the point and of the point \(G\) at time \(t\) equals \(\mat Q(t)\) times the difference of their positions at time \(0\), that is \(\vek r_0\). The theorem therefore states the form among its assumptions. The mass of a piece of a rigid body does not change, so we compute the integral over the body over the points \(\vek r_0\) with the density \(\rho(\vek r_0)\) and take it as independent of time.

Theorem 8.82 Newton-Euler equations of a rigid body (gibalni enačbi togega telesa)

Let a rigid body of mass \(m\) and centre of mass \(G\) (definition 8.81) at the instant \(t\) be as in corollary 8.80, but without constraints: we count the constraint forces, if it has any, among the forces on it. Let its motion be \(\vek x(t) = \vek x_{\mathrm{G}}(t) + \mat Q(t)\,\vek r_0\) for every point \(\vek r_0\) of the body, with an orthogonal matrix \(\mat Q(t)\) whose elements are twice continuously differentiable, and with \(\vek x_{\mathrm{G}}(t)\) twice continuously differentiable. Let \(\vek F\) be the sum of all forces on it, body forces and point forces, and \(\vek M_{\mathrm{O}}\) the sum of their moments about a chosen point \(O\) with position vector \(\vek O\). Then

\[\vek F = \int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\vek a\,\mathrm{d}V = m\,\vek a_{\mathrm{G}} , \qquad \vek M_{\mathrm{O}} = \int_{\mathrm{V}} (\vek x - \vek O)\times\rho\,\vek a\,\mathrm{d}V , \tag{8.77}\]

where \(\vek a_{\mathrm{G}} = \mathrm{d}^2\vek x_{\mathrm{G}}/\mathrm{d}t^2\) is the acceleration of the centre of mass.

Proof

We must show that from the principle for one rigid body, in which the displacement \(\delta\vek a\) and the rotation \(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi\) are arbitrary, we obtain both equations, and that we then compute the acceleration from the description of the motion, without differentiating under the integral.

  1. In the principle (8.76) we take one body without constraints. The virtual displacement of the point with position vector \(\vek x\) is \(\delta\vek u = \delta\vek a + \delta\boldsymbol\varphi\times\vek r\) for \(\vek r = \vek x - \vek O\), so a translation and a rotation.
  2. By theorem 4.73, equation (4.44), the virtual work of the point forces in the displacement and the rotation is \(\delta\vek a\cdot\sum_{\mathrm{i}}\vek F_{\mathrm{i}} + \delta\boldsymbol\varphi\cdot\sum_{\mathrm{i}}\vek r_{\mathrm{i}}\times\vek F_{\mathrm{i}}\). The same rearrangement of the scalar triple product holds at every point for the body force, \(\vek b\cdot\delta\vek u = \delta\vek a\cdot\vek b + \delta\boldsymbol\varphi\cdot(\vek r\times\vek b)\) (step 2 of the proof of the same theorem). Since \(\delta\vek a\) and \(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi\) do not depend on the point, we take them out by the linearity of the integral (lemma 4.10, point 1), and the virtual work of all forces is \(\delta\vek a\cdot\vek F + \delta\boldsymbol\varphi\cdot\vek M_{\mathrm{O}}\) with \(\vek F = \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\vek F_{\mathrm{i}} + \int_{\mathrm{V}} \vek b\,\mathrm{d}V\) and \(\vek M_{\mathrm{O}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\vek r_{\mathrm{i}}\times\vek F_{\mathrm{i}} + \int_{\mathrm{V}} \vek r\times\vek b\,\mathrm{d}V\).
  3. In the same way the work of the inertial forces of the pieces is \(-\int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\vek a\cdot\delta\vek u\,\mathrm{d}V = -\delta\vek a\cdot\int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\vek a\,\mathrm{d}V - \delta\boldsymbol\varphi\cdot\int_{\mathrm{V}} \vek r\times\rho\,\vek a\,\mathrm{d}V\). In the right half of figure 8.129, for the planar case, these works come to this: a body with its centre of mass \(G\) has one force \(-m\,\vek a_{\mathrm{G}}\), which works on the displacement \(\delta\vek a\), and a moment \(-J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\ddot\varphi\), which works on the rotation \(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi\).
  4. By (8.76) the sum of the works is zero for all \(\delta\vek a\) and \(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi\): \(\delta\vek a\cdot(\vek F - \int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\vek a\,\mathrm{d}V) + \delta\boldsymbol\varphi\cdot(\vek M_{\mathrm{O}} - \int_{\mathrm{V}} \vek r\times\rho\,\vek a\,\mathrm{d}V) = 0\).
  5. We choose first a pure displacement, \(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi = \vek 0\) and \(\delta\vek a = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}, \vek e_{\mathrm{y}}, \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), then a pure rotation, \(\delta\vek a = \vek 0\) and \(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}, \vek e_{\mathrm{y}}, \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), as in steps 5 and 6 of the proof of theorem 4.73. Both brackets are zero vectors, so \(\vek F = \int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\vek a\,\mathrm{d}V\) and the second part of equation (8.77).
  6. The acceleration of a point is the second derivative of its position at constant \(\vek r_0\), componentwise: \(\vek a = \vek a_{\mathrm{G}} + \ddot{\mat Q}(t)\,\vek r_0\). The matrix \(\ddot{\mat Q}\) and the vector \(\vek a_{\mathrm{G}}\) do not depend on the point, so we take them out by the linearity of the integral: \(\int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\vek a\,\mathrm{d}V = \vek a_{\mathrm{G}}\int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\mathrm{d}V + \ddot{\mat Q}\int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\vek r_0\,\mathrm{d}V = m\,\vek a_{\mathrm{G}} + \vek 0\), because \(\int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\vek r_0\,\mathrm{d}V = \vek 0\) by the definition of the centre of mass. On the body of two pieces in figure 8.130 these are the opposite and equal \(m_1\vek r_1\) and \(m_2\vek r_2\) (left), and so the inertial forces of the turning cancel in the sum (right). On the displacement \(\delta\vek r_{\mathrm{G}}\) the tangential forces do the work \(+0{,}08\) J and \(-0{,}08\) J, the normal forces \(-0{,}03\) J and \(+0{,}03\) J, and each sum is zero.

Source: SHABANA (3.269), (3.270) (PDF p. 165), who derives them with the Lagrange equation. The proof from theorem 8.79 is not from the sources.

The resultant of the forces is the mass times the acceleration of the centre of mass, and the moment of the forces about a point \(O\) is the negative of the moment of the inertial forces about it. Why is it the centre of mass that appears in the first equation? The parts of the acceleration that the turning about \(G\) brings cancel over the body, because \(\int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\vek r_0\,\mathrm{d}V = \vek 0\): to a piece on one side of the centre of mass corresponds mass on the other side, which the turning accelerates in the opposite direction (figure 8.130).

Figure 8.130 shows this on the simplest body, two small pieces joined by a light bar, for which the integral over the body is a sum of two terms. The piece of mass \(m_1 = 2\) kg is \(0{,}5\) m to the left of the centre of mass \(G\), the piece of mass \(m_2 = 1\) kg \(1\) m to the right. The lighter piece is twice as far from \(G\), so \(m_1\vek r_1\) and \(m_2\vek r_2\) are opposite and equal, both of size \(1\) kg m (left). The turning accelerates each piece in proportion to its distance from \(G\), in opposite directions on the two sides (right). At the angular acceleration \(\ddot\varphi = 2\) rad/s\(^2\) and the angular velocity \(\dot\varphi = 1\) rad/s the inertial forces along the circle are \(2 \cdot 2 \cdot 0{,}5 = 2\) N and \(1 \cdot 2 \cdot 1 = 2\) N, and the forces away from \(G\) are \(2 \cdot 1^2 \cdot 0{,}5 = 1\) N and \(1 \cdot 1^2 \cdot 1 = 1\) N, both times opposite. The sum of the inertial forces of the turning is zero, and in the first equation of (8.77) only \(m\,\vek a_{\mathrm{G}}\) remains. In the middle, the virtual rotation \(\delta\varphi = 0{,}1\) rad moves the piece \(m_1\) by \(0{,}05\) m down and the piece \(m_2\) by \(0{,}1\) m up, and the forces do the work \(+0{,}2\) J and \(-0{,}2\) J. The middle part of the figure and the displacement \(\delta\vek r_{\mathrm{G}}\) on the right will be needed in the proofs of corollaries 8.83 and 8.84. For a body that moves in a plane the computation is short, and it gives what we need.

Let a rigid body move in the plane \(xy\), so with the axis of rotation in the direction \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), and let its points move only in \(x\) and \(y\). The components \(x\) and \(y\) of the vector \(\vek r = \mat Q\,\vek r_0\) from theorem 8.82 are then \(\vek r = \mat R(\varphi)\,\vek r_0\) with a rotation by the angle \(\varphi(t)\) and a constant \(\vek r_0\), where \(\mat R(\varphi) = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\varphi & -\sin\varphi \\ \sin\varphi & \cos\varphi \end{bmatrix}\) (the transpose of the rotation matrix \(\mat Q\) of definition 2.44 for axes rotated by \(\varphi\)). This is plane motion (ravninsko gibanje), and that every motion of a rigid body in the plane has this form is stated by lemma 8.41. The assumption holds for bodies that guides or symmetry keep in the plane, like the bars of examples B and C. It does not hold for a spinning top or for a wheel that leans, because there the direction of the axis of rotation changes. There the moment of the inertial forces is no longer \(-J\,\ddot\varphi\), and one number \(J\) is not enough (section 8.8.3). The component \(z\) of the moment \(\vek r\times\vek a\) is \(r_{\mathrm{x}} a_{\mathrm{y}} - r_{\mathrm{y}} a_{\mathrm{x}}\) and depends only on the components \(x\) and \(y\), so a computation in the plane suffices.

In figure 8.131 we draw the computation. A point of the body circles about the centre of mass \(G\) on a circle, because in a rigid body the length of the vector \(\vek r\) does not change. The vector \(\vek r_0\) turns by the angle \(\varphi\) into the vector \(\vek r = \mat R(\varphi)\,\vek r_0\), 1 m long in the figure (left). Let \(\mat J = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\). The matrix \(\mat J\) makes \((-r_{\mathrm{y}}, r_{\mathrm{x}})\) out of \((r_{\mathrm{x}}, r_{\mathrm{y}})\), so it turns a vector by a right angle counterclockwise (middle), and therefore \(\mat J\,\vek r = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\times\vek r\). By the derivatives of sine and cosine (theorem 8.13), \(\mathrm{d}\mat R/\mathrm{d}\varphi = \mat J\,\mat R\). The derivative is the limit of difference quotients. In the left of the figure the quotients \((\mat R(\varphi + h)\,\vek r_0 - \mat R(\varphi)\,\vek r_0)/h\) for \(h = 0{,}8\) and \(0{,}4\) are the chords from the point to its turned positions, stretched by the factor \(1/h\), and as \(h\) gets smaller they turn toward the tangent \(\mat J\,\vek r\) to the circle. By the chain rule, therefore, \(\dot{\vek r} = \dot\varphi\,\mat J\,\vek r\). One more derivative gives \(\ddot{\vek r} = \ddot\varphi\,\mat J\,\vek r + \dot\varphi\,\mat J\,\dot{\vek r} = \ddot\varphi\,\mat J\,\vek r + \dot\varphi^2\,\mat J\,\mat J\,\vek r\), and two turns by a right angle reverse the vector, \(\mat J\,\mat J = -\mat I\) (middle). The position of the point is \(\vek x = \vek x_{\mathrm{G}} + \vek r\), so its acceleration is

\[\vek a = \vek a_{\mathrm{G}} + \ddot\varphi\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\times\vek r - \dot\varphi^2\,\vek r . \tag{8.78}\]

The tangential part \(\ddot\varphi\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\times\vek r\) lies along the circle, the normal part \(-\dot\varphi^2\,\vek r\) points toward \(G\). At \(\dot\varphi = 2\) rad/s and \(\ddot\varphi = 3\) rad/s\(^2\) they are \(3\) and \(4\) m/s\(^2\) in the right of the figure. Both parts are the same as for the pendulum in (8.73).

In figure 8.129 (left) we draw this. The centre of mass has the acceleration \(\vek a_{\mathrm{G}}\), and the body turns about it with the angular acceleration \(\ddot\varphi\) counterclockwise. We draw both orange. The piece at the place \(\vek r\) has by (8.78) an acceleration of three terms, so its inertial force \(-\rho\,\vek a\) has three parts, which we draw blue and dashed. The first, \(-\rho\,\vek a_{\mathrm{G}}\), is the same for all pieces and opposite to the acceleration of the centre of mass. The second, \(-\rho\,\ddot\varphi\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\times\vek r\), lies along the circle about \(G\), points against the angular acceleration and is the larger, the farther the piece is from \(G\). The third, \(\rho\,\dot\varphi^2\,\vek r\), points from \(G\) outward. This is the force that pulls us outward on a merry-go-round. The virtual displacement of the piece in the left panel is made of the displacement of the centre of mass \(\delta\vek r_{\mathrm{G}}\) and the displacement from the rotation \(\delta\varphi\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\times\vek r\), both drawn as bars.

Corollary 8.83 Plane motion of a rigid body (ravninsko gibanje togega telesa)

Let a rigid body (definition 1.11) at the instant \(t\) move in the plane \(xy\) with the angle \(\varphi\), as in theorem 8.82 with \(\mat Q = \mat R(\varphi)\), so with the acceleration from equation (8.78), and let \(M_{\mathrm{G}}\) be the component \(z\) of the moment of all forces on it about the centre of mass \(G\). Then \(J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\ddot\varphi\)\( = M_{\mathrm{G}}\), where \(J_{\mathrm{G}}\) is the moment of inertia about the axis through \(G\) in the direction \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) (definition 8.81). If the body has a point \(O\) at rest (a pin), \(J_{\mathrm{O}}\,\ddot\varphi = M_{\mathrm{O}}\), where \(M_{\mathrm{O}}\) is the moment of the forces about \(O\).

Proof

We must show that in (8.77), for the component \(z\) of the moment, only the term with \(\ddot\varphi\) remains.

  1. We take \(O = G\) in (8.77) and \(\vek r = \vek x - \vek x_{\mathrm{G}}\). By (8.78) the component \(z\) of the vector \(\vek r\times\vek a\) is a sum of three terms. For \(\vek a_{\mathrm{G}}\) the \(z\)-component of \(\vek r \times \vek a_{\mathrm{G}}\) is \(r_{\mathrm{x}} a_{\mathrm{G},\mathrm{y}} - r_{\mathrm{y}} a_{\mathrm{G},\mathrm{x}}\), and after integration with \(\rho\) it gives the component \(z\) of the vector \((\int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\vek r\,\mathrm{d}V)\times\vek a_{\mathrm{G}} = \vek 0\), because the centre of mass is at \(G\). In figure 8.129 these are the moments of the parts \(-\rho\,\vek a_{\mathrm{G}}\), which cancel about the centre of mass. Figure 8.130 (middle) shows it with numbers: the acceleration of the centre of mass gives the heavier piece the force 4 N and the lighter one the force 2 N, both downward. Their moments about the centre of mass are \((-0{,}5)\cdot(-4) = 2\) N m and \(1 \cdot (-2) = -2\) N m, because the smaller force has a lever arm twice as long.
  2. For \(\ddot\varphi\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\times\vek r = \ddot\varphi\,(-r_{\mathrm{y}}, r_{\mathrm{x}}, 0)\) the \(z\)-component is \(r_{\mathrm{x}}\,\ddot\varphi\,r_{\mathrm{x}} - r_{\mathrm{y}}\,(-\ddot\varphi\,r_{\mathrm{y}}) = \ddot\varphi\,(r_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + r_{\mathrm{y}}^2) = \ddot\varphi\,\ell^2\), where \(\ell\) is the distance from the axis through \(G\). This is the moment of the part along the circle: the force is proportional to \(\ell\) and has the arm \(\ell\).
  3. For \(-\dot\varphi^2\,\vek r\) the \(z\)-component is \(-\dot\varphi^2\,(r_{\mathrm{x}} r_{\mathrm{y}} - r_{\mathrm{y}} r_{\mathrm{x}}) = 0\). The part along \(\vek r\) passes through \(G\) and has no arm.
  4. The integral with \(\rho\) gives \(M_{\mathrm{G}} = \ddot\varphi \int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\ell^2\,\mathrm{d}V = J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\ddot\varphi\).
  5. Let \(O\) be a point of the body at rest, like the pin of the bar of example B. Let \(\vek s_0\) be the vector from \(O\) to a point of the body in the position at the time \(0\). The vector from \(O\) to the point turns with the same matrix as the vector from \(G\) (the difference of two vectors \(\mat R\,\vek r_0\) is \(\mat R\) times the difference), as in figure 8.67 the vector \(\vek r_0\) from the point \(O\) of the plate goes over into the vector \(\mat R(\varphi)\,\vek r_0\), so \(\vek x = \vek O + \mat R(\varphi)\,\vek s_0\) with constant \(\vek s_0\), and \(\vek a_{\mathrm{O}} = \vek 0\). For \(\vek r = \vek x - \vek O\) equation (8.78) holds with \(\vek a_{\mathrm{G}}\) replaced by \(\vek a_{\mathrm{O}} = \vek 0\), so the term from step 1 is absent, and steps 2 to 4 give \(M_{\mathrm{O}} = J_{\mathrm{O}}\,\ddot\varphi\). The pin is one of the constraint forces, its moment about \(O\) is zero because its arm is zero, so \(M_{\mathrm{O}}\) is the moment of the remaining forces.

The rigid bar of length 2 m and mass 30 kg (\(J_{\mathrm{G}} = 10\) kg m\(^2\)) is held under its ends by the vertical springs \(k_1 = 20\) kN/m on the left and \(k_2 = 30\) kN/m on the right, which stand on the ground, and a guide excludes horizontal displacement (an idealization from subsection 8.5.1, which carries nothing here, because all forces on the bar are vertical). The displacements of the ends are \(y_1 = y - \sin\varphi\) on the left and \(y_2 = y + \sin\varphi\) on the right, because the ends are 1 m from \(G\) along the bar. By theorem 8.82 and corollary 8.83

\[\begin{aligned} m\,\ddot y &= -k_1\,y_1 - k_2\,y_2 - m g , \\ J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\ddot\varphi &= -\cos\varphi\,(-k_1\,y_1) \\ &\quad + \cos\varphi\,(-k_2\,y_2) , \end{aligned} \tag{8.79}\]

since the vertical force on the left end is \(-k_1 y_1\) at the arm \(-\cos\varphi\) and on the right end \(-k_2 y_2\) at the arm \(+\cos\varphi\) (the moment of a vertical force \(F\) at the arm \(x\) is \(x F\)). From rest at \(y = \varphi = 0\), \(\ddot y = -g = -9{,}81\) m/s\(^2\) and \(\ddot\varphi = 0\): the unstretched springs hold nothing back, and the bar falls like a free body. At \(y = 0\) and \(\varphi = 0{,}001\) rad, \(y_1 = -0{,}001\) m and \(y_2 = 0{,}001\) m, the spring forces are \(+20\) N at the left end and \(-30\) N at the right end, the weight is \(294{,}3\) N, so \(\ddot y = (20 - 30 - 294{,}3)/30 = -10{,}143\) m/s\(^2\) and the moment about \(G\) is \((-1)\cdot 20 + 1\cdot(-30) = -50\) N m, so \(\ddot\varphi = -5{,}000\) rad/s\(^2\). Here we replaced \(\sin 0{,}001\) and \(\cos 0{,}001\) by \(0{,}001\) and \(1\), which by lemma 8.20 brings a relative error of at most \(1{,}7 \cdot 10^{-7}\) and \(5 \cdot 10^{-7}\), far below the digits written. In figure 8.132 (right) this is the inertial force \(-m\,\ddot y = 304{,}3\) N at \(G\) and the moment of the inertial forces \(-J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\ddot\varphi = 50\) N m, both blue and dashed. The virtual displacement of the centre of mass \(\delta y\) and the virtual rotation \(\delta\varphi\) are the two displacements for which the principle gives the equations (8.79). The same moment is given by the linearization with the stiffness matrix \(\mat K\) of example C, \(-(\mat K \vek q)_\varphi = -10\,000\,y - 50\,000\,\varphi = -50\) N m. The factor 30 at \(\ddot y\) and 10 at \(\ddot\varphi\) will be the matrix \(\mat M = \mathrm{diag}(30, 10)\) in sections 8.9 and 8.13.

8.7.5 The working form of the principle for a rigid body in the plane (delovna oblika načela za togo telo v ravnini)

The principle (8.76) contains an integral of the inertial forces over every body. For the weight of the pendulum there is no integral, because the weight is a point. Bar C, however, translates and turns at once: each of its pieces has a different inertial force and a different virtual displacement. We will show that all of this adds up to two numbers, the work of one force at the centre of mass and the work of one moment. In this form the principle is used for computing, so we call it the working form (delovna oblika).

Let us return to figure 8.129. On the left the three parts of the inertial force of the piece are already drawn: \(-\rho\,\vek a_{\mathrm{G}}\), \(-\rho\,\ddot\varphi\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\times\vek r\) and \(\rho\,\dot\varphi^2\,\vek r\). Let us add the virtual displacement. By lemma 4.72 a rigid virtual displacement is a translation and a rotation, and for the chosen point we take the centre of mass. All points of the body move by \(\delta\vek r_{\mathrm{G}}\), as much as the centre of mass moves. In addition the body turns about the centre of mass by \(\delta\varphi\), which gives the piece at the place \(\vek r\) the additional displacement \(\delta\varphi\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\times\vek r\), perpendicular to \(\vek r\) and the larger, the farther the piece is from \(G\). We draw both parts as dimensions with end bars, the second at the end of the first. The work of the inertial force of the piece is the dot product of the three parts of the force with the two parts of the displacement, so six products. The corollary says that after summing over the body two remain (figure 8.129, right): the force \(-m\,\vek a_{\mathrm{G}}\) at the centre of mass of the body works on the displacement of the centre of mass \(\delta\vek r_{\mathrm{G}}\), and the moment \(-J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\ddot\varphi\) works on the rotation \(\delta\varphi\).

Corollary 8.84 Working form of d'Alembert's principle for a plane rigid body (delovna oblika d'Alembertovega načela za togo telo v ravnini)

Let a rigid body of mass \(m\), centre of mass \(G\) and moment of inertia \(J_{\mathrm{G}}\) about the axis through \(G\) in the direction \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) (definition 8.81) at the instant \(t\) move in the plane \(xy\) as in corollary 8.83, with the acceleration of the centre of mass \(\vek a_{\mathrm{G}}\), the angular velocity \(\dot\varphi\) and the angular acceleration \(\ddot\varphi\), so with the acceleration of its points from equation (8.78). Let the density \(\rho\) and the acceleration be continuous on the body.

  1. Let \(\delta\vek u = \delta\vek r_{\mathrm{G}} + \delta\varphi\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\times\vek r\) for \(\vek r = \vek x - \vek x_{\mathrm{G}}\) be a rigid virtual displacement in the plane, with an arbitrary vector \(\delta\vek r_{\mathrm{G}}\) in the plane \(xy\) and an arbitrary number \(\delta\varphi\). Then the virtual work of the inertial forces of the body is
    \[-\int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\vek a\cdot\delta\vek u\,\mathrm{d}V = -m\,\vek a_{\mathrm{G}}\cdot\delta\vek r_{\mathrm{G}} - J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\ddot\varphi\,\delta\varphi . \tag{8.80}\]
  2. If the body has a point \(O\) that stays at rest for all time (a pin, a fixed axis), so that \(\vek a_{\mathrm{O}} = \vek 0\), and the virtual displacement is a rotation about it, \(\delta\vek u = \delta\varphi\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\times\vek s\) for \(\vek s = \vek x - \vek O\), then \(-\int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\vek a\cdot\delta\vek u\,\mathrm{d}V = -J_{\mathrm{O}}\,\ddot\varphi\,\delta\varphi\), where \(J_{\mathrm{O}}\) is the moment of inertia about the axis through \(O\) in the direction \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\).

The vector \(-m\,\vek a_{\mathrm{G}}\) is called the inertial force of the body and the number \(-J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\ddot\varphi\) the moment of the inertial forces (moment vztrajnostnih sil) about the centre of mass.

Proof

We must show that of the six products that arise when the three terms of the acceleration (centre of mass, tangential and normal part) are multiplied by the two terms of the virtual displacement (translation and rotation), two remain after integration over the body.

1. The centre of mass.

  1. By (8.78), \(\vek a = \vek a_{\mathrm{G}} + \ddot\varphi\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\times\vek r - \dot\varphi^2\,\vek r\). The dot product with \(\delta\vek u = \delta\vek r_{\mathrm{G}} + \delta\varphi\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\times\vek r\) has six terms, in figure 8.129 each of the three parts of the force with each of the two parts of the displacement. We multiply each by \(\rho\) and integrate it separately, which we may do by the linearity of the integral (lemma 4.10, point 1).
  2. Translation with translation. The product \(\vek a_{\mathrm{G}}\cdot\delta\vek r_{\mathrm{G}}\) is the same at all points, so \(\int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\vek a_{\mathrm{G}}\cdot\delta\vek r_{\mathrm{G}}\,\mathrm{d}V = \vek a_{\mathrm{G}}\cdot\delta\vek r_{\mathrm{G}} \int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\mathrm{d}V = m\,\vek a_{\mathrm{G}}\cdot\delta\vek r_{\mathrm{G}}\) by the definition of mass. In the figure this is the part \(-\rho\,\vek a_{\mathrm{G}}\) on the displacement \(\delta\vek r_{\mathrm{G}}\).
  3. Rotation with rotation. The vector \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\times\vek r = (-r_{\mathrm{y}}, r_{\mathrm{x}}, 0)\) has the squared length \(r_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + r_{\mathrm{y}}^2 = \ell^2\), where \(\ell\) is the distance of the point from the axis through \(G\). So \((\ddot\varphi\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\times\vek r)\cdot(\delta\varphi\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\times\vek r) = \ddot\varphi\,\delta\varphi\,\ell^2\) and \(\int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\ddot\varphi\,\delta\varphi\,\ell^2\,\mathrm{d}V = J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\ddot\varphi\,\delta\varphi\) by the definition of the moment of inertia. In the figure the force and the displacement both lie along the circle about \(G\) and both are proportional to \(\ell\).
  4. Translation of the acceleration with rotation of the displacement. We rearrange the scalar triple product, \(\vek a_{\mathrm{G}}\cdot(\delta\varphi\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\times\vek r) = \delta\varphi\,\vek r\cdot(\vek a_{\mathrm{G}}\times\vek e_{\mathrm{z}})\), and the vector \(\vek a_{\mathrm{G}}\times\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) does not depend on the point. The integral is \(\delta\varphi\,\big(\int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\vek r\,\mathrm{d}V\big)\cdot(\vek a_{\mathrm{G}}\times\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}) = 0\), because \(\int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\vek r\,\mathrm{d}V = \vek 0\) for vectors from the centre of mass (definition 8.81). In figure 8.130 (middle) the virtual rotation \(\delta\varphi = 0{,}1\) rad moves the heavier piece \(0{,}05\) m down and the lighter one \(0{,}1\) m up, and the forces 4 N and 2 N downward do the work \(+0{,}2\) J and \(-0{,}2\) J.
  5. Rotation of the acceleration with translation of the displacement. In the same way \((\ddot\varphi\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\times\vek r)\cdot\delta\vek r_{\mathrm{G}} = \ddot\varphi\,\vek r\cdot(\delta\vek r_{\mathrm{G}}\times\vek e_{\mathrm{z}})\), and the integral is \(\ddot\varphi\,\big(\int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\vek r\,\mathrm{d}V\big)\cdot(\delta\vek r_{\mathrm{G}}\times\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}) = 0\). In the figure the parts along the circle have opposite directions on opposite sides of the centre of mass. In figure 8.130 (right) the forces along the circle are both 2 N and opposite, so on the common displacement \(\delta\vek r_{\mathrm{G}}\) they do the work \(+0{,}08\) J and \(-0{,}08\) J.
  6. The part toward the centre of mass with translation. The integral of the product \(-\dot\varphi^2\,\vek r\cdot\delta\vek r_{\mathrm{G}}\) is \(-\dot\varphi^2\,\big(\int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\vek r\,\mathrm{d}V\big)\cdot\delta\vek r_{\mathrm{G}} = 0\) for the same reason. In figure 8.130 (right) the forces away from \(G\) are both 1 N and opposite, and on the same displacement they do the work \(-0{,}03\) J and \(+0{,}03\) J.
  7. The part toward the centre of mass with rotation. At every point \(\vek r\cdot(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\times\vek r) = r_{\mathrm{x}}\,(-r_{\mathrm{y}}) + r_{\mathrm{y}}\,r_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\): the force along \(\vek r\) is perpendicular to the displacement along the circle.
  8. The products of steps 2 and 3 remain. With the minus before the integral we obtain (8.80).

2. A point at rest.

  1. By step 5 of the proof of corollary 8.83, for \(\vek s = \vek x - \vek O\) the acceleration is \(\vek a = \ddot\varphi\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\times\vek s - \dot\varphi^2\,\vek s\), because \(\vek a_{\mathrm{O}} = \vek 0\). The virtual displacement has one part only, \(\delta\varphi\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\times\vek s\), as for the bar of example B, which turns about the pin.
  2. There are two products. The first is as in step 3, with the distance \(\ell\) from the axis through \(O\), so its integral is \(J_{\mathrm{O}}\,\ddot\varphi\,\delta\varphi\). The second is zero as in step 7. With the minus we obtain the claim.

Source: SHABANA (3.121) (PDF p. 135) writes the virtual work of the inertial forces of a rigid body as an integral over the body, and on PDF p. 137, for a pendulum, the form \(m\,\ddot R_1\,\delta R_1 + m\,\ddot R_2\,\delta R_2 + I_{\mathrm{c}}\,\ddot\theta\,\delta\theta\), without a derivation. The proof from equation (8.78) is not from the sources.

The corollary says how the inertia of a rigid body enters the virtual work: the inertial force \(-m\,\vek a_{\mathrm{G}}\) works on the displacement of the centre of mass, the moment of the inertial forces \(-J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\ddot\varphi\) on the rotation. Translation goes with translation and rotation with rotation, and there are no mixed terms. In the lectures \(\vek F_{\mathrm{v}} = -m\,\ddot{\vek r}\) is the inertial force and \(M_{\mathrm{v}} = -J\,\ddot\varphi\) the inertial moment, and the number \(J\) is the mass moment of inertia.

Why do the mixed terms drop out? They all contain \(\int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\vek r\,\mathrm{d}V\), which is zero for vectors from the centre of mass: the parts along the circle have opposite directions on opposite sides of the centre of mass, and on the common displacement \(\delta\vek r_{\mathrm{G}}\) their works cancel (figure 8.130, right). This holds only if we measure the translation and the rotation at the centre of mass. If we took the left end \(A\) of bar C for the chosen point, \(\int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\vek r\,\mathrm{d}V = m\,(\vek x_{\mathrm{G}} - \vek x_{\mathrm{A}})\) would have the magnitude \(30 \cdot 1 = 30\) kg m, and the mixed terms would remain. The form is just as short for a point of the body with zero acceleration, for example on a fixed axis (item 2). For the pendulum with a weight \(J_{\mathrm{O}} = m\,l^2\) and the work of the inertial forces is \(-m\,l^2\,\ddot\theta\,\delta\theta\), which in section 8.7.6 we also get directly from the acceleration of the weight. The corollary also assumes plane motion and a rigid body, each with its own limit from sections 8.7.4 and 8.7.3.

The instantaneous centre of rotation (the contact point \(Q\) of a rolling body) is not a point with zero acceleration, so item 2 does not hold for it in general. In figure 8.133, left, a homogeneous cylinder of radius \(r = 0{,}2\) m and mass \(m = 10\) kg rolls on fixed flat ground with the angular velocity \(\dot\varphi = 3\) rad/s and the angular acceleration \(\ddot\varphi = 5\) rad/s\(^2\) counterclockwise. The velocity of the point \(Q\) is zero, its acceleration is not. For the vector from the centre \(C\) to a point of the cylinder, (8.78) holds with \(C\) in place of \(G\), as in step 5 of the proof of corollary 8.83. By the relation \(x = r\varphi\) the centre moves along the ground with the acceleration \(r\,\ddot\varphi = 1\) m/s\(^2\). The point \(Q\) lies on the rim, a radius \(r\) below \(C\), so the angular acceleration gives it an equal and opposite part \(r\,\ddot\varphi\), and only the normal part \(r\,\dot\varphi^2 = 1{,}8\) m/s\(^2\) toward \(C\) remains.

What does this mean for the work? For a virtual rotation \(\delta\varphi\) about \(Q\), steps 9 and 10 of the proof of corollary 8.84 with \(Q\) in place of \(O\) give the term \(-J_{\mathrm{Q}}\,\ddot\varphi\,\delta\varphi\), where \(J_{\mathrm{Q}}\) is the moment of inertia about the axis through \(Q\), and also a term with the acceleration \(\vek a_{\mathrm{Q}}\), which was not there at \(O\). As in step 4 its integral is \(\delta\varphi\,m\,(\vek x_{\mathrm{G}} - \vek x_{\mathrm{Q}})\cdot(\vek a_{\mathrm{Q}}\times\vek e_{\mathrm{z}})\), which is now not zero, because \(Q\) is not the centre of mass. With the minus before the integral this is the moment of the force \(-m\,\vek a_{\mathrm{Q}}\) of 18 N at the centre of mass about \(Q\), multiplied by \(\delta\varphi\). For the homogeneous cylinder the centre of mass is at \(C\), the line of this force passes through \(Q\), its moment is zero, and the formula \(-J_{\mathrm{Q}}\,\ddot\varphi\,\delta\varphi\) gives the right work. In the figure on the right the wheel has the same radius, mass and motion, but its centre of mass \(G\) is \(e = 0{,}05\) m to the right of the centre \(C\). The acceleration of the point \(Q\) is the same as on the left, because it depends only on the rolling. But the force 18 N at \(G\) has the lever arm \(e\) and the moment \(18 \cdot 0{,}05 = 0{,}9\) N m clockwise, so the formula with \(J_{\mathrm{Q}}\) leaves out the work \(-0{,}9\,\delta\varphi\) J. The lever arm \(e\) is the horizontal offset of the centre of mass from \(C\) and changes during rolling, so for such a wheel we use item 1.

The most common mistake in using the principle is that only \(-m\,\vek a_{\mathrm{G}}\cdot\delta\vek r_{\mathrm{G}}\) is left in the virtual work, and the term \(-J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\ddot\varphi\,\delta\varphi\) is missing. In the computation the body then moves as if it could be turned without resistance. For bar C below, the equation for the rotation would demand that the moment of the spring forces be zero, although it is \(-50\) N m. In the example with the cylinder in section 8.7.7 this mistake gives an acceleration of the block that is fourteen times too large.

We test the corollary on bar C at the instant of figure 8.132 (right), at \(y = 0\) and \(\varphi = 0{,}001\) rad. The bar has two degrees of freedom, so there are two virtual displacements: the displacement of the centre of mass \(\delta y\) and the rotation \(\delta\varphi\). The ends are at the horizontal distance \(\mp\cos\varphi\) from \(G\). The left end therefore moves vertically by \(\delta y - \cos\varphi\,\delta\varphi\) and the right one by \(\delta y + \cos\varphi\,\delta\varphi\), which are the changes of the positions \(y_1 = y - \sin\varphi\) and \(y_2 = y + \sin\varphi\) (lemma 8.48). The smooth guide is an ideal constraint: its force on the bar is horizontal, and in \(\delta y\) and \(\delta\varphi\) the centre of mass does not move horizontally, so its work is zero. Here this force is even zero, since the spring forces and the weight are vertical and the centre of mass has no horizontal acceleration, so friction in the guide, which is proportional to this force, would also stay zero. With an inclined spring the guide would press and friction would work on \(\delta y\). Into the principle (8.76) we insert the spring forces, the weight and, by corollary 8.84, the inertia:

\[\begin{aligned} &\underbrace{(-k_1 y_1)\,(\delta y - \cos\varphi\,\delta\varphi)}_{\text{left spring}} + \underbrace{(-k_2 y_2)\,(\delta y + \cos\varphi\,\delta\varphi)}_{\text{right spring}} \; \underbrace{{}- m g\,\delta y}_{\text{weight}} \\ &\quad \underbrace{{}- m\,\ddot y\,\delta y}_{\text{translation}} \; \underbrace{{}- J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\ddot\varphi\,\delta\varphi}_{\text{rotation}} = 0 . \end{aligned} \tag{8.81}\]

The displacements \(\delta y\) and \(\delta\varphi\) are independent. The terms at \(\delta y\) give \(-k_1 y_1 - k_2 y_2 - m g - m\,\ddot y = 0\), and the terms at \(\delta\varphi\) give \(\cos\varphi\,k_1 y_1 - \cos\varphi\,k_2 y_2 - J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\ddot\varphi = 0\). These are equations (8.79). In figure 8.132 we see this with numbers. The force of the left spring \(+20\) N, the force of the right one \(-30\) N, the weight \(294{,}3\) N downward and the inertial force \(-m\,\ddot y = 304{,}3\) N upward give at \(\delta y\) the sum \(20 - 30 - 294{,}3 + 304{,}3 = 0\). The moments of the spring forces \(-20\) and \(-30\) N m and the moment of the inertial forces \(-J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\ddot\varphi = +50\) N m give at \(\delta\varphi\) the sum zero. The two blue arrows are therefore exactly what the forces lack for equilibrium.

From history

The idea of virtual displacements was written down in a letter to Varignon by Johann Bernoulli (TIMO, PDF p. 35), who in it used in 1717 the expression virtual velocities (EP, PDF p. 2). We met the same idea on the lever in chapter 4. The Traité de dynamique of d'Alembert appeared in 1743 (BALL, PDF p. 403, TIMO, PDF p. 43), and in it d'Alembert wrote down the principle that bears his name: the internal forces of inertia, that is, the forces that resist acceleration, are equal and opposite to the forces that produce the acceleration (BALL, PDF p. 403). This is the expression \(\vek F - m\,\vek a = \vek 0\) from the beginning of the section, and with it, according to d'Alembert, the equations of motion of any rigid system can be obtained (BALL, PDF p. 403). Lagrange in his book Mécanique analytique introduced generalized coordinates and generalized forces with d'Alembert's principle and the principle of virtual displacements (TIMO, PDF p. 45). EP says of him that with d'Alembert's principle he converted dynamics to statics and then with the principle of virtual displacements derived the equations of motion with the energies (EP, PDF p. 3). Why this was a step forward is told by BALL. Instead of following the motion of each part of the system separately, as d'Alembert and Euler had done, he showed that it is enough to determine the position of the system with as many variables as it has degrees of freedom, since the kinetic and potential energy are expressed with them and the equations of motion follow by differentiation (BALL, PDF p. 435). There is not a single figure in the book, because the methods do not require geometrical or mechanical considerations, but only algebraic operations in a prescribed order (TIMO, PDF p. 45). It appeared in 1788 (BALL, PDF p. 436, EP, PDF p. 3, GR, PDF p. 43). The principle that we will meet in section 8.10 is according to GR the time-integrated form of the principle of virtual work (GR, PDF p. 41). Hamilton published it in 1834 (GR, PDF p. 41, EP, PDF p. 3).

Let us return to what we looked at before the story: the principle for a system of rigid bodies (8.76) is statics with the added forces \(-\rho\,\vek a\), and the equations of motion of a rigid body (8.77) give from it for plane motion \(m\,\vek a_{\mathrm{G}} = \vek F\) and \(J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\ddot\varphi = M_{\mathrm{G}}\). The working form (8.80) replaces the integral of the inertial forces of a rigid body by a force at the centre of mass and a moment. We test the principle first on the pendulum with a weight, where in the end we must show that the force of the bar drops out, then on a system of two bodies.

8.7.6 Example: the pendulum (zgled: nihalo)

Take the pendulum of example A at the instant from the beginning of the section (figure 8.134).

The point of the weight \(m = 1\) kg on a rigid bar of length \(l = 1\) m, the pin \(O\) at the origin, \(\theta\) from the vertical downward (example A). The weight is a point body, so the inertial force is \(-m\,\vek a\) and there is no integral. The configuration is \(\vek x = l\,(\sin\theta, -\cos\theta)\), there is one degree of freedom, and the compatible virtual displacement for the constraint \(|\vek x| = l\) is a displacement along the circle, \(\delta\vek x = l\,\delta\theta\,(\cos\theta, \sin\theta) = l\,\delta\theta\,\vek e_{\mathrm{t}}\) (figure 8.134). The forces on the weight: the weight force \(\vek W = (0, -m g)\) and the force of the bar \(-N\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\), where \(N\) is the force in the bar (a bar in tension has \(N > 0\)). The acceleration is (8.73). By equation (8.76):

  1. The force of the bar, \(-N\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\), is perpendicular to the displacement \(\delta\vek x\): \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{t}} = \sin\theta\cos\theta - \cos\theta\sin\theta = 0\). Its virtual work is zero, which is the property of an ideal constraint.
  2. The work of the weight force is \(\vek W\cdot\delta\vek x = -m g\,l\,\sin\theta\,\delta\theta\).
  3. The work of the inertial force is \(-m\,\vek a\cdot\delta\vek x = -m\,l\,\delta\theta\,(\vek a\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{t}})\). The normal part of the acceleration, \(-l\,\dot\theta^2\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\), is perpendicular to \(\vek e_{\mathrm{t}}\) and drops out, and with it the normal part of the inertial force. What remains is \(\vek a\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{t}} = l\,\ddot\theta\), so the work of the tangential part is \(-m\,l^2\,\ddot\theta\,\delta\theta\).
  4. The sum is zero for every \(\delta\theta\): \((-m g\,l\sin\theta - m\,l^2\,\ddot\theta)\,\delta\theta = 0\). Since \(\delta\theta\) is arbitrary,
    \[\ddot\theta + \frac gl\,\sin\theta = 0 . \tag{8.82}\]

This is the equation of example A. It agrees with corollary 8.83: \(J_{\mathrm{O}} = m l^2\) and the moment of the weight force about \(O\) in the direction of increasing \(\theta\) is \(-m g\,l\,\sin\theta\). It also agrees with item 2 of corollary 8.84: the work of the inertial forces is \(-J_{\mathrm{O}}\,\ddot\theta\,\delta\theta = -m\,l^2\,\ddot\theta\,\delta\theta\), as in step 3. Numbers: at \(\theta = 30^\circ\) (\(0{,}5236\) rad) and \(\dot\theta = 2\) rad/s, \(\ddot\theta = -9{,}81 \cdot 0{,}5 = -4{,}905\) rad/s\(^2\), the acceleration has the components \(\vek a = (-6{,}248, 1{,}012)\) m/s\(^2\) (from (8.73), SymPy), \(\vek a\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{t}} = -4{,}905\) m/s\(^2\) and \(\vek a\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{r}} = -4{,}000\) m/s\(^2\), and the work of the weight force \(-4{,}905\,\delta\theta\) J and the work of the tangential part of the inertial force, \(4{,}905\) N, \(+4{,}905\,\delta\theta\) J cancel. When released from \(\theta = 90^\circ\) from rest, \(\ddot\theta = -g/l = -9{,}81\) rad/s\(^2\).

Equation (8.82) does not contain the force of the bar. Where did it go? The normal component of Newton's law, the \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\)-component of the equation \(m\,\vek a = \vek W - N\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\), is \(-m\,l\,\dot\theta^2 = m g\cos\theta - N\), so \(N = m\,(g\cos\theta + l\,\dot\theta^2)\), and at \(\theta = 30^\circ\) and \(\dot\theta = 2\) rad/s, \(N = 8{,}496 + 4 = 12{,}50\) N: the bar carries the component of the weight force along the bar, \(8{,}496\) N, and the normal part of the inertial force, \(4{,}000\) N. This component is perpendicular to all admissible virtual displacements, so virtual work does not see it. This is the same as in statics: we do not compute the constraint force, because the equilibrium equation does not contain it along the admissible displacements, and we will get it with a Lagrange multiplier when we write the constraint as an equation (section 8.9).

8.7.7 Example: a cylinder on a block (zgled: valj na kladi)

The pendulum has one body and one degree of freedom. Now take a system of two bodies with two degrees of freedom, for which the working form pays off (figure 8.135).

On the horizontal ground lies the block \(B\) of mass \(m_{\mathrm{B}} = 5\) kg. On the block stands the cylinder \(A\) of mass \(m_{\mathrm{A}} = 10\) kg and radius \(r = 0{,}2\) m. Its moment of inertia about the axis through the centre of mass is \(J_{\mathrm{G}} = 0{,}2\) kg m\(^2\) (this is what a solid homogeneous cylinder has, \(\tfrac12 m_{\mathrm{A}} r^2\), which we take here as given). A light rope is wound on the cylinder and leaves it at the top in the horizontal direction, and we pull its end to the right with the constant force \(F = 120\) N. At the start everything is at rest, and we draw this position grey and dashed. We look for the accelerations of both bodies.

We describe the position with two coordinates. The first is the displacement of the block to the right, \(x\). The second is the rotation of the cylinder \(\varphi\) clockwise, which the orange line on the cylinder shows in the figure: at the start it pointed vertically upward. We measure the angle clockwise, because the cylinder turns that way under a pull to the right. Corollary 8.84 holds for either sense of measuring, if \(\varphi\) and \(\delta\varphi\) are measured in the same sense. The cylinder rolls on the block without slipping. Relative to the block it therefore moves like a wheel on ground at rest (figure 8.5, right): the centre moves by \(r\varphi\) and the top by \(2r\varphi\). The block carries all of it along by \(x\). The centre of the cylinder therefore moves by \(x + r\varphi\), and the end of the rope, which moves like the top of the cylinder, by \(x + 2r\varphi\).

The model has four assumptions. For each we say why we may take it, when it does not hold and what it does to the computation.

  1. The bodies are rigid. A cylinder and a block of steel or wood deform under forces of a few hundred newtons much less than they move. So it is with the steel bar on the crane from section 8.7.3, which stretches by less than \(0{,}03\) mm but moves by \(2{,}45\) m. It fails for a rubber cylinder that visibly flattens at the contact. The assumption erases the internal virtual work (section 8.7.3).
  2. The rope is light and inextensible. The force in it is therefore equal to \(F\) everywhere: a piece of rope without mass needs, by Newton's law, no resultant to accelerate. The force \(F\) therefore acts on the cylinder where the rope leaves it, at the top. For a heavy rope we would have to add its inertia, for a rope that stretches its force as for a spring. A rope of mass 50 g would need \(0{,}05\cdot 32{,}42 = 1{,}6\) N at the acceleration \(32{,}42\) m/s\(^2\) of its end, which is \(1{,}4\) % of the force \(F\).
  3. The cylinder rolls on the block without slipping. The contact points of the cylinder and of the block then have the same displacement, and the forces between them are equal and opposite, \(T\) on the cylinder and \(-T\) on the block. On the same virtual displacement \(\delta u\) they do the work \(T\,\delta u - T\,\delta u = 0\), so this is an ideal constraint (section 8.1.2), although the block moves. It holds as long as the required force \(T\) is smaller than the largest friction force between the cylinder and the block, which we check at the end. If it does not hold, the cylinder slips, and the system gets a third degree of freedom.
  4. The block slides on the ground with friction. By Coulomb's law of sliding friction (section 8.1.2, BOWER, PDF p. 213) the friction force has the magnitude \(\mu N\), where \(N\) is the normal force of the ground and \(\mu\) the coefficient of friction, and points against the direction of sliding. Take \(\mu = 0{,}2\). Friction is not an ideal constraint, so we count it among the applied forces. We must guess the direction of sliding and check it at the end: assume that the block slides to the left, \(\dot x < 0\). If the pull is too small, the block stays at rest, \(x\) is no longer a coordinate, and the system has one degree of freedom. We compute the limit at the end.

The normal force of the ground follows from the vertical direction. Neither body accelerates vertically, so by theorem 8.82 the ground carries the weight of both bodies, \(N = (m_{\mathrm{A}} + m_{\mathrm{B}})\,g = 15 \cdot 9{,}81 = 147{,}15\) N, and the friction force is \(\mu N = 0{,}2 \cdot 147{,}15 = 29{,}43\) N. Now let us draw the forces that do virtual work in the principle (8.76) (figure 8.136, top).

On the block, which lies on the ground, stands the cylinder. The rope leaves it at the top, and at its end pulls the force \(F\). The block slides to the left, so the friction force \(\mu N\) on it points to the right. The weights and the normal force of the ground are vertical and the displacements horizontal, so they do no work, and we do not draw them here. The forces at the contact of the cylinder and the block are an ideal constraint. We add the inertial forces, blue and dashed. The block only translates, so it has a single one, \(-m_{\mathrm{B}}\,\ddot x\). The cylinder translates and turns, so by corollary 8.84 it has two: the force \(-m_{\mathrm{A}}\,a_{\mathrm{A}}\) at the centre of mass, where \(a_{\mathrm{A}} = \ddot x + r\,\ddot\varphi\) is the acceleration of the centre, so the second derivative of the displacement \(x + r\varphi\), and the moment \(-J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\ddot\varphi\). The arrows point in the direction of the computed values: \(\ddot x\) will be negative, \(a_{\mathrm{A}}\) and \(\ddot\varphi\) positive.

There are two virtual displacements, \(\delta x\) and \(\delta\varphi\), and they are independent. The displacements of the points are small changes of the three blue distances of figure 8.135: the block moves by \(\delta x\), the centre of the cylinder by \(\delta x + r\,\delta\varphi\) and the end of the rope by \(\delta x + 2r\,\delta\varphi\) (lemma 8.48). We write the virtual work by bodies and by kind of motion, each term with its name:

\[\begin{aligned} &\underbrace{{}-m_{\mathrm{B}}\,\ddot x\,\delta x}_{\text{block, translation}} \; \underbrace{{}-m_{\mathrm{A}}\,(\ddot x + r\,\ddot\varphi)\,(\delta x + r\,\delta\varphi)}_{\text{cylinder, translation}} \; \underbrace{{}-J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\ddot\varphi\,\delta\varphi}_{\text{cylinder, rotation}} \\ &\quad + \underbrace{F\,(\delta x + 2r\,\delta\varphi)}_{\text{force on the rope}} + \underbrace{\mu N\,\delta x}_{\text{friction of the ground}} = 0 . \end{aligned} \tag{8.83}\]

Each inertial force works on the displacement of its own centre of mass and the moment of the inertial forces on the rotation of its own body. The force \(F\) works on the displacement of the point at which it acts, so of the end of the rope, and not on the displacement of the centre of the cylinder. The friction points to the right, so its work is \(+\mu N\,\delta x\).

The equation holds for all \(\delta x\) and \(\delta\varphi\). We collect the terms at each separately and obtain the equations of motion

\[\begin{aligned} (m_{\mathrm{A}} + m_{\mathrm{B}})\,\ddot x + m_{\mathrm{A}} r\,\ddot\varphi &= F + \mu N , \\ m_{\mathrm{A}} r\,\ddot x + (m_{\mathrm{A}} r^2 + J_{\mathrm{G}})\,\ddot\varphi &= 2 r F . \end{aligned} \tag{8.84}\]

The first is in newtons, the second in newton metres. With numbers, \(15\,\ddot x + 2\,\ddot\varphi = 149{,}43\) and \(2\,\ddot x + 0{,}6\,\ddot\varphi = 48\), and the solution (SymPy) is:

quantity value
acceleration of the block \(\ddot x\) \(-1{,}268\) m/s\(^2\)
angular acceleration of the cylinder \(\ddot\varphi\) \(84{,}23\) rad/s\(^2\)
acceleration of the centre of the cylinder \(a_{\mathrm{A}} = \ddot x + r\,\ddot\varphi\) \(15{,}58\) m/s\(^2\)
acceleration of the end of the rope \(\ddot x + 2r\,\ddot\varphi\) \(32{,}42\) m/s\(^2\)

The block does go to the left, as we assumed, although we pull the rope to the right. Why, the cut below shows.

The force on the rope can also be counted differently (figure 8.137). By theorem 4.73 the virtual work of a force on a rigid body equals the work of the same force moved to a chosen point of the body, plus the work of its moment about this point.

On the left we draw the cylinder with its centre of mass \(G\) and the force \(F\) at the top. In a virtual displacement the top moves by \(\delta x + 2r\,\delta\varphi\), and the force works on this displacement. On the right we draw the same cylinder and move the force \(F\) to the centre of mass, which moves by \(\delta x + r\,\delta\varphi\). The original force lies on the line through the top, at the arm \(r\) above the centre of mass, so we add to the moved force the moment \(F r\) clockwise, which works on the rotation \(\delta\varphi\). Both drawings have the same virtual work:

\[\underbrace{F\,(\delta x + r\,\delta\varphi)}_{\text{force at the centre of mass}} + \underbrace{F r\,\delta\varphi}_{\text{moment}} = F\,(\delta x + 2r\,\delta\varphi) .\]

Both ways give the same. The first looks at the point at which the force acts, the second at the body on which it acts, and in the second the moment is easily forgotten. Without it the force would work only on the displacement of the centre. The second equation would have \(r F = 24\) on the right instead of \(2 r F = 48\), and the solution would be different (figure 8.138). On the axis of the acceleration of the block \(\ddot x\) the correct value \(-1{,}268\) m/s\(^2\) lies left of zero, and the solution without the moment \(\ddot x = +8{,}33\) m/s\(^2\) to the right, so a block that slides in the wrong direction. On the axis of the angular acceleration \(\ddot\varphi\) the same mistake gives \(12{,}23\) rad/s\(^2\) instead of \(84{,}23\) rad/s\(^2\).

The second mistake is the one from section 8.7.5: the term \(-J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\ddot\varphi\,\delta\varphi\) is missing. The second equation then reads \(2\,\ddot x + 0{,}4\,\ddot\varphi = 48\), and the solution is \(\ddot x = -18{,}11\) m/s\(^2\) and \(\ddot\varphi = 210{,}6\) rad/s\(^2\) instead of \(-1{,}268\) m/s\(^2\) and \(84{,}23\) rad/s\(^2\). A cylinder that does not resist turning spins up too easily, and the block gets an acceleration to the left fourteen times as large. Why exactly so much, the cut below shows.

Equations (8.84) do not contain the forces at the contact. Once the accelerations are known, we get them by cutting a body free and writing theorem 8.82 for it (figure 8.136, bottom). On the cylinder cut free act the force of the rope \(120\) N, the weight \(98{,}1\) N and the force of the block at the contact point, which we resolve into the vertical part \(N_{\mathrm{A}}\) and the part \(T\) along the block. Vertically the cylinder does not accelerate, so \(N_{\mathrm{A}} = m_{\mathrm{A}} g = 98{,}1\) N. Horizontally \(m_{\mathrm{A}}\,a_{\mathrm{A}} = F + T\) with the acceleration \(a_{\mathrm{A}}\), so

\[T = m_{\mathrm{A}}\,a_{\mathrm{A}} - F = 10 \cdot 15{,}577 - 120 = 35{,}77\ \mathrm{N} .\]

The force \(T\) is positive: the block pushes the cylinder forward. The cylinder therefore pushes the block backward with an equal force and presses it down with the force \(N_{\mathrm{A}}\). Why the block pushes the cylinder forward, a cylinder on a frictionless block shows. The force \(F\) would give its centre of mass the acceleration \(F/m_{\mathrm{A}} = 12\) m/s\(^2\) forward and its turning the angular acceleration \(F r/J_{\mathrm{G}} = 120\) rad/s\(^2\). The bottom point of the cylinder would therefore accelerate at \(12 - 0{,}2 \cdot 120 = -12\) m/s\(^2\), so backward, and it would slip on the block. The force \(T\) prevents this, and in doing so the cylinder pushes the block away. On the block act in addition the weight \(49{,}05\) N, the normal force of the ground \(N = 147{,}15\) N and the friction \(29{,}43\) N to the right. Horizontally \(m_{\mathrm{B}}\,\ddot x = -T + \mu N = -35{,}77 + 29{,}43 = -6{,}34\) N, so \(\ddot x = -1{,}268\) m/s\(^2\), as above. The turning of the cylinder agrees too: the moment of the forces about the centre of mass clockwise is \((F - T)\,r = (120 - 35{,}77) \cdot 0{,}2 = 16{,}85\) N m, and \(J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\ddot\varphi = 0{,}2 \cdot 84{,}23 = 16{,}85\) N m. In the same way we get the force in a rope when the rope joins two bodies and the force in it is not known in advance: we cut one of the bodies free, and in its equation the force of the rope is the only unknown. Here the other end of the rope is in our hand, and the force in it is given.

The equation of the turning of the cylinder also explains the mistake without the term \(J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\ddot\varphi\,\delta\varphi\) (figure 8.138, bottom). With \(J_{\mathrm{G}} = 0\) the equation \((F - T)\,r = J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\ddot\varphi\) has zero on the right, so \(T = F\). The cylinder would push the block away with the force \(T = 120\) N instead of \(35{,}77\) N, so only \(3{,}4\) times as hard. But from this force in both cases the same friction of the ground \(29{,}43\) N is subtracted, so the resultant on the block grows from \(6{,}34\) N to \(90{,}57\) N, which is \(14{,}3\) times. The correct resultant is a small difference of two forces of similar size, so even a moderate change of the force \(T\) makes it much larger.

It remains to check assumptions 3 and 4. Rolling requires the force \(T = 35{,}77\) N at the normal force \(N_{\mathrm{A}} = 98{,}1\) N, so the ratio \(T/N_{\mathrm{A}} = 0{,}365\). The cylinder rolls without slipping only if the coefficient of friction between the cylinder and the block is at least that large, so this contact must be rougher than the contact of the block with the ground, where \(\mu = 0{,}2\). The block slides if the cylinder pushes it away harder than friction can hold it (figure 8.139).

On the horizontal axis we put the pulling force \(F\), on the vertical one the force in newtons. As long as the block is at rest, the cylinder is a wheel on ground at rest, and the same derivation with \(\ddot x = 0\) gives the force with which the cylinder pushes the block, \(T = F\,(m_{\mathrm{A}} r^2 - J_{\mathrm{G}})/(m_{\mathrm{A}} r^2 + J_{\mathrm{G}}) = F/3\). This line rises with the force \(F\). The ground holds the block with at most \(\mu N = 29{,}43\) N, which is a horizontal line, since the normal force of the ground does not depend on \(F\). The block starts to move when \(F/3 > \mu N\), so at \(F > 3 \cdot 29{,}43 = 88{,}29\) N. Left of this limit the block stays at rest, to the right it slides. Here we take the coefficient of friction at rest to be the same as in sliding, as Coulomb's law with a single coefficient does (BOWER, PDF p. 213). Our force of \(120\) N is above the limit: the block would stay at rest at it only with a friction of \(40\) N, and the ground gives at most \(29{,}43\) N. If the coefficient at rest were larger, the limit would rise, and at \(120\) N the block would stay at rest if this coefficient reached \(40/147{,}15 = 0{,}272\). Below the limit the block stays at rest, and one equation remains, \((m_{\mathrm{A}} r^2 + J_{\mathrm{G}})\,\ddot\varphi = 2 r F\).

For example D, along this route we would need, for each of the two bars and for the slider, the acceleration of the centre of mass and the angular acceleration, expressed with the angle \(\theta\). We therefore write the equation of motion of example D only in section 8.9, where we obtain it from the energy of motion in the coordinate \(\theta\).
