Chapter 2 Stress and equilibrium

2.10 Mathematics: the determinant

In section 2.9 we wrote the stress at a point as a matrix \(\ten\sigma\) whose rows are the traction vectors \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\), \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}})\) and \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}})\), and Cauchy's formula as \(\vek t(\vek n) = \ten\sigma\,\vek n\) ((2.42)). A question from section 2.6.6 is still open: on which face through a point is the normal stress largest? In pure shear we found faces there on which the traction vector points exactly along the normal. The cube was loaded only by forces along its faces, of size \(\tau_0\) per unit area, and the stress matrix had the rows \((0, 0, \tau_0)\), \((0, 0, 0)\) and \((\tau_0, 0, 0)\). On the face perpendicular to the diagonal \(AC\) of the cube, with the normal \(\vek n = (1, 0, 1)/\sqrt2\), we had \(\vek t(\vek n) = \tau_0\,\vek n\), that is, \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \tau_0\,\vek n\). The identity matrix does not change a vector, \(\mat I\,\vek n = \vek n\) (definition 1.105), so we can write this equality as

\[(\ten\sigma - \tau_0\,\mat I)\,\vek n = \vek 0 .\]

So the matrix \(\ten\sigma - \tau_0\,\mat I\) sends the nonzero vector \(\vek n\) to the zero vector. In the next section we will look for such faces for every stress matrix. For this we need a test that tells, from the nine entries of a matrix, whether the matrix sends some nonzero vector to the zero vector.

The same test answers the question from section 1.16 of when a matrix has an inverse matrix. A matrix that sends a nonzero vector to the zero vector has none. If \(\mat B\) were the inverse matrix of \(\mat M = \ten\sigma - \tau_0\,\mat I\), then by item 1 of lemma 1.99 on the properties of the matrix product we would have \(\vek n = \mat I\,\vek n = (\mat B\mat M)\,\vek n = \mat B\,(\mat M\vek n) = \mat B\,\vek 0 = \vek 0\), but we know that \(\vek n\) is not the zero vector. A third question was left open in chapter 1. In section 1.17 we said that for small strains the volume of a small piece of a body changes, relative to itself, by about \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\), and we saved the exact relation for this chapter.

From history

The question of when several equations have a common solution is older than matrices. Already before 1683 Seki Kowa in Japan treated \(n\) equations and knew how to form an expression from their coefficients that must be zero for the equations to have a common solution. He knew that this expression, when expanded, has \(n!\) terms and that rows and columns are interchangeable (CAJORI, PDF p. 96). Why so many terms, we see as follows. Each term is a product of one coefficient from each row and each column of the table of coefficients. For the first row we have \(n\) choices of column, for the second the \(n - 1\) remaining ones, and so on, in all \(n \cdot (n - 1) \cdots 1 = n!\) choices. Rows and columns appear here on equal terms, so they can be interchanged.

On 28 April 1693 Leibniz argued in a letter to the Marquis Guillaume de l'Hôpital that numbers in long calculations can be as useful as letters. For letters do not tell how the quantities they denote are related to each other, whereas numbers do (MUIR, PDF p. 24-25). He showed three equations in two unknowns from which he wanted to eliminate both unknowns by one general rule. He labelled the coefficients with two numbers, the first telling in which equation a coefficient stands and the second beside which unknown (MUIR, PDF p. 24-25). If we treat the number \(1\) as a third unknown, these are three equations in three unknowns with zero right-hand side. His question is therefore our question of when a \(3 \times 3\) matrix sends the nonzero vector \((1, x, y)\) to the zero vector. In the elimination he noticed a regularity and derived from it a rule for which products of coefficients appear in the result and with what sign. The rule held for any number of equations and unknowns (MUIR, PDF p. 26). The letter was published only in 1850 and did not influence later development (MUIR, PDF p. 27).

Gabriel Cramer came to a similar rule in 1750 from a geometric question. A curve of the second degree (krivulja druge stopnje) is a curve whose equation contains, besides the coordinates themselves, also their squares and the product \(xy\), like an ellipse. Such an equation has six coefficients, but it does not change if we multiply it by the same number, so only five are independent. Each point through which the curve passes gives one equation for these coefficients, so the curve is determined by five points (MUIR, PDF p. 30). Because the calculation is long, he published in an appendix to his book on algebraic curves a general rule for solving \(n\) equations in \(n\) unknowns (MUIR, PDF p. 29-31). His rule spread and was taught in schools for many years, while Leibniz's stayed hidden (MUIR, PDF p. 32).

On 12 January 1771 Alexandre-Théophile Vandermonde read to the Paris Academy a memoir on elimination (MUIR, PDF p. 35). He labelled the coefficients by their place in the table, as Leibniz did, and wrote that the sign of the expression reverses if two indices in it are interchanged (MUIR, PDF p. 40). He was the first to give a connected and logical exposition of the theory of these expressions (CAJORI, PDF p. 282, MUIR, PDF p. 42). In 1772 Pierre-Simon Laplace called the expression that arises in elimination a resultant (resultanta) (MUIR, PDF p. 42 and 43). Thus arose an expression in the coefficients of equations, built by a definite rule, which must be zero for the equations to have a common solution.

All three questions, that is whether a matrix sends a nonzero vector to the zero vector, whether it has an inverse matrix and how the volume of a small piece of a body changes, are answered by one number computed from the nine entries of a matrix. We shall reach this number geometrically. A matrix sends the cube with the edges \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) to a body whose faces are parallelograms, and this number is the volume of that body, equipped with a sign. If it is zero, the body is flattened into a plane, and then the matrix sends some direction to zero. The route is this. First we compute the volume of such a body with the scalar and the vector product. Then we define this number for every matrix, prove its rules of calculation and the rule for getting it for the product of two matrices. With them we show when a matrix sends a nonzero vector to zero and when it has an inverse matrix. Finally we compute how the volume of a small piece of a body changes.

In this section all vectors are written with components in the same right-handed rectangular Cartesian coordinate system, of the kind we needed for the vector product in section 2.8. The matrices are \(3 \times 3\), in a few places \(2 \times 2\), and we label their rows and columns with \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\), as for the components of vectors. When we multiply a matrix by a vector, we write the vector as a column.

2.10.1 The volume of a parallelepiped

Let us begin in the plane. In section 2.8 we saw that the length of the vector product \(|\vek b\times\vek c|\) is the area of the parallelogram spanned by the vectors \(\vek b\) and \(\vek c\) drawn from the same point. We computed the vector product with components,

\[\vek b\times\vek c = (b_{\mathrm{y}} c_{\mathrm{z}} - b_{\mathrm{z}} c_{\mathrm{y}},\ b_{\mathrm{z}} c_{\mathrm{x}} - b_{\mathrm{x}} c_{\mathrm{z}},\ b_{\mathrm{x}} c_{\mathrm{y}} - b_{\mathrm{y}} c_{\mathrm{x}}) .\]

Now let us add a third vector. The vectors \(\vek a\), \(\vek b\) and \(\vek c\), drawn from the same point \(P\), span a parallelepiped (paralelepiped). It is the body made of all points \(P + s\,\vek a + t\,\vek b + w\,\vek c\) for which the numbers \(s\), \(t\) and \(w\) lie between \(0\) and \(1\) (figure 2.55). Its faces are six parallelograms, in pairs of parallel ones. The parallelogram of the vectors \(\vek b\) and \(\vek c\), the face at \(s = 0\), is called the base (osnovna ploskev). The opposite face at \(s = 1\) is the base moved by the vector \(\vek a\). The box of section 2.7 is a parallelepiped with mutually perpendicular edges. The sheared cube of section 1.6.2 also became a parallelepiped after the push, since each of its faces became a parallelogram.

We know its volume from school. It is the area of the base times the height, and the height is the distance between the plane of the base and the plane of the opposite face, measured perpendicular to the base. From school geometry we also take as given that a vector perpendicular to the normal of a plane is parallel to the plane: whoever moves along it does not move away from the plane. In the same way, in lemma 2.22 on the faces and volume of the tetrahedron, we took the formula for the volume of a pyramid from school.

How do we compute the height with vectors? We need a direction perpendicular to the base. The vector product \(\vek b\times\vek c\) is perpendicular to \(\vek b\) and to \(\vek c\) (item 4 of lemma 2.31 on the properties of the vector product). So it is also perpendicular to every vector \(t\,\vek b + w\,\vek c\) between two points of the base, since by the properties of the scalar product (lemma 2.12)

\[(\vek b\times\vek c)\cdot(t\,\vek b + w\,\vek c) = t\,(\vek b\times\vek c)\cdot\vek b + w\,(\vek b\times\vek c)\cdot\vek c = t \cdot 0 + w \cdot 0 = 0 .\]

If \(\vek b\times\vek c\) is not the zero vector, then \(\vek n = (\vek b\times\vek c)/|\vek b\times\vek c|\) is the unit vector in its direction (lemma 1.19), so it is the unit normal of the base. We split the edge \(\vek a\) into a part in the direction of \(\vek n\) and a part perpendicular to \(\vek n\) (lemma 2.14 on splitting a vector):

\[\vek a = (\vek a\cdot\vek n)\,\vek n + \big(\vek a - (\vek a\cdot\vek n)\,\vek n\big) .\]

In figure 2.55 the first part is the blue vertical arrow and the second the blue dashed arrow. The second part is perpendicular to the normal, so it is parallel to the base, and when we move along it we do not move away from the plane of the base. We move away from it only by the first part. So the height is its length, \(|\vek a\cdot\vek n|\), the absolute value of the projection of the edge \(\vek a\) onto the normal (definition 2.13). We take the absolute value because the projection is negative when \(\vek a\) points to the other side of the base than \(\vek n\). The area of the base times the height is

\[|\vek b\times\vek c|\,\Big|\vek a\cdot\frac{\vek b\times\vek c}{|\vek b\times\vek c|}\Big| = |\vek b\times\vek c|\,\frac{|\vek a\cdot(\vek b\times\vek c)|}{|\vek b\times\vek c|} = |\vek a\cdot(\vek b\times\vek c)| ,\]

where we took out the positive number \(1/|\vek b\times\vek c|\) by item 3 of the properties of the scalar product. The length of the normal cancels, and what remains is a number computed from the components of all three vectors with both products. The number gets a name.

Definition 2.46 Scalar triple product (mešani produkt)

The scalar triple product (mešani produkt) of the vectors \(\vek a\), \(\vek b\) and \(\vek c\), written in a chosen right-handed rectangular Cartesian coordinate system, is the number \(\vek a\cdot(\vek b\times\vek c)\).

Source: KREYSZIG §9.3 (10*) (PDF p. 399), CM §2.2.2.3 (PDF p. 45).

The name says that it involves three vectors and gives a number, a scalar, because the last product taken is the scalar product. KREYSZIG also calls it the mixed product, since both products appear in it, and writes it \((\vek a\ \vek b\ \vek c)\), while CM writes \([\mathbf{ABC}]\) (KREYSZIG §9.3 (10), PDF p. 399, CM (2.2.20), PDF p. 45). We will simply write it with the dot and the cross. Let us state the computation above as a claim.

Lemma 2.47 Volume of a parallelepiped (prostornina paralelepipeda)

The volume of the parallelepiped spanned by the vectors \(\vek a\), \(\vek b\) and \(\vek c\) is \(|\vek a\cdot(\vek b\times\vek c)|\).

Proof

We compute the volume as the area of the base times the height and express both numbers with the two products.

  1. From school we take that the volume of a parallelepiped is the area of the base times the height, the distance between the plane of the base and the plane of the opposite face. The base is the parallelogram of the vectors \(\vek b\) and \(\vek c\). Its area is \(|\vek b\times\vek c|\) (section 2.8).
  2. If \(\vek b\times\vek c = \vek 0\), the area of the base is zero, the parallelepiped is flattened, and its volume is zero. Also \(\vek a\cdot\vek 0 = 0\), so the claim holds.
  3. Let \(\vek b\times\vek c \neq \vek 0\). The vector \(\vek b\times\vek c\) is perpendicular to \(\vek b\) and \(\vek c\) (item 4 of lemma 2.31 on the properties of the vector product), so \(\vek n = (\vek b\times\vek c)/|\vek b\times\vek c|\) is a unit vector perpendicular to the base (lemma 1.19).
  4. By lemma 2.14 on splitting a vector, \(\vek a = (\vek a\cdot\vek n)\,\vek n + \vek r\), where \(\vek r\) is perpendicular to \(\vek n\). From school we take that a vector perpendicular to the normal of a plane is parallel to the plane. When we move from \(P\) by \(\vek a\), we therefore move away from the plane of the base only by the length of the part \((\vek a\cdot\vek n)\,\vek n\). The height is \(|\vek a\cdot\vek n|\).
  5. By item 3 of the properties of the scalar product (lemma 2.12), \(\vek a\cdot\vek n = \vek a\cdot(\vek b\times\vek c)/|\vek b\times\vek c|\).
  6. The volume is \(|\vek b\times\vek c| \cdot |\vek a\cdot(\vek b\times\vek c)|/|\vek b\times\vek c| = |\vek a\cdot(\vek b\times\vek c)|\).

Source: KREYSZIG §9.3, Theorem 2(b) (PDF pp. 399-400), which proves the claim with the same idea, as the height \(|\vek a|\,|\cos\gamma|\) times the area of the base, where \(\gamma\) is the angle between \(\vek a\) and \(\vek b\times\vek c\). CM §2.2.2.3, Fig. 2.2.5 (PDF p. 45), states the claim with a figure. The steps with the projection onto the normal are not from the sources.

Let us test it on the parallelepiped of figure 2.55, with the edges \(\vek a = (1, 1, 2)\), \(\vek b = (2, 0, 0)\) and \(\vek c = (1, 3, 0)\). First,

\[\vek b\times\vek c = (0 \cdot 0 - 0 \cdot 3,\ 0 \cdot 1 - 2 \cdot 0,\ 2 \cdot 3 - 0 \cdot 1) = (0, 0, 6) ,\]

and then \(\vek a\cdot(\vek b\times\vek c) = 1 \cdot 0 + 1 \cdot 0 + 2 \cdot 6 = 12\). Let us check it the school way. The vectors \(\vek b\) and \(\vek c\) have the component \(z\) equal to zero, so the base lies in the plane \(z = 0\). It is a parallelogram with the base side \(2\) along the axis \(x\) and the height \(3\) in the direction of the axis \(y\), so its area is \(2 \cdot 3 = 6\), which is the length of the vector \((0, 0, 6)\). The normal is \(\vek n = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), and the height of the parallelepiped is \(\vek a\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = 2\), the component \(z\) of the edge \(\vek a\). The volume is \(6 \cdot 2 = 12\). The components \(x\) and \(y\) of the edge \(\vek a\) do not count here. The edge \(\vek a = (5, -2, 2)\) would tilt the parallelepiped differently, but the volume would stay \(12\), because the base and the height stay the same.

The scalar triple product tells us one more thing that the volume loses, a sign. Since \(\vek a\cdot(\vek b\times\vek c) = |\vek b\times\vek c|\,(\vek a\cdot\vek n)\), it has the sign of the projection \(\vek a\cdot\vek n\). It is positive when the edge \(\vek a\) points to the same side of the base as the vector \(\vek b\times\vek c\), and negative when it points to the other side. This is exactly what we need for the test promised in section 2.8. There, following KREYSZIG, we called a triple of vectors right-handed when, in this order, it points like the thumb, the index finger and the middle finger of the right hand. The right-hand rule describes a position in space and does not calculate with components, so we cannot prove it with numbers, but we can check it with numbers. For the unit vectors of the axes, \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\times\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = (1 \cdot 1 - 0 \cdot 0,\ 0 \cdot 0 - 0 \cdot 1,\ 0 \cdot 0 - 1 \cdot 0) = (1, 0, 0) = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), so

\[\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\cdot(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\times\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}) = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = 1 > 0 ,\]

and the triple \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) is right-handed, because our coordinate system is. The order of the vectors counts. Since \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = (0 \cdot 1 - 0 \cdot 0,\ 0 \cdot 0 - 1 \cdot 1,\ 1 \cdot 0 - 0 \cdot 0) = (0, -1, 0)\), we get \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\cdot(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}) = -1 < 0\), and the triple \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) points like the thumb, the index finger and the middle finger of the left hand. CM therefore takes the sign of the scalar triple product as the test: it calls a triple whose scalar triple product is greater than zero positively oriented (CM, PDF p. 126). We do the same and replace the right-hand rule by the sign of the scalar triple product.

Definition 2.48 Orientation of a triple (orientacija trojice)

A triple of vectors \(\vek a\), \(\vek b\), \(\vek c\) in this order, written in a chosen right-handed rectangular Cartesian coordinate system, is right-handed (desnosučna) when its scalar triple product is positive, \(\vek a\cdot(\vek b\times\vek c) > 0\), and left-handed (levosučna) when it is negative, \(\vek a\cdot(\vek b\times\vek c) < 0\). Whether a triple is right-handed or left-handed is called its orientation (orientacija). A triple whose scalar triple product is zero, whose parallelepiped is flattened, has no orientation.

Source: CM (PDF p. 126), KREYSZIG §9.3 (PDF p. 395). KREYSZIG describes a right-handed triple by the right-hand rule, and CM calls a triple with a positive scalar triple product positively oriented.

A left-handed triple looks like the mirror image of a right-handed one, like a left hand in a mirror held in front of a right hand (figure 2.56). Let us check this on the unit vectors of the axes. A mirror in the plane of the axes \(y\) and \(z\) changes the sign of the components \(x\) and leaves the other components. The triple \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) is mirrored into the triple \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), and \((-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\cdot(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\times\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}) = -\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = -1\). The mirror image of a right-handed triple is left-handed.

Now we can write with numbers the right-hand rule for the vector product, which in section 2.8 we checked only for the unit vectors of the axes, and prove it for any two vectors whose vector product is not the zero vector. The statement that the vectors \(\vek a\), \(\vek b\) and \(\vek a\times\vek b\), in this order, point like the thumb, the index finger and the middle finger of the right hand means, by definition 2.48 of the orientation of a triple, that the scalar triple product of this triple is positive.

Lemma 2.49 Direction of the vector product (smer vektorskega produkta)

Let \(\vek a\) and \(\vek b\) be vectors written in a chosen right-handed rectangular Cartesian coordinate system, and let \(\vek a\times\vek b \neq \vek 0\). Then the triple \(\vek a\), \(\vek b\), \(\vek a\times\vek b\) is right-handed, since

\[\vek a\cdot\big(\vek b\times(\vek a\times\vek b)\big) = |\vek a\times\vek b|^2 > 0 . \tag{2.50}\]
Proof

We must show that the scalar triple product of the triple equals \(|\vek a\times\vek b|^2\). First we show with components that the scalar triple product does not change when we move the last vector to the first place.

  1. Let \(\vek u\), \(\vek v\) and \(\vek w\) be any vectors. By (2.32) and (2.6),
    \[\vek u\cdot(\vek v\times\vek w) = u_{\mathrm{x}} (v_{\mathrm{y}} w_{\mathrm{z}} - v_{\mathrm{z}} w_{\mathrm{y}}) + u_{\mathrm{y}} (v_{\mathrm{z}} w_{\mathrm{x}} - v_{\mathrm{x}} w_{\mathrm{z}}) + u_{\mathrm{z}} (v_{\mathrm{x}} w_{\mathrm{y}} - v_{\mathrm{y}} w_{\mathrm{x}}) .\]
    Removing the brackets gives six terms, \(u_{\mathrm{x}} v_{\mathrm{y}} w_{\mathrm{z}}\), \(-u_{\mathrm{x}} v_{\mathrm{z}} w_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}} v_{\mathrm{z}} w_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(-u_{\mathrm{y}} v_{\mathrm{x}} w_{\mathrm{z}}\), \(u_{\mathrm{z}} v_{\mathrm{x}} w_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(-u_{\mathrm{z}} v_{\mathrm{y}} w_{\mathrm{x}}\).
  2. In the same way,
    \[\vek w\cdot(\vek u\times\vek v) = w_{\mathrm{x}} (u_{\mathrm{y}} v_{\mathrm{z}} - u_{\mathrm{z}} v_{\mathrm{y}}) + w_{\mathrm{y}} (u_{\mathrm{z}} v_{\mathrm{x}} - u_{\mathrm{x}} v_{\mathrm{z}}) + w_{\mathrm{z}} (u_{\mathrm{x}} v_{\mathrm{y}} - u_{\mathrm{y}} v_{\mathrm{x}}) ,\]
    which is the six terms \(w_{\mathrm{x}} u_{\mathrm{y}} v_{\mathrm{z}}\), \(-w_{\mathrm{x}} u_{\mathrm{z}} v_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(w_{\mathrm{y}} u_{\mathrm{z}} v_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(-w_{\mathrm{y}} u_{\mathrm{x}} v_{\mathrm{z}}\), \(w_{\mathrm{z}} u_{\mathrm{x}} v_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(-w_{\mathrm{z}} u_{\mathrm{y}} v_{\mathrm{x}}\).
  3. In a product of numbers the order of the factors does not matter. So the term \(w_{\mathrm{z}} u_{\mathrm{x}} v_{\mathrm{y}}\) of step 2 is the term \(u_{\mathrm{x}} v_{\mathrm{y}} w_{\mathrm{z}}\) of step 1, the term \(-w_{\mathrm{y}} u_{\mathrm{x}} v_{\mathrm{z}}\) is \(-u_{\mathrm{x}} v_{\mathrm{z}} w_{\mathrm{y}}\), the term \(w_{\mathrm{x}} u_{\mathrm{y}} v_{\mathrm{z}}\) is \(u_{\mathrm{y}} v_{\mathrm{z}} w_{\mathrm{x}}\), the term \(-w_{\mathrm{z}} u_{\mathrm{y}} v_{\mathrm{x}}\) is \(-u_{\mathrm{y}} v_{\mathrm{x}} w_{\mathrm{z}}\), the term \(w_{\mathrm{y}} u_{\mathrm{z}} v_{\mathrm{x}}\) is \(u_{\mathrm{z}} v_{\mathrm{x}} w_{\mathrm{y}}\), and the term \(-w_{\mathrm{x}} u_{\mathrm{z}} v_{\mathrm{y}}\) is \(-u_{\mathrm{z}} v_{\mathrm{y}} w_{\mathrm{x}}\). Both sums have the same terms with the same signs, so \(\vek u\cdot(\vek v\times\vek w) = \vek w\cdot(\vek u\times\vek v)\).
  4. We insert \(\vek u = \vek a\), \(\vek v = \vek b\) and \(\vek w = \vek a\times\vek b\). By step 3, \(\vek a\cdot\big(\vek b\times(\vek a\times\vek b)\big) = (\vek a\times\vek b)\cdot(\vek a\times\vek b)\).
  5. By item 4 of the properties of the scalar product (lemma 2.12) this number is \(|\vek a\times\vek b|^2\). It is positive, since \(\vek a\times\vek b\) is not the zero vector. By definition 2.48 of orientation the triple \(\vek a\), \(\vek b\), \(\vek a\times\vek b\) is therefore a right-handed triple.

Source: KREYSZIG §9.3 (PDF p. 394) and CM (PDF p. 43) take the fact that the triple \(\vek a\), \(\vek b\), \(\vek a\times\vek b\) points like the right hand as part of the definition of the vector product. The proof is not from the sources.

Let us test it on the fly's flight \(\vek a = (1, 2, -2)\) and the vector \(\vek b = (2, -1, 0)\) from section 2.8.1, for which \(\vek a\times\vek b = (-2, -4, -5)\). By (2.32),

\[\begin{aligned} \vek b\times(\vek a\times\vek b) &= (2, -1, 0)\times(-2, -4, -5) \\ &= \big((-1)(-5) - 0 \cdot (-4),\ \ 0 \cdot (-2) - 2 \cdot (-5),\ \ 2 \cdot (-4) - (-1)(-2)\big) \\ &= (5, 10, -10) , \end{aligned}\]

and the scalar triple product is \(1 \cdot 5 + 2 \cdot 10 + (-2)(-10) = 45\), which is \(|\vek a\times\vek b|^2 = 4 + 16 + 25\). So the triple is right-handed. The right-hand rule, which the sources take as the definition of the direction, is therefore a lemma for our formula, proved with components. We will need the exchange of order from steps 1 to 3 of the proof, which holds for all vectors \(\vek u\), \(\vek v\) and \(\vek w\), in the next section too.

2.10.2 The determinant of a matrix

Now to matrices. What does a \(3 \times 3\) matrix \(\mat A\) do with the unit cube (enotska kocka), the cube with the edges \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) at the origin? By (1.38) the component \(i\) of the vector \(\mat A\vek v\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} A_{\mathrm{ij}} v_{\mathrm{j}}\), where the indices \(i\) and \(j\) run over \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\). For \(\vek v = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = (1, 0, 0)\) all factors \(v_{\mathrm{j}}\) in the sum are zero except \(v_{\mathrm{x}} = 1\), so only the term with \(j = x\) remains:

\[(\mat A\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})_{\mathrm{i}} = A_{\mathrm{ix}} .\]

So the components of the vector \(\mat A\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) are \(A_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(A_{\mathrm{yx}}\) and \(A_{\mathrm{zx}}\), the entries of the column \(x\) of the matrix. In the same way \(\mat A\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) is the column \(y\) and \(\mat A\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) the column \(z\). We call the columns \(\vek a\), \(\vek b\) and \(\vek c\). A point of the cube \(s\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} + t\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} + w\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) with the numbers \(s\), \(t\), \(w\) between \(0\) and \(1\) goes, by the linearity of the product with a matrix (lemma 1.97), to the point

\[\mat A\,(s\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} + t\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} + w\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}) = s\,\mat A\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} + t\,\mat A\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} + w\,\mat A\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = s\,\vek a + t\,\vek b + w\,\vek c .\]

So the matrix sends the unit cube, which has the volume \(1\), to the parallelepiped spanned by its columns (figure 2.57). The scalar triple product of the columns tells how many times larger the volume of this parallelepiped is than that of the cube, and whether the triple \(\vek a\), \(\vek b\), \(\vek c\) has the same orientation as \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\).

Definition 2.50 Determinant (determinanta)

Let \(\mat A\) be a \(3 \times 3\) matrix with the columns \(\vek a = (A_{\mathrm{xx}}, A_{\mathrm{yx}}, A_{\mathrm{zx}})\), \(\vek b = (A_{\mathrm{xy}}, A_{\mathrm{yy}}, A_{\mathrm{zy}})\) and \(\vek c = (A_{\mathrm{xz}}, A_{\mathrm{yz}}, A_{\mathrm{zz}})\). The determinant (determinanta) of the matrix \(\mat A\) is the number \(\det\mat A = \vek a\cdot(\vek b\times\vek c)\). With the entries of the matrix it reads

\[\begin{aligned} \det\mat A &= A_{\mathrm{xx}} A_{\mathrm{yy}} A_{\mathrm{zz}} + A_{\mathrm{xy}} A_{\mathrm{yz}} A_{\mathrm{zx}} + A_{\mathrm{xz}} A_{\mathrm{yx}} A_{\mathrm{zy}} \\ &\quad - A_{\mathrm{xx}} A_{\mathrm{yz}} A_{\mathrm{zy}} - A_{\mathrm{xy}} A_{\mathrm{yx}} A_{\mathrm{zz}} - A_{\mathrm{xz}} A_{\mathrm{yy}} A_{\mathrm{zx}} . \end{aligned} \tag{2.51}\]

The determinant of a \(2 \times 2\) matrix with the entries \(A_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(A_{\mathrm{xy}}\), \(A_{\mathrm{yx}}\) and \(A_{\mathrm{yy}}\) is the number \(\det\mat A = A_{\mathrm{xx}} A_{\mathrm{yy}} - A_{\mathrm{xy}} A_{\mathrm{yx}}\).

Source: CM (2.3.9) (PDF p. 66), KREYSZIG §9.3 (10) (PDF p. 399) and §7.6 (1), (4) (PDF pp. 317-318).

We get the formula (2.51) from the scalar triple product of the columns in three steps.

  1. Into the formula for the vector product we insert the components of the columns \(\vek b\) and \(\vek c\):
    \[\vek b\times\vek c = \big(A_{\mathrm{yy}} A_{\mathrm{zz}} - A_{\mathrm{zy}} A_{\mathrm{yz}},\ A_{\mathrm{zy}} A_{\mathrm{xz}} - A_{\mathrm{xy}} A_{\mathrm{zz}},\ A_{\mathrm{xy}} A_{\mathrm{yz}} - A_{\mathrm{yy}} A_{\mathrm{xz}}\big) .\]
  2. We compute the scalar product with the column \(\vek a = (A_{\mathrm{xx}}, A_{\mathrm{yx}}, A_{\mathrm{zx}})\) by (2.6):
    \[\begin{aligned} \det\mat A &= A_{\mathrm{xx}}\,(A_{\mathrm{yy}} A_{\mathrm{zz}} - A_{\mathrm{zy}} A_{\mathrm{yz}}) \\ &\quad + A_{\mathrm{yx}}\,(A_{\mathrm{zy}} A_{\mathrm{xz}} - A_{\mathrm{xy}} A_{\mathrm{zz}}) \\ &\quad + A_{\mathrm{zx}}\,(A_{\mathrm{xy}} A_{\mathrm{yz}} - A_{\mathrm{yy}} A_{\mathrm{xz}}) . \end{aligned} \tag{2.52}\]
  3. We remove the brackets and rearrange the six terms so that the three with a plus come first. In each term we order the factors by rows, first the entry from the row \(x\), then from \(y\) and last from \(z\). We get (2.51).

There is a pattern in the formula worth noticing. Each of the six terms is a product of three entries, one from each row and one from each column. There are exactly six such choices: the entry from the row \(x\) can be taken from three columns, the entry from the row \(y\) from the two remaining ones, and the entry from the row \(z\) from the last one left, and \(3 \cdot 2 \cdot 1 = 6\). Three choices come with a plus, three with a minus. A term with a plus is for example \(A_{\mathrm{xx}} A_{\mathrm{yy}} A_{\mathrm{zz}}\), the product of the entries on the main diagonal. If in each term we write down in order the columns of the entries from the rows \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\), the terms with a plus give the sequences \(xyz\), \(yzx\) and \(zxy\), which follow the cycle \(x \to y \to z \to x\) and only start at another letter, and the terms with a minus give \(xzy\), \(yxz\) and \(zyx\), in which two letters of the sequence \(xyz\) are exchanged. That exchanging two columns reverses the sign of the determinant, we will prove in the lemma below. The formula (2.52), called the expansion by the first column (razvoj po prvem stolpcu), tells how the determinant is easiest to compute: each entry of the first column is multiplied by the determinant of the \(2 \times 2\) matrix that remains when we cross out its row and its column, and the signs alternate, \(+\), \(-\), \(+\) (KREYSZIG §7.6 (4), PDF p. 318). For the entry \(A_{\mathrm{yx}}\) this determinant is \(A_{\mathrm{xy}} A_{\mathrm{zz}} - A_{\mathrm{xz}} A_{\mathrm{zy}}\), and the bracket in (2.52) is its negative.

Let us test it on the matrix of figure 2.57. Its columns are the edges of the parallelepiped of figure 2.55:

\[\mat A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 3 \\ 2 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} .\]

By (2.51),

\[\det\mat A = 1 \cdot 0 \cdot 0 + 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 2 + 1 \cdot 1 \cdot 0 - 1 \cdot 3 \cdot 0 - 2 \cdot 1 \cdot 0 - 1 \cdot 0 \cdot 2 = 12 ,\]

as we computed above with the scalar triple product. The matrix sends the unit cube to a parallelepiped with twelve times the volume, and the triple of its columns is right-handed. For the identity matrix the columns are \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), and \(\det\mat I = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\cdot(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\times\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}) = 1\). The identity matrix does not change the cube.

And the determinant of a \(2 \times 2\) matrix? Such a matrix sends vectors of the plane of the axes \(x\) and \(y\) to vectors of the same plane. Let us put it into a \(3 \times 3\) matrix that has zeros in the row \(z\) and the column \(z\), except for the number \(1\) at the place \(zz\). By (2.51) this larger matrix has the determinant

\[\begin{aligned} &A_{\mathrm{xx}} A_{\mathrm{yy}} \cdot 1 + A_{\mathrm{xy}} \cdot 0 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot A_{\mathrm{yx}} \cdot 0 \\ &\quad - A_{\mathrm{xx}} \cdot 0 \cdot 0 - A_{\mathrm{xy}} A_{\mathrm{yx}} \cdot 1 - 0 \cdot A_{\mathrm{yy}} \cdot 0 = A_{\mathrm{xx}} A_{\mathrm{yy}} - A_{\mathrm{xy}} A_{\mathrm{yx}} , \end{aligned}\]

which is exactly the determinant of the \(2 \times 2\) matrix. The columns \((A_{\mathrm{xx}}, A_{\mathrm{yx}}, 0)\) and \((A_{\mathrm{xy}}, A_{\mathrm{yy}}, 0)\) of the larger matrix lie in the plane \(z = 0\), and the third column is \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\). So the parallelepiped has the parallelogram of the two columns in the plane as its base and the height \(1\). The absolute value of the determinant of a \(2 \times 2\) matrix is therefore the area of the parallelogram spanned by its two columns (figure 2.58). We got the same number in section 2.8 as the length of the vector product of two vectors in the plane of the axes \(x\) and \(y\). The matrix with the columns \((3, 1)\) and \((1, 2)\) has the determinant \(3 \cdot 2 - 1 \cdot 1 = 5\), and it sends the unit square with the area \(1\) to a parallelogram with the area \(5\).

The determinant exists for every square matrix, that is, for every matrix with as many rows as columns, of size \(n \times n\). CM and KREYSZIG define it by an expansion by a row or a column, which expresses a determinant of size \(n\) by determinants of size \(n - 1\) (CM (2.3.7), PDF p. 65, KREYSZIG §7.7 (3a), PDF p. 320). For \(n = 3\) this definition gives the formula (2.51), for \(n = 2\) our formula for the \(2 \times 2\) matrix. The absolute value of the determinant is an area for \(n = 2\), a volume for \(n = 3\), and for larger \(n\) a volume in a space with \(n\) dimensions. We will need only \(2 \times 2\) and \(3 \times 3\) matrices. CM also writes the determinant as \(|A|\) (CM, PDF p. 65). A matrix that is not square has no determinant.

From history

Solving equations also determined the name and the notation. The name determinant (determinanta) was introduced in 1812 by Cauchy (CAJORI, PDF p. 356, MUIR, PDF p. 110 and 111). We meet his paper of that year in section 2.10.3. The two upright lines around the table of elements were first used for a determinant by Cayley in 1841 (CAJNOT2, PDF p. 116). The notation shows all the elements in a clear arrangement, and so it caught on (CAJNOT2, PDF p. 116). From this comes the notation \(|A|\).

To calculate with the determinant of a square matrix we need rules of calculation for it. We will write the determinant of the matrix with the columns \(\vek u\), \(\vek v\) and \(\vek w\) briefly as \(\det(\vek u, \vek v, \vek w)\). By the definition, \(\det(\vek u, \vek v, \vek w) = \vek u\cdot(\vek v\times\vek w)\).

Lemma 2.51 Properties of the determinant (lastnosti determinante)

For all vectors \(\vek u\), \(\vek u'\), \(\vek v\), \(\vek w\), all numbers \(k\) and every \(3 \times 3\) matrix \(\mat A\):

  1. Cyclic exchange: \(\det(\vek u, \vek v, \vek w) = \det(\vek v, \vek w, \vek u) = \det(\vek w, \vek u, \vek v)\).
  2. Linearity in each column: \(\det(\vek u + \vek u', \vek v, \vek w) = \det(\vek u, \vek v, \vek w) + \det(\vek u', \vek v, \vek w)\) and \(\det(k\,\vek u, \vek v, \vek w) = k\det(\vek u, \vek v, \vek w)\), and the same in the second and the third column.
  3. The determinant of a matrix with two equal columns is zero.
  4. If we exchange two columns, the determinant changes its sign, for example \(\det(\vek v, \vek u, \vek w) = -\det(\vek u, \vek v, \vek w)\).
  5. \(\det\mat I = 1\).
  6. \(\det\mat A^\T = \det\mat A\).
Proof

We compute every item with components or reduce it to earlier items.

1. Cyclic exchange.

  1. By the formula for the vector product and (2.6),
    \[\vek u\cdot(\vek v\times\vek w) = u_{\mathrm{x}} (v_{\mathrm{y}} w_{\mathrm{z}} - v_{\mathrm{z}} w_{\mathrm{y}}) + u_{\mathrm{y}} (v_{\mathrm{z}} w_{\mathrm{x}} - v_{\mathrm{x}} w_{\mathrm{z}}) + u_{\mathrm{z}} (v_{\mathrm{x}} w_{\mathrm{y}} - v_{\mathrm{y}} w_{\mathrm{x}}) .\]
  2. We remove the brackets. We get six terms, \(u_{\mathrm{x}} v_{\mathrm{y}} w_{\mathrm{z}}\), \(-u_{\mathrm{x}} v_{\mathrm{z}} w_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}} v_{\mathrm{z}} w_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(-u_{\mathrm{y}} v_{\mathrm{x}} w_{\mathrm{z}}\), \(u_{\mathrm{z}} v_{\mathrm{x}} w_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(-u_{\mathrm{z}} v_{\mathrm{y}} w_{\mathrm{x}}\).
  3. In the same way we compute \(\vek v\cdot(\vek w\times\vek u) = v_{\mathrm{x}} (w_{\mathrm{y}} u_{\mathrm{z}} - w_{\mathrm{z}} u_{\mathrm{y}}) + v_{\mathrm{y}} (w_{\mathrm{z}} u_{\mathrm{x}} - w_{\mathrm{x}} u_{\mathrm{z}}) + v_{\mathrm{z}} (w_{\mathrm{x}} u_{\mathrm{y}} - w_{\mathrm{y}} u_{\mathrm{x}})\) and get the terms \(v_{\mathrm{x}} w_{\mathrm{y}} u_{\mathrm{z}}\), \(-v_{\mathrm{x}} w_{\mathrm{z}} u_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(v_{\mathrm{y}} w_{\mathrm{z}} u_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(-v_{\mathrm{y}} w_{\mathrm{x}} u_{\mathrm{z}}\), \(v_{\mathrm{z}} w_{\mathrm{x}} u_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(-v_{\mathrm{z}} w_{\mathrm{y}} u_{\mathrm{x}}\).
  4. When numbers are multiplied, the order of the factors does not matter. So the term \(v_{\mathrm{y}} w_{\mathrm{z}} u_{\mathrm{x}}\) of step 3 is the term \(u_{\mathrm{x}} v_{\mathrm{y}} w_{\mathrm{z}}\) of step 2, the term \(-v_{\mathrm{z}} w_{\mathrm{y}} u_{\mathrm{x}}\) is \(-u_{\mathrm{x}} v_{\mathrm{z}} w_{\mathrm{y}}\), the term \(v_{\mathrm{x}} w_{\mathrm{y}} u_{\mathrm{z}}\) is \(u_{\mathrm{z}} v_{\mathrm{x}} w_{\mathrm{y}}\), the term \(-v_{\mathrm{x}} w_{\mathrm{z}} u_{\mathrm{y}}\) is \(-u_{\mathrm{y}} v_{\mathrm{x}} w_{\mathrm{z}}\), the term \(-v_{\mathrm{y}} w_{\mathrm{x}} u_{\mathrm{z}}\) is \(-u_{\mathrm{z}} v_{\mathrm{y}} w_{\mathrm{x}}\) and the term \(v_{\mathrm{z}} w_{\mathrm{x}} u_{\mathrm{y}}\) is \(u_{\mathrm{y}} v_{\mathrm{z}} w_{\mathrm{x}}\). So each of the six terms of step 3 is exactly one of the six terms of step 2, with the same sign. Therefore \(\det(\vek u, \vek v, \vek w) = \det(\vek v, \vek w, \vek u)\).
  5. We apply the equality of step 4 also to the triple \(\vek v\), \(\vek w\), \(\vek u\) and get \(\det(\vek v, \vek w, \vek u) = \det(\vek w, \vek u, \vek v)\).

2. Linearity.

  1. The vector \(\vek m = \vek v\times\vek w\) does not depend on the first column. By items 1 to 3 of the properties of the scalar product (lemma 2.12), \((\vek u + \vek u')\cdot\vek m = \vek u\cdot\vek m + \vek u'\cdot\vek m\) and \((k\,\vek u)\cdot\vek m = k\,(\vek u\cdot\vek m)\). This is linearity in the first column.
  2. By item 1, \(\det(\vek u, \vek v, \vek w) = \det(\vek v, \vek w, \vek u)\), where the second column \(\vek v\) has come to the first place. Linearity in the second column is therefore linearity in the first column of the determinant \(\det(\vek v, \vek w, \vek u)\), which holds by step 1.
  3. In the same way, by item 1, linearity in the third column follows from \(\det(\vek u, \vek v, \vek w) = \det(\vek w, \vek u, \vek v)\).

3. Two equal columns.

  1. For every vector \(\vek v\), by the formula for the vector product, \(\vek v\times\vek v = (v_{\mathrm{y}} v_{\mathrm{z}} - v_{\mathrm{z}} v_{\mathrm{y}},\ v_{\mathrm{z}} v_{\mathrm{x}} - v_{\mathrm{x}} v_{\mathrm{z}},\ v_{\mathrm{x}} v_{\mathrm{y}} - v_{\mathrm{y}} v_{\mathrm{x}}) = \vek 0\).
  2. If the second and the third column are equal, \(\det(\vek u, \vek v, \vek v) = \vek u\cdot(\vek v\times\vek v) = \vek u\cdot\vek 0 = 0\).
  3. If the first and the second column are equal, then by item 1 \(\det(\vek u, \vek u, \vek w) = \det(\vek w, \vek u, \vek u)\), which is zero by step 2.
  4. If the first and the third column are equal, then by item 1 \(\det(\vek u, \vek v, \vek u) = \det(\vek v, \vek u, \vek u)\), which is zero by step 2.

4. Exchanging two columns.

  1. Let us exchange the first and the second column. By item 3 the determinant with two equal columns \(\vek u + \vek v\) is zero: \(\det(\vek u + \vek v, \vek u + \vek v, \vek w) = 0\).
  2. By item 2 we split the left side first in the first and then in the second column:
    \[0 = \det(\vek u, \vek u, \vek w) + \det(\vek u, \vek v, \vek w) + \det(\vek v, \vek u, \vek w) + \det(\vek v, \vek v, \vek w) .\]
  3. The first and the last term have two equal columns and are zero by item 3. There remains \(0 = \det(\vek u, \vek v, \vek w) + \det(\vek v, \vek u, \vek w)\), so \(\det(\vek v, \vek u, \vek w) = -\det(\vek u, \vek v, \vek w)\).
  4. For the second and third or for the first and third column we repeat steps 1 to 3 with the sum \(\vek v + \vek w\) or \(\vek u + \vek w\) in the two places we exchange.

5. Identity matrix.

  1. The columns of the identity matrix are \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) (definition 1.105).
  2. By the formula for the vector product, \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\times\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = (1 \cdot 1 - 0 \cdot 0,\ 0 \cdot 0 - 0 \cdot 1,\ 0 \cdot 0 - 1 \cdot 0) = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), so \(\det\mat I = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = 1\).

6. Transposed matrix.

  1. The transposed matrix has the entries \((\mat A^\T)_{\mathrm{ij}} = A_{\mathrm{ji}}\) (definition 1.100). We get \(\det\mat A^\T\) by exchanging the two indices of every entry in (2.51).
  2. The term \(A_{\mathrm{xx}} A_{\mathrm{yy}} A_{\mathrm{zz}}\) does not change. The term \(A_{\mathrm{xy}} A_{\mathrm{yz}} A_{\mathrm{zx}}\) becomes \(A_{\mathrm{yx}} A_{\mathrm{zy}} A_{\mathrm{xz}}\), which with the factors rearranged is the third term with a plus, \(A_{\mathrm{xz}} A_{\mathrm{yx}} A_{\mathrm{zy}}\). The third term with a plus becomes \(A_{\mathrm{zx}} A_{\mathrm{xy}} A_{\mathrm{yz}}\), which is the second term with a plus.
  3. The terms with a minus: \(A_{\mathrm{xx}} A_{\mathrm{yz}} A_{\mathrm{zy}}\) becomes \(A_{\mathrm{xx}} A_{\mathrm{zy}} A_{\mathrm{yz}}\), \(A_{\mathrm{xy}} A_{\mathrm{yx}} A_{\mathrm{zz}}\) becomes \(A_{\mathrm{yx}} A_{\mathrm{xy}} A_{\mathrm{zz}}\), and \(A_{\mathrm{xz}} A_{\mathrm{yy}} A_{\mathrm{zx}}\) becomes \(A_{\mathrm{zx}} A_{\mathrm{yy}} A_{\mathrm{xz}}\). With the factors rearranged, each is the same term as before.
  4. All six terms appear with the same signs in both determinants, so \(\det\mat A^\T = \det\mat A\).

Source: CM, properties (2), (4), (5), (6) (PDF p. 66), CM (2.2.21), (2.2.22) (PDF p. 45), KREYSZIG §7.7, Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 (PDF pp. 321-323). The proofs are not from the sources.

Every item has a geometric meaning. Item 2 says that the volume doubles when we double one edge. Item 3 says that a parallelepiped with two equal edges has no volume, since it is flattened into a plane. Item 4 says that exchanging two edges reverses the orientation but does not change the volume. Item 1 is the exchange with which we proved the right-hand rule in lemma 2.49 on the direction of the vector product, written with the determinant: for vectors \(\vek a\) and \(\vek b\) with \(\vek a\times\vek b \neq \vek 0\), \(\det(\vek a, \vek b, \vek a\times\vek b) = \det(\vek a\times\vek b, \vek a, \vek b) = (\vek a\times\vek b)\cdot(\vek a\times\vek b) = |\vek a\times\vek b|^2 > 0\). And item 6 says that everything that holds for columns also holds for rows, since the rows of the matrix \(\mat A\) are the columns of the matrix \(\mat A^\T\). So it does not matter that CM and KREYSZIG write the scalar triple product as the determinant of the matrix whose rows are the vectors \(\vek a\), \(\vek b\), \(\vek c\) (CM (2.3.9), PDF p. 66, KREYSZIG §9.3 (10), PDF p. 399): it is the determinant of the transposed matrix and therefore the same number.

Items 2 and 3 give one more rule, which we will need for the sheared cube. If we add a multiple of the second column to the first, the determinant does not change:

\[\det(\vek a + k\,\vek b, \vek b, \vek c) = \det(\vek a, \vek b, \vek c) + k\det(\vek b, \vek b, \vek c) = \det(\vek a, \vek b, \vek c) .\]

The first equality is linearity in the first column, the second is item 3. In figure 2.59 the opposite face then moves by \(k\,\vek b\), parallel to the base. The base stays the same, and so does the height, because \((\vek a + k\,\vek b)\cdot\vek n = \vek a\cdot\vek n + k\,\vek b\cdot\vek n = \vek a\cdot\vek n\), since \(\vek b\) is perpendicular to the normal \(\vek n\) of the base. So the volume stays the same too. This is the reasoning of section 1.6.2, where the front face of the sheared cube became a parallelogram with the same base side and the same height, so its area did not change.

2.10.3 The determinant of a product

Two matrices can act one after the other. We got the matrix product in section 1.16 exactly from this: \((\mat A\mat B)\,\vek v = \mat A\,(\mat B\vek v)\) (item 1 of lemma 1.99, which follows from definition 1.98). We first send the vector with the matrix \(\mat B\), and then the result with the matrix \(\mat A\). How do volumes change on the way? Let us look in the plane, with the \(2 \times 2\) matrices

\[\mat B = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} , \qquad \mat A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} , \qquad \mat A\mat B = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix} .\]

The matrix \(\mat B\) sends the unit square to the parallelogram of its columns \((2, 0)\) and \((1, 1)\), with the area \(\det\mat B = 2 \cdot 1 - 1 \cdot 0 = 2\). The matrix \(\mat A\) sends this parallelogram to the parallelogram of the columns of the matrix \(\mat A\mat B\), the vectors \((2, 2)\) and \((1, 3)\), with the area \(\det(\mat A\mat B) = 2 \cdot 3 - 1 \cdot 2 = 4\) (figure 2.60). The matrix \(\mat A\) alone enlarges the unit square by \(\det\mat A = 1 \cdot 2 - 0 \cdot 1 = 2\), and it enlarged the parallelogram with the area \(2\) to the area \(4\), also twice. So the areas multiply: \(4 = 2 \cdot 2\). Does a matrix enlarge every area and every volume by the same factor, the absolute value of its determinant? If so, the determinant of the matrix \(\mat A\mat B\) is the product of the determinants, at least in absolute value.

From history

The same question occupied two mathematicians independently in 1812. In the summer Cauchy, in Cherbourg, where he worked as an engineer, was looking for a generalisation of the formulas of Carl Friedrich Gauss, and found a theorem by which the determinant is multiplied by the determinant of the other matrix when one matrix is multiplied by another (MUIR, PDF p. 111, TIMO, PDF p. 116). When he returned to Paris, he visited Jacques Binet, who showed him a similar theorem. Binet had reached it by another route. The difference was only in the name: Binet called a resultant what Cauchy called a determinant (MUIR, PDF p. 111). On the same day, 30 November 1812, both read their memoirs at the Institute (MUIR, PDF p. 110). MUIR concludes that the theorem should bear both names. Binet obtained the more general theorem, of which Cauchy's is a special case, and Cauchy gave a satisfactory proof (MUIR, PDF p. 127).

Whether the determinant of the product of two matrices is the product of their determinants is answered by the following theorem: the determinants multiply in a product of matrices, as Binet and Cauchy found, and this holds even with the sign.

Theorem 2.52 Determinant of a product (determinanta produkta)

For \(3 \times 3\) matrices \(\mat A\) and \(\mat B\),

\[\det(\mat A\mat B) = \det\mat A\,\det\mat B . \tag{2.53}\]
Proof

We write the columns of the matrix \(\mat A\mat B\) with the columns of the matrix \(\mat A\) and split the determinant by linearity. We call the columns of the matrix \(\mat A\) \(\vek a\), \(\vek b\) and \(\vek c\).

  1. By (1.38) and definition 1.98 the matrix \(\mat A\mat B\) has the entries \((\mat A\mat B)_{\mathrm{ik}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} A_{\mathrm{ij}} B_{\mathrm{jk}} = A_{\mathrm{ix}} B_{\mathrm{xk}} + A_{\mathrm{iy}} B_{\mathrm{yk}} + A_{\mathrm{iz}} B_{\mathrm{zk}}\).
  2. The numbers \(A_{\mathrm{ix}}\), \(A_{\mathrm{iy}}\) and \(A_{\mathrm{iz}}\) are the components \(i\) of the columns \(\vek a\), \(\vek b\) and \(\vek c\). So the column \(k\) of the matrix \(\mat A\mat B\) is the vector \(B_{\mathrm{xk}}\,\vek a + B_{\mathrm{yk}}\,\vek b + B_{\mathrm{zk}}\,\vek c\).
  3. The determinant of the matrix \(\mat A\mat B\) is therefore
    \[\det(B_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\vek a + B_{\mathrm{yx}}\,\vek b + B_{\mathrm{zx}}\,\vek c,\ \ B_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\vek a + B_{\mathrm{yy}}\,\vek b + B_{\mathrm{zy}}\,\vek c,\ \ B_{\mathrm{xz}}\,\vek a + B_{\mathrm{yz}}\,\vek b + B_{\mathrm{zz}}\,\vek c) .\]
  4. By linearity in the first column (item 2 of lemma 2.51 on the properties of the determinant) we split it into three determinants, each of them by linearity in the second column into three, and each of these by linearity in the third column into three. We get \(3 \cdot 3 \cdot 3 = 27\) terms. Each term chooses for each column one of the vectors \(\vek a\), \(\vek b\), \(\vek c\), for example the term \(B_{\mathrm{xx}} B_{\mathrm{xy}} B_{\mathrm{zz}}\det(\vek a, \vek a, \vek c)\).
  5. A term in which some vector repeats is zero by item 3 of the same lemma, like the example of step 4. Six terms remain, in which all three vectors appear, each once:
    \[\begin{aligned} \det(\mat A\mat B) = {}& B_{\mathrm{xx}} B_{\mathrm{yy}} B_{\mathrm{zz}} \det(\vek a, \vek b, \vek c) + B_{\mathrm{xx}} B_{\mathrm{zy}} B_{\mathrm{yz}} \det(\vek a, \vek c, \vek b) + B_{\mathrm{yx}} B_{\mathrm{xy}} B_{\mathrm{zz}} \det(\vek b, \vek a, \vek c) \\ &+ B_{\mathrm{yx}} B_{\mathrm{zy}} B_{\mathrm{xz}} \det(\vek b, \vek c, \vek a) + B_{\mathrm{zx}} B_{\mathrm{xy}} B_{\mathrm{yz}} \det(\vek c, \vek a, \vek b) + B_{\mathrm{zx}} B_{\mathrm{yy}} B_{\mathrm{xz}} \det(\vek c, \vek b, \vek a) . \end{aligned}\]
  6. We reduce each of the six determinants to \(\det(\vek a, \vek b, \vek c) = \det\mat A\). By item 4 of the lemma, \(\det(\vek a, \vek c, \vek b)\), \(\det(\vek b, \vek a, \vek c)\) and \(\det(\vek c, \vek b, \vek a)\) equal \(-\det\mat A\), since each arises by one exchange of two columns. By item 1, \(\det(\vek b, \vek c, \vek a)\) and \(\det(\vek c, \vek a, \vek b)\) equal \(\det\mat A\).
  7. We take out \(\det\mat A\):
    \[\det(\mat A\mat B) = \det\mat A\,\big(B_{\mathrm{xx}} B_{\mathrm{yy}} B_{\mathrm{zz}} - B_{\mathrm{xx}} B_{\mathrm{zy}} B_{\mathrm{yz}} - B_{\mathrm{yx}} B_{\mathrm{xy}} B_{\mathrm{zz}} + B_{\mathrm{yx}} B_{\mathrm{zy}} B_{\mathrm{xz}} + B_{\mathrm{zx}} B_{\mathrm{xy}} B_{\mathrm{yz}} - B_{\mathrm{zx}} B_{\mathrm{yy}} B_{\mathrm{xz}}\big) .\]
  8. We compare the bracket with (2.51) for the matrix \(\mat B\). The term \(B_{\mathrm{yx}} B_{\mathrm{zy}} B_{\mathrm{xz}}\) is, with the factors rearranged, \(B_{\mathrm{xz}} B_{\mathrm{yx}} B_{\mathrm{zy}}\), the term \(B_{\mathrm{zx}} B_{\mathrm{xy}} B_{\mathrm{yz}}\) is \(B_{\mathrm{xy}} B_{\mathrm{yz}} B_{\mathrm{zx}}\), \(B_{\mathrm{xx}} B_{\mathrm{zy}} B_{\mathrm{yz}}\) is \(B_{\mathrm{xx}} B_{\mathrm{yz}} B_{\mathrm{zy}}\), \(B_{\mathrm{yx}} B_{\mathrm{xy}} B_{\mathrm{zz}}\) is \(B_{\mathrm{xy}} B_{\mathrm{yx}} B_{\mathrm{zz}}\), and \(B_{\mathrm{zx}} B_{\mathrm{yy}} B_{\mathrm{xz}}\) is \(B_{\mathrm{xz}} B_{\mathrm{yy}} B_{\mathrm{zx}}\). The signs agree, so the bracket is \(\det\mat B\).

Source: KREYSZIG §7.8, Theorem 4 (10) (PDF p. 333), for \(n \times n\) matrices, CM, property (1) (PDF p. 66). The proof is not from the sources.

The proof showed where the rule comes from. The columns of the product are combinations of the columns of the matrix \(\mat A\) with numbers from the matrix \(\mat B\). Linearity splits them, equal columns drop out, and exchanges of columns give exactly the signs with which the determinant of \(\mat B\) is built from its numbers. For \(2 \times 2\) matrices the theorem holds as well. We put both into a \(3 \times 3\) matrix with \(1\) at the place \(zz\) and zeros elsewhere in the row and the column \(z\), as above. In the product of such larger matrices the entry \(zz\) is \(0 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot 0 + 1 \cdot 1 = 1\), the other entries of the row and the column \(z\) are zero, because each of their terms has a factor zero, and the remaining four entries are the entries of the product of the \(2 \times 2\) matrices. So the product of the larger matrices is the larger matrix of the product, and the determinants of the larger matrices are the determinants of the smaller ones. This agrees with figure 2.60, where \(\det(\mat A\mat B) = 4 = 2 \cdot 2\).

The first use concerns turned axes. In section 2.9 we wrote the rotation matrix \(\mat Q\) so that its rows are the unit vectors of the turned axes \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{z}}\) (definition 2.37), and we saw that it is orthogonal, \(\mat Q\mat Q^\T = \mat I\) (lemma 2.40 on the orthogonality of the rotation matrix). By theorem 2.52 on the determinant of a product and items 5 and 6 of the lemma on the properties of the determinant,

\[\det\mat Q\,\det\mat Q^\T = \det\mat I = 1 , \qquad \det\mat Q^\T = \det\mat Q , \qquad \text{so} \qquad (\det\mat Q)^2 = 1 .\]

The determinant of an orthogonal matrix is therefore \(1\) or \(-1\). The columns of the matrix \(\mat Q^\T\) are the rows of the matrix \(\mat Q\), so \(\det\mat Q = \det\mat Q^\T = \bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{x}}\cdot(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{y}}\times\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{z}})\). Since the determinant is \(1\) or \(-1\), this scalar triple product is \(1\) when the turned axes are again right-handed, and \(-1\) when they are the mirror image of right-handed ones. Take the axes turned about the axis \(z\) with the pair \(c = 0{,}6\), \(s = 0{,}8\), for which \(c^2 + s^2 = 1\), so that \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{x}} = (c, s, 0)\), \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{y}} = (-s, c, 0)\) and \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{z}} = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\). Then

\[\mat Q = \begin{bmatrix} 0{,}6 & 0{,}8 & 0 \\ -0{,}8 & 0{,}6 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} ,\]

and by (2.51) \(\det\mat Q = 0{,}6 \cdot 0{,}6 \cdot 1 - 0{,}8 \cdot (-0{,}8) \cdot 1 = 0{,}36 + 0{,}64 = 1\), since the other four terms are zero. Turning the axes changes neither volumes nor orientation.

2.10.4 Zero determinant and the inverse matrix

Let us return to the question from the beginning of the section: when does a matrix \(\mat A\) send some nonzero vector \(\vek v\) to the zero vector? The product of a matrix and a vector can be read in two ways. The component \(i\) of the vector \(\mat A\vek v\) is

\[(\mat A\vek v)_{\mathrm{i}} = A_{\mathrm{ix}} v_{\mathrm{x}} + A_{\mathrm{iy}} v_{\mathrm{y}} + A_{\mathrm{iz}} v_{\mathrm{z}} .\]

The numbers \(A_{\mathrm{ix}}\), \(A_{\mathrm{iy}}\) and \(A_{\mathrm{iz}}\) are the components \(i\) of the columns \(\vek a\), \(\vek b\) and \(\vek c\), so \(\mat A\vek v = v_{\mathrm{x}}\,\vek a + v_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek b + v_{\mathrm{z}}\,\vek c\), the sum of the columns multiplied by the components of the vector \(\vek v\). But the same numbers \(A_{\mathrm{ix}}\), \(A_{\mathrm{iy}}\), \(A_{\mathrm{iz}}\) are also the components of the row \(i\) written as a vector, and by (2.6) \((\mat A\vek v)_{\mathrm{i}}\) is the scalar product of the row \(i\) with the vector \(\vek v\). So the equality \(\mat A\vek v = \vek 0\) means that \(\vek v\) is perpendicular to all three rows.

The first way gives us the idea for one direction. If \(v_{\mathrm{x}}\,\vek a + v_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek b + v_{\mathrm{z}}\,\vek c = \vek 0\) and, for example, \(v_{\mathrm{x}} \neq 0\), the column \(\vek a\) is a combination of the other two, and the parallelepiped of the columns is flattened. The second way gives us the idea for the other direction. We call the rows of the matrix \(\mat A\), written as vectors, \(\vek p\), \(\vek q\) and \(\vek r\). If the determinant is zero, then by item 6 of lemma 2.51 on the properties of the determinant the rows too are the edges of a flattened parallelepiped, so they lie in one plane. A vector perpendicular to this plane is perpendicular to all three rows. Such a vector is given by the vector product of two rows.

Lemma 2.53 Zero determinant (ničelna determinanta)

For a \(3 \times 3\) matrix \(\mat A\), \(\det\mat A = 0\) exactly when there is a vector \(\vek v \neq \vek 0\) with \(\mat A\vek v = \vek 0\).

Proof

We must prove two directions. We call the columns of the matrix \(\vek a\), \(\vek b\), \(\vek c\), and its rows, written as vectors, \(\vek p = (A_{\mathrm{xx}}, A_{\mathrm{xy}}, A_{\mathrm{xz}})\), \(\vek q = (A_{\mathrm{yx}}, A_{\mathrm{yy}}, A_{\mathrm{yz}})\) and \(\vek r = (A_{\mathrm{zx}}, A_{\mathrm{zy}}, A_{\mathrm{zz}})\).

1. From \(\mat A\vek v = \vek 0\) with \(\vek v \neq \vek 0\) follows \(\det\mat A = 0\).

  1. As we computed before the lemma, \(\mat A\vek v = v_{\mathrm{x}}\,\vek a + v_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek b + v_{\mathrm{z}}\,\vek c\), so \(v_{\mathrm{x}}\,\vek a + v_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek b + v_{\mathrm{z}}\,\vek c = \vek 0\).
  2. The vector \(\vek v\) is not the zero vector, so at least one of its components is not zero. Let it be \(v_{\mathrm{x}}\). We divide the equality of step 1 by \(v_{\mathrm{x}}\) and express \(\vek a = -(v_{\mathrm{y}}/v_{\mathrm{x}})\,\vek b - (v_{\mathrm{z}}/v_{\mathrm{x}})\,\vek c\).
  3. By linearity in the first column (item 2 of lemma 2.51 on the properties of the determinant),
    \[\det\mat A = \det(\vek a, \vek b, \vek c) = -\frac{v_{\mathrm{y}}}{v_{\mathrm{x}}}\det(\vek b, \vek b, \vek c) - \frac{v_{\mathrm{z}}}{v_{\mathrm{x}}}\det(\vek c, \vek b, \vek c) .\]
  4. Both determinants on the right have two equal columns and are zero by item 3 of the same lemma. So \(\det\mat A = 0\).
  5. If \(v_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\) but \(v_{\mathrm{y}}\) or \(v_{\mathrm{z}}\) is not zero, we proceed in the same way. We express the column \(\vek b\) or \(\vek c\), split it by linearity in the second or the third column, and in each term one column repeats.

2. From \(\det\mat A = 0\) follows that there is \(\vek v \neq \vek 0\) with \(\mat A\vek v = \vek 0\).

  1. The matrix with the columns \(\vek p\), \(\vek q\), \(\vek r\) is \(\mat A^\T\) (definition 1.100). By item 6 of the lemma, \(\det(\vek p, \vek q, \vek r) = \det\mat A^\T = \det\mat A = 0\). By the definition this is \(\vek p\cdot(\vek q\times\vek r) = 0\), and by item 1 of the same lemma also \(\det(\vek r, \vek p, \vek q) = \vek r\cdot(\vek p\times\vek q)\) and \(\det(\vek q, \vek r, \vek p) = \vek q\cdot(\vek r\times\vek p)\) are zero.
  2. Let \(\vek p\times\vek q \neq \vek 0\). We take \(\vek v = \vek p\times\vek q\). By item 3 of the lemma, \(\vek p\cdot\vek v = \det(\vek p, \vek p, \vek q) = 0\) and \(\vek q\cdot\vek v = \det(\vek q, \vek p, \vek q) = 0\), and by step 1, \(\vek r\cdot\vek v = 0\). All three components of the vector \(\mat A\vek v\) are zero, and \(\vek v \neq \vek 0\).
  3. If \(\vek p\times\vek q = \vek 0\) but \(\vek q\times\vek r\) or \(\vek r\times\vek p\) is not zero, we proceed as in step 2 with \(\vek v = \vek q\times\vek r\) or \(\vek v = \vek r\times\vek p\). The scalar product with the third row is zero by step 1.
  4. There remains the case in which all three vector products of rows are zero. If all rows are zero, then \(\mat A\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = \vek 0\), and we take \(\vek v = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). Otherwise at least one row is nonzero. We call it \(\vek m\).
  5. Let us show that every row \(\vek w\) is a multiple of the row \(\vek m\). We have \(\vek m\times\vek w = \vek 0\), because this is one of the products of step 4 or its negative, or else \(\vek w = \vek m\) and \(\vek m\times\vek m = \vek 0\) (item 1 of lemma 2.31 on the properties of the vector product). Let \(\vek e = \vek m/|\vek m|\). By item 5 of lemma 2.31 on the properties of the vector product, \(|\vek m\times\vek w|^2 = |\vek m|^2|\vek w|^2 - (\vek m\cdot\vek w)^2\), so by item 3 of lemma 2.14 on splitting a vector
    \[|\vek w - (\vek w\cdot\vek e)\,\vek e|^2 = |\vek w|^2 - \frac{(\vek m\cdot\vek w)^2}{|\vek m|^2} = \frac{|\vek m\times\vek w|^2}{|\vek m|^2} = 0 .\]
    A vector of length zero is the zero vector, so \(\vek w = (\vek w\cdot\vek e)\,\vek e\), a multiple of the row \(\vek m\).
  6. We look for a nonzero vector \(\vek v\) perpendicular to \(\vek m = (m_{\mathrm{x}}, m_{\mathrm{y}}, m_{\mathrm{z}})\). If \(m_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(m_{\mathrm{y}}\) are not both zero, we take \(\vek v = (-m_{\mathrm{y}}, m_{\mathrm{x}}, 0)\), which is not the zero vector and for which \(\vek m\cdot\vek v = -m_{\mathrm{x}} m_{\mathrm{y}} + m_{\mathrm{y}} m_{\mathrm{x}} + 0 = 0\). Otherwise \(\vek m = (0, 0, m_{\mathrm{z}})\), and we take \(\vek v = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\).
  7. By step 5 every row equals \(k\,\vek m\) for some number \(k\), and \((k\,\vek m)\cdot\vek v = k\,(\vek m\cdot\vek v) = 0\). So \(\mat A\vek v = \vek 0\).

Source: KREYSZIG §7.7, Theorem 4(b) (PDF p. 324), for \(n \times n\) matrices. CM, property (6) (PDF pp. 66-67), treats only the direction from a column that is a multiple of another column to a zero determinant. The proof is not from the sources.

The lemma says that the determinant detects whether a matrix loses some nonzero vector. Let us test it on the pure shear from the beginning of the section. The cube is pulled by four forces along its faces, of size \(\tau_0\) per unit area, and the stress matrix has the rows \((0, 0, \tau_0)\), \((0, 0, 0)\) and \((\tau_0, 0, 0)\). The matrix \(\ten\sigma - \tau_0\,\mat I\) has the rows

\[\vek p = (-\tau_0, 0, \tau_0) , \qquad \vek q = (0, -\tau_0, 0) , \qquad \vek r = (\tau_0, 0, -\tau_0) .\]

By (2.51) its determinant is \((-\tau_0)(-\tau_0)(-\tau_0) + 0 + 0 - 0 - 0 - \tau_0 \cdot (-\tau_0) \cdot \tau_0 = -\tau_0^3 + \tau_0^3 = 0\), since of the six terms only the first and the last have no zero factor. By the lemma there is therefore a nonzero vector that the matrix sends to zero. The proof also finds it. The vector product of the first two rows is

\[\vek p\times\vek q = \big(0 \cdot 0 - \tau_0 \cdot (-\tau_0),\ \tau_0 \cdot 0 - (-\tau_0) \cdot 0,\ (-\tau_0)(-\tau_0) - 0 \cdot 0\big) = \tau_0^2\,(1, 0, 1) .\]

This is the direction of the normal of the face perpendicular to the diagonal \(AC\), which we found in section 2.6.6. All three rows lie in the plane perpendicular to the vector \((1, 0, 1)\), since \(\vek r = -\vek p\) and \(\vek p\cdot(1, 0, 1) = 0\), \(\vek q\cdot(1, 0, 1) = 0\) (figure 2.61). For the matrix \(\mat A\) of figure 2.57, with the determinant \(12\), the lemma says the opposite: \(\mat A\vek v = \vek 0\) holds only for \(\vek v = \vek 0\).

Now we can also answer the question from section 1.16 of when a matrix has an inverse matrix, that is, a matrix \(\mat B\) with \(\mat B\mat A = \mat A\mat B = \mat I\) (definition 1.107). The scalar triple product gives us the idea. The vector \(\vek b\times\vek c\) is perpendicular to the columns \(\vek b\) and \(\vek c\), and its scalar product with the column \(\vek a\) is \(\det\mat A\). If we divide it by \(\det\mat A\), its scalar products with the columns \(\vek a\), \(\vek b\), \(\vek c\) are \(1\), \(0\), \(0\), exactly like those of the first row of the identity matrix with the columns of the identity matrix. We do the same with the vectors \(\vek c\times\vek a\) and \(\vek a\times\vek b\).

Theorem 2.54 Inverse via the determinant (inverzna matrika z determinanto)

A \(3 \times 3\) matrix \(\mat A\) has an inverse matrix exactly when \(\det\mat A \neq 0\). Then the inverse matrix \(\mat A^{-1}\) is the matrix with the rows

\[\frac{\vek b\times\vek c}{\det\mat A} , \qquad \frac{\vek c\times\vek a}{\det\mat A} , \qquad \frac{\vek a\times\vek b}{\det\mat A} , \tag{2.54}\]

where \(\vek a\), \(\vek b\) and \(\vek c\) are the columns of the matrix \(\mat A\).

Proof

First we show that a matrix with a zero determinant has no inverse matrix. Then we show that for \(\det\mat A \neq 0\) the matrix with the rows (2.54) is the inverse matrix.

  1. Suppose that \(\mat A\) has an inverse matrix \(\mat A^{-1}\). By theorem 2.52 on the determinant of a product and item 5 of lemma 2.51 on the properties of the determinant, \(\det\mat A\,\det\mat A^{-1} = \det(\mat A\mat A^{-1}) = \det\mat I = 1\). So \(\det\mat A\) is not zero.
  2. Let \(\det\mat A \neq 0\) and let \(\mat B\) be the matrix with the rows (2.54). By definition 1.98 the entry \(ij\) of the matrix \(\mat B\mat A\) is the sum \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} B_{\mathrm{ik}} A_{\mathrm{kj}}\), that is, the scalar product of the row \(i\) of the matrix \(\mat B\) with the column \(j\) of the matrix \(\mat A\).
  3. Row \(x\): the scalar product of the vector \(\vek b\times\vek c\) with the column \(\vek a\) is \(\det\mat A\) (item 1 of lemma 2.12 and definition 2.50), and with the columns \(\vek b\) and \(\vek c\) it is \(\det(\vek b, \vek b, \vek c) = 0\) and \(\det(\vek c, \vek b, \vek c) = 0\) (item 3 of the lemma on the properties of the determinant). After dividing by \(\det\mat A\), the entries of the row \(x\) of the matrix \(\mat B\mat A\) are \(1\), \(0\), \(0\).
  4. Row \(y\): the scalar product of the vector \(\vek c\times\vek a\) with the column \(\vek b\) is \(\det(\vek b, \vek c, \vek a) = \det\mat A\) (item 1 of the lemma), and with the columns \(\vek a\) and \(\vek c\) it is \(\det(\vek a, \vek c, \vek a) = 0\) and \(\det(\vek c, \vek c, \vek a) = 0\). The entries of the row \(y\) are \(0\), \(1\), \(0\).
  5. Row \(z\): the scalar product of the vector \(\vek a\times\vek b\) with the column \(\vek c\) is \(\det(\vek c, \vek a, \vek b) = \det\mat A\), and with the columns \(\vek a\) and \(\vek b\) it is \(\det(\vek a, \vek a, \vek b) = 0\) and \(\det(\vek b, \vek a, \vek b) = 0\). The entries of the row \(z\) are \(0\), \(0\), \(1\).
  6. By steps 3 to 5, \(\mat B\mat A = \mat I\).
  7. We still have to show \(\mat A\mat B = \mat I\). By the theorem on the determinant of a product, \(\det\mat B\,\det\mat A = \det(\mat B\mat A) = 1\), so \(\det\mat B \neq 0\). We repeat steps 2 to 6 for the matrix \(\mat B\) and get a matrix \(\mat C\) with \(\mat C\mat B = \mat I\).
  8. In a product of three matrices we may group the factors as we like (item 2 of lemma 1.99 on the properties of the matrix product), so \(\mat C = \mat C\mat I = \mat C\,(\mat B\mat A) = (\mat C\mat B)\,\mat A = \mat I\mat A = \mat A\). From \(\mat C\mat B = \mat I\) therefore follows \(\mat A\mat B = \mat I\).
  9. The matrix \(\mat B\) fulfils both conditions of definition 1.107, so it is the inverse matrix of the matrix \(\mat A\).

Source: KREYSZIG §7.8, Theorem 1 (PDF p. 327) and Theorem 2 (4) (PDF p. 330), for \(n \times n\) matrices, CM (2.3.11) (PDF p. 67). The sources write the inverse matrix with cofactors. The proof is not from the sources.

Let us test it on the matrix \(\mat A\) of figure 2.57, with the columns \(\vek a = (1, 1, 2)\), \(\vek b = (2, 0, 0)\), \(\vek c = (1, 3, 0)\) and the determinant \(12\). We compute the vector products by the formula for the vector product: \(\vek b\times\vek c = (0, 0, 6)\), as above, \(\vek c\times\vek a = (3 \cdot 2 - 0 \cdot 1,\ 0 \cdot 1 - 1 \cdot 2,\ 1 \cdot 1 - 3 \cdot 1) = (6, -2, -2)\) and \(\vek a\times\vek b = (1 \cdot 0 - 2 \cdot 0,\ 2 \cdot 2 - 1 \cdot 0,\ 1 \cdot 0 - 1 \cdot 2) = (0, 4, -2)\). Therefore

\[\mat A^{-1} = \frac{1}{12}\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 6 \\ 6 & -2 & -2 \\ 0 & 4 & -2 \end{bmatrix} .\]

Let us check two entries of the product \(\mat A^{-1}\mat A\). The row \(y\) times the column \(x\) is \((6 \cdot 1 - 2 \cdot 1 - 2 \cdot 2)/12 = 0\), and the row \(y\) times the column \(y\) is \((6 \cdot 2 - 2 \cdot 0 - 2 \cdot 0)/12 = 1\), as the identity matrix requires. The other seven entries also agree with the identity matrix, and so do all nine entries of the product \(\mat A\mat A^{-1}\).

The theorem and the lemma together say this. For a \(3 \times 3\) matrix three claims are equivalent: the determinant is not zero, the matrix sends no nonzero vector to the zero vector, and the matrix has an inverse matrix. This keeps the promise of section 1.16.5: a \(3 \times 3\) matrix that sends no nonzero vector to the zero vector has an inverse matrix. By lemma 2.53 on a zero determinant its determinant is then not zero, and by theorem 2.54 it therefore has the inverse matrix with the rows (2.54). In section 1.16 we said that books call a matrix without an inverse matrix singular (singularna). By the theorem this is exactly a matrix with determinant zero, so CM uses the word in both senses (CM, PDF pp. 65, 67). The inverse matrix has the determinant \(1/\det\mat A\), which follows from step 1 of the proof. CM writes the matrix (2.54) as \(\adj\mat A/\det\mat A\) (CM (2.3.11), PDF p. 67). The matrix \(\adj\mat A\), which CM calls the adjunct, has exactly the vectors \(\vek b\times\vek c\), \(\vek c\times\vek a\) and \(\vek a\times\vek b\) as its rows.

2.10.5 Change of volume

Let us return to the body. Let the displacement field of the body be continuously differentiable (definition 1.90), let \(P\) be a point inside the body, \(\mat H\) the displacement gradient at \(P\), the matrix with the entries \(H_{\mathrm{ij}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}\,(P)\) (definition 1.109), and \(\mat F = \mat I + \mat H\) the deformation gradient at \(P\) (definition 1.110). From \(P\) we draw three short fibres with the vectors \(h\,\vek a\), \(h\,\vek b\) and \(h\,\vek c\), where \(\vek a\), \(\vek b\), \(\vek c\) are chosen vectors that form a right-handed triple, and \(h > 0\) is a small number. The fibres span a small parallelepiped, a piece of the body at \(P\) (figure 2.62, left). By lemma 2.47 on the volume of a parallelepiped and linearity of the determinant in each column, it has the volume

\[\det(h\,\vek a, h\,\vek b, h\,\vek c) = h^3\det(\vek a, \vek b, \vek c) ,\]

which is positive because the triple is right-handed.

Where do the fibres go? Let \(A = P + h\,\vek a\) be the end point of the first fibre. By (1.15) the vector of the fibre after loading is \(\overrightarrow{P_1 A_1} = h\,\vek a + \big(\vek u(A) - \vek u(P)\big)\). We write the difference of displacements by theorem 1.88 on the change of a field over a short step, for each component separately, with the step \(h\,\vek a\):

\[u_{\mathrm{i}}(A) - u_{\mathrm{i}}(P) = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} H_{\mathrm{ij}}\,h\,a_{\mathrm{j}} + R_{\mathrm{i}} ,\]

where \(R_{\mathrm{i}}\) is the remainder of the component \(u_{\mathrm{i}}\). The sum is the component \(i\) of the vector \(\mat H(h\,\vek a)\) ((1.38)). The component \(i\) of the vector \(h\,\vek a\) is \(h\,a_{\mathrm{i}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} I_{\mathrm{ij}}\,h\,a_{\mathrm{j}}\), because the row \(i\) of the identity matrix has a single nonzero entry, \(1\) in the column \(i\). We add the two sums:

\[\overrightarrow{P_1 A_1} = (\mat I + \mat H)(h\,\vek a) + (R_{\mathrm{x}}, R_{\mathrm{y}}, R_{\mathrm{z}}) = \mat F(h\,\vek a) + (R_{\mathrm{x}}, R_{\mathrm{y}}, R_{\mathrm{z}}) .\]

The remainders are small compared with the length of the fibre. By theorem 1.88, for every \(\eta > 0\) and small enough \(h\) each of them is at most \(\eta\,h\,|\vek a|\) in absolute value. The same holds for the other two fibres. If we leave the remainders out, the vectors of the fibres after loading are \(\mat F(h\,\vek a)\), \(\mat F(h\,\vek b)\) and \(\mat F(h\,\vek c)\), and the piece after loading is the parallelepiped they span (figure 2.62, right). This is a simplification. The real piece differs from this parallelepiped by the remainders: its edges curve slightly, and the end points of the fibres are not exactly at the tips of the vectors \(\mat F(h\,\vek a)\), \(\mat F(h\,\vek b)\), \(\mat F(h\,\vek c)\). How large the error is we will check on the hanging bar below. The volume of the parallelepiped spanned by \(\mat F\) times the edge vectors is given by the first item of the next lemma. The second item tells what the determinant of the matrix \(\mat F = \mat I + \mat H\) becomes when all entries of the matrix \(\mat H\) are small, which we will need for the relation with strains.

Lemma 2.55 Change of volume (sprememba prostornine)

Let \(\mat F\) be a \(3 \times 3\) matrix and \(\mat H = \mat F - \mat I\). Then:

  1. For all vectors \(\vek a\), \(\vek b\), \(\vek c\),
    \[\det(\mat F\vek a, \mat F\vek b, \mat F\vek c) = \det\mat F\,\det(\vek a, \vek b, \vek c) . \tag{2.55}\]
    So the parallelepiped spanned by the vectors \(\mat F\vek a\), \(\mat F\vek b\) and \(\mat F\vek c\) has \(|\det\mat F|\) times the volume of the parallelepiped spanned by \(\vek a\), \(\vek b\) and \(\vek c\). When \(\det(\vek a, \vek b, \vek c) \neq 0\), that is, when the parallelepiped of the vectors \(\vek a\), \(\vek b\), \(\vek c\) is not flat, the two triples have the same orientation exactly when \(\det\mat F > 0\).
  2. If all entries of the matrix \(\mat H\) are at most \(g\) in absolute value, then \(\det\mat F\) differs from \(1 + H_{\mathrm{xx}} + H_{\mathrm{yy}} + H_{\mathrm{zz}}\) by at most \(6g^2 + 6g^3\).

When \(\mat F\) is the deformation gradient at a point of a body, the number \(J = \det\mat F\) is called the Jacobian (Jacobijeva determinanta).

Proof

In item 1 we write the matrix with the columns \(\mat F\vek a\), \(\mat F\vek b\), \(\mat F\vek c\) as a product of two matrices, in item 2 we write out the determinant of the matrix \(\mat I + \mat H\) by (2.51).

1. Volume of the parallelepiped.

  1. Let \(\mat M\) be the matrix with the columns \(\vek a\), \(\vek b\) and \(\vek c\). By definition 1.98 the entry \(ik\) of the matrix \(\mat F\mat M\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} F_{\mathrm{ij}} M_{\mathrm{jk}}\). The numbers \(M_{\mathrm{jk}}\) with \(k\) fixed and \(j = x, y, z\) are the components of the column \(k\) of the matrix \(\mat M\), so by (1.38) the column \(k\) of the matrix \(\mat F\mat M\) is \(\mat F\) times the column \(k\) of the matrix \(\mat M\).
  2. So the columns of the matrix \(\mat F\mat M\) are \(\mat F\vek a\), \(\mat F\vek b\) and \(\mat F\vek c\), and \(\det(\mat F\vek a, \mat F\vek b, \mat F\vek c) = \det(\mat F\mat M)\).
  3. By theorem 2.52 on the determinant of a product, \(\det(\mat F\mat M) = \det\mat F\,\det\mat M = \det\mat F\,\det(\vek a, \vek b, \vek c)\). This is (2.55).
  4. By lemma 2.47 on the volume of a parallelepiped, the volumes of the two parallelepipeds are the absolute values of the two sides of (2.55). The absolute value of a product is the product of the absolute values, so the first volume is \(|\det\mat F|\) times the second.
  5. Let \(\det(\vek a, \vek b, \vek c) \neq 0\). By definition 2.48 the orientation of a triple is the sign of its scalar triple product, that is, of the determinant of its vectors. By (2.55) the scalar triple product of the triple \(\mat F\vek a\), \(\mat F\vek b\), \(\mat F\vek c\) is the number \(\det\mat F\) times the nonzero number \(\det(\vek a, \vek b, \vek c)\). For \(\det\mat F > 0\) it therefore has the same sign as \(\det(\vek a, \vek b, \vek c)\), for \(\det\mat F < 0\) the opposite sign, and for \(\det\mat F = 0\) it is zero, and the triple \(\mat F\vek a\), \(\mat F\vek b\), \(\mat F\vek c\) has no orientation. So the two triples have the same orientation exactly when \(\det\mat F > 0\).

2. Small entries of the matrix \(\mat H\).

  1. The entries of the matrix \(\mat F = \mat I + \mat H\) are \(F_{\mathrm{xx}} = 1 + H_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(F_{\mathrm{yy}} = 1 + H_{\mathrm{yy}}\), \(F_{\mathrm{zz}} = 1 + H_{\mathrm{zz}}\) on the diagonal and \(F_{\mathrm{ij}} = H_{\mathrm{ij}}\) off it.
  2. The first term of (2.51) is the product of the diagonal entries. When we multiply out the brackets, we get
    \[(1 + H_{\mathrm{xx}})(1 + H_{\mathrm{yy}})(1 + H_{\mathrm{zz}}) = 1 + (H_{\mathrm{xx}} + H_{\mathrm{yy}} + H_{\mathrm{zz}}) + (H_{\mathrm{xx}} H_{\mathrm{yy}} + H_{\mathrm{xx}} H_{\mathrm{zz}} + H_{\mathrm{yy}} H_{\mathrm{zz}}) + H_{\mathrm{xx}} H_{\mathrm{yy}} H_{\mathrm{zz}} .\]
  3. The second and the third term of (2.51), \(H_{\mathrm{xy}} H_{\mathrm{yz}} H_{\mathrm{zx}}\) and \(H_{\mathrm{xz}} H_{\mathrm{yx}} H_{\mathrm{zy}}\), have no diagonal entries and are products of three entries of the matrix \(\mat H\).
  4. The terms with a minus have one diagonal entry each. For example \(-F_{\mathrm{xx}} F_{\mathrm{yz}} F_{\mathrm{zy}} = -(1 + H_{\mathrm{xx}})\,H_{\mathrm{yz}} H_{\mathrm{zy}} = -H_{\mathrm{yz}} H_{\mathrm{zy}} - H_{\mathrm{xx}} H_{\mathrm{yz}} H_{\mathrm{zy}}\). Likewise \(-F_{\mathrm{xy}} F_{\mathrm{yx}} F_{\mathrm{zz}} = -H_{\mathrm{xy}} H_{\mathrm{yx}} - H_{\mathrm{xy}} H_{\mathrm{yx}} H_{\mathrm{zz}}\) and \(-F_{\mathrm{xz}} F_{\mathrm{yy}} F_{\mathrm{zx}} = -H_{\mathrm{xz}} H_{\mathrm{zx}} - H_{\mathrm{xz}} H_{\mathrm{yy}} H_{\mathrm{zx}}\).
  5. We order the terms by how many entries of the matrix \(\mat H\) they contain. The products of three entries from steps 2, 3 and 4 are exactly the six terms of (2.51) for the matrix \(\mat H\), so
    \[\begin{aligned} \det\mat F = 1 &+ (H_{\mathrm{xx}} + H_{\mathrm{yy}} + H_{\mathrm{zz}}) \\ &+ \big(H_{\mathrm{xx}} H_{\mathrm{yy}} + H_{\mathrm{xx}} H_{\mathrm{zz}} + H_{\mathrm{yy}} H_{\mathrm{zz}} \\ &\qquad - H_{\mathrm{xy}} H_{\mathrm{yx}} - H_{\mathrm{xz}} H_{\mathrm{zx}} - H_{\mathrm{yz}} H_{\mathrm{zy}}\big) \\ &+ \det\mat H . \end{aligned} \tag{2.56}\]
  6. The second bracket has six products of two entries of the matrix \(\mat H\). The absolute value of a product is the product of the absolute values, so each of them is at most \(g \cdot g = g^2\) in absolute value, and the bracket is at most \(6g^2\) in absolute value.
  7. The determinant of \(\mat H\) has six products of three entries, each at most \(g^3\) in absolute value, so \(|\det\mat H| \le 6g^3\).
  8. By steps 5 to 7, \(\big|\det\mat F - (1 + H_{\mathrm{xx}} + H_{\mathrm{yy}} + H_{\mathrm{zz}})\big| \le 6g^2 + 6g^3\).

Source: CM (3.3.18)-(3.3.20) (PDF pp. 125-126), BOWER §2.1.4 (PDF pp. 46-47) and §2.1.9 (PDF p. 51). The name: CM (PDF p. 119), BOWER (PDF p. 46). The error bound and the proofs are not from the sources.

For our three fibres the ratio between the volume of the parallelepiped after loading and the volume of the piece before loading is therefore

\[\frac{\det\big(\mat F(h\,\vek a), \mat F(h\,\vek b), \mat F(h\,\vek c)\big)}{\det(h\,\vek a, h\,\vek b, h\,\vek c)} = \frac{\det\mat F\ h^3\det(\vek a, \vek b, \vek c)}{h^3\det(\vek a, \vek b, \vek c)} = J ,\]

if \(J > 0\). The ratio depends neither on the length of the fibres nor on their directions. The Jacobian is a property of the displacement field at the point \(P\), not of the chosen piece. CM therefore describes it as the local ratio between the volume after loading and the volume before loading (CM, PDF p. 126). It is named after the German mathematician Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi (1804-1851), who also contributed to mechanics (KREYSZIG §10.3, PDF p. 456, footnote 3).

How good is the simplification of leaving out the remainders? Let us check on the hanging bar. A steel bar of length 10 m hangs under its own weight, held only at the centre \(O\) of its upper end, with the top face pulled up evenly, as in section 1.7.1. The axis \(x\) points from \(O\) vertically down, and \(P = (5\ \text{m},\ 4\ \text{mm},\ 0)\). From the table of partial derivatives at the point \(P\) in section 1.7.2,

\[\mat H = \begin{bmatrix} 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6} & -4{,}685 \cdot 10^{-10} & 0 \\ 4{,}685 \cdot 10^{-10} & -5{,}856 \cdot 10^{-7} & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -5{,}856 \cdot 10^{-7} \end{bmatrix} ,\]

and by (2.51) \(J - 1 = 7{,}80798 \cdot 10^{-7}\). Now let us take three fibres of length \(h = 1\) mm from \(P\) in the directions of the axes, \(\vek a = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\vek b = \vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(\vek c = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), compute their vectors after loading exactly from the field (1.18), with no remainders left out, and compute the scalar triple product of these three vectors. The ratio of the volumes is \(1 + 7{,}80603 \cdot 10^{-7}\), which differs from \(J\) by \(1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-10}\). For fibres of length \(0{,}1\) mm the difference is \(1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-11}\), ten times smaller. The difference is proportional to the length of the fibres, because the remainders of this field are proportional to the square of the length of the step, as we saw in section 1.15. When we shorten the fibres, the ratio of the volumes approaches \(J\). In general, however, we have shown this here only for this example. The sources do the same as we do: CM replaces the fibres by the vectors \(\mat F\) times the fibres and leaves the remainders out (CM (3.3.18), PDF p. 125). The exact claim needs two things. First, the volume of a body with curved faces, such as the true piece after loading, which exact adding up over the body gives. Second, the rule for how such adding up changes when we describe the points of the body by other variables, and in this rule exactly the Jacobian appears (KREYSZIG §10.3, PDF pp. 455-456, for areas). We develop both in chapter 4, where in section 4.3.5 we prove that the ratio of the volumes of the true piece after and before loading approaches \(J\) for every piece that shrinks to the point \(P\), not only for the parallelepiped of the fibres.

What sign does \(J\) have? Before loading \(\mat H\) is the zero matrix, \(\mat F = \mat I\) and \(J = 1\). If \(J\) were \(0\), then by lemma 2.53 on a zero determinant there would be a nonzero vector \(\vek v\) with \(\mat F\vek v = \vek 0\). A short fibre in the direction \(\vek v\) would then shrink to its remainder, which is small compared with its length, and the piece would be flattened. But matter cannot be squeezed into nothing. CM therefore excludes \(J = 0\) as physically not realistic (CM, PDF pp. 119-120). If \(J\) were negative, the piece would change its orientation and become the mirror image of itself. Such a deformation is described, for example, by the displacement field \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = -2x\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\). The point with the coordinates \((x, y, z)\) moves to the point \((x - 2x, y, z) = (-x, y, z)\), its mirror image in the plane \(x = 0\). The only nonzero partial derivative is \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial x = -2\), so the deformation gradient \(\mat F = \mat I + \mat H\) has the numbers \(1 - 2 = -1\), \(1\) and \(1\) on the diagonal and zeros elsewhere, and by (2.51) \(J = (-1) \cdot 1 \cdot 1 = -1\). This field changes no distance. The differences of the coordinates \(y\) and \(z\) of two points stay the same, and the difference of their coordinates \(x\) only changes sign, \((-x_1) - (-x_2) = -(x_1 - x_2)\), so its square stays the same. By definition 1.17 the distance between two points is the square root of the sum of these squares. So no fibre shrinks to nothing. Why then do we not count such a deformation as possible?

The answer lies in how the body reaches its final shape. We apply the load gradually, and the body passes through intermediate shapes (CM §3.2.1, PDF p. 111). For the mirror image the simplest way to imagine them is to multiply the displacements by a number \(t\) between \(0\) and \(1\): \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = -2t\,x\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\). At \(t = 0\) nothing moves, at \(t = 1\) we get the mirror image, and the values of \(t\) in between give the intermediate shapes. The deformation gradient has the numbers \(1 - 2t\), \(1\) and \(1\) on the diagonal, so on this path

\[J(t) = 1 - 2t .\]

At \(t = \tfrac12\) we have \(J = 0\). Then the point \((x, y, z)\) moves to \((x - x, y, z) = (0, y, z)\), and the whole body is squeezed into the plane \(x = 0\) (figure 2.63, top). On this path the body would have to be flattened before it is mirrored, and matter cannot do that.

Can we avoid the flattening if we go to the mirror image along another path? Let us add to the path two terms that are zero at the start and at the end:

\[u_{\mathrm{x}} = -2t\,x + 2t(1 - t)\,y , \qquad u_{\mathrm{y}} = -2t(1 - t)\,x , \qquad u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0 .\]

At \(t = 0\) we have \(2t(1 - t) = 0\), and at \(t = 1\) as well, because then \(1 - t = 0\). So the path starts in the shape before loading and ends in the same mirror image as the first one. The deformation gradient is

\[\mat F(t) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 - 2t & 2t(1 - t) & 0 \\ -2t(1 - t) & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} .\]

Of the six terms of (2.51) only the first, \(F_{\mathrm{xx}} F_{\mathrm{yy}} F_{\mathrm{zz}} = 1 - 2t\), and the fifth, \(-F_{\mathrm{xy}} F_{\mathrm{yx}} F_{\mathrm{zz}} = -2t(1 - t) \cdot \big(-2t(1 - t)\big) = 4t^2(1 - t)^2\), are nonzero. Each of the other four terms contains \(F_{\mathrm{xz}}\) or \(F_{\mathrm{yz}}\), which are zero. So on this path

\[J(t) = 1 - 2t + 4t^2(1 - t)^2 . \tag{2.57}\]

At \(t = \tfrac12\) we now have \(J = 0 + 4 \cdot \tfrac14 \cdot \tfrac14 = 0{,}25\), and the piece is still a proper parallelogram (figure 2.63, bottom). At \(t = 1\), however, \(J = 1 - 2 + 0 = -1\). The graph in figure 2.64 shows that this \(J\) also passes through zero, somewhere near \(t = 0{,}61\). We cannot read this place off the formula (2.57), since we would have to solve an equation of the fourth degree.

We would like the conclusion the graph suggests for every path, not only for these two. It reads: a function that changes without jumps and is positive at the start and negative at the end is equal to zero somewhere in between. In the figure it looks self-evident, but it is not. The function \(f(x) = x^2 - 3\) is a polynomial, so by corollary 1.64 on the sum and product of continuous functions, item 2, it is continuous. We have \(f(1) = -2 < 0\) and \(f(2) = 1 > 0\), but between \(1\) and \(2\) the function \(f\) is zero only at the point \(\sqrt 3\). No rational number has square 3 (lemma 1.27), so \(\sqrt 3\) is not a fraction. If we knew only fractions, this function would go from a negative value to a positive one without being equal to zero anywhere. So the conclusion depends on the number line having no holes, and we must prove it.

We have in fact already seen the proof. A number \(c\) with \(f(c) = 0\) is called a zero (ničla) of the function \(f\). Bisection in section 1.10.1 searched for exactly the zero of the function \(f(x) = x^2 - 3\). The rule kept the right half when \(m_{\mathrm{k}}^2 < 3\), that is, \(f(m_{\mathrm{k}}) < 0\), and the left half when \(f(m_{\mathrm{k}}) \ge 0\). The first property of bisection, \(a_{\mathrm{k}}^2 < 3 \le b_{\mathrm{k}}^2\), says \(f(a_{\mathrm{k}}) < 0 \le f(b_{\mathrm{k}})\). We can use the same rule for every continuous function that is negative at the left endpoint and positive at the right one. The left endpoints then converge by the monotone convergence theorem 1.51, and the right ones converge to the same limit, because the lengths of the intervals are halved. Continuity then carries the inequalities \(f(a_{\mathrm{k}}) < 0\) and \(f(b_{\mathrm{k}}) \ge 0\) over to the limit.

Lemma 2.56 Zero of a continuous function (ničla zvezne funkcije)

Let \(a < b\) be real numbers and \(f\) a function whose input is a number \(x\) from the closed interval \([a, b]\) and whose output is a number \(f(x)\), and let \(f\) be continuous on \([a, b]\). If \(f(a) < 0 < f(b)\), there is a number \(c\) in the open interval \((a, b)\) with \(f(c) = 0\).

Proof

We must show that \(f\) has a zero strictly between \(a\) and \(b\). We find it by bisection, just as \(\sqrt 3\) in section 1.10.1.

Building the intervals. We build the intervals \([a_{\mathrm{k}}, b_{\mathrm{k}}]\) step by step, so that at every \(k\) two requirements hold. The first: \(a \le a_{\mathrm{k}} < b_{\mathrm{k}} \le b\). The second: \(f(a_{\mathrm{k}}) < 0 \le f(b_{\mathrm{k}})\).

  1. Basis. Let \(a_1 = a\) and \(b_1 = b\). The first requirement holds because \(a < b\), the second because \(f(a) < 0\) and \(f(b) > 0\).
  2. For some \(k\) let \(a_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(b_{\mathrm{k}}\) be already determined so that both requirements hold, and let \(m_{\mathrm{k}} = \tfrac12(a_{\mathrm{k}} + b_{\mathrm{k}})\) be the midpoint of the interval. The midpoint lies strictly between the endpoints (section 1.10.1), so by the first requirement \(a < m_{\mathrm{k}} < b\), and the value \(f(m_{\mathrm{k}})\) is defined.
  3. If \(f(m_{\mathrm{k}}) < 0\), let \(a_{\mathrm{k}+1} = m_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(b_{\mathrm{k}+1} = b_{\mathrm{k}}\). Otherwise \(f(m_{\mathrm{k}}) \ge 0\), and let \(a_{\mathrm{k}+1} = a_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(b_{\mathrm{k}+1} = m_{\mathrm{k}}\).
  4. In both cases both requirements also hold for \([a_{\mathrm{k}+1}, b_{\mathrm{k}+1}]\). The new endpoints are two of the numbers \(a_{\mathrm{k}} < m_{\mathrm{k}} < b_{\mathrm{k}}\), which all lie in \([a, b]\), and the left one is smaller than the right one. The new left endpoint is \(a_{\mathrm{k}}\) or the midpoint with \(f(m_{\mathrm{k}}) < 0\), and the new right endpoint is \(b_{\mathrm{k}}\) or the midpoint with \(f(m_{\mathrm{k}}) \ge 0\).
  5. Step 1 is the basis, and steps 2 to 4 are the induction step. So by the principle of induction 1.44, \(a_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(b_{\mathrm{k}}\) are determined for every natural number \(k\), and both requirements hold at every \(k\).

Properties.

  1. The left endpoints do not decrease and the right ones do not increase, \(a_{\mathrm{k}} \le a_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) and \(b_{\mathrm{k}+1} \le b_{\mathrm{k}}\), because a new endpoint is an old endpoint or the midpoint \(m_{\mathrm{k}}\), which lies between the old endpoints.
  2. The length of the interval is \(b_{\mathrm{k}} - a_{\mathrm{k}} = (b - a)/2^{k-1}\). At \(k = 1\) this is \(b - a = (b - a)/2^0\). Every step halves the length, because each half has the length \(m_{\mathrm{k}} - a_{\mathrm{k}} = b_{\mathrm{k}} - m_{\mathrm{k}} = \tfrac12(b_{\mathrm{k}} - a_{\mathrm{k}})\). So the formula at \(k\) gives the formula at \(k + 1\), and by the principle of induction the formula holds for every \(k\), just as in step 11 of the proof of the Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem 1.57.

The limit.

  1. The sequence of left endpoints \((a_{\mathrm{k}})\) is increasing by step 6 and bounded by the first requirement, since all its terms lie in \([a, b]\). So by the monotone convergence theorem 1.51 it converges to the number \(c = \sup\{a_{\mathrm{k}} : k \in \mathbb N\}\).
  2. By the first requirement \(a \le a_{\mathrm{k}} \le b\) for every \(k\), so by lemma 1.48 that limits preserve non-strict inequalities, item 2, also \(a \le c \le b\). So the function \(f\) is defined and continuous at \(c\).
  3. By step 7 the right endpoints are \(b_{\mathrm{k}} = a_{\mathrm{k}} + (b - a) \cdot 1/2^{k-1}\). The sequence \((1/2^{k-1})\) converges to \(0\) by corollary 1.47 on the limit of the powers of one half. So by lemma 1.60 on limit laws for sequences, item 2, the sequence \(\big((b - a)/2^{k-1}\big)\) converges to \((b - a) \cdot 0 = 0\), and by item 1 of the same lemma the sequence \((b_{\mathrm{k}})\) converges to \(c + 0 = c\).
  4. The terms of both sequences of endpoints lie in \([a, b]\), and both sequences converge to \(c\). So by lemma 1.61 on continuity through sequences, first direction, the sequences \(\big(f(a_{\mathrm{k}})\big)\) and \(\big(f(b_{\mathrm{k}})\big)\) converge to \(f(c)\).
  5. By the second requirement \(f(a_{\mathrm{k}}) < 0\), so also \(f(a_{\mathrm{k}}) \le 0\), for every \(k\). The constant sequence \(0, 0, 0, \dots\) converges to \(0\) (section 1.9.2). So by lemma 1.48 that limits preserve non-strict inequalities, item 1, \(f(c) \le 0\).
  6. By the second requirement \(0 \le f(b_{\mathrm{k}})\) for every \(k\). By the same item of the lemma, with the constant sequence \(0, 0, 0, \dots\) on the left side of the inequality, \(0 \le f(c)\).
  7. By steps 12 and 13, \(f(c) = 0\).
  8. Since \(f(c) = 0\), \(f(a) < 0\) and \(f(b) > 0\), the number \(c\) is neither \(a\) nor \(b\). So by step 9 \(a < c < b\), that is, \(c \in (a, b)\).

Source: LEBL1 Lemma 3.3.7 (PDF pp. 132-133), with the same rule for the halves. The source shows that the two sequences of endpoints have the same limit by computing the limit of the difference \(b_{\mathrm{n}} - a_{\mathrm{n}}\) as \(\lim 2^{1-n}(b - a) = 0\). We write the right endpoints through the left ones and the length of the interval and use the limit laws for sequences.

The proof repeated bisection for \(\sqrt 3\) from section 1.10.1, except that instead of the square of the midpoint it looks at the sign of the value \(f(m_{\mathrm{k}})\). The number \(c\) was supplied by the completeness axiom, hidden in the monotone convergence theorem, as for the Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem 1.57. That the length of the interval goes to zero is the corollary on the limit of the powers of one half, which rests on the Archimedean property 1.35 through lemma 1.39 on the limit of the sequence 1/n. We needed continuity in step 11, where we concluded from the convergence of the endpoints that the values converge. The strict inequality \(f(a_{\mathrm{k}}) < 0\) turned in step 12 into the non-strict \(f(c) \le 0\), because a limit preserves only non-strict inequalities. So the left endpoints give only \(f(c) \le 0\), the right ones \(f(c) \ge 0\), and only both inequalities together give \(f(c) = 0\). And what if at some \(k\) the value \(f(m_{\mathrm{k}})\) is exactly zero? Then we have already found the zero. The rule does not check for this separately. It keeps the left half, which has \(m_{\mathrm{k}}\) as its right endpoint, and the proof goes on unchanged.

The lemma speaks only about the value \(0\) and only about a function that is negative at the left endpoint. The Jacobian on our paths, however, is positive at the start, and in general we are interested in any value between the values at the endpoints. We get both extensions by subtracting a number from the function or the function from a number.

Theorem 2.57 Intermediate value theorem (izrek o vmesni vrednosti)

Let \(a < b\) be real numbers and \(f\) a function whose input is a number \(x\) from the closed interval \([a, b]\) and whose output is a number \(f(x)\), and let \(f\) be continuous on \([a, b]\). If \(y\) is a number with \(f(a) < y < f(b)\) or \(f(a) > y > f(b)\), there is a number \(c\) in the open interval \((a, b)\) with \(f(c) = y\).

Proof

We must show that \(f\) takes the value \(y\) strictly between \(a\) and \(b\). In both cases we build a function \(g\) that is negative at \(a\), positive at \(b\) and zero exactly where \(f\) equals \(y\), and we use the lemma on the zero of a continuous function.

1. The case \(f(a) < y < f(b)\).

  1. Let \(g(x) = f(x) + (-y)\) for every \(x \in [a, b]\).
  2. The constant function with the value \(-y\) is continuous at every point, because \(|(-y) - (-y)| = 0 < \eta\) for every \(\eta > 0\) and every \(x\), so every band \(\delta\) will do. So by corollary 1.64 on the sum and product of continuous functions, item 1, the sum \(g\) is continuous at every point of the interval \([a, b]\).
  3. We have \(g(a) = f(a) - y < 0\) and \(g(b) = f(b) - y > 0\).
  4. By lemma 2.56 on the zero of a continuous function there is \(c \in (a, b)\) with \(g(c) = 0\). Then \(f(c) - y = 0\), that is, \(f(c) = y\).

2. The case \(f(a) > y > f(b)\).

  1. Let \(g(x) = y + (-1) \cdot f(x)\) for every \(x \in [a, b]\).
  2. By corollary 1.64 on the sum and product of continuous functions, item 1, the multiple \((-1) \cdot f\) is continuous at every point of the interval \([a, b]\). The constant function with the value \(y\) is continuous as in step 2 of the first case, so by the same item of the corollary the sum \(g\) is continuous.
  3. We have \(g(a) = y - f(a) < 0\) and \(g(b) = y - f(b) > 0\).
  4. By lemma 2.56 on the zero of a continuous function there is \(c \in (a, b)\) with \(g(c) = 0\). Then \(y - f(c) = 0\), that is, \(f(c) = y\).

Source: LEBL1 Thm 3.3.8 (PDF p. 133), where it is called Bolzano's intermediate value theorem, with the same proof.

The theorem says that a function without jumps cannot skip any intermediate value on its way from \(f(a)\) to \(f(b)\) (figure 2.65, left). It says only that such a \(c\) exists, not how many there are. In the figure the equation \(f(c) = y\) has three solutions, \(c_1\), \(c_2\) and \(c_3\), and the proof of the lemma finds only one of them.

Continuity is needed. Take the steel bar of length 10 m from section 1.12.2, which hangs under its own weight and has at the depth 5 m a pin with two weights that together weigh 20 N. The force \(S(x)\) with which the cross section at the depth \(x\) carries everything below it falls from \(S(0) = 80{,}10\) N at the top to \(S(10\ \text{m}) = 0\) at the lower end. But it takes the value \(40\) N, which lies in between, nowhere (figure 2.65, right). Above the pin \(S(x) \ge 50{,}05\) N by (1.27), below it \(S(x) < 30{,}05\) N, and at 5 m the function jumps over all values between them. The completeness axiom is needed too, as the function \(x^2 - 3\) showed, which has no zero among the fractions.

Let us return to the second path to the mirror image. Its Jacobian \(J(t) = 1 - 2t + 4t^2(1 - t)^2\) from (2.57) is a polynomial in the variable \(t\), so by corollary 1.64 on the sum and product of continuous functions, item 2, it is continuous on the interval \([0, 1]\). We have \(J(0) = 1 > 0 > -1 = J(1)\). So by the intermediate value theorem with \(y = 0\), which lies between \(J(0)\) and \(J(1)\), there is \(c \in (0, 1)\) with \(J(c) = 0\), and this without solving an equation of the fourth degree. Since the value at the left endpoint is larger than \(y = 0\), the proof of the theorem used the lemma on the zero of a continuous function on the function \(g(t) = 0 - J(t) = -J(t)\). Bisection for \(g\) keeps the right half when \(g(m_{\mathrm{k}}) < 0\), that is, \(J(m_{\mathrm{k}}) > 0\), and the left half when \(J(m_{\mathrm{k}}) \le 0\). Now we can state the conclusion about the sign of \(J\) precisely. First let us say what we assume about the loading.

  1. Loading parameter. We describe the loading by a number \(t\) from the interval \([0, 1]\), which we call the loading parameter (parameter obremenitve). At \(t = 0\) the body is before loading, at \(t = 1\) in the final state, and the values of \(t\) in between correspond to the intermediate states. We may think of \(t\) as the fraction of the loading time that has already passed. CM describes a body loaded slowly in this way: it occupies one intermediate shape after another, and CM calls the shape at the time \(t\) a configuration (CM §3.2.1, PDF p. 111).
  2. A displacement field for every \(t\). For every \(t\) the body has a continuously differentiable displacement field (definition 1.90), measured from the shape before loading, and \(P\) is an interior point of the body. We write \(\mat H(t)\) for the displacement gradient at \(P\) at this \(t\), \(\mat F(t) = \mat I + \mat H(t)\) for the deformation gradient, and \(J(t) = \det\mat F(t)\) for the Jacobian. Before loading nothing moves, so \(\mat H(0) = \mat 0\).
  3. No jumps. Each of the nine entries \(H_{\mathrm{ij}}(t)\), the partial derivative \(\partial u_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}\) at the point \(P\) at the parameter \(t\), is a continuous function of the variable \(t\) on \([0, 1]\) (definition 1.58 of continuity). This is an idealization of gradual loading: the shape of the piece at \(P\) does not change in an instant. Assumptions 2 and 3 fail if the body tears at \(P\).
  4. Matter is not squeezed into nothing. At no \(t\) is \(J(t) = 0\). Every intermediate state is a shape of the same body, so the argument by which we excluded \(J = 0\) above holds for it: the piece at \(P\) would be flattened (CM, PDF pp. 119-120).
Corollary 2.58 Positive Jacobian (pozitivna Jacobijeva determinanta)

For every number \(t\) from the interval \([0, 1]\) let a \(3 \times 3\) matrix \(\mat H(t)\) be given whose entries \(H_{\mathrm{ij}}(t)\) are continuous functions of the variable \(t\) on \([0, 1]\). Let \(\mat H(0) = \mat 0\), and let \(J(t) = \det\big(\mat I + \mat H(t)\big) \neq 0\) for every \(t \in [0, 1]\). Then \(J(t) > 0\) for every \(t \in [0, 1]\).

Proof

We must show that \(J\) is negative at no point of the interval \([0, 1]\). We argue by contradiction, with the intermediate value theorem.

  1. The entries of the matrix \(\mat F(t) = \mat I + \mat H(t)\) are \(F_{\mathrm{ij}}(t) = 1 + H_{\mathrm{ij}}(t)\) for \(\ix{i} = \ix{j}\) and \(F_{\mathrm{ij}}(t) = H_{\mathrm{ij}}(t)\) for \(\ix{i} \neq \ix{j}\). The constant function with the value \(1\) is continuous at every point, because \(|1 - 1| = 0 < \eta\) for every \(\eta > 0\). So by corollary 1.64 on the sum and product of continuous functions, item 1, all entries \(F_{\mathrm{ij}}\) are continuous on \([0, 1]\).
  2. By (2.51), \(J(t)\) is the sum of six products of three entries of the matrix \(\mat F(t)\) each, three of which are multiplied by \(-1\). A product of three functions is a product of two, multiplied by the third. So by item 1 of the same corollary, applied to each product, multiple and sum separately, \(J\) is continuous on \([0, 1]\).
  3. We have \(J(0) = \det\mat I = 1\). By (2.51), for the matrix \(\mat I\) only the first term, \(1 \cdot 1 \cdot 1\), is nonzero, because every other term contains an entry off the diagonal, which is zero.
  4. Suppose that \(J(s) < 0\) for some \(s \in [0, 1]\). Since \(J(0) = 1\), we have \(s \neq 0\), so \(s > 0\), and \([0, s]\) is an interval.
  5. The function \(J\) is continuous on \([0, s]\) too. The continuity condition (1.26) on the set \([0, s]\) checks only some of the points that it checks on \([0, 1]\), so the same band \(\delta\) will do for it.
  6. We have \(J(0) = 1 > 0 > J(s)\). By the intermediate value theorem 2.57 on the interval \([0, s]\), with the value \(y = 0\), which lies between \(J(0)\) and \(J(s)\), there is \(c \in (0, s)\) with \(J(c) = 0\).
  7. The number \(c\) lies in \([0, 1]\), so by the hypothesis \(J(c) \neq 0\). This contradicts step 6, so the supposition of step 4 is false.
  8. So \(J(t) \ge 0\) for every \(t \in [0, 1]\), and since \(J(t) \neq 0\) by the hypothesis, \(J(t) > 0\).

Source: The corollary and its proof are not from the sources. CM assumes that the Jacobian is positive (CM, PDF p. 126), BOWER requires it for every physically possible displacement field (BOWER §2.1.4, PDF p. 47).

Assumptions 1 to 4 give exactly the hypotheses of the corollary. So \(J(t) > 0\) at every \(t\), in particular in the final state at \(t = 1\). Both paths to the mirror image violate the fourth assumption, the first at \(t = \tfrac12\), the second at \(t = c\), and the corollary says that every path that ends at \(J < 0\) would violate it. CM and BOWER only require the Jacobian to be positive. CM assumes it so that the orientation of the fibres is preserved (CM, PDF p. 126), and BOWER says that it must hold for every physically possible displacement field, because matter cannot disappear (BOWER §2.1.4, PDF p. 47). The corollary shows that for gradual loading the requirement that matter is not squeezed into nothing is enough. The mirror image need not be excluded separately.

For \(J > 0\) the parallelepiped after loading has \(J\) times the volume it had before. The volume grows if \(J > 1\) and shrinks if \(J < 1\). For \(J = 1\) it does not change. In section 1.6.2 we called such a deformation isochoric (CM §3.3.2.1, PDF p. 122).

There remains the relation with strains. By (1.51) the diagonal entries of the small strain tensor are \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \tfrac12(H_{\mathrm{xx}} + H_{\mathrm{xx}}) = H_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = H_{\mathrm{yy}}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = H_{\mathrm{zz}}\). The sum \(H_{\mathrm{xx}} + H_{\mathrm{yy}} + H_{\mathrm{zz}}\) in item 2 of the lemma is therefore \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\), the trace of the small strain tensor from section 1.17. For small partial derivatives therefore

\[J - 1 \approx \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} ,\]

with an error of at most \(6g^2 + 6g^3\), where \(g\) is the largest absolute value of the nine partial derivatives of the displacements. For \(g = 0{,}001\) this is at most \(6{,}006 \cdot 10^{-6}\). The normal strains are at most \(g\) in absolute value, and the error is smaller by about a factor \(g\), just as for the sheared cube the term with the square of the shift was smaller than the term with the shift itself by a factor of the ratio of the shift to the height. This is the relation we announced in section 1.17, now with a derivation and with a bound for the error (BOWER §2.1.9, PDF p. 51). For small strains the relative change of volume of a small piece is the sum of the normal strains. The shear strains, which are off the diagonal, appear only in the second bracket of (2.56) and change the volume only by terms of size \(g^2\).

Let us test this on all three stories we know.

The sheared cube. A steel cube with the edge 10 mm is glued to a table by its bottom face, and we pushed its top face by 10 µm in the direction of the axis \(x\), with the axis \(z\) pointing up. The displacement field was \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = 0{,}001\,z\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\) ((1.16)). The only nonzero partial derivative is \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial z = 0{,}001\), so \(\mat F\) has ones on the diagonal, the number \(0{,}001\) at the place \(xz\), and zeros elsewhere. Of the six terms of (2.51) only the product of the diagonal entries is nonzero. Every other term contains at least one entry below the diagonal, at the place \(yx\), \(zx\) or \(zy\), and these are all zero. So \(J = 1 \cdot 1 \cdot 1 = 1\), exactly, not only approximately. The same holds for every push, even a large one, since the number \(0{,}001\) does not appear in \(J\) at all. The column \(z\) of the matrix \(\mat F\) is \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} + 0{,}001\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), so a multiple of the column \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) has been added to the column \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\). As in figure 2.59 this does not change the determinant: \(\det(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}, \vek e_{\mathrm{y}}, \vek e_{\mathrm{z}} + 0{,}001\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = \det(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}, \vek e_{\mathrm{y}}, \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}) + 0{,}001\det(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}, \vek e_{\mathrm{y}}, \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = 1 + 0\). The approximation agrees too: \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\). Simple shear is isochoric, as CM also says for the same kind of example (CM Example 3.3.1, PDF pp. 120 and 122, CM (3.3.15), PDF p. 124).

The hanging bar. At the point \(P\) the sum of the normal strains is

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6} - 2 \cdot 5{,}856 \cdot 10^{-7} = 7{,}808 \cdot 10^{-7} ,\]

and the exact value is \(J - 1 = 7{,}80798 \cdot 10^{-7}\). The difference is \(1{,}9 \cdot 10^{-12}\), four hundred thousand times less than the change itself. So the volume of a piece at \(P\) grows by \(7{,}808 \cdot 10^{-7}\) of its value. Why exactly \(7{,}808 \cdot 10^{-7}\), which is \(0{,}4\) times the strain \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6}\) along the bar? The piece gets longer along the bar by the fraction \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), and across the bar it shrinks in both directions by \(0{,}3\) times this fraction, because \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = -0{,}3\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) (figure 2.67). So its volume is multiplied by

\[(1 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}})(1 - 0{,}3\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}})(1 - 0{,}3\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}) \approx 1 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} - 0{,}3\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} - 0{,}3\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = 1 + 0{,}4\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} ,\]

where we left out the products of two or three strains. The lengthening along the bar is larger than the total shrinking across it, so the volume of steel in a bar under tension grows a little. This holds as long as the field (1.18) holds, that is, far from the place where a real ceiling holds the bar, and as long as the steel does not deform permanently.

A rigid rotation. In section 1.17 we turned a thin square plate lying in the plane of the axes \(x\) and \(y\) as a whole about the axis \(z\), without deforming it. The rotation was given by the pair \(c = 0{,}6\), \(s = 0{,}8\), for which \(c^2 + s^2 = 1\). The displacement field was \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = (c - 1)\,x - s\,y\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = s\,x + (c - 1)\,y\), \(u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\), and the deformation gradient

\[\mat F = \begin{bmatrix} 0{,}6 & -0{,}8 & 0 \\ 0{,}8 & 0{,}6 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} .\]

By (2.51), \(J = 0{,}6 \cdot 0{,}6 \cdot 1 - (-0{,}8) \cdot 0{,}8 \cdot 1 = 0{,}36 + 0{,}64 = 1\). A rigid rotation does not change the volume, as it must not, since it changes no distance. But the sum of the normal strains is \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = (c - 1) + (c - 1) + 0 = -0{,}8\). The approximation would predict that the volume shrinks by \(80\) %, which is wrong. Here the partial derivatives are not small, since \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial y = -0{,}8\), and the error bound \(6g^2 + 6g^3\) with \(g = 0{,}8\) is not small. In (2.56) the second bracket, which is \(0{,}8\), exactly cancels the first, and \(\det\mat H\) is zero. So the relation \(J - 1 \approx \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\) holds only when all partial derivatives of the displacements are small, also those that describe a rotation. The exact formula \(J = \det\mat F\) holds always.

The determinant has given us three tools. It tells whether a matrix sends some nonzero vector to zero, which we proved with the columns and the rows as edges of parallelepipeds. It tells when a matrix has an inverse matrix, and it also computes the inverse from the vector products of the columns. It tells how many times the volume of a small piece of a body grows, for which we needed the rule for the determinant of a product. With it we can now look for faces on which the traction vector points along the normal. In pure shear we saw that for such a face \(\det(\ten\sigma - \tau_0\,\mat I) = 0\). In the next section we will find, for any stress matrix, all numbers \(\lambda\) with \(\det(\ten\sigma - \lambda\,\mat I) = 0\), and the faces that belong to them.
