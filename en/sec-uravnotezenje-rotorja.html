Chapter 23 Reciprocating engines: dynamics, balancing and vibration isolation

23.1 Balancing a rigid rotor

A car wheel that is badly balanced after a tyre change shakes the steering wheel at 100 km/h. The drum of a washing machine, in which the laundry has clumped on one side, moves the machine across the floor during the spin. In both cases a body turns about an axis held by two bearings (ležaja), the parts on which the shaft rests and which allow it no motion except turning. Every part of a turning body runs on a circle, so it needs a force towards the axis. If these forces cancel inside the body, the bearings feel nothing. If they do not, the bearings must be pulled now one way, now the other, and this at every turn. In section 9.8.1 we computed this for one small mass \(m_{\mathrm{u}}\) at a distance \(a_{\mathrm{u}}\) from the axis, which gives the force \(m_{\mathrm{u}} a_{\mathrm{u}}\Omega^2\) (example 9.76). A rotor (rotor, named after turning) has two bearings and is long, so its mass is spread along its length. Three questions interest us. What force must each bearing give? When are both forces zero? Where must we add how much mass to make them zero?

We build the rotor in figure 23.2 and state the model as we go. The rotor \(F\) is our example, not data from a source.

We begin with the axis \(z\) about which the rotor turns. The axis is a fixed line, and the angular speed \(\Omega\) is constant. It is held by bearing \(A\) at \(z = 0\) and bearing \(B\) at \(z = L\), at a distance \(L\). The bearings are fixed supports, which is an idealization: in real machines they sit on springy mounts, which we treat later in this chapter. Each bearing may give a force perpendicular to the axis. The axial position is held by bearing \(A\) with an axial force, but this force is zero in our motion, since no point of the rotor moves along the axis. We do not include the weight of the rotor or the torque of the drive. For a horizontal axis the weight gives each bearing a constant force that does not depend on the turn of the rotor, and for a vertical axis it acts along the axis. The bearings cannot give a torque about the axis, the drive gives it. These forces and the torque add to what we are looking for and do not change it.

About the axis is the rotor itself, a rigid body (definition 1.11): the distances between its points do not change, so it does not bend. This is the second idealization. At high speeds a real shaft bends and behaves differently, which chapter 24 treats. Here we assume that the rotational frequency is well below the first bending natural frequency of the shaft, so that there is no bending. How far below, we do not compute here, because that needs the bending model of that chapter. FPGL (PDF p. 358) notes in this connection that a "flexible machine" is a machine that operates near or above the first natural frequency of the system, and that such a machine can still have a rigid rotor. Let the rotor without the weights that we shall add be symmetric: after a half turn about the axis it passes into itself, for example a cylinder of uniform material. To each of its points there corresponds a point on the opposite side of the axis, at the same height \(z\), and their accelerations are opposite.

To this rotor we add two small weights, each of mass \(0{,}05\) kg at the radius \(e = 0{,}2\) m from the axis. Weight 1 is in the plane \(z_1 = 0{,}1\) m, above the axis at time \(0\), and weight 2 is in the plane \(z_2 = 0{,}4\) m on the opposite side. The distances \(z_1\) and \(z_2\) are measured from bearing \(A\). The view from the end of the axis is on the right: in it the weights lie on a horizontal diameter, one on each side, and the rotation \(\Omega\) is counterclockwise, the radius \(e\) is the distance of the weights from the centre.

What must the bearings give? Each weight runs on a circle of radius \(e\) with angular speed \(\Omega\), so it has an acceleration \(e\Omega^2\) towards the axis (example 9.76). By Newton's second law it therefore needs a force \(F_{\mathrm{m}} = m\,e\,\Omega^2\) towards the axis, which the rest of the rotor provides. At \(3000\) rpm we have \(\Omega = 2\pi\cdot 3000/60 = 314{,}16\) 1/s and

\[F_{\mathrm{m}} = 0{,}05\cdot 0{,}2\cdot 314{,}16^2 = 986{,}96\ \text{N} .\]

At time \(0\) weight 1 is on the \(x\) axis upwards, so it needs the force \(-F_{\mathrm{m}}\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), and weight 2 needs the force \(+F_{\mathrm{m}}\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). The rest of the rotor, that is the symmetric part, needs no resultant and no moment, since the pairs of points on opposite sides of the axis cancel. Therefore, by theorem 8.82 (the resultant of the external forces is the sum of \(m\,\vek a\) of the parts of the body, the moment about a point \(O\) is the sum of the moments of these products), the system of external forces, which are only the forces \(A_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(B_{\mathrm{x}}\) of the bearings, must have the same resultant and the same moment about \(A\) as the forces on the weights. The moment of a force \(F_{\mathrm{x}}\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) at the point \(z\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) about \(A\) is \(z\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\times F_{\mathrm{x}}\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = zF_{\mathrm{x}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\). Thus

\[A_{\mathrm{x}} + B_{\mathrm{x}} = -F_{\mathrm{m}} + F_{\mathrm{m}} = 0 , \qquad L\,B_{\mathrm{x}} = z_1(-F_{\mathrm{m}}) + z_2 F_{\mathrm{m}} = 0{,}3\,F_{\mathrm{m}} .\]

Hence \(B_{\mathrm{x}} = 0{,}3\cdot 986{,}96/0{,}5 = 592{,}18\) N upwards and \(A_{\mathrm{x}} = -592{,}18\) N, as the figure shows.

The sum of the needed forces is zero, so the rotor is not pulled to either side, yet the bearings are loaded with \(592{,}18\) N. Why? The needed forces do not lie on the same line, but in the planes \(z_1\) and \(z_2\) on opposite sides of the axis. They form a couple with moment \(0{,}3\,F_{\mathrm{m}} = 296{,}09\) N m on the arm \(z_2 - z_1 = 0{,}3\) m, and the bearings balance it with a couple with arm \(L = 0{,}5\) m, that is with a force \(296{,}09/0{,}5 = 592{,}18\) N. When the rotor turns, the whole picture turns with it, so the bearing forces turn too. Each bearing feels a force of constant magnitude that goes round the axis once per turn, at \(3000\) rpm fifty times per second. Since \(F_{\mathrm{m}}\) is proportional to \(\Omega^2\), at \(1500\) rpm the force is four times smaller, \(148{,}04\) N.

The computation used only the product of the mass and the distance from the axis, \(m e\) (radius \(e\) in the end view), and the plane \(z\) of each weight (distances \(z_1\) and \(z_2\) from bearing \(A\)). We collect both quantities in the complex numbers of chapter 9 (definition 9.24): in the end view the point at position \((x, y)\) corresponds to the number \(\zeta = x + \mathrm{i}\,y\), whose modulus is the distance from the axis and whose argument is the angle from the \(x\) axis (definition 9.29). A weight of mass \(m\) at distance \(e\) and angle \(\alpha\) then has \(m e\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\alpha}\), and this product is our measure. A rotor may be any rigid body, so we define the measure by an integral over the body.

Definition 23.1 Unbalance of a rotor (neuravnoteženost rotorja)

Let a rigid body of density \(\rho\) and volume \(V\) turn about the axis \(z\) and let \(\zeta_0 = x + \mathrm{i}\,y\) be the complex number of the point \((x, y, z)\) of the body at time \(0\) (end view, \(x\) and \(y\) as in figure 23.2). The unbalance (neuravnoteženost) of the body is the complex number

\[U = \int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\zeta_0\,\mathrm{d}V , \tag{23.1}\]

and the moment of unbalance (moment neuravnoteženosti) about the plane \(z = 0\) is

\[W = \int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,z\,\zeta_0\,\mathrm{d}V . \tag{23.2}\]

The units are kg m and kg m\(^2\), and both numbers depend only on the body and on the choice of coordinates: the axis \(z\), the direction of the axis \(x\) at time \(0\) and, for \(W\), the plane \(z = 0\). A part of the body of mass \(m_{\mathrm{i}}\) whose centre of mass lies in the plane \(z_{\mathrm{i}}\) at distance \(e_{\mathrm{i}}\) from the axis and at angle \(\alpha_{\mathrm{i}}\) has the unbalance \(U_{\mathrm{i}} = m_{\mathrm{i}} e_{\mathrm{i}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\alpha_{\mathrm{i}}}\) and, if we treat it as a point, \(W_{\mathrm{i}} = z_{\mathrm{i}} U_{\mathrm{i}}\). Unbalances and moments of parts add, because the integral is linear.

Source: FPGL §8.2 (PDF p. 359), equation (8.1): the force and moment of unbalance \(\big(\sum m_{\mathrm{i}}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{i}}\big)\Omega^2\) and \(\big(\sum m_{\mathrm{i}}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{i}} z_{\mathrm{i}}\big)\Omega^2\) with a complex radius \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{i}}\). NORTON §12.2 (PDF p. 675) the same sums by components \(x\) and \(y\) (equations 12.4). The notation with an integral over the body is ours. In FPGL \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{i}}\) has the same meaning as our \(e_{\mathrm{i}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\alpha_{\mathrm{i}}}\).

The name: a rotor is balanced when all the forces required by the turning of its parts cancel each other and nothing is left for the bearings. The unbalance tells how much is left. For us \(\lvert U\rvert\) is the same quantity that we wrote \(m_{\mathrm{u}} a_{\mathrm{u}}\) in chapter 9. The case of the two weights of rotor \(F\) (figure 23.2): \(U = 0{,}05\cdot 0{,}2\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\cdot 0} + 0{,}05\cdot 0{,}2\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\pi} = 0{,}01 - 0{,}01 = 0\) kg m and \(W = 0{,}1\cdot 0{,}01 - 0{,}4\cdot 0{,}01 = -0{,}003\) kg m\(^2\).

A point weight, like the weights in figure 23.2, is a simplification, and we say how good it is. For a part of mass \(m_{\mathrm{i}}\) with its centre at \((\zeta_{\mathrm{i}}, z_{\mathrm{i}})\), \(U_{\mathrm{i}}\) is exact, since that is the definition of the centre of mass (definition 8.81). For \(W_{\mathrm{i}}\) we write \(z = z_{\mathrm{i}} + (z - z_{\mathrm{i}})\) and \(\zeta_0 = \zeta_{\mathrm{i}} + (\zeta_0 - \zeta_{\mathrm{i}})\). The terms that contain only one difference are zero by the definition of the centre of mass, and what remains is \(W_{\mathrm{i}} = z_{\mathrm{i}} U_{\mathrm{i}} + \int\rho\,(z - z_{\mathrm{i}})(\zeta_0 - \zeta_{\mathrm{i}})\,\mathrm{d}V\). If the whole part lies in a ball of radius \(r\) about its centre of mass, each of the two differences is at most \(r\) in magnitude, so the last term is at most \(m_{\mathrm{i}} r^2\) in magnitude. A weight of mass \(0{,}05\) kg made of steel (\(7800\) kg/m\(^3\)) is a ball of diameter \(23{,}0\) mm and lies in a ball of radius \(r = 12{,}5\) mm about its centre. The bound of the error is therefore \(0{,}05\cdot 0{,}0125^2 = 7{,}8\cdot 10^{-6}\) kg m\(^2\), which compared with \(W_{\mathrm{i}} = 0{,}001\) kg m\(^2\) is \(0{,}78\ \%\). For a homogeneous ball the error is even zero. The reflection in the plane \(z = z_{\mathrm{i}}\) maps the ball onto itself, reverses the sign of \(z - z_{\mathrm{i}}\) and keeps \(\zeta_0 - \zeta_{\mathrm{i}}\), so the contributions of a point and of its mirror image cancel. We therefore compute rotor \(F\) with point weights, which are exact for homogeneous balls.

It is now not hard to state the general result. We took rotor \(F\) and obtained both bearing forces from equilibrium. The same step for an arbitrary rigid body gives the theorem.

Theorem 23.2 Bearing forces of a rigid rotor (sile ležajev togega rotorja)

Let a rigid body of mass \(m\) turn with constant angular speed \(\Omega\) about the axis \(z\) as the rotor in figure 23.2, held by bearing \(A\) in the plane \(z = 0\) and bearing \(B\) in the plane \(z = L > 0\). The bearings give forces perpendicular to the axis, and bearing \(A\) may also give an axial force. We do not include the weights and the torque of the drive. Let \(U\) and \(W\) be the unbalance and its moment from definition 23.1. Denote by \(A\) and \(B\) the complex numbers \(A_{\mathrm{x}} + \mathrm{i}A_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(B_{\mathrm{x}} + \mathrm{i}B_{\mathrm{y}}\) of the bearing forces on the body perpendicular to the axis. Then for every time \(t\)

\[B = -\frac{\Omega^2 W}{L}\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t} , \qquad A = -\Omega^2\Big(U - \frac{W}{L}\Big)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t} , \tag{23.3}\]

and the axial force on \(A\) is zero. The force of each bearing therefore has constant magnitude, proportional to \(\Omega^2\), and turns with the rotor once per turn. The forces of the bearings on the body are opposite to the forces of the body on the bearings.

Proof

We must show that both formulas follow from the two equations of theorem 8.82, which is the computation with rotor \(F\) in figure 23.2 for an arbitrary body. First we compute the acceleration of a point, then the resultant and the moment, then we equate them with the bearings.

  1. At time \(t\) a point of the body is in the end view at \(\zeta = \zeta_0\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\) and at the same height \(z\), since the motion is a rotation about the axis \(z\). By the theorem on the properties of the complex exponential function (9.34, item 3) \(\ddot\zeta = (\mathrm{i}\Omega)^2\zeta = -\Omega^2\zeta\) and \(\ddot z = 0\). The acceleration thus has components \(a_{\mathrm{x}} = -\Omega^2 x\), \(a_{\mathrm{y}} = -\Omega^2 y\), \(a_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\), which means \(a_{\mathrm{x}} + \mathrm{i}a_{\mathrm{y}} = -\Omega^2\zeta\). The body moves in the form that theorem 8.82 requires. The matrix \(\mat Q(t)\) of the turn about the axis \(z\) by the angle \(\Omega t\) is orthogonal and has twice continuously differentiable elements, and \(\vek x(t) = \mat Q(t)\,\vek x(0) = \mat Q(t)\,\vek x_{\mathrm{G}}(0) + \mat Q(t)\,\vek r_0\), so the position of the centre of mass \(\vek x_{\mathrm{G}}(t) = \mat Q(t)\,\vek x_{\mathrm{G}}(0)\) is twice continuously differentiable too.
  2. The resultant of the forces is \(\int_{\mathrm{V}}\rho\,\vek a\,\mathrm{d}V\) (equation (8.77)). The components \(x\) and \(y\) together give \(\int_{\mathrm{V}}\rho\,(a_{\mathrm{x}} + \mathrm{i}a_{\mathrm{y}})\,\mathrm{d}V = -\Omega^2\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\int_{\mathrm{V}}\rho\,\zeta_0\,\mathrm{d}V = -\Omega^2 U\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\), because the factor \(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\) does not depend on the point. The component \(z\) is zero.
  3. The only external forces are the bearing forces. Their sum perpendicular to the axis is therefore \(A + B = -\Omega^2U\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\) and the axial force is zero, because \(\int\rho\,a_{\mathrm{z}}\,\mathrm{d}V = 0\).
  4. The moment about the point \(A\) (the origin) is \(\int_{\mathrm{V}}\vek r\times\rho\,\vek a\,\mathrm{d}V\) with \(\vek r = (x, y, z)\) and \(\vek a = (-\Omega^2x, -\Omega^2y, 0)\). By the definition of the vector product \(\vek r\times\vek a = \Omega^2(zy,\ -zx,\ 0)\), because \(x\,(-\Omega^2y) - y\,(-\Omega^2x) = 0\). The component about the axis \(z\) is therefore zero and \(M_{\mathrm{x}} + \mathrm{i}M_{\mathrm{y}} = \Omega^2\int\rho\,z\,(y - \mathrm{i}x)\,\mathrm{d}V = -\mathrm{i}\,\Omega^2\int\rho\,z\,\zeta\,\mathrm{d}V = -\mathrm{i}\,\Omega^2 W\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\).
  5. Bearing \(A\) has no moment about \(A\). The force \(\vek B = (B_{\mathrm{x}}, B_{\mathrm{y}}, 0)\) at the point \((0, 0, L)\) has the moment \((0, 0, L)\times(B_{\mathrm{x}}, B_{\mathrm{y}}, 0) = (-L B_{\mathrm{y}},\ L B_{\mathrm{x}},\ 0)\), so \(M_{\mathrm{x}} + \mathrm{i}M_{\mathrm{y}} = \mathrm{i}\,L\,B\).
  6. We equate steps 4 and 5: \(\mathrm{i}\,L\,B = -\mathrm{i}\,\Omega^2W\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\), whence \(B = -\Omega^2W\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}/L\). From step 3, \(A = -\Omega^2U\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t} - B\), which is the second formula.
  7. The modulus \(\lvert\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\rvert = 1\) (theorem 9.34, item 2) is constant, so \(\lvert A\rvert\) and \(\lvert B\rvert\) are constant and the argument grows with \(\Omega t\), so the forces turn once per turn.

Source: The proof is not from a source. FPGL §8.2 (PDF p. 359) and NORTON §12.2 (PDF pp. 673-677) use the sum of the forces and moments of unbalance, see definition 23.1. Theorem 8.82 gives the resultant and moment of the body.

The theorem says that everything the bearings (\(A\) and \(B\)) feel from the rotor at constant speed is written in two complex numbers, \(U\) and \(W\), that is in four real numbers. Two bearings, each with the magnitude and the angle of its force, give exactly four numbers. The body enters only through \(U\) and \(W\), and \(\Omega\) only as the factor \(\Omega^2\). A check on rotor \(F\): \(U = 0\) and \(W = -0{,}003\) kg m\(^2\) give \(B = -314{,}16^2\cdot(-0{,}003)/0{,}5 = 592{,}18\) N and \(A = -314{,}16^2\cdot(0 + 0{,}006) = -592{,}18\) N at \(t = 0\), as we obtained from equilibrium. The force \(F_{\mathrm{m}}\) has disappeared from the theorem, and \(U\) and \(W\) remain. One more check (NumPy, six random weights, \(t = 0{,}0123\) s): the difference between the sum of \(m\vek a\) and \(A + B\) is \(10^{-12}\) N, the difference of the moments \(2\cdot 10^{-13}\) N m, so rounding.

An example that the theorem still tests is a rotor with a single weight (in figure 23.2 it would be in the plane midway between the bearings). With \(U_1 = 0{,}01\) kg m at angle \(0\) in the plane \(z = 0{,}25\) m, midway between the bearings, we have \(W = zU_1 = 0{,}0025\) kg m\(^2\) and \(B = -98\,696\cdot 0{,}0025/0{,}5 = -493{,}48\) N and \(A = -98\,696\,(0{,}01 - 0{,}005) = -493{,}48\) N at \(3000\) rpm. Each bearing carries half of the force \(\Omega^2 U_1 = 986{,}96\) N, as we would expect for a weight in the middle.

Definition 23.3 Statically balanced rotor (statično uravnotežen rotor)

A rigid rotor is statically balanced (statično uravnotežen) when its unbalance is \(U = 0\) (figure 23.2). By the definition of the centre of mass (definition 8.81) \(U = m\,\zeta_{\mathrm{G}}\) for the centre of mass \(G\) with \(\zeta_{\mathrm{G}}\) at time \(0\), so this means that the centre of mass lies on the axis of rotation.

Source: NORTON §12.1 (PDF p. 670): static balance means that the sum of all forces, d'Alembert forces included, is zero, and he also calls it single-plane balancing. FPGL §8.2 (PDF p. 358): in designing a rotor the centre of mass should be on the axis. The notation with \(U = 0\) is ours.

The name is so because this balance can already be seen at rest. A rotor on horizontal knife edges, without friction, stays turned in any position exactly when the weight has zero torque about the axis in every position. The torque of the weight about the axis is \(m g\,\lvert\zeta_{\mathrm{G}}\rvert\sin\varphi\), where \(\varphi\) is the angle between the vertical through the axis and the segment from the axis to the centre of mass, since \(\lvert\zeta_{\mathrm{G}}\rvert\sin\varphi\) is the horizontal distance of the centre of mass from the axis, and it is zero at every \(\varphi\) only when \(\zeta_{\mathrm{G}} = 0\), that is, when the centre of mass is on the axis. A car wheel that a fitter lays horizontally on a cone and moves weights on with a spirit level does exactly this (NORTON §12.7, PDF pp. 688-689). NORTON (PDF p. 670) at the same time warns that the name misleads: static balance also holds for motion, since it fulfils the sum of the forces that the turning gives. Theorem 23.2 shows why: the condition \(U = 0\) cancels the sum \(A + B\) at every \(\Omega\). Do the bearings then get nothing? Rotor \(F\) has \(U = 0\), so it is statically balanced, yet we computed \(592{,}18\) N on each bearing. Static balance is therefore not enough.

Definition 23.4 Dynamically balanced rotor (dinamično uravnotežen rotor)

A rigid rotor is dynamically balanced (dinamično uravnotežen) when \(U = 0\) and \(W = 0\) (figure 23.2, rotor \(F\) has \(W \ne 0\)). By theorem 23.2 we then have \(A = B = 0\) at every speed \(\Omega\), so the bearings give no force because of the rotation.

Source: NORTON §12.2 (PDF p. 673): dynamic balance requires the sum of forces zero (static balance) and the sum of moments zero, and he calls it two-plane balancing. FPGL §8.2 (PDF pp. 358-359): equations (8.2) and (8.3). The notation with \(U = 0\) and \(W = 0\) is ours.

The name: the unbalance that is in \(W\) (weights in different planes) shows itself only in rotation, when the bearings feel forces, so we call the condition \(W = 0\) dynamic. For rotor \(F\) we have \(W = -0{,}003 \ne 0\), so the rotor is not dynamically balanced. NORTON (PDF p. 673, figure 12-2) has the same case: two equal masses at the same radius, one opposite the other and separated along the shaft, have the sum of forces zero, but their forces form a couple that turns with the shaft and alternately raises and lowers the bearings. This is rotor \(F\) as we computed it.

What is the condition \(W = 0\) in the language of chapter 8 (rotor \(F\) in figure 23.2)? We related the unbalance \(U\) to the centre of mass, and \(W\) shows itself as the products of inertia.

Corollary 23.5 Dynamic balance and the principal axis of inertia (dinamično uravnoteženje in glavna os vztrajnosti)

A rigid rotor (figure 23.2) with inertia tensor \(\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\) about the centre of mass (definition 8.89) is dynamically balanced exactly when the centre of mass lies on the axis \(z\) and the axis \(z\) is a principal axis of inertia, that is \(\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = J_{\mathrm{zz}}\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) (corollary 8.95).

Proof

We must show that for \(U = 0\) the condition \(W = 0\) is the same as the condition \(J_{\mathrm{xz}} = J_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\) for axes parallel to \(z\) through the centre of mass (centre of mass on the axis).

  1. By definition 23.3, \(U = 0\) exactly when the centre of mass lies on the axis \(z\), at the point \((0, 0, z_{\mathrm{G}})\).
  2. Let \(U = 0\). The vector from the centre of mass to a point of the body is \(\vek r = (x, y, z - z_{\mathrm{G}})\). By the definition of the tensor (definition 8.89) \(\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = (J_{\mathrm{xz}}, J_{\mathrm{yz}}, J_{\mathrm{zz}})\) with \(J_{\mathrm{xz}} = -\int\rho\,x\,(z - z_{\mathrm{G}})\,\mathrm{d}V\) and \(J_{\mathrm{yz}} = -\int\rho\,y\,(z - z_{\mathrm{G}})\,\mathrm{d}V\).
  3. Hence \(J_{\mathrm{xz}} + \mathrm{i}J_{\mathrm{yz}} = -\int\rho\,(z - z_{\mathrm{G}})\,\zeta_0\,\mathrm{d}V = -(W - z_{\mathrm{G}} U) = -W\), because \(U = 0\).
  4. Therefore \(W = 0\) exactly when \(J_{\mathrm{xz}} = J_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\), that is \(\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = J_{\mathrm{zz}}\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\). At time \(t\), \(\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}(t) = \mat Q\,\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}(0)\,\mat Q^\T\) with the rotation \(\mat Q\) about \(z\) (lemma 8.90, item 3), which leaves \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) at rest, so the property holds at every time.

Source: The proof is not from a source. NORTON §12.2 (PDF p. 673) does not mention the inertia tensor, and neither does FPGL §8.2 (PDF pp. 358-359). The link to the principal axes of inertia (corollary 8.95) is ours.

For rotor \(F\) (figure 23.2), step 3 gives \(J_{\mathrm{xz}} = 0{,}003\) kg m\(^2\) and \(J_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\): the centre of mass is on the axis, but \(\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) has, besides \(J_{\mathrm{zz}}\), a component \(0{,}003\) in the direction \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), so the axis of rotation is not a principal axis and the rotor is not dynamically balanced. Its principal axis is tilted towards the axis \(z\).

A rotor from the factory often has small \(U\) and \(W\) that we must remove. We add or remove mass at chosen places of rotor \(F\) (figure 23.3). At how many places we must and where, the next theorem tells us.

Theorem 23.6 Two-plane balancing (uravnoteženje v dveh ravninah)

Let a rigid rotor have unbalance \(U\) and moment \(W\) (definition 23.1, figure 23.3) and let \(z_{\mathrm{c}1} \ne z_{\mathrm{c}2}\) be two planes on the rotor in which we may add a correction weight.

  1. There is exactly one pair of complex numbers \(U_{\mathrm{c}1}\), \(U_{\mathrm{c}2}\) such that the rotor with added weights (figure 23.3), with unbalances \(U_{\mathrm{c}1}\) in the plane \(z_{\mathrm{c}1}\) and \(U_{\mathrm{c}2}\) in the plane \(z_{\mathrm{c}2}\), satisfies \(U + U_{\mathrm{c}1} + U_{\mathrm{c}2} = 0\) and \(W + z_{\mathrm{c}1}U_{\mathrm{c}1} + z_{\mathrm{c}2}U_{\mathrm{c}2} = 0\), namely
    \[U_{\mathrm{c}1} = \frac{W - z_{\mathrm{c}2}U}{z_{\mathrm{c}2} - z_{\mathrm{c}1}} , \qquad U_{\mathrm{c}2} = \frac{z_{\mathrm{c}1}U - W}{z_{\mathrm{c}2} - z_{\mathrm{c}1}} . \tag{23.4}\]
    The correction weights have mass \(m_{\mathrm{c}} = \lvert U_{\mathrm{c}}\rvert/e_{\mathrm{c}}\) at the chosen radius \(e_{\mathrm{c}}\) at the angle \(\arg U_{\mathrm{c}}\) (or removed mass at the angle \(\arg U_{\mathrm{c}} + \pi\)). The rotor is then dynamically balanced at every speed.
  2. For \(z_{\mathrm{c}1} = z_{\mathrm{c}2} = z\) (both weights in the same plane in figure 23.3) balancing has a solution exactly when \(W = zU\), and the solution is not unique. If \(U = 0\) and \(W \ne 0\), balancing in one plane is not possible.
Proof

We must show (figure 23.3) that the system of two complex equations for two unknowns is solvable exactly when the determinant is non-zero, and what happens when it is zero.

  1. Unbalances and moments of parts add (definition 23.1). The balance conditions of definition 23.4 are therefore \(U_{\mathrm{c}1} + U_{\mathrm{c}2} = -U\) and \(z_{\mathrm{c}1}U_{\mathrm{c}1} + z_{\mathrm{c}2}U_{\mathrm{c}2} = -W\).
  2. The unknowns are \(U_{\mathrm{c}1}\) and \(U_{\mathrm{c}2}\) for the weights \(c_1\) and \(c_2\) of figure 23.3. We multiply the first equation by \(z_{\mathrm{c}1}\) and subtract it from the second: \((z_{\mathrm{c}2} - z_{\mathrm{c}1})\,U_{\mathrm{c}2} = z_{\mathrm{c}1}U - W\). For \(z_{\mathrm{c}2} \ne z_{\mathrm{c}1}\) this is \(U_{\mathrm{c}2}\) from (23.4), and \(U_{\mathrm{c}1} = -U - U_{\mathrm{c}2} = \dfrac{W - z_{\mathrm{c}2}U}{z_{\mathrm{c}2} - z_{\mathrm{c}1}}\). Each unknown is thus uniquely determined, so there is only one solution. Substitution confirms it: \(U_{\mathrm{c}1} + U_{\mathrm{c}2} = \dfrac{W - z_{\mathrm{c}2}U + z_{\mathrm{c}1}U - W}{z_{\mathrm{c}2} - z_{\mathrm{c}1}} = -U\) and \(z_{\mathrm{c}1}U_{\mathrm{c}1} + z_{\mathrm{c}2}U_{\mathrm{c}2} = \dfrac{z_{\mathrm{c}1}W - z_{\mathrm{c}1}z_{\mathrm{c}2}U + z_{\mathrm{c}2}z_{\mathrm{c}1}U - z_{\mathrm{c}2}W}{z_{\mathrm{c}2} - z_{\mathrm{c}1}} = -W\).
  3. A weight (like \(c_1\) in figure 23.3) of mass \(m_{\mathrm{c}}\) at radius \(e_{\mathrm{c}}\) at angle \(\alpha\) has unbalance \(m_{\mathrm{c}} e_{\mathrm{c}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\alpha}\) (definition 23.1), so \(m_{\mathrm{c}} e_{\mathrm{c}} = \lvert U_{\mathrm{c}}\rvert\) and \(\alpha = \arg U_{\mathrm{c}}\) (definition 9.29). A removed mass \(m_{\mathrm{c}}\) at angle \(\alpha + \pi\) has unbalance \(-m_{\mathrm{c}} e_{\mathrm{c}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\alpha + \pi)} = m_{\mathrm{c}} e_{\mathrm{c}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\alpha}\), because \(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\pi} = -1\) (theorem 9.34), the same as the added one. By step 1 the rotor is dynamically balanced, and by theorem 23.2 \(A = B = 0\) at every \(\Omega\), because \(U\) and \(W\) do not depend on \(\Omega\).
  4. For \(z_{\mathrm{c}1} = z_{\mathrm{c}2} = z\) the second equation is \(z\,(U_{\mathrm{c}1} + U_{\mathrm{c}2}) = -W\), and by the first \(U_{\mathrm{c}1} + U_{\mathrm{c}2} = -U\), so \(zU = W\). If this holds, every pair with sum \(-U\) solves both equations, so there are infinitely many solutions. If it does not hold, there is no solution. For \(U = 0\) the condition is \(W = 0\).

Source: FPGL §8.2 (PDF p. 359), equations (8.2) to (8.5): two balancing planes and a \(2\times 2\) matrix whose inverse gives an equation equal to equation (23.4). NORTON §12.2 (PDF pp. 674-677): two-plane balancing as a procedure and example 12-2. The proof by steps, part 2 too, is not from a source.

We check on rotor \(F\) and draw it once more in figure 23.3. On the same axis between bearings \(A\) and \(B\) at a distance \(L\) is the same rotor with weights 1 and 2. Let the correction planes be \(z_{\mathrm{c}1} = 0{,}05\) m and \(z_{\mathrm{c}2} = 0{,}45\) m, near the two ends of the rotor. With \(U = 0\) and \(W = -0{,}003\) kg m\(^2\), equation (23.4) gives

\[U_{\mathrm{c}1} = \frac{-0{,}003 - 0}{0{,}4} = -0{,}0075\ \text{kg m} , \qquad U_{\mathrm{c}2} = \frac{0 + 0{,}003}{0{,}4} = +0{,}0075\ \text{kg m} .\]

This is the unbalance \(0{,}0075\) kg m at angle \(\pi\) in the plane \(z_{\mathrm{c}1}\) and at angle \(0\) in the plane \(z_{\mathrm{c}2}\), so at radius \(0{,}15\) m it is \(50\) g each. The correction weights are drawn: \(c_1\) below the axis at bearing \(A\) and \(c_2\) above the axis at bearing \(B\). The needed forces on these two weights (orange) form a couple with the opposite moment to the couple of needed forces on the original weights \(1\) and \(2\) (blue): \(0{,}0075\cdot 314{,}16^2 = 740{,}22\) N on the arm \(0{,}4\) m gives \(296{,}09\) N m, as \(986{,}96\) N on the arm \(0{,}3\) m. By theorem 23.2, therefore, \(A = B = 0\): the total sum \(U + U_{\mathrm{c}1} + U_{\mathrm{c}2}\) is \(0\) and \(W + 0{,}05\,U_{\mathrm{c}1} + 0{,}45\,U_{\mathrm{c}2} = -0{,}003 - 0{,}000375 + 0{,}003375 = 0\).

The choice of planes is free (figure 23.3). If we add the correction weights in the planes of the bearings, \(z_{\mathrm{c}1} = 0\) and \(z_{\mathrm{c}2} = L\), then \(U_{\mathrm{c}1} = W/L = -0{,}006\) kg m and \(U_{\mathrm{c}2} = -W/L = +0{,}006\) kg m. A weight in the plane \(z = 0\) has \(W = 0\), so by equation (23.3) it does not change the force \(B\) and loads only bearing \(A\), and similarly a weight in the plane \(L\) by the second formula loads only bearing \(B\).

A check with another source (as in figure 23.3, but with other numbers): FPGL (PDF pp. 359-360), example 8.2.1, has three weights with \(0{,}2\) m radius and masses \(1{,}0\), \(1{,}1\), \(1{,}0\) kg at angles \(0\), \(126^\circ\), \(258^\circ\) in the planes \(0{,}45\), \(0{,}90\), \(1{,}35\) m, and balancing planes \(z = 0\) and \(z = 1{,}5\) m. Equation (23.4) gives (NumPy) \(U_{\mathrm{c}1} = -0{,}08412 - 0{,}05163\,\mathrm{i}\) and \(U_{\mathrm{c}2} = 0{,}05501 + 0{,}06928\,\mathrm{i}\) kg m, that is \(0{,}09870\) kg m at angle \(-148{,}46^\circ\) and \(0{,}08846\) kg m at angle \(51{,}55^\circ\), equal to the source (masses \(0{,}4935\) kg and \(0{,}4423\) kg at radius \(0{,}2\) m).

A test of the hypothesis \(z_{\mathrm{c}1} \ne z_{\mathrm{c}2}\) (figure 23.3): for rotor \(F\) a correction in one plane does not exist. Let the plane be \(z = 0{,}25\) m, in the middle. The conditions would be \(U_{\mathrm{c}} = -U = 0\) and \(z\,U_{\mathrm{c}} = -W = 0{,}003\), which contradict each other (step 4 of the proof). A weight in one plane could cancel \(W\) only with \(U_{\mathrm{c}} = -W/z\), but it would spoil \(U\), which was zero, and the rotor would become statically unbalanced. Such a correction, \(U_{\mathrm{c}} = 0{,}003/0{,}25 = 0{,}012\) kg m, gives by theorem 23.2 \(B = 0\), but \(\lvert A\rvert = 98\,696\cdot 0{,}012 = 1184{,}4\) N, twice as much as without a correction. Without a correction, \(U_{\mathrm{c}} = 0\), a couple with moment \(0{,}003\cdot 314{,}16^2 = 296{,}09\) N m remains, as before. This is why the balancing of a car wheel has two correction planes, the inner and outer edge of the rim, and is called "two-plane" (NORTON §12.2, PDF p. 674).

The unbalance need not be guessed, we measure it (as the bearing forces \(A_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(B_{\mathrm{x}}\) in figure 23.2). A balancing machine (stroj za uravnoteževanje) simply inverts theorem 23.2. NORTON §12.7 (PDF p. 689) describes a machine in which the rotor sits on a mandrel in two bearings, each on a suspension with a force gauge. At every turn the gauges give the magnitude of the force peak and its phase angle relative to a pulse that an encoder on the shaft gives once per turn. The rotor is released to spin on its own ("freewheel") so that the drive forces are not measured. In our notation this corresponds as follows. The magnitude and phase at a bearing are the complex number \(A\) or \(B\) of the theorem, and the encoder pulse fixes \(t = 0\) and with it the angle \(\alpha = 0\) on the rotor. From equation (23.3) follow \(W = -L\,B\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\Omega t}/\Omega^2\) and \(U = -(A + B)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\Omega t}/\Omega^2\), and from \(U\), \(W\) the correction weights by theorem 23.6. For rotor \(F\) the computation from \(A\) and \(B\) of the theorem gives \(U = 1{,}2\cdot 10^{-18}\) kg m, that is zero, and \(W = -0{,}003\) kg m\(^2\), so the same correction weights as above.

What does this bring us? The unbalance \(U\) and \(W\) are all that the rotor (figure 23.2) tells its bearings, and they do not depend on the speed. A rotor balanced at one speed is therefore balanced at every speed, as long as it is rigid, which is the assumption of this section. We shall need the same \(U\) and \(W\) later in this chapter for the counterweights of the crankshaft of an engine. The balancing of a rotor that bends, and balancing in the field from measurements at the bearings, is treated in chapter 24.
