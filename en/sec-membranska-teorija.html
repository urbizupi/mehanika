Chapter 22 Shells

22.4 Membrane theory of shells of revolution

A steel tube with mean radius \(R = 50\) mm and wall thickness \(h = 2\) mm is closed at both ends and filled with oil at pressure \(p = 3\) MPa. What does its wall carry? Do we need to know how the wall bends, or is it enough that the wall only pulls, like a rubber inner tube inflated with air? The pressure acts perpendicular to the wall, and the forces in the wall lie along it. So we ask what forces the wall must carry for every part of the tube to be in equilibrium.

The model is a thin shell (lupina, section 22.1) of isotropic elastic steel, \(E = 196\) GPa and \(\nu = 0{,}3\). The wall is so thin that we add the stresses over the thickness into forces per unit length, and the pressure acts on the middle surface. We shall name both simplifications and give each a bound when we meet numbers.

We cut the tube in two ways. Figure 22.12 shows the cross section perpendicular to the axis (left) and the longitudinal section (right).

In the left picture we keep the upper arc of the tube and cut it along the axis. From inside, the pressure \(p\) pushes on it perpendicular to the wall. The surface of the arc is curved and the pressure points in a different direction at every point, so we find the resultant (the sum of all forces) by components. Horizontal components cancel in every pair of points that are symmetric about the vertical axis. Vertical components add up to a force equal to the pressure times the area of the horizontal projection of the arc, as for the hydrostatic force on a curved wall. The projection is a rectangle of length \(L\) and width \(2R\), so the resultant of the pressure upward is \(p\cdot 2R\cdot L\). The body is at rest, so the wall below the cut must balance it. The wall pulls the upper arc downward in both cuts with a force \(N_\theta\) per unit length, together \(2N_\theta L\). From \(2N_\theta L = 2pRL\) it follows that \(N_\theta = pR = 150\) N/mm. We call \(N_\theta\) the hoop force (obročna sila), because it is the force in the iron hoops that hold a barrel together: it pulls along the circumference.

In the right picture we cut the tube across the axis and keep the left part. It is bounded by the end (dno), on which the oil pushes outward with the force \(p\pi R^2\) (pressure times the area of the end, a circle of radius \(R\)). The body is at rest, so the wall at the cut must hold it back with an equal force in the axial direction. The cut has circumference \(2\pi R\), and the wall pulls with a force \(N_\varphi\) per unit length of circumference. From \(N_\varphi\cdot 2\pi R = p\pi R^2\) it follows that \(N_\varphi = pR/2 = 75\) N/mm. For now we call \(N_\varphi\) the axial force, because it pulls along the tube axis. For a general shell we shall find it a name that holds there too. At \(h = 2\) mm the stresses are \(N_\theta/h = 75\) MPa and \(N_\varphi/h = 37{,}5\) MPa.

How good is this calculation? The cut goes through the wall and the pressure acts on its inner side, at radius \(a = R - h/2 = 49\) mm. The exact balance of half the tube is therefore \(2L\int\sigma\,\mathrm{d}r = p\cdot 2aL\), so the true force per unit length is \(pa = 147\) N/mm. Our \(pR = 150\) N/mm differs from it by \(2\) %, which is exactly \(h/(2R) = 0{,}02\). This is the size of the error we make by treating the wall as a surface of radius \(R\) (the explanation is in section 22.3, where we dropped the factor \(1 + z/R\)). For a thicker wall, for example the tube with radii 40 and 60 mm of theorem 11.27 (\(h/R = 0{,}4\)), the error is \(20\) %, and the calculation is no longer good.

From history

Navier's book on the strength of materials first appeared in print in 1826, and copies of his lectures had been given to his students since 1819 (TIMO, PDF p. 81). The last chapter of the book deals with thin shells (TIMO, PDF p. 86). Navier begins with a string that is perfectly flexible and inextensible and is loaded in the plane of the curve by a pressure \(p\) perpendicular to it. From the equilibrium of a piece of the string he finds that the tension \(S\) in the string is constant and that \(S/\rho = p\), where \(\rho\) is the radius of curvature: the curvature is proportional to the pressure at every point (TIMO, PDF p. 87). We see the reason on an arc of radius \(\rho\) and angle \(\psi\). The string pulls at both ends with a force \(S\), and the resultant of these two forces toward the center is \(2S\sin(\psi/2)\). The pressure \(p\) on this arc gives the resultant \(p\cdot 2\rho\sin(\psi/2)\), because the resultant of a pressure equals the pressure times the length of the chord \(2\rho\sin(\psi/2)\). Equating the two gives \(S = p\rho\).

He uses the same idea for a channel full of liquid, held up by uniformly distributed forces \(S\). He asks when the wall is not bent and answers that this happens when the curvature at a point is proportional to the depth \(y\) (TIMO, PDF p. 87). This is the same law, because the pressure of a liquid grows with depth. Finally he treats a thin shell pulled equally in two directions by a tension \(S\) and loaded by a pressure \(p\). From equilibrium he obtains the equation

\[S\Big(\frac1\rho + \frac1{\rho_1}\Big) = p ,\]

in which \(\rho\) and \(\rho_1\) are the radii of curvature of the shell in two directions. For a sphere he derives from it the stress \(\sigma = S/h = p\rho/(2h)\) for thickness \(h\) (TIMO, PDF p. 87). Navier also did an experiment. He loaded thin iron spheres of about 1 ft diameter (about 30 cm) and \(0{,}1\) in thickness (about 2,5 mm) with internal pressure up to rupture, and found that the breaking strength of the material was about the same as in a simple tension test (TIMO, PDF p. 87). The comparison is made possible by the equation \(\sigma = p\rho/(2h)\), which gives from the bursting pressure the stress in the wall at rupture, in the wall of a sphere the same in every direction of the plane. The agreement tells that the wall broke when this stress reached the same value as in a bar in a tension test, although the wall was stretched in two directions and the bar in only one. For his spheres the radius is \(6\) in and the thickness \(0{,}1\) in, so \(R/h = 60\) (our calculation).

Back to the tube. For a sphere of radius \(R\), Navier's equation has \(\rho = \rho_1 = R\), so \(S\cdot 2/R = p\) and \(S = pR/2\). This is the same force as the axial force of the tube \(N_\varphi = pR/2\), which we obtained with the cut across the axis. The tube has two different forces, \(N_\theta = pR\) is twice as large as \(N_\varphi = pR/2\), and for each we needed its own cut. For a general shell, where the radii \(R_1\) and \(R_2\) differ from point to point and the load is not the same everywhere, we cannot guess such cuts. We ask when the forces in the wall follow from equilibrium alone, without any equations for displacements. This is possible when the wall carries no bending moments and no transverse shear forces.

Definition 22.30 Membrane state of a shell (membransko stanje lupine)

A shell (section 22.1) has at every point its internal forces per unit length (definition 22.27): membrane forces \(N_1\), \(N_2\), \(N_{12}\) (along the middle surface, in N/mm), transverse shear forces \(Q_1\), \(Q_2\) and moments \(M_1\), \(M_2\), \(M_{12}\) (N mm/mm). The shell is in a membrane state (membransko stanje) if at every point all three moments and both transverse forces are zero,

\[M_1 = M_2 = M_{12} = 0 , \qquad Q_1 = Q_2 = 0 , \tag{22.27}\]

so that only \(N_1\), \(N_2\) and \(N_{12}\) can be nonzero. The adjective membrane has the same meaning as for a skin: Latin membrana is a thin skin, and the skin of a drum carries a load only by pulling in its own plane, not by bending.

Source: VK section 13.1 (PDF p. 354) and equations (13.1), (13.3) (PDF p. 355): membrane theory neglects all moments and with them also the transverse forces. The definition follows this source, the notation is from section 22.3.

Is it then possible for the wall to be in this state? VK (PDF p. 357) lists four conditions under which it is possible and gives them without proof. The edge must not carry a transverse force or a moment, the normal displacement and the rotation of the edge must be free, the surface must be smooth, and the load continuous. We shall meet each one while working the examples, and for each we shall show where our calculation would stop.

Before we state the theorem we fix the shell on which we shall prove it. Take two families of lines on the middle surface that we know from a globe. A vertical plane through the axis \(z\) cuts the surface in a curve that we call a meridian (meridian). The name comes from the Latin word for midday: all points on the same meridian have the sun at its highest at the same time. A horizontal plane cuts the surface in a circle that we call a parallel (vzporednik), because all such circles are parallel to each other. The surface obtained by rotating a meridian about the axis \(z\) is a surface of revolution, and a thin shell on it a shell of revolution (lupina vrtenine).

Every meridian lies in its own plane of the axis, so we describe it in the plane \((r, z)\), where \(r\) is the distance from the axis. On the meridian we measure the arc length \(s\) from a starting point \(s_0\). Let the unit tangent vector of the meridian be \(\vek e_\varphi\), the outward unit normal of the surface (definition 2.17) \(\vek n\), and the angle between the normal and the axis \(z\) be \(\varphi(s)\). The normal is the outward one, as in section 22.3 (there the tangent vector is called \(\vek e_{\mathrm{s}}\), here \(\vek e_\varphi\), because \(\varphi\) is the main variable of our calculation). In section 22.2 the normal points inward for the sign of the curvature, and every formula there holds here too. As in spherical coordinates (lemma 11.15), in the plane \((r, z)\)

\[\vek e_\varphi = (\cos\varphi,\ -\sin\varphi) , \qquad \vek n = (\sin\varphi,\ \cos\varphi) . \tag{22.28}\]

Since \(\vek e_\varphi\) is the derivative of the meridian point with respect to arc length, \(r'(s) = \cos\varphi\) and \(z'(s) = -\sin\varphi\) (a prime is the derivative with respect to \(s\)). The radius of curvature \(R_1\) of the meridian is fixed by the definition of the curvature of a curve (section 22.2) through \(1/R_1 = \varphi'(s)\): the angle of the tangent turns at the rate \(1/R_1\). The second principal radius \(R_2\) is the distance along the normal from the surface to the axis (lemma 22.18), so in the right triangle formed by the axis, the normal and the parallel, \(r = R_2\sin\varphi\). For a sphere \(R_1 = R_2 = R\), for a cylinder \(\varphi = \pi/2\), \(1/R_1 = 0\) and \(R_2 = r = R\).

Definition 22.31 Axisymmetric state of a shell of revolution (osnosimetrično stanje lupine vrtenine)

Let a shell of revolution be given by a meridian \(s \mapsto (r(s), z(s))\), \(s \ge s_0\), with a continuously differentiable angle \(\varphi(s)\) and \(\sin\varphi > 0\), \(r > 0\) for \(s > s_0\). The load per unit area of the middle surface has a component \(p_\varphi\) along \(\vek e_\varphi\) and a component \(p_{\mathrm{n}}\) along the outward normal \(\vek n\), both continuous functions of the arc length \(s\), and no circumferential component. The shell is in an axisymmetric state (osnosimetrično stanje) if its membrane forces and displacements do not depend on the angle \(\theta\) that fixes the meridian: \(N_\varphi(s)\), \(N_\theta(s)\) and \(N_{\varphi\theta}(s)\), where \(N_\varphi = N_1\) is the force in the meridian direction, \(N_\theta = N_2\) the force in the parallel direction and \(N_{\varphi\theta} = N_{12}\), and \(N_\varphi\) and \(N_\theta\) are continuous. The displacement of a point is then the vector \(\vek U = u\,\vek e_\varphi + w\,\vek n\) with no circumferential component.

Source: Notation after VK section 13.4 (PDF p. 357-358) and section 13.5 (PDF p. 361-362), where \(\varphi\) is the angle between the normal and the axis and \(r = R_2\sin\varphi\). VK denotes the load components \(p_1\) and \(p_3\), and their directions differ from ours by a sign, so we derive all the equations below again.

We now call \(N_\varphi\) the meridional force (meridianska sila), because it acts along the meridian, and \(N_\theta\) the hoop force, as for the tube, because it acts along the parallel. For the tube the meridional force is the same as the axial one. The load can be weight, gas pressure or snow.

We find \(N_\varphi\) first with a cap. Figure 22.13 shows a shell that has its top on the axis, and one parallel below it.

We draw the axis \(z\) and then, in grey, the meridian of the shell, which in the picture is a circular arc of radius 3 cm (a sphere, so \(R_1 = R_2 = 3\) cm). We choose a parallel at the angle \(\varphi_0 = 50^\circ\) and call the part of the shell above it the cap (kapa). The word is the everyday one, like a cap on a head: the part that covers the top. We cut the cap off, and the wall below the cut must hold it. At the parallel of radius \(r_0\) (in the figure the distance \(r_0\)) the normal of the surface is the dashed line to the axis, its length is \(R_2\) and its angle with the axis is \(\varphi_0\), because this is the definition of the angle \(\varphi\) and because \(r_0 = R_2\sin\varphi_0\). The wall below the cut pulls the cap with a force \(N_\varphi\) per unit length along the tangent of the meridian, away from the cap, starting at the point of the cut. Only two vectors in the plane of the section are drawn. In reality the force \(N_\varphi\) acts all around the circle.

Its axial component is \(N_\varphi\sin\varphi_0\) downward, because by equation (22.28) the vector \(\vek e_\varphi\) has the axial component \(-\sin\varphi\). The horizontal component points outward at every point of the circle, as the view from above shows. In a pair of opposite points the two are equal and opposite, so around the whole circle they cancel. What remains is the axial component, which is the same along the whole length \(2\pi r_0\) of the circle. The total load on the cap has an axial component \(P_{\mathrm{z}}\): for a pressure \(p\) it is the pressure on a horizontal circle of the same radius, \(P_{\mathrm{z}} = p\pi r_0^2\). The body is at rest, so the sum of the forces on the cap in the direction \(z\) must be zero: \(P_{\mathrm{z}} - 2\pi r_0 N_\varphi\sin\varphi_0 = 0\). The pressure gives an axial force as at the end of the tube: pressure times the area of the horizontal projection. For a sphere with pressure this gives \(N_\varphi = p\pi r_0^2/(2\pi r_0\sin\varphi_0) = p R_2/2\), which agrees with the end of the tube and with Navier's sphere.

Does this hold for every load, not only for pressure? The cap does not suffice for the force \(N_\theta\), because \(N_\theta\) acts across a cut along a meridian, and the cut of the cap runs along a parallel. So we take a band between two parallels and two meridians. Figure 22.14 shows it.

On the left we draw again the axis \(z\) and the meridian, and choose the band (pas) between the angles \(\varphi_{\mathrm{a}} = 30^\circ\) and \(\varphi_{\mathrm{b}} = 65^\circ\). The load \(p_{\mathrm{n}}\) acts on the band in the direction of the outward normal, drawn as a pressure that pushes the wall outward from inside. On both parallels acts the meridional force \(N_\varphi\), on the lower one in the direction of increasing \(s\), on the upper one the opposite way, always away from the band. On the right we draw the view from above. The meridian \(\theta = 0\) lies on the axis \(x\). The band is a piece of an annulus between the meridians at the angles \(\pm\alpha\) from this axis. On both parallels acts the force \(N_\varphi\), seen from above as the horizontal component along the radius. Across the edge at each meridian acts the hoop force \(N_\theta\), in the direction of the tangent of the parallel, so in a different direction at every point of the edge. Without it the band would not be in equilibrium, because the forces that pull it toward the axis would be missing.

We write the equilibrium of the band in the direction of the axis \(x\). We chose the band symmetric about this axis. The tangent of a parallel is \(\vek e_\theta(\theta) = (-\sin\theta, \cos\theta, 0)\), so the hoop force at the edge \(\theta = \alpha\) has the component \(-N_\theta\sin\alpha\) in the direction \(x\). At the edge \(\theta = -\alpha\) it pulls away from the band in the direction \(-\vek e_\theta(-\alpha)\), and its component is again \(-N_\theta\sin\alpha\). Both pull the band toward the axis of revolution, so they add and do not cancel.

We write the cap and the band together. Let \(s_0\) be the start of the shell, that is the apex or an edge with a known axial force, and let the cap mean the part of the shell between \(s_0\) and the chosen parallel. The theorem gives the forces \(N_\varphi\) and \(N_\theta\) for every parallel.

Theorem 22.32 Equilibrium of a shell of revolution in the membrane state (ravnotežje lupine vrtenine v membranskem stanju)

Let a shell of revolution be in an axisymmetric state (definition 22.31) and in a membrane state (definition 22.30). Let \(F_0\) be a number and for \(s > s_0\) let

\[P_{\mathrm{z}}(s) = F_0 + 2\pi\int_{\mathrm{s}_0}^{s}\big(-p_\varphi\sin\varphi + p_{\mathrm{n}}\cos\varphi\big)\,r\,\mathrm{d}\bar s , \tag{22.29}\]

be the axial component of the resultant of the load on the part of the shell between \(s_0\) and \(s\), in which the axial force \(F_0\) on the edge \(s = s_0\) is included (at an apex \(s_0 = 0\) we have \(r(0) = 0\) and \(F_0 = 0\)). Let there be no moment about the axis at the edge \(s_0\). Then:

  1. The shear force is \(N_{\varphi\theta} = 0\).
  2. The meridional force is
    \[N_\varphi(s) = \frac{P_{\mathrm{z}}(s)}{2\pi\,r(s)\,\sin\varphi(s)} . \tag{22.30}\]
  3. The forces \(N_\varphi\) and \(N_\theta\) satisfy the equilibrium equation in the direction of the normal
    \[\frac{N_\varphi}{R_1} + \frac{N_\theta}{R_2} = p_{\mathrm{n}} . \tag{22.31}\]
Proof

We must show three items. The first two follow from the equilibrium of the cap, the third from the equilibrium of the band, written in two directions that we combine. A body in equilibrium is at rest, so the sum of forces and the sum of moments on each of its parts is zero (chapter 2, theorems 2.28 and 2.36). The force that the wall transmits across a cut is by definition 22.27 the force per unit length of the cut, the resultant of the stresses over the thickness. We drop the factor \(1 + z/R\) here (a simplification from section 22.3, error at most \(h/(2R)\)).

  1. Let \(B\) be the part of the shell between \(s_0\) and \(s\) (the cap if \(s_0 = 0\)). The vectors \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}(\theta) = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta, 0)\), \(\vek e_\theta(\theta) = (-\sin\theta, \cos\theta, 0)\) and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) are the local basis of cylindrical coordinates (lemma 11.15). At the cut at \(s\) the outward direction for \(B\) is \(+\vek e_\varphi\), so the wall transmits to \(B\) per unit length of the circle the force \(N_\varphi\vek e_\varphi + N_{\varphi\theta}\vek e_\theta\) (the transverse forces are zero by definition 22.30), where \(\vek e_\varphi = \cos\varphi\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}} - \sin\varphi\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\).
  2. The moment about the axis \(z\). The force \(N_\varphi\vek e_\varphi\) and the load \(p_\varphi\vek e_\varphi + p_{\mathrm{n}}\vek n\) lie in a plane that contains the axis, so their moment about the axis is zero. The force \(N_{\varphi\theta}\vek e_\theta\) has on the lever arm \(r\) the moment \(rN_{\varphi\theta}\) per unit length, together \(2\pi r^2N_{\varphi\theta}\), and by assumption the edge \(s_0\) contributes no moment. The moment balance gives \(2\pi r^2N_{\varphi\theta} = 0\), hence item 1.
  3. The component of the forces in the direction \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\). The force of the wall gives \(\int_0^{2\pi}(-N_\varphi\sin\varphi)\,r\,\mathrm{d}\theta = -2\pi rN_\varphi\sin\varphi\), because \(N_\varphi\) and \(r\) do not depend on \(\theta\). For the description of the middle surface by the parameters \((s, \theta)\) we have \(|\vek N| = |\partial\vek r/\partial s\times\partial\vek r/\partial\theta| = |\vek e_\varphi\times r\vek e_\theta| = r\), so a surface integral over the middle surface contains the weight \(r\) (definition 4.61, equation (4.33)) and the axial component of the load is \(2\pi\int(-p_\varphi\sin\varphi + p_{\mathrm{n}}\cos\varphi)\,r\,\mathrm{d}\bar s\), together with the edge \(s_0\) exactly \(P_{\mathrm{z}}(s)\). The equilibrium is \(P_{\mathrm{z}}(s) - 2\pi rN_\varphi\sin\varphi = 0\), which is equation (22.30).
  4. A band between \(s_1 < s_2\) and the meridians \(\theta = \pm\alpha\) with \(0 < \alpha < \pi\). Four edge forces and the load act on it. On the parallel \(s_2\) the wall pulls away from the band in the direction \(+\vek e_\varphi\), on the parallel \(s_1\) in the direction \(-\vek e_\varphi\), together \(\int_{-\alpha}^{\alpha}N_\varphi(s_2)\,r(s_2)\,\vek e_\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\theta - \int_{-\alpha}^{\alpha}N_\varphi(s_1)\,r(s_1)\,\vek e_\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\theta\). On the meridians it pulls in the direction \(+\vek e_\theta(\alpha)\) at \(\theta = \alpha\) and in the direction \(-\vek e_\theta(-\alpha)\) at \(\theta = -\alpha\), together \(\int_{\mathrm{s}_1}^{s_2}N_\theta\,\big(\vek e_\theta(\alpha) - \vek e_\theta(-\alpha)\big)\,\mathrm{d}s\). The load is \(\int_{-\alpha}^{\alpha}\int_{\mathrm{s}_1}^{s_2}(p_\varphi\vek e_\varphi + p_{\mathrm{n}}\vek n)\,r\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}\theta\) with the area weight of item 3. The sum of these forces is zero. The scalar product with \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) gives, because of \(\vek e_\theta\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = -\sin\theta\), \(\vek e_\varphi\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = \cos\varphi\cos\theta\), \(\vek n\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = \sin\varphi\cos\theta\) and \(\int_{-\alpha}^{\alpha}\cos\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta = 2\sin\alpha\) and \(\vek e_\theta(\alpha)\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} - \vek e_\theta(-\alpha)\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = -2\sin\alpha\), after dividing by \(2\sin\alpha > 0\) the equality
    \[\Big[N_\varphi\,r\cos\varphi\Big]_{\mathrm{s}_1}^{s_2} - \int_{\mathrm{s}_1}^{s_2}N_\theta\,\mathrm{d}s + \int_{\mathrm{s}_1}^{s_2}\big(p_\varphi\cos\varphi + p_{\mathrm{n}}\sin\varphi\big)\,r\,\mathrm{d}s = 0 \tag{22.32}\]
    for every pair \(s_1 < s_2\).
  5. Differentiation. By item 3, \(B_1(s) = rN_\varphi\sin\varphi = P_{\mathrm{z}}(s)/(2\pi)\), and by the fundamental theorem of calculus (theorem 3.40, item 2) \(B_1\) is continuously differentiable with derivative \(B_1' = (-p_\varphi\sin\varphi + p_{\mathrm{n}}\cos\varphi)\,r\), because the integrand is continuous. The function \(A_1(s) = rN_\varphi\cos\varphi = B_1\cot\varphi\) is also continuously differentiable, because \(\varphi\) is continuously differentiable and \(\sin\varphi > 0\). From the equality (22.32) for every pair \(s_1 < s_2\) it follows, if we vary \(s_2\) and differentiate with respect to it (again the fundamental theorem of calculus, \(N_\theta\) and \(p\) are continuous), that \(A_1' - N_\theta + (p_\varphi\cos\varphi + p_{\mathrm{n}}\sin\varphi)\,r = 0\).
  6. Let \(a = rN_\varphi\), so \(A_1 = a\cos\varphi\), \(B_1 = a\sin\varphi\). By the product rule (lemma 1.81), \(A_1' = a'\cos\varphi - a\varphi'\sin\varphi\) and \(B_1' = a'\sin\varphi + a\varphi'\cos\varphi\). Hence \(\cos\varphi\,B_1' - \sin\varphi\,A_1' = a\varphi'(\cos^2\varphi + \sin^2\varphi) = a\varphi'\), and from step 5
    \[a\varphi' = \cos\varphi\,(-p_\varphi\sin\varphi + p_{\mathrm{n}}\cos\varphi)\,r - \sin\varphi\,\big(N_\theta - (p_\varphi\cos\varphi + p_{\mathrm{n}}\sin\varphi)\,r\big) = -N_\theta\sin\varphi + p_{\mathrm{n}}\,r .\]
    The terms with \(p_\varphi\) cancel, because \(-\sin\varphi\cos\varphi + \sin\varphi\cos\varphi = 0\), and the terms with \(p_{\mathrm{n}}\) give \(p_{\mathrm{n}} r(\cos^2\varphi + \sin^2\varphi)\).
  7. We divide by \(r > 0\) and use \(a/r = N_\varphi\), \(\varphi' = 1/R_1\) and \(\sin\varphi/r = 1/R_2\): \(N_\varphi/R_1 + N_\theta/R_2 = p_{\mathrm{n}}\), which is equation (22.31).

Source: VK (13.7) (PDF p. 358), its third line, is equation (22.31) (VK calls it the Laplace equation), and (13.33a), (13.36), (13.37) (PDF p. 362-363) is equation (22.30) with a line load on the edge. VK derives both equations from the general equations of chapter 12 (PDF p. 355). The proof with finite pieces, a cap and a band, is not from the source. Here \(p_{\mathrm{n}}\) points outward, in VK the sign is the opposite (\(\kappa_1N_1 + \kappa_2N_2 + p_3 = 0\)).

The theorem tells how to obtain \(N_\varphi\) and \(N_\theta\) from equilibrium. We wrote the equilibrium of the band as an equality for every pair of parallels, that is for finite pieces, and only then turned it into a local equation with the fundamental theorem of calculus (theorem 3.40). We try the theorem on a sphere under pressure and on the tube.

Example 22.33 Sphere and tube under pressure (krogla in cev pod tlakom)

For a sphere \(R_1 = R_2 = R\) and \(r = R\sin\varphi\). The pressure \(p\) acts outward, \(p_\varphi = 0\), \(p_{\mathrm{n}} = p\), so the axial component of the load on the cap is \(2\pi\int_0^{\varphi}p\cos\bar\varphi\,R\sin\bar\varphi\,R\,\mathrm{d}\bar\varphi = \pi pR^2\sin^2\varphi\) (SymPy). From equation (22.30), \(N_\varphi = \pi pR^2\sin^2\varphi/(2\pi R\sin^2\varphi) = pR/2\), and from equation (22.31) \(N_\theta = R(p - N_\varphi/R) = pR/2\). At \(R = 50\) mm, \(p = 3\) MPa we get \(N_\varphi = N_\theta = 75\) N/mm, and the stress at \(h = 2\) mm is \(37{,}5\) MPa.

For the tube \(\varphi = \pi/2\) and \(1/R_1 = 0\). The load \(p\vek n = p\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) has no axial component, so \(P_{\mathrm{z}} = F_0\). The end (a flat disc) passes the force of the pressure \(p\pi R^2\) to the wall, so \(F_0 = p\pi R^2\) and \(N_\varphi = p\pi R^2/(2\pi R) = pR/2 = 75\) N/mm. Equation (22.31) is \(N_\theta/R = p\), so \(N_\theta = pR = 150\) N/mm and the stress is \(75\) MPa. This is exactly the result of the two cuts in figure 22.12.

The theorem is what we were after: the forces of the wall in an axisymmetric state follow from equilibrium alone, so without material constants.

Corollary 22.34 Static determinacy of the membrane state (statična določenost membranskega stanja)

Let a shell of revolution be in an axisymmetric membrane state (definition 22.31) and let the shape of the meridian, the load \(p_\varphi\), \(p_{\mathrm{n}}\) and the axial force \(F_0\) on the edge \(s_0\) (theorem 22.32) be given. Then \(N_\varphi\) and \(N_\theta\) are uniquely determined by equations (22.30) and (22.31) and contain neither the modulus \(E\), nor Poisson's ratio \(\nu\), nor the thickness \(h\) (unless the load itself contains \(h\), like weight).

Proof

We must show that the equations give both forces and that they contain no material constants.

  1. By theorem 22.32, item 2, \(N_\varphi = P_{\mathrm{z}}/(2\pi r\sin\varphi)\) is fixed by the shape and the load, because \(P_{\mathrm{z}}\), \(r\) and \(\varphi\) contain only \(p_\varphi\), \(p_{\mathrm{n}}\), \(F_0\) and the geometry of the meridian. This equation does not contain \(E\), \(\nu\) and \(h\).
  2. By item 3 of the same theorem \(N_\theta = R_2(p_{\mathrm{n}} - N_\varphi/R_1)\), which is again fixed by the geometry and by \(N_\varphi\). Each equation has one unknown: the first only \(N_\varphi\), the second, after inserting \(N_\varphi\), only \(N_\theta\). So the solution is unique.

The meaning of the corollary: when we change the material, for example steel to aluminium, the forces \(N_\varphi\) and \(N_\theta\) do not change, but the displacements do, as the lemma below gives them. For a weight \(q\) per unit area that is proportional to \(h\), the forces \(N\) are proportional to \(h\) and the stresses are not (the dome in the next section).

The corollary is not a proof that a shell really gets into a membrane state. We have shown only that \(N\) exists if the state is a membrane one. Let us look at two cases in which the theorem is not enough.

The first case is a load that is not axisymmetric. The equilibrium of the cap gives only six equations (three for forces and three for moments), but the unknowns are functions of the angle \(\theta\). Suppose the meridional force at the cut has, besides its mean, a term \(c\cos 2\theta\) with arbitrary \(c\) (for a load that is not axisymmetric, such as wind, this is possible). The force resultant of this term is \(\int_0^{2\pi}c\cos 2\theta\,r_0\vek e_\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\theta = \vek 0\), because \(\int_0^{2\pi}\cos2\theta\cos\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta = \int_0^{2\pi}\cos2\theta\sin\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta = \int_0^{2\pi}\cos2\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta = 0\), and its moment about the center of the circle is also zero (SymPy). The equilibrium of the cap does not notice how large \(c\) is. So from the cap we cannot determine \(N_\varphi\) at every point. The six equations contain only the mean of \(N_\varphi\) along the parallel and its terms with \(\cos\theta\) and \(\sin\theta\), not the higher terms such as \(c\cos 2\theta\). The equations around the circumference contain three functions of two variables, \(N_\varphi\), \(N_\theta\), \(N_{\varphi\theta}\), with partial derivatives with respect to \(\theta\) (VK equations (13.7), PDF p. 358), and VK solves them by expanding the load in a Fourier series in \(\theta\) (PDF p. 360-361). This is outside our chapter and is stated without proof.

The second case is a support that does not match the direction of the force in the wall. Figure 22.15 shows a dome in the shape of a spherical cap with the angle \(30^\circ\) at its edge (\(R = 5\) m, steel of density \(\rho = 7800\) kg/m\(^3\), \(h = 20\) mm, weight per unit area \(q = \rho g h = 7800\cdot 9{,}81\cdot 0{,}02 = 1530{,}4\) N/m\(^2\)) and a hemisphere.

The weight points down, \(-q\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\). By equation (22.28) its components are \(p_\varphi = -q\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\cdot\vek e_\varphi = q\sin\varphi\) and \(p_{\mathrm{n}} = -q\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\cdot\vek n = -q\cos\varphi\), so the integrand in equation (22.29) is \(-q\sin^2\varphi - q\cos^2\varphi = -q\). The cap has an apex, so \(F_0 = 0\). For a sphere \(r = R\sin\bar\varphi\) and \(s = R\bar\varphi\), so \(P_{\mathrm{z}} = -2\pi q\int_0^{\varphi_0}R\sin\bar\varphi\,R\,\mathrm{d}\bar\varphi = -2\pi qR^2(1 - \cos\varphi_0)\), which is the weight of the cap. Equation (22.30) gives \(N_\varphi = -2\pi qR^2(1 - \cos\varphi_0)/(2\pi R\sin^2\varphi_0) = -qR/(1 + \cos\varphi_0)\), because \(\sin^2\varphi_0 = (1 - \cos\varphi_0)(1 + \cos\varphi_0)\) (SymPy). At \(\varphi_0 = 30^\circ\) this is \(N_\varphi = -4100{,}6\) N/m (a negative force means compression in the wall). The direction of the force is the tangent of the meridian, which at \(\varphi_0 = 30^\circ\) is inclined by \(30^\circ\) to the horizontal, so it has the horizontal component \(H = |N_\varphi|\cos\varphi_0 = 3551{,}2\) N/m. A vertical support cannot carry it, because by its definition it has only a vertical reaction. The dome would therefore spread outward at its edge, if there were no ring to hold it. The ring must withstand the tension \(T = H r_0 = 3551{,}2\cdot 2{,}5 = 8878\) N (\(r_0 = R\sin 30^\circ = 2{,}5\) m). For a hemisphere, \(\varphi_0 = 90^\circ\), the force in the wall is vertical, \(H = 0\) and the vertical support is enough: the expression for \(N_\varphi\) is the same, but \(\cos\varphi_0 = 0\).

The theorem gives in both cases the equilibrium force \(N_\varphi\), but a vertical support carries it only at \(90^\circ\). The difficulty is not in the equilibrium of the wall but in the edge: condition (a) of VK (PDF p. 357) says that the load on the edge must lie in the tangent plane of the surface. The ring takes the horizontal component, so that the force on the edge of the shell is tangential again.

So far we found the forces from equilibrium. What displacements belong to these forces? We get displacements from Hooke's law for a shell. By theorem 22.28, \(N_1 = C(\varepsilon_1^0 + \nu\varepsilon_2^0)\) and \(N_2 = C(\varepsilon_2^0 + \nu\varepsilon_1^0)\) hold with \(C = Eh/(1 - \nu^2)\) and \(M_{\mathrm{i}} = D(\kappa_{\mathrm{i}} + \nu\kappa_{\mathrm{j}})\) with \(D = Eh^3/(12(1 - \nu^2))\). For the axisymmetric state \(\varepsilon_\varphi^0\) and \(\varepsilon_\theta^0\) are the strains of the middle surface in the direction of the meridian and of the parallel, and \(\kappa_{\mathrm{i}}\) are the changes of curvature. The letter \(D\) is here the bending stiffness of the shell, not the amplification factor or the flexibility matrix of earlier chapters. The letter \(h\) is the thickness of the shell, not the scale factor \(h_{\mathrm{i}}\) of chapter 11. The forces \(N\) and moments \(M\) are per unit length of the cut and are not the force and moment of a bar.

Lemma 22.35 Displacements of a membrane shell of revolution (pomiki membranske lupine vrtenine)

Let a shell of revolution of thickness \(h\) made of an isotropic material \(E\), \(\nu\) be in an axisymmetric membrane state with the forces \(N_\varphi\) and \(N_\theta\), and let the displacement of a point of the middle surface be \(\vek U = u\,\vek e_\varphi + w\,\vek n\) with continuously differentiable functions \(u(s)\) and \(w(s)\). Then:

  1. The strains of the middle surface are
    \[\varepsilon_\varphi = \frac{N_\varphi - \nu N_\theta}{Eh} = u' + \frac{w}{R_1} , \qquad \varepsilon_\theta = \frac{N_\theta - \nu N_\varphi}{Eh} = \frac{u\cos\varphi + w\sin\varphi}{r} . \tag{22.33}\]
  2. The function \(u/\sin\varphi\) has the derivative
    \[\Big(\frac{u}{\sin\varphi}\Big)' = \frac{1}{\sin\varphi}\Big(\varepsilon_\varphi - \frac{R_2}{R_1}\,\varepsilon_\theta\Big) , \tag{22.34}\]
    and \(w = (r\varepsilon_\theta - u\cos\varphi)/\sin\varphi\). The solution is fixed up to a rigid displacement along the axis, \(u = -c\sin\varphi\), \(w = c\cos\varphi\), where \(c\) is an arbitrary number.
Proof

We must show that from \(N\) we get \(\varepsilon\) by Hooke's law and that the displacements give them in the form of item 1, and then derive the solution.

  1. By the definition of the membrane state the moments are \(M_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\), so from \(M_{\mathrm{i}} = D(\kappa_{\mathrm{i}} + \nu\kappa_{\mathrm{j}})\) with \(D > 0\) it follows that \(\kappa_1 = \kappa_2 = 0\) (the matrix \(\begin{pmatrix}1 & \nu\\ \nu & 1\end{pmatrix}\) has determinant \(1 - \nu^2 > 0\)). The strain through the thickness is then \(\varepsilon^0\), up to the factor \(1 + z/R_{\mathrm{i}}\) that we drop (theorem 22.26, item 3). From \(N_\varphi = C(\varepsilon_\varphi + \nu\varepsilon_\theta)\) and \(N_\theta = C(\varepsilon_\theta + \nu\varepsilon_\varphi)\) we get \(N_\varphi - \nu N_\theta = C(1 - \nu^2)\varepsilon_\varphi = Eh\varepsilon_\varphi\), and the same for \(\theta\). This is the first equality in each line of equation (22.33).
  2. The hoop strain. The cylindrical component of the displacement is \(U_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek U\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{r}} = u\cos\varphi + w\sin\varphi\) by equation (22.28). By corollary 11.21, for \(u_\theta = 0\) and \(\partial/\partial\theta = 0\) the strain is \(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} = U_{\mathrm{r}}/r\). This is the second equality for \(\varepsilon_\theta\).
  3. The meridional strain. The normal strain in the direction of a unit vector \(\vek t\) is \(\vek t\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon\vek t = \vek t\cdot\nabla\vek U\,\vek t\), because the antisymmetric part of the gradient tensor gives \(\vek t\cdot W\vek t = 0\) (definition 1.115). For \(\vek t = \vek e_\varphi\) the vector \(\nabla\vek U\,\vek t\) is the derivative of the displacement with respect to the arc length \(s\) along the meridian. By equation (22.28) \(\vek e_\varphi' = -\varphi'\vek n\) and \(\vek n' = \varphi'\vek e_\varphi\), so \(\vek U' = (u' + \varphi'w)\,\vek e_\varphi + (w' - \varphi'u)\,\vek n\), and \(\varepsilon_\varphi = \vek e_\varphi\cdot\vek U' = u' + w/R_1\).
  4. From the second equality for \(\varepsilon_\theta\), \(w = (r\varepsilon_\theta - u\cos\varphi)/\sin\varphi\). We insert it into \(u' + \varphi'w = \varepsilon_\varphi\): \(u' - \varphi'\cot\varphi\,u = \varepsilon_\varphi - \varphi'r\varepsilon_\theta/\sin\varphi\). The left side is \(\sin\varphi\,(u/\sin\varphi)'\), because \((u/\sin\varphi)' = (u' - \varphi'\cot\varphi\,u)/\sin\varphi\), and \(\varphi'r/\sin\varphi = R_2/R_1\). This is equation (22.34).
  5. If two solutions have the same forces \(N\), their difference solves the equations with \(\varepsilon = 0\), because the equations are linear. The homogeneous equation (with \(\varepsilon = 0\)) has the solution \(u/\sin\varphi = C\) constant, so \(u = C\sin\varphi\) and \(w = -u\cos\varphi/\sin\varphi = -C\cos\varphi\). With \(C = -c\) this is the rigid displacement by \(c\) along the axis: the components of the vector \(c\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) are \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\cdot\vek e_\varphi = -\sin\varphi\) and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\cdot\vek n = \cos\varphi\).

Source: VK (13.16) (PDF p. 359) are the kinematic equations of shells of revolution in spherical coordinates, VK section 13.2 (PDF p. 355) and equation (13.5) give the displacements from the forces. We get the same forms of \(\varepsilon_\varphi\) and \(\varepsilon_\theta\) in item 2 of theorem 22.26. Our proof with corollary 11.21 and with the derivative along the arc is not from the source.

We try the lemma on the sphere and the tube of example 22.33. For the sphere \(\varepsilon_\varphi = \varepsilon_\theta = (1 - \nu)pR/(2Eh)\) and \(R_1 = R_2\), so the right side of equation (22.34) is zero: \(u = C\sin\varphi\), and with the choice \(C = 0\) we get \(w = r\varepsilon_\theta/\sin\varphi = R\varepsilon_\theta = pR^2(1 - \nu)/(2Eh) = 6{,}696\) µm (SymPy). The sphere inflates equally at every cross section. For the tube \(\varphi = \pi/2\), \(\sin\varphi = 1\), \(R_1 = \infty\) (\(R_2/R_1 = 0\)), so \(u' = \varepsilon_\varphi = (N_\varphi - \nu N_\theta)/(Eh) = 7{,}653\cdot 10^{-5}\) and \(w = R\varepsilon_\theta = pR^2(1 - \nu/2)/(Eh) = 16{,}263\) µm. Over a length of \(100\) mm the tube lengthens along its axis by \(7{,}653\) µm.

Now let us see what the lemma cannot do. Let us clamp the tube at one end, \(u(0) = 0\) and \(w(0) = 0\). By the lemma \(w\) is constant along the whole tube, \(16{,}263\) µm, but at the clamp it would have to be zero. The lemma has for the whole shell only one free number, \(c\) (the rigid displacement), and does not allow us to prescribe \(w\) and the rotation at the edge as well. VK (PDF p. 356) states the same conclusion: in membrane theory we cannot prescribe the displacements \(w\) and the rotation of the edge. The difference in \(w\) is small, \(0{,}81\) % of the thickness \(h\). But the force with which the clamp removes this difference cannot be obtained from the membrane forces, because it is a bending force, and we must find it another way. Let us also compare the tube with an inflated hemisphere of equal thickness: \(w_\text{tube} - w_\text{sphere} = 9{,}566\) µm \(= pR^2/(2Eh)\). If we join them at a common parallel, then by membrane theory they do not match, because at the edge they move apart, which is physically impossible: the wall is one, and so bending and a transverse force appear near the joint, which membrane theory leaves out. This shows up in one of the following sections.

When can we then trust a membrane result? In the calculation of the displacements we put the moments equal to zero. The displacement \(w\) that we computed has a change of curvature that is not exactly zero when \(w\) varies over a length \(\ell\). An order of magnitude: the bending stress is \(6M/h^2\) with \(M \approx D\kappa\) and \(\kappa \approx w/\ell^2\), so \(Ehw/(2(1 - \nu^2)\ell^2)\). The membrane stress is \(Ew/R\). The ratio is \(hR/(2(1 - \nu^2)\ell^2)\). At \(h = 2\) mm, \(R = 50\) mm the ratio is \(0{,}0055\) at \(\ell = 100\) mm, \(0{,}14\) at \(\ell = 20\) mm and \(0{,}55\) at \(\ell = 10\) mm, where the membrane calculation cannot be used. The limit is \(\ell \approx \sqrt{Rh} = 10\) mm. This is an estimate of the order of magnitude, not a limit with a proof.

With theorem 22.32 we thus have the forces in the wall of a shell of revolution, with lemma 22.35 the displacements, and with the estimate \(\ell \gtrsim \sqrt{Rh}\) a guide where this holds. The next section applies the theorem to vessels, a tank and a dome, and compares the membrane forces with the Lamé solution (chapter 11).
