Chapter 2 Stress and equilibrium

2.11 Principal stresses and principal strains

At the end of section 2.6.6 three questions remained. Two of them we have already answered. Section 2.9 tells us how the stress components change when we turn the axes. Theorem 2.36 on the symmetry of stress tells us that \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zy}}\). What remains is the question on which face through a point the normal stress is largest, and on which the shear stress. In design this is decisive. A material fails when the size of the normal or shear stress exceeds an allowable value, which is called the strength (trdnost) of the material, so we must know where the largest stress is and how large it is (CM §4.3.2, PDF p. 189). In this section we answer for the normal stress, in section 2.12 for the shear stress.

Two answers we know already. In a bar pulled by an axial force, among the faces tilted about the axis \(z\), the normal stress was largest on the cross section, where it equals \(\sigma\) and where there is no shear stress (section 2.4). In pure shear the two faces perpendicular to the diagonals of the cube had the normal stress \(\tau_0\) and \(-\tau_0\) respectively, and no shear stress (section 2.6.6). In both cases there was no shear on the face with the largest normal stress. We will show that this is no coincidence.

The goal of the section is three claims and one procedure. First, at every point of a body there are three mutually perpendicular faces on which there is no shear stress. Second, on one of them the normal stress is the largest among all faces through the point, on another the smallest. Third, the stresses on these faces depend only on the stress at the point, not on how we placed the axes. The procedure tells us how to compute these faces and stresses. At the end we show the same for strains. With this we prove what in section 1.1 we only stated from a source: for every small deformation there are three mutually perpendicular directions in which the material is not sheared but only stretched or shortened.

The model is the same as in sections 2.6 to 2.9. The body is in equilibrium, \(P\) is a point in its interior, and the axes of a rectangular Cartesian coordinate system are chosen. The stress at \(P\) is described by nine stress components, which we write in the matrix \(\ten\sigma\) with the rows \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\), \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}})\) and \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}})\) (definition 2.41). By the theorem on the symmetry of stress this matrix is symmetric, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{ji}}\), where the indices \(i\) and \(j\) run over \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\). So by Cauchy's formula in matrix form (2.42) the traction vector on the face through \(P\) with the unit normal \(\vek n\) is

\[\vek t(\vek n) = \ten\sigma\,\vek n ,\]

where the vector \(\vek n\) is written as a column. How the body is loaded and what material it is made of does not matter for this section. Everything that follows we derive from nine numbers at one point, and it holds at every point separately. At another point of the same body the faces without shear are in general turned differently. For strains we will also assume that the partial derivatives of the displacements are small, so that the strain is described by the small strain tensor of section 1.17.

2.11.1 Faces without shear

Let us begin with an example with numbers. At the point \(P\) let the matrix of stress components in MPa be

\[\ten\sigma = \begin{bmatrix} 67 & 24 & 0 \\ 24 & 53 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} ,\]

so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 67\) MPa, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = 53\) MPa and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}} = 24\) MPa, and all other components are zero. We chose the numbers ourselves, so that the results come out round. On the faces perpendicular to the axes \(x\) and \(y\) acts a force that lies in the plane of the axes \(x\) and \(y\), and on the faces perpendicular to the axis \(z\) no force acts (figure 2.65, left).

Let us look at the faces through \(P\) tilted about the axis \(z\). As in section 2.4, such a face has the unit normal \(\vek n = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta, 0)\), where \(\theta\) is the angle between the normal and the axis \(x\). The components of the traction vector \(\vek t(\vek n) = \ten\sigma\,\vek n\) follow from the rule for the product of a matrix and a vector (1.38). Each is the sum of the products of one row of the matrix with the components of the vector \(\vek n\):

\[\vek t(\vek n) = (67\cos\theta + 24\sin\theta,\ \ 24\cos\theta + 53\sin\theta,\ \ 0) .\]

The normal stress is the projection of this vector onto the normal (definition 2.18), so by (2.6)

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{n}} = \vek t\cdot\vek n = 67\cos^2\theta + 48\sin\theta\cos\theta + 53\sin^2\theta ,\]

where we added the products \(24\sin\theta\cos\theta\) from the first and the second component into \(48\sin\theta\cos\theta\). The shear stress is, by item 3 of lemma 2.14 on splitting a vector, \(\tau = \sqrt{|\vek t|^2 - \sigma_{\mathrm{n}}^2}\). The table shows the values at a few angles, computed with NumPy, and figure 2.64 shows both stresses at all angles from \(0^\circ\) to \(180^\circ\). At the angle \(\theta + 180^\circ\) the normal is the opposite one, \(-\vek n\), but the face is the same, so after that the values repeat.

In the table the normal stress is largest between \(30^\circ\) and \(45^\circ\), and the graph shows that it is at about \(37^\circ\). There the shear stress is zero. The normal stress is smallest a quarter turn later, at about \(127^\circ\), and there too the shear stress is zero. Why exactly a quarter turn? In section 2.12.3 we will see that the normal stress on these faces depends on the cosine of an angle that changes twice as fast as \(\theta\). The cosine is largest and smallest at angles half a turn apart, and in the meantime \(\theta\) changes by a quarter turn. Let us try the normal for which \(\cos\theta = \tfrac45\) and \(\sin\theta = \tfrac35\). This is the angle \(\theta = 36{,}87^\circ\) in the right triangle with sides 3, 4 and 5, and the vector is a unit vector because \(\tfrac{16}{25} + \tfrac{9}{25} = 1\). For \(\vek n_1 = (\tfrac45, \tfrac35, 0)\)

\[\vek t(\vek n_1) = \left(\frac{67 \cdot 4 + 24 \cdot 3}{5},\ \frac{24 \cdot 4 + 53 \cdot 3}{5},\ 0\right) = (68,\ 51,\ 0) = 85\,\vek n_1 ,\]

since \(85 \cdot \tfrac45 = 68\) and \(85 \cdot \tfrac35 = 51\). The traction vector points exactly in the direction of the normal. The normal stress is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}} = 85\,(\vek n_1\cdot\vek n_1) = 85\) MPa, and the shear part \(\vek t - \sigma_{\mathrm{n}}\vek n_1\) is the zero vector. The normal \(\vek n_2 = (-\tfrac35, \tfrac45, 0)\) belongs to the angle \(126{,}87^\circ\) and is perpendicular to \(\vek n_1\), since \(\vek n_1\cdot\vek n_2 = -\tfrac{12}{25} + \tfrac{12}{25} = 0\). For it

\[\vek t(\vek n_2) = \left(\frac{-67 \cdot 3 + 24 \cdot 4}{5},\ \frac{-24 \cdot 3 + 53 \cdot 4}{5},\ 0\right) = (-21,\ 28,\ 0) = 35\,\vek n_2 ,\]

again only a normal stress, 35 MPa. One more face has this property, the one with the normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\). On it \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}) = \ten\sigma\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = \vek 0 = 0\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), so the normal and the shear stress are zero. We have found three mutually perpendicular faces on which there is no shear (figure 2.65, right).

For the bar, for pure shear and in this example we have therefore found faces on which the traction vector points in the direction of the normal, \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \lambda\,\vek n\) for some number \(\lambda\). The matrix \(\ten\sigma\) maps the vector \(\vek n\) to a multiple of it. For most directions this is not so. The vector \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n\) points elsewhere than \(\vek n\), and the traction vector is tilted relative to the face (figure 2.66). The directions that the matrix keeps have a name in mathematics.

Definition 2.59 Eigenvalue and eigenvector (lastna vrednost in lastni vektor)

Let \(\mat A\) be a square matrix, that is, a matrix with equal numbers of rows and columns, \(m\). A real number \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue (lastna vrednost) of the matrix \(\mat A\) if there is a nonzero vector \(\vek v\) with \(m\) components for which

\[\mat A\,\vek v = \lambda\,\vek v . \tag{2.58}\]

Every such vector \(\vek v\) is an eigenvector (lastni vektor) of the matrix \(\mat A\) belonging to the eigenvalue \(\lambda\).

Source: CM (2.5.31) (PDF p. 91), SADD (1.6.1) (PDF pp. 20-21), KREYSZIG §8.1 (1) (PDF p. 349).

Why does the definition ask for \(\vek v\) to be nonzero? The zero vector satisfies the equation for every number, since \(\mat A\,\vek 0 = \vek 0 = \lambda\,\vek 0\). Without this condition every number would be an eigenvalue of every matrix, and the notion would tell us nothing about the matrix. An eigenvector, though, is not unique. For every number \(c \neq 0\), by lemma 1.97 on the linearity of the product with a matrix,

\[\mat A\,(c\,\vek v) = c\,(\mat A\,\vek v) = c\,\lambda\,\vek v = \lambda\,(c\,\vek v) ,\]

so \(c\,\vek v\) is also an eigenvector, with the same eigenvalue. Only the direction matters, and among the eigenvectors we can always choose a unit one, \(\vek v/|\vek v|\) (lemma 1.19). In the example above the matrix \(\ten\sigma\) has the eigenvalues \(85\), \(35\) and \(0\) with the unit eigenvectors \(\vek n_1\), \(\vek n_2\) and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\).

Not every matrix has eigenvectors. The matrix with two rows and two columns

\[\mat A = \begin{bmatrix} \tfrac35 & -\tfrac45 \\ \tfrac45 & \tfrac35 \end{bmatrix}\]

turns every vector of the plane, for example the vector \((1, 0)\) into the vector \((\tfrac35, \tfrac45)\). The equation \(\mat A\,\vek v = \lambda\,\vek v\) reads, component by component, \(\tfrac35 v_1 - \tfrac45 v_2 = \lambda v_1\) and \(\tfrac45 v_1 + \tfrac35 v_2 = \lambda v_2\). We multiply the first equation by \(v_2\), the second by \(v_1\), and subtract the second from the first. The terms with \(\lambda\) and the terms with \(\tfrac35\) cancel, and what is left is \(-\tfrac45\,(v_1^2 + v_2^2) = 0\), which holds only for \(v_1 = v_2 = 0\). So there is no eigenvector for any real number \(\lambda\). This matrix is not symmetric, since \(A_{12} = -\tfrac45\) and \(A_{21} = \tfrac45\). For such matrices books allow eigenvalues that are complex numbers (CM §2.5.6.1, PDF p. 91), but we will not need them, because we will look for eigenvalues only of symmetric matrices. More generally, eigenvalues are also defined for rules that assign a vector to a vector and preserve sums and multiples, not only for matrices. This is how CM introduces them for tensors (CM §2.5.6.1, PDF p. 91).

For stress the notion gets its own name.

Definition 2.60 Principal stress and principal direction (glavna napetost in glavna smer)

Let \(\ten\sigma\) be the matrix of stress components at the point \(P\) in a chosen rectangular Cartesian coordinate system. A unit vector \(\vek n\) is a principal direction (glavna smer) of the stress at \(P\), and the number \(\lambda\) the corresponding principal stress (glavna napetost), if

\[\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \lambda\,\vek n ,\]

that is, if \(\vek n\) is a unit eigenvector and \(\lambda\) an eigenvalue of the matrix \(\ten\sigma\).

Source: CM §4.3.2, (4.3.11) (PDF p. 189), SADD §3.4 (PDF p. 69).

What does the definition mean on a face? By Cauchy's formula \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n\) is the traction vector \(\vek t(\vek n)\). A principal direction is therefore the normal of a face on which the traction vector points in the direction of the normal, \(\vek t(\vek n) = \lambda\,\vek n\). The normal stress on this face is \(\vek t\cdot\vek n = \lambda\,(\vek n\cdot\vek n) = \lambda\), the shear part \(\vek t - \lambda\,\vek n\) is the zero vector, and the shear stress is zero. The converse also holds. If the shear stress on the face with the normal \(\vek n\) is zero, the shear part is the zero vector, and \(\vek t(\vek n) = \sigma_{\mathrm{n}}\,\vek n\). Then \(\vek n\) is a principal direction with the principal stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}}\). The principal directions are therefore exactly the normals of the faces on which there is no shear stress. A positive principal stress means tension, a negative one compression. CM calls the faces whose normal is a principal direction principal planes (glavne ravnine) (CM, PDF p. 189). The normal of a plane is determined only up to its sign, but this does no harm: if \(\vek n\) is a principal direction, so is \(-\vek n\), with the same principal stress, since \(\ten\sigma\,(-\vek n) = -\lambda\,\vek n = \lambda\,(-\vek n)\).

Let us look at both examples we already know in the new words. In the bar \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma\) is the only nonzero component, so \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = (\sigma\,n_{\mathrm{x}}, 0, 0)\). Let \(\sigma \neq 0\). The equation \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \lambda\,\vek n\) reads, component by component, \(\sigma\,n_{\mathrm{x}} = \lambda\,n_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(0 = \lambda\,n_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(0 = \lambda\,n_{\mathrm{z}}\). If \(\lambda \neq 0\), the last two equations give \(n_{\mathrm{y}} = n_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\), so the unit vector is \(\vek n = \pm\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), and the first equation gives \(\lambda = \sigma\). If \(\lambda = 0\), the first equation gives \(n_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\), and every unit vector perpendicular to the axis of the bar is a principal direction. The principal stresses are therefore \(\sigma\) on the cross section and \(0\) on every lengthwise face. In pure shear \(\vek t(\vek n) = \tau_0\,(n_{\mathrm{z}}, 0, n_{\mathrm{x}})\). The normals \((1, 0, 1)/\sqrt2\) and \((-1, 0, 1)/\sqrt2\) of the faces perpendicular to the diagonals \(AC\) and \(BD\) point along these diagonals and are principal directions with the principal stresses \(\tau_0\) and \(-\tau_0\), which we checked in section 2.6.6. The third principal direction is \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) with the principal stress \(0\), since \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}) = \vek 0\).

In all three cases we found the principal directions, and each time there were three of them, mutually perpendicular. Is this so for every stress? And is the largest normal stress always on a face without shear? The next section shows that it is so for every symmetric matrix, which the matrix of stress components is.

2.11.2 Three perpendicular principal directions

The idea is to reverse the conclusion of the example. There we found the largest normal stress on a face without shear. Now we will first look for the face with the largest normal stress and then show that there is no shear on it. Why should this be so? Suppose a shear part acted on this face. It lies in the face and points along it. If we tilt the normal of the face a little toward the direction of the shear part, this part starts to contribute to the normal stress, which therefore increases. But that is impossible, because the normal stress was already the largest. In the example with numbers, at \(\theta = 30^\circ\) the shear stress is \(5{,}94\) MPa, and the shear part points in the direction in which the normal tilts when we increase the angle \(\theta\). The normal stress indeed grows, from \(84{,}28\) MPa at \(30^\circ\) to \(85\) MPa at \(36{,}87^\circ\).

To be able to compute, let us write the normal stress with the components of the normal. We will need two rules for the scalar product with a matrix. Let \(\mat A\) be a matrix with three rows and three columns, and \(\vek v\) and \(\vek w\) vectors written as columns.

  1. By (1.38) the component \(i\) of the vector \(\mat A\,\vek w\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} A_{\mathrm{ij}} w_{\mathrm{j}}\).
  2. By (2.6) the scalar product is the sum of the products of corresponding components, so
    \[\vek v\cdot(\mat A\,\vek w) = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} v_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} A_{\mathrm{ij}} w_{\mathrm{j}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} A_{\mathrm{ij}}\,v_{\mathrm{i}}\,w_{\mathrm{j}} = \vek v^\T\mat A\,\vek w . \tag{2.59}\]
    The double sum has nine terms, one for each pair of indices, and the notation \(\vek v^\T\mat A\,\vek w\) for it was introduced in (1.41). For the stress we did the same computation in section 2.9.2.
  3. For \(\mat A = \ten\sigma\) and \(\vek v = \vek w = \vek n\) we get the normal stress of (2.43), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}} = \vek t\cdot\vek n = \vek n\cdot(\ten\sigma\,\vek n) = \vek n^\T\ten\sigma\,\vek n\). As there, because \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\), the terms \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}n_{\mathrm{x}}n_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}n_{\mathrm{y}}n_{\mathrm{x}}\) add up to \(2\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}n_{\mathrm{x}}n_{\mathrm{y}}\), and likewise for the other two pairs. Of the nine terms six remain:
    \[\sigma_{\mathrm{n}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}n_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}n_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}n_{\mathrm{z}}^2 + 2\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}n_{\mathrm{x}}n_{\mathrm{y}} + 2\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}n_{\mathrm{x}}n_{\mathrm{z}} + 2\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}n_{\mathrm{y}}n_{\mathrm{z}} .\]
    In the example with numbers, for \(\vek n = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta, 0)\) this is \(67\cos^2\theta + 53\sin^2\theta + 48\cos\theta\sin\theta\), as above.
  4. Let \(\mat A\) be symmetric. Then
    \[\vek v\cdot(\mat A\,\vek w) = \vek w\cdot(\mat A\,\vek v) . \tag{2.60}\]
    By (2.59) the right side is the sum of the terms \(A_{\mathrm{ij}}\,w_{\mathrm{i}}\,v_{\mathrm{j}}\). The term with the pair of indices \((i, j)\) on the right equals the term with the pair \((j, i)\) on the left, \(A_{\mathrm{ij}}\,w_{\mathrm{i}}\,v_{\mathrm{j}} = A_{\mathrm{ji}}\,v_{\mathrm{j}}\,w_{\mathrm{i}}\), because \(A_{\mathrm{ij}} = A_{\mathrm{ji}}\). For example, the term \(A_{\mathrm{yx}}\,w_{\mathrm{y}}\,v_{\mathrm{x}}\) of the right sum equals the term \(A_{\mathrm{xy}}\,v_{\mathrm{x}}\,w_{\mathrm{y}}\) of the left one. When the pair \((i, j)\) runs through all nine pairs, so does the pair \((j, i)\), so the two sums are equal.

Now we can state the theorem. It holds for every symmetric matrix, so we state it for a matrix \(\mat A\), and we will use it for stress and for strain.

Theorem 2.61 Principal directions of a symmetric matrix (glavne smeri simetrične matrike)

Let \(\mat A\) be a symmetric matrix with three rows and three columns and real entries \(A_{\mathrm{ij}} = A_{\mathrm{ji}}\). Then there are three mutually perpendicular unit vectors \(\vek n_1\), \(\vek n_2\) and \(\vek n_3 = \vek n_1\times\vek n_2\) and real numbers \(\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \lambda_3\) for which

\[\mat A\,\vek n_1 = \lambda_1\,\vek n_1 , \qquad \mat A\,\vek n_2 = \lambda_2\,\vek n_2 , \qquad \mat A\,\vek n_3 = \lambda_3\,\vek n_3 . \tag{2.61}\]

The number \(\lambda_1\) is the largest value of the expression \(\vek n^\T\mat A\,\vek n\) among all unit vectors \(\vek n\).

Proof

We find the directions one after the other. The first we obtain as the unit vector for which \(\vek n^\T\mat A\,\vek n\) is largest, and we show that it is an eigenvector. The second we find in the same way among the unit vectors perpendicular to the first, and the third is the vector product of the first two. For every vector \(\vek v\) we write \(q(\vek v) = \vek v^\T\mat A\,\vek v\).

1. The first direction.

  1. By (2.59), \(q(\vek v) = \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} A_{\mathrm{ij}}\,v_{\mathrm{i}}\,v_{\mathrm{j}}\). Each of the nine terms is the number \(A_{\mathrm{ij}}\) multiplied by the product of two components of the vector \(\vek v\). To the vector \(\vek v\) belongs the point \(O + \vek v\) with the coordinates \(v_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(v_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(v_{\mathrm{z}}\), so \(q\) is a polynomial in the coordinates of this point. By item 2 of corollary 1.69 on the continuity of sums, products and polynomials in space it is a continuous function of the point in all of space. So it is also continuous on every set of points, since the condition of continuity in definition 1.67 asks only for less on a smaller set.
  2. The set \(S\) of all unit vectors, that is, of the points at the distance \(1\) from \(O\), is the unit sphere of section 1.13, which is nonempty, closed and bounded. By theorem 1.78 on the largest value in space, \(q\) attains a largest value on \(S\). So there is a \(\vek n_1\) in \(S\) such that \(q(\vek n) \le q(\vek n_1)\) for every \(\vek n\) in \(S\). We write \(\lambda_1 = q(\vek n_1)\). This proves the last sentence of the theorem.
  3. Each term of the sum \(q(c\,\vek v)\) contains two components of the vector \(c\,\vek v\), each with the factor \(c\), so \(q(c\,\vek v) = c^2\,q(\vek v)\) for every number \(c\). Let \(\vek v \neq \vek 0\). The vector \(\vek v/|\vek v|\) is a unit vector (lemma 1.19), so by step 2
    \[q(\vek v) = |\vek v|^2\,q(\vek v/|\vek v|) \le \lambda_1\,|\vek v|^2 .\]
    For \(\vek v = \vek 0\) both sides are zero. So the inequality \(q(\vek v) \le \lambda_1|\vek v|^2\) holds for every vector.
  4. Let \(\mat B\) be the matrix obtained from \(-\mat A\) by adding \(\lambda_1\) to every diagonal entry: \(B_{\mathrm{xx}} = \lambda_1 - A_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(B_{\mathrm{xy}} = -A_{\mathrm{xy}}\), and so on. We write \(\mat B = \lambda_1\mat I - \mat A\). The matrix \(\mat B\) is symmetric because \(\mat A\) is symmetric. The component \(i\) of the vector \(\mat B\,\vek v\) is \(\lambda_1 v_{\mathrm{i}} - \sum_{\mathrm{j}} A_{\mathrm{ij}} v_{\mathrm{j}}\), so \(\mat B\,\vek v = \lambda_1\vek v - \mat A\,\vek v\). By (2.59), items 2 to 4 of the properties of the scalar product (lemma 2.12) and step 3,
    \[\vek v^\T\mat B\,\vek v = \vek v\cdot(\lambda_1\vek v - \mat A\,\vek v) = \lambda_1|\vek v|^2 - q(\vek v) \ge 0\]
    for every vector \(\vek v\). For \(\vek v = \vek n_1\) the value is \(\lambda_1 \cdot 1 - \lambda_1 = 0\).
  5. Let \(\vek m\) be any vector and \(t\) any number. Into step 4 we put \(\vek v = \vek n_1 + t\,\vek m\). By lemma 1.97 on the linearity of the product with a matrix, \(\mat B\,(\vek n_1 + t\,\vek m) = \mat B\,\vek n_1 + t\,\mat B\,\vek m\), and by items 1, 2 and 3 of the properties of the scalar product
    \[(\vek n_1 + t\,\vek m)\cdot(\mat B\,\vek n_1 + t\,\mat B\,\vek m) = \vek n_1\cdot(\mat B\,\vek n_1) + t\,\vek n_1\cdot(\mat B\,\vek m) + t\,\vek m\cdot(\mat B\,\vek n_1) + t^2\,\vek m\cdot(\mat B\,\vek m) .\]
    The first term is zero by step 4. The matrix \(\mat B\) is symmetric, so by (2.60) \(\vek n_1\cdot(\mat B\,\vek m) = \vek m\cdot(\mat B\,\vek n_1)\), and the two middle terms are equal. We write \(a = \vek m\cdot(\mat B\,\vek n_1)\) and \(b = \vek m\cdot(\mat B\,\vek m)\). Step 4 then says that
    \[2t\,a + t^2 b \ge 0 \qquad \text{for every number } t .\]
    Here \(b \ge 0\), which is step 4 for \(\vek v = \vek m\).
  6. Let us show that \(a = 0\). Suppose that \(a \neq 0\), and take \(t = -a/(b + 1)\), which we may, because \(b + 1 \ge 1\). We substitute and bring the expression to the common denominator \((b + 1)^2\):
    \[2t\,a + t^2 b = -\frac{2a^2}{b + 1} + \frac{a^2 b}{(b + 1)^2} = \frac{-2a^2(b + 1) + a^2 b}{(b + 1)^2} = -\frac{a^2\,(b + 2)}{(b + 1)^2} .\]
    Because \(a^2 > 0\) and \(b + 2 > 0\), this is a negative number, which contradicts step 5 (figure 2.67). So \(a = \vek m\cdot(\mat B\,\vek n_1) = 0\) for every vector \(\vek m\).
  7. Take \(\vek m = \mat B\,\vek n_1\). By step 6, \((\mat B\,\vek n_1)\cdot(\mat B\,\vek n_1) = 0\), so by item 4 of the properties of the scalar product \(\mat B\,\vek n_1 = \vek 0\). By step 4, \(\mat B\,\vek n_1 = \lambda_1\vek n_1 - \mat A\,\vek n_1\), hence \(\mat A\,\vek n_1 = \lambda_1\vek n_1\).

2. The second direction.

  1. The matrix \(\mat A\) maps every vector perpendicular to \(\vek n_1\) to a vector perpendicular to \(\vek n_1\). Indeed, if \(\vek w\cdot\vek n_1 = 0\), then by (2.60) and step 7
    \[\vek n_1\cdot(\mat A\,\vek w) = \vek w\cdot(\mat A\,\vek n_1) = \vek w\cdot(\lambda_1\vek n_1) = \lambda_1\,(\vek w\cdot\vek n_1) = 0 .\]
  2. Let \(S_1\) be the set of unit vectors perpendicular to \(\vek n_1 = (n_{1\mathrm{x}}, n_{1\mathrm{y}}, n_{1\mathrm{z}})\). The set is not empty. If \(n_{1\mathrm{x}}\) and \(n_{1\mathrm{y}}\) are not both zero, the vector \((-n_{1\mathrm{y}}, n_{1\mathrm{x}}, 0)\) is nonzero and perpendicular to \(\vek n_1\), since the scalar product is \(-n_{1\mathrm{y}}n_{1\mathrm{x}} + n_{1\mathrm{x}}n_{1\mathrm{y}} + 0 = 0\). Divided by its length, it lies in \(S_1\). Otherwise \(\vek n_1 = \pm\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) lies in \(S_1\).
  3. The set \(S_1\) is bounded because it is part of the set \(S\). By definition 1.74 it is closed if the limit of every convergent sequence of vectors from \(S_1\) lies in \(S_1\) again. Let such a sequence converge to \(\vek w\). The vector \(\vek w\) lies in \(S\), because \(S\) is closed. The scalar products of the terms with \(\vek n_1\) are all zero. Each such product is the sum of three coordinates multiplied by the fixed numbers \(n_{1\mathrm{x}}\), \(n_{1\mathrm{y}}\) and \(n_{1\mathrm{z}}\). By lemma 1.66 the coordinates converge to the coordinates of the vector \(\vek w\), so by lemma 1.60 the products converge to \(\vek w\cdot\vek n_1\). The limit of a sequence of zeros is zero, so \(\vek w\cdot\vek n_1 = 0\), and \(\vek w\) lies in \(S_1\).
  4. By the theorem on the largest value in space, \(q\) attains on \(S_1\) a largest value \(\lambda_2\) at some \(\vek n_2\) in \(S_1\). Since \(S_1\) is part of the set \(S\), on which the largest value is \(\lambda_1\), \(\lambda_2 \le \lambda_1\).
  5. We repeat steps 3 to 6 with \(\lambda_2\), \(\vek n_2\) and \(\mat B_2 = \lambda_2\mat I - \mat A\) in place of \(\lambda_1\), \(\vek n_1\) and \(\mat B\), except that we take only vectors \(\vek v\) and \(\vek m\) perpendicular to \(\vek n_1\). For a nonzero \(\vek v\) perpendicular to \(\vek n_1\), \(\vek v/|\vek v|\) lies in \(S_1\), so \(q(\vek v) \le \lambda_2|\vek v|^2\) and \(\vek v^\T\mat B_2\,\vek v \ge 0\). If \(\vek m\) is perpendicular to \(\vek n_1\), so is \(\vek n_2 + t\,\vek m\), since \((\vek n_2 + t\,\vek m)\cdot\vek n_1 = 0 + t \cdot 0\). Steps 5 and 6 therefore give \(\vek m\cdot(\mat B_2\,\vek n_2) = 0\) for every \(\vek m\) perpendicular to \(\vek n_1\).
  6. The vector \(\mat B_2\,\vek n_2 = \lambda_2\vek n_2 - \mat A\,\vek n_2\) is perpendicular to \(\vek n_1\). Indeed, the vector \(\vek n_2\) is perpendicular to \(\vek n_1\) because it lies in \(S_1\), and the vector \(\mat A\,\vek n_2\) by step 8. So we may take it as \(\vek m\) in step 12, and as in step 7 we get \(\mat B_2\,\vek n_2 = \vek 0\), hence \(\mat A\,\vek n_2 = \lambda_2\vek n_2\).

3. The third direction.

  1. Let \(\vek n_3 = \vek n_1\times\vek n_2\). By lemma 2.31 on the properties of the vector product it is perpendicular to \(\vek n_1\) and to \(\vek n_2\), and \(|\vek n_3|^2 = |\vek n_1|^2|\vek n_2|^2 - (\vek n_1\cdot\vek n_2)^2 = 1 - 0 = 1\).
  2. The vector \(\mat A\,\vek n_3\) is perpendicular to \(\vek n_1\) by step 8, because \(\vek n_3\) is perpendicular to \(\vek n_1\). It is also perpendicular to \(\vek n_2\), since by (2.60) and step 13 \(\vek n_2\cdot(\mat A\,\vek n_3) = \vek n_3\cdot(\mat A\,\vek n_2) = \lambda_2\,(\vek n_3\cdot\vek n_2) = 0\).
  3. The vectors \(\vek n_1\), \(\vek n_2\) and \(\vek n_3\) are mutually perpendicular unit vectors, so they are the unit vectors of the axes of a turned coordinate system, as we saw at the end of section 2.9.1. By lemma 2.38 on the components of a vector in turned axes, the components of the vector \(\mat A\,\vek n_3\) in this system are its scalar products with \(\vek n_1\), \(\vek n_2\) and \(\vek n_3\). By step 15 these are the numbers \(0\), \(0\) and \(\lambda_3 = \vek n_3\cdot(\mat A\,\vek n_3)\).
  4. By lemma 1.21 on a vector as a sum of steps along the axes, used in this system, \(\mat A\,\vek n_3 = 0\,\vek n_1 + 0\,\vek n_2 + \lambda_3\,\vek n_3 = \lambda_3\vek n_3\).
  5. We have \(\lambda_3 = q(\vek n_3)\), and \(\vek n_3\) lies in \(S_1\), because it is a unit vector perpendicular to \(\vek n_1\). Since \(\lambda_2\) is the largest value of \(q\) on \(S_1\), \(\lambda_3 \le \lambda_2\).

Source: The theorem is stated in CM §2.5.6.3 (PDF p. 93) and SADD §1.6 (PDF pp. 21-22). CM shows that the eigenvalues of a symmetric matrix are real and that eigenvectors with different eigenvalues are perpendicular (PDF pp. 92-93). The proof through the largest value is not from the sources.

The proof has shown why principal directions exist. The expression \(\vek n^\T\mat A\,\vek n\) attains a largest value on the unit vectors, because it is a polynomial and because the unit vectors form a closed and bounded set. Steps 5 to 7 are the precise form of the idea from the beginning of the section. If \(\mat A\,\vek n_1\) were not a multiple of the vector \(\vek n_1\), we could tilt \(\vek n_1\) toward the vector \(-\vek m = \mat A\,\vek n_1 - \lambda_1\vek n_1\), where \(\vek m = \mat B\,\vek n_1\) is the vector of step 7, so that the expression \(\vek n^\T\mat A\,\vek n\) on unit vectors would grow. The vector \(-\vek m\) is the part of \(\mat A\,\vek n_1\) perpendicular to \(\vek n_1\) (lemma 2.14 on splitting a vector), for stress therefore the shear part. In step 5 the value of the expression \(\vek v^\T\mat B\,\vek v\) at the tilted vector \(\vek v = \vek n_1 + t\,\vek m\) is \(2t\,a + t^2 b\). For small \(t\) the term \(t^2 b\) is much smaller than the term \(2t\,a\), so the sum is negative for \(t\) with the sign opposite to that of \(a\) (figure 2.67). For \(\vek m = \mat B\,\vek n_1\) we have \(a = |\vek m|^2 > 0\), so this happens for \(t < 0\), when we tilt \(\vek n_1\) toward \(-\vek m\). A negative value means that \(\vek v^\T\mat A\,\vek v > \lambda_1|\vek v|^2\) (step 4), so the expression at the unit vector \(\vek v/|\vek v|\) is larger than \(\lambda_1\) (step 3). The second direction we obtained with the same move on a smaller set, the circle of unit vectors perpendicular to \(\vek n_1\) (figure 2.68). The third direction is the vector product of the first two. The matrix maps it to a vector that is perpendicular to \(\vek n_1\) (step 8) and to \(\vek n_2\) (step 15), and so to a multiple of it (steps 16 and 17).

We used the symmetry of the matrix in steps 5, 8 and 15. Without it the theorem fails. Take the matrix with three rows and three columns that has the matrix with two rows and two columns of the previous section in its upper left corner, the number \(1\) at the place \((z, z)\), and zeros elsewhere. It is not symmetric. The first two equations of the system \(\mat A\,\vek v = \lambda\,\vek v\) are for it the equations of the previous section for the components \(v_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(v_{\mathrm{y}}\), so these components are zero, and the third equation is \(v_{\mathrm{z}} = \lambda\,v_{\mathrm{z}}\). Its eigenvectors are therefore only the multiples of \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), with the eigenvalue \(1\), and this matrix does not have three perpendicular eigenvectors. The theorem also holds for symmetric matrices with \(m\) rows and \(m\) columns, which have \(m\) mutually perpendicular unit eigenvectors (CM §2.5.6.2, PDF p. 92). The proof would then repeat steps 8 to 13, each time on the unit vectors perpendicular to all directions found so far. The vector product, which exists only in a space with three dimensions, would not be needed.

The matrix of stress components is symmetric, so the theorem says that the stress at every point of every body in equilibrium has three mutually perpendicular principal directions. We denote the principal stresses, ordered by size, by \(\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \sigma_3\), as SADD does (SADD §3.4, PDF pp. 69-71). They are the numbers \(\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \lambda_3\) of the theorem for \(\mat A = \ten\sigma\). The indices \(1\), \(2\) and \(3\) do not mean the axes \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\) here, but the order by size. The directions \(\vek n_1\), \(\vek n_2\) and \(\vek n_3 = \vek n_1\times\vek n_2\) of the theorem are the unit vectors of the axes of a new rectangular Cartesian coordinate system with its origin at \(P\). Because \(\vek n_3 = \vek n_1\times\vek n_2\), the axes follow each other in the same order as the axes \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\), for which \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\). There are no other principal stresses. Indeed, let \(\ten\sigma\,\vek v = \mu\,\vek v\) for some nonzero vector \(\vek v\). By lemma 1.21 on a vector as a sum of steps along the axes, applied in these axes as in step 17 of the proof, \(\vek v = \bar v_1\vek n_1 + \bar v_2\vek n_2 + \bar v_3\vek n_3\), and by lemma 1.97 on the linearity of the product with a matrix, \(\vek 0 = \ten\sigma\,\vek v - \mu\,\vek v = (\sigma_1 - \mu)\,\bar v_1\vek n_1 + (\sigma_2 - \mu)\,\bar v_2\vek n_2 + (\sigma_3 - \mu)\,\bar v_3\vek n_3\). The scalar product with \(\vek n_{\mathrm{a}}\) gives \((\sigma_{\mathrm{a}} - \mu)\,\bar v_{\mathrm{a}} = 0\) for \(a = 1, 2, 3\), because the directions are unit vectors and mutually perpendicular. At least one of the numbers \(\bar v_{\mathrm{a}}\) is not zero, because \(\vek v\) is not the zero vector, so \(\mu\) is one of the numbers \(\sigma_1\), \(\sigma_2\), \(\sigma_3\). That the triple does not depend on which direction with the largest value of \(\vek n^\T\ten\sigma\,\vek n\) we choose in step 2 of the proof, we will show in corollary 2.67. We will use the system with its axes in the principal directions often, so we give it a name. The theorem holds for every symmetric matrix, so we define the name for every such matrix.

Definition 2.62 Principal axes (glavne osi)

Let \(\mat A\) be a symmetric matrix with three rows and three columns and \(P\) a point. The principal axes (glavne osi) of the matrix \(\mat A\) at \(P\) are a rectangular Cartesian coordinate system with its origin at \(P\) whose axes \(1\), \(2\) and \(3\) have the unit vectors \(\vek n_1\), \(\vek n_2\) and \(\vek n_3\), where \(\vek n_1\), \(\vek n_2\) and \(\vek n_3 = \vek n_1\times\vek n_2\) are mutually perpendicular unit eigenvectors of the matrix \(\mat A\) with eigenvalues \(\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \lambda_3\), as in theorem 2.61:

\[\mat A\,\vek n_1 = \lambda_1\,\vek n_1 , \qquad \mat A\,\vek n_2 = \lambda_2\,\vek n_2 , \qquad \mat A\,\vek n_3 = \lambda_3\,\vek n_3 .\]

When \(\mat A = \ten\sigma\) is the matrix of stress components at \(P\), these are the principal axes of the stress at \(P\). The directions of their axes are then principal directions, and the numbers \(\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \lambda_3\) are the principal stresses \(\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \sigma_3\). When \(\mat A = \ten\varepsilon\) is the small strain matrix at \(P\) (definition 1.115), these are the principal axes of the strain at \(P\).

Source: SADD §1.6 (PDF pp. 21-22), where the axes in the principal directions of a symmetric tensor are called the principal axes of the tensor, SADD §3.4 (PDF p. 69) for stress, where they are also called the principal coordinate system, and SADD §2.4 (PDF pp. 48-49) for strain. CM speaks only of principal directions and principal planes (CM §4.3.2, PDF p. 189).

Principal axes exist for every symmetric matrix, because the directions of theorem 2.61 give them, as we saw before the definition. But they are not always determined uniquely. In a bar with the stress \(\sigma > 0\), a principal direction with the principal stress \(0\) is every unit vector perpendicular to the axis of the bar, as we saw in section 2.11. Axis \(1\) of the principal axes therefore lies along the axis of the bar, and axes \(2\) and \(3\) can be any two perpendicular directions in the cross section, as long as \(\vek n_3 = \vek n_1\times\vek n_2\). The definition allows every such choice, because it only requires that the unit vectors of the axes be eigenvectors with eigenvalues ordered by size. What does the stress look like, written in principal axes?

Corollary 2.63 Stress in principal axes (napetost v glavnih oseh)

Let \(\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \sigma_3\) be the principal stresses at the point \(P\), \(\vek n_1\), \(\vek n_2\), \(\vek n_3 = \vek n_1\times\vek n_2\) the corresponding principal directions of theorem 2.61 for \(\mat A = \ten\sigma\), and the principal axes of the stress at \(P\) the coordinate system with its axes in these directions (definition 2.62). Then:

  1. In the principal axes the matrix of stress components is diagonal, with \(\sigma_1\), \(\sigma_2\) and \(\sigma_3\) on the diagonal.
  2. Let the unit normal \(\vek n\) have the components \(\bar n_1 = \vek n\cdot\vek n_1\), \(\bar n_2 = \vek n\cdot\vek n_2\) and \(\bar n_3 = \vek n\cdot\vek n_3\) in the principal axes. The normal stress on the face with the normal \(\vek n\) is
    \[\sigma_{\mathrm{n}} = \sigma_1\,\bar n_1^2 + \sigma_2\,\bar n_2^2 + \sigma_3\,\bar n_3^2 , \tag{2.62}\]
    and \(\sigma_3 \le \sigma_{\mathrm{n}} \le \sigma_1\). The normal stress is largest on the face with the normal \(\vek n_1\) and smallest on the face with the normal \(\vek n_3\).
Proof

We must show both items. For the first we use the definition of the stress components in the principal axes, for the second we split the normal into steps along the principal axes. The indices \(a\) and \(b\) run over \(1\), \(2\) and \(3\).

1. A diagonal matrix.

  1. By definition 2.25, used in the principal axes, the row \(a\) of the matrix of components is the traction vector \(\vek t(\vek n_{\mathrm{a}})\) on the face with the normal \(\vek n_{\mathrm{a}}\). Its components are, by lemma 2.38, the scalar products with \(\vek n_1\), \(\vek n_2\) and \(\vek n_3\). The entry in row \(a\) and column \(b\) is therefore \(\bar\sigma_{\mathrm{ab}} = \vek t(\vek n_{\mathrm{a}})\cdot\vek n_{\mathrm{b}}\).
  2. By Cauchy's formula and theorem 2.61, \(\vek t(\vek n_{\mathrm{a}}) = \ten\sigma\,\vek n_{\mathrm{a}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{a}}\vek n_{\mathrm{a}}\).
  3. So \(\bar\sigma_{\mathrm{ab}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{a}}\,(\vek n_{\mathrm{a}}\cdot\vek n_{\mathrm{b}})\). This is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}\) for \(a = b\), because \(\vek n_{\mathrm{a}}\) is a unit vector, and \(0\) for \(a \neq b\), because \(\vek n_{\mathrm{a}}\) and \(\vek n_{\mathrm{b}}\) are perpendicular.

2. The normal stress.

  1. By lemma 1.21 on a vector as a sum of steps along the axes, used in the principal axes, \(\vek n = \bar n_1\vek n_1 + \bar n_2\vek n_2 + \bar n_3\vek n_3\).
  2. By lemma 1.97 on the linearity of the product with a matrix and by theorem 2.61, \(\vek t(\vek n) = \ten\sigma\,\vek n = \bar n_1\sigma_1\vek n_1 + \bar n_2\sigma_2\vek n_2 + \bar n_3\sigma_3\vek n_3\).
  3. The normal stress is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}} = \vek t(\vek n)\cdot\vek n\). We substitute steps 4 and 5 and multiply out by the properties of the scalar product. We get nine terms of the form \(\bar n_{\mathrm{a}}\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}\bar n_{\mathrm{b}}\,(\vek n_{\mathrm{a}}\cdot\vek n_{\mathrm{b}})\). The terms with \(a \neq b\) are zero, because the directions are perpendicular, and in the terms with \(a = b\), \(\vek n_{\mathrm{a}}\cdot\vek n_{\mathrm{a}} = 1\). What remains is (2.62).
  4. In the same way \(1 = \vek n\cdot\vek n = \bar n_1^2 + \bar n_2^2 + \bar n_3^2\).
  5. From (2.62) we subtract \(\sigma_3 = \sigma_3\,(\bar n_1^2 + \bar n_2^2 + \bar n_3^2)\) and get \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}} - \sigma_3 = (\sigma_1 - \sigma_3)\,\bar n_1^2 + (\sigma_2 - \sigma_3)\,\bar n_2^2\). Both brackets are nonnegative, because \(\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \sigma_3\), and so are the squares. Hence \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}} \ge \sigma_3\).
  6. Likewise \(\sigma_1 - \sigma_{\mathrm{n}} = (\sigma_1 - \sigma_2)\,\bar n_2^2 + (\sigma_1 - \sigma_3)\,\bar n_3^2 \ge 0\).
  7. For \(\vek n = \vek n_1\), \(\bar n_1 = 1\) and \(\bar n_2 = \bar n_3 = 0\), so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}} = \sigma_1\). For \(\vek n = \vek n_3\) likewise \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}} = \sigma_3\).

Source: SADD (3.4.3), (3.4.5) (PDF pp. 69-70). SADD §1.6 (PDF p. 22) states that the largest eigenvalue is the largest diagonal entry the matrix can have in any axes, and the smallest the smallest. The proof is not from the sources.

The formula (2.62) has a simple picture. The numbers \(\bar n_1^2\), \(\bar n_2^2\) and \(\bar n_3^2\) are nonnegative and add up to \(1\), so the normal stress on every face is a weighted average of the three principal stresses, with the weights \(\bar n_1^2\), \(\bar n_2^2\) and \(\bar n_3^2\). An average cannot be larger than the largest value nor smaller than the smallest. The principal axes show something more. Every stress at a point is, seen in the right axes, only tension or compression in three mutually perpendicular directions, without any shear (SADD, figure 3.5, PDF p. 69).

In the example with numbers, where \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 67\) MPa, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = 53\) MPa and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}} = 24\) MPa and the other components are zero, the principal stresses are \(\sigma_1 = 85\) MPa, \(\sigma_2 = 35\) MPa and \(\sigma_3 = 0\), with the principal directions \(\vek n_1 = (\tfrac45, \tfrac35, 0)\), \(\vek n_2 = (-\tfrac35, \tfrac45, 0)\) and \(\vek n_3 = \vek n_1\times\vek n_2 = (0, 0, \tfrac{16}{25} + \tfrac{9}{25}) = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\). The largest normal stress among all faces through \(P\), not only among the faces tilted about the axis \(z\), is therefore 85 MPa. The smallest, though, is not 35 MPa, the smallest value in figure 2.64, but \(0\) on the face perpendicular to the axis \(z\), which is not among the faces of that figure. Let us also try the formula (2.62) at \(\theta = 0^\circ\), that is, for \(\vek n = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). Then \(\bar n_1 = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\cdot\vek n_1 = \tfrac45\), \(\bar n_2 = -\tfrac35\) and \(\bar n_3 = 0\), and

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{n}} = 85 \cdot \frac{16}{25} + 35 \cdot \frac{9}{25} = 54{,}4 + 12{,}6 = 67\ \text{MPa} ,\]

which is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), as it must be.

2.11.3 The characteristic equation and the invariants

Theorem 2.61 tells us that principal directions exist, but not how to compute them. We cannot simply find the largest value of the expression \(\vek n^\T\ten\sigma\,\vek n\) over all unit vectors, and in the example with numbers we guessed the principal direction from the graph. We need an equation from which to compute the principal stresses. The condition \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \lambda\,\vek n\) is a system of three equations for the three unknown components of the vector \(\vek n\), in which the unknown number \(\lambda\) also appears. We eliminate the components with the determinant, which tells us when such a system has a nonzero solution (lemma 2.53 on a zero determinant). Recall the formula for the determinant of a matrix \(\mat M\) with three rows and three columns (equation (2.51) in definition 2.50):

\[\begin{aligned} \det\mat M &= M_{\mathrm{xx}}M_{\mathrm{yy}}M_{\mathrm{zz}} + M_{\mathrm{xy}}M_{\mathrm{yz}}M_{\mathrm{zx}} + M_{\mathrm{xz}}M_{\mathrm{yx}}M_{\mathrm{zy}} \\ &\quad - M_{\mathrm{xz}}M_{\mathrm{yy}}M_{\mathrm{zx}} - M_{\mathrm{xy}}M_{\mathrm{yx}}M_{\mathrm{zz}} - M_{\mathrm{xx}}M_{\mathrm{yz}}M_{\mathrm{zy}} . \end{aligned}\]

Each of the six terms is a product of three entries, one from each row and each column.

Lemma 2.64 Characteristic equation (karakteristična enačba)

Let \(\mat A\) be a matrix with three rows and three columns and \(\lambda\) a number. By \(\mat A - \lambda\mat I\) we denote the matrix obtained from \(\mat A\) by subtracting \(\lambda\) from every diagonal entry. Then:

  1. The number \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue of the matrix \(\mat A\) if and only if \(\det(\mat A - \lambda\mat I) = 0\).
  2. For every number \(\lambda\)
    \[\det(\mat A - \lambda\mat I) = -\lambda^3 + I_1\lambda^2 - I_2\lambda + I_3 ,\]
    where
    \[\begin{aligned} I_1 &= A_{\mathrm{xx}} + A_{\mathrm{yy}} + A_{\mathrm{zz}} , \\ I_2 &= A_{\mathrm{xx}}A_{\mathrm{yy}} + A_{\mathrm{yy}}A_{\mathrm{zz}} + A_{\mathrm{zz}}A_{\mathrm{xx}} - A_{\mathrm{xy}}A_{\mathrm{yx}} - A_{\mathrm{yz}}A_{\mathrm{zy}} - A_{\mathrm{zx}}A_{\mathrm{xz}} , \\ I_3 &= \det\mat A . \end{aligned}\]

The eigenvalues of the matrix \(\mat A\) are therefore the real solutions of the equation

\[\lambda^3 - I_1\lambda^2 + I_2\lambda - I_3 = 0 . \tag{2.63}\]
Proof

We must show both items. The first we reduce to the lemma on a zero determinant, the second we get by writing out the formula for the determinant.

1. The eigenvalues.

  1. The matrix \(\mat A - \lambda\mat I\) differs from \(\mat A\) only in the diagonal entry of each row, so the component \(i\) of the vector \((\mat A - \lambda\mat I)\,\vek v\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} A_{\mathrm{ij}}v_{\mathrm{j}} - \lambda v_{\mathrm{i}}\). This is the component \(i\) of the vector \(\mat A\,\vek v - \lambda\,\vek v\).
  2. The equation \(\mat A\,\vek v = \lambda\,\vek v\) is therefore equivalent to the equation \((\mat A - \lambda\mat I)\,\vek v = \vek 0\).
  3. By lemma 2.53 on a zero determinant, this equation has a nonzero solution \(\vek v\) if and only if \(\det(\mat A - \lambda\mat I) = 0\). By definition 2.59 this happens exactly when \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue.

2. The polynomial.

  1. Into the formula for the determinant we put \(M_{\mathrm{xx}} = A_{\mathrm{xx}} - \lambda\), \(M_{\mathrm{yy}} = A_{\mathrm{yy}} - \lambda\), \(M_{\mathrm{zz}} = A_{\mathrm{zz}} - \lambda\), and leave the other entries, \(M_{\mathrm{xy}} = A_{\mathrm{xy}}\), and so on.
  2. The first term contains all three diagonal entries. Multiplying out the brackets,
    \[(A_{\mathrm{xx}} - \lambda)(A_{\mathrm{yy}} - \lambda)(A_{\mathrm{zz}} - \lambda) = -\lambda^3 + (A_{\mathrm{xx}} + A_{\mathrm{yy}} + A_{\mathrm{zz}})\,\lambda^2 - (A_{\mathrm{xx}}A_{\mathrm{yy}} + A_{\mathrm{yy}}A_{\mathrm{zz}} + A_{\mathrm{zz}}A_{\mathrm{xx}})\,\lambda + A_{\mathrm{xx}}A_{\mathrm{yy}}A_{\mathrm{zz}} .\]
  3. The last three terms contain one diagonal entry each. For example, \(-(A_{\mathrm{xx}} - \lambda)\,A_{\mathrm{yz}}A_{\mathrm{zy}} = -A_{\mathrm{xx}}A_{\mathrm{yz}}A_{\mathrm{zy}} + \lambda\,A_{\mathrm{yz}}A_{\mathrm{zy}}\). In the same way \(-A_{\mathrm{xy}}A_{\mathrm{yx}}(A_{\mathrm{zz}} - \lambda)\) and \(-A_{\mathrm{xz}}(A_{\mathrm{yy}} - \lambda)A_{\mathrm{zx}}\) give the terms \(\lambda\,A_{\mathrm{xy}}A_{\mathrm{yx}}\) and \(\lambda\,A_{\mathrm{xz}}A_{\mathrm{zx}}\).
  4. The second and the third term, \(A_{\mathrm{xy}}A_{\mathrm{yz}}A_{\mathrm{zx}}\) and \(A_{\mathrm{xz}}A_{\mathrm{yx}}A_{\mathrm{zy}}\), contain no diagonal entries and therefore no \(\lambda\).
  5. We collect the terms by powers of the number \(\lambda\). At \(\lambda^3\) the coefficient is \(-1\), at \(\lambda^2\) it is \(I_1\). At \(\lambda\) it is \(-(A_{\mathrm{xx}}A_{\mathrm{yy}} + A_{\mathrm{yy}}A_{\mathrm{zz}} + A_{\mathrm{zz}}A_{\mathrm{xx}}) + A_{\mathrm{xy}}A_{\mathrm{yx}} + A_{\mathrm{yz}}A_{\mathrm{zy}} + A_{\mathrm{zx}}A_{\mathrm{xz}}\), which is \(-I_2\). The terms without \(\lambda\) are the six terms of the formula for the determinant with \(\lambda = 0\) put in, that is, \(\det\mat A = I_3\). We checked the algebra with SymPy.
  6. By item 1 the eigenvalues are the solutions of the equation \(-\lambda^3 + I_1\lambda^2 - I_2\lambda + I_3 = 0\). Multiplying it by \(-1\) we get (2.63).

Source: CM (2.5.33), (2.5.42), (2.5.43) (PDF pp. 91, 94), CM (4.3.11), (4.3.12) (PDF p. 189), SADD (1.6.2), (1.6.3) (PDF p. 21). The sources write \(-\lambda^3 + I_1\lambda^2 - I_2\lambda + I_3 = 0\), which is the same equation multiplied by \(-1\). The expansion of the determinant is not from the sources.

We have reduced the search for an eigenvector, three unknown components together with an unknown number, to one equation for a single unknown \(\lambda\). The equation (2.63) is called the characteristic equation (karakteristična enačba) of the matrix (CM, PDF p. 91), and the expression \(\det(\mat A - \lambda\mat I)\) as a function of the number \(\lambda\) its characteristic polynomial (karakteristični polinom) (KREYSZIG §8.1, PDF p. 352). The solutions of the equation are also called the roots of the polynomial, because at them the value of the polynomial is zero. Once we know an eigenvalue \(\lambda\), we get the eigenvector from the system \((\mat A - \lambda\mat I)\,\vek v = \vek 0\). The coefficients of the equation for stress get a name.

Definition 2.65 Stress invariants (invariante napetosti)

Let \(\ten\sigma\) be the symmetric matrix of stress components at the point \(P\). The stress invariants (invariante napetosti) at \(P\) are the numbers

\[\begin{aligned} I_1 &= \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} , \\ I_2 &= \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} - \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}^2 - \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}^2 - \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}^2 , \\ I_3 &= \det\ten\sigma , \end{aligned} \tag{2.64}\]

the coefficients of the characteristic equation of the matrix \(\ten\sigma\). The number \(I_1\) is the trace of the matrix \(\ten\sigma\), the sum of its diagonal entries, \(\tr\ten\sigma\) (section 1.17.4).

Source: CM (4.3.1), (4.3.2) (PDF p. 186), SADD (3.4.1), (3.4.2) (PDF p. 69).

The formula for \(I_2\) is the formula of lemma 2.64 in which we wrote the products \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\) as squares because of the symmetry. The invariants have units: \(I_1\) is in MPa, \(I_2\) in MPa², \(I_3\) in MPa³. CM writes the second invariant with the trace, \(I_2 = \tfrac12\big((\tr\ten\sigma)^2 - \tr(\ten\sigma\ten\sigma)\big)\) (CM (2.5.16), PDF p. 85, and CM (4.3.1), PDF p. 186). This is the same number. By the rule for the matrix product the diagonal entry \(i\) of the matrix \(\ten\sigma\ten\sigma\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}}\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\sigma_{\mathrm{ji}}\), so

\[\begin{aligned} \tr(\ten\sigma\ten\sigma) &= \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}^2 + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}^2 + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}^2 + 2\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}^2 + 2\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}^2 + 2\sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}^2 , \\ (\tr\ten\sigma)^2 &= \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}^2 + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}^2 + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}^2 + 2\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}) . \end{aligned}\]

The squares of the diagonal entries cancel in the subtraction, and half of what remains is \(I_2\) of (2.64). SADD uses the same signs (SADD (1.6.3), PDF p. 21).

The word invariant (invarianta) means something that does not change. The next lemma justifies the name.

Lemma 2.66 The invariants do not change when the axes turn (invariante se ne spremenijo, ko zasučemo osi)

Let \(\ten\sigma\) be the matrix of stress components at the point \(P\) in the chosen axes and \(\bar{\ten\sigma}\) the matrix of components of the same stress in turned axes. Then \(\det(\bar{\ten\sigma} - \lambda\mat I) = \det(\ten\sigma - \lambda\mat I)\) for every number \(\lambda\), and the stress invariants computed from \(\bar{\ten\sigma}\) are equal to the invariants computed from \(\ten\sigma\).

Proof

We must show that the two matrices have the same characteristic polynomial, and from this that they have the same coefficients.

  1. By theorem 2.42, \(\bar{\ten\sigma} = \mat Q\ten\sigma\mat Q^\T\), where \(\mat Q\) is the rotation matrix whose rows are the unit vectors of the turned axes. By lemma 2.40 on the orthogonality of the rotation matrix, \(\mat Q\mat Q^\T = \mat I\).
  2. The matrix \(\lambda\mat I\) has the number \(\lambda\) on its diagonal and zeros elsewhere. By the rule for the matrix product, therefore, \(\mat Q\,(\lambda\mat I) = \lambda\mat Q\) and \(\mat Q\,(\lambda\mat I)\,\mat Q^\T = \lambda\,\mat Q\mat Q^\T = \lambda\mat I\). By the distributivity of the matrix product (lemma 1.99) therefore
    \[\mat Q\,(\ten\sigma - \lambda\mat I)\,\mat Q^\T = \mat Q\ten\sigma\mat Q^\T - \lambda\mat I = \bar{\ten\sigma} - \lambda\mat I .\]
  3. By theorem 2.52 on the determinant of a product, used twice, \(\det\big(\mat Q\,(\ten\sigma - \lambda\mat I)\,\mat Q^\T\big) = \det\mat Q\,\det(\ten\sigma - \lambda\mat I)\,\det\mat Q^\T\).
  4. By the same theorem and lemma 2.51, \(\det\mat Q\,\det\mat Q^\T = \det(\mat Q\mat Q^\T) = \det\mat I = 1\). Steps 2 and 3 give \(\det(\bar{\ten\sigma} - \lambda\mat I) = \det(\ten\sigma - \lambda\mat I)\) for every number \(\lambda\).
  5. By lemma 2.64 both sides are polynomials of the form \(-\lambda^3 + I_1\lambda^2 - I_2\lambda + I_3\), one with the invariants \(\bar I_1\), \(\bar I_2\), \(\bar I_3\) of the matrix \(\bar{\ten\sigma}\), the other with the invariants \(I_1\), \(I_2\), \(I_3\) of the matrix \(\ten\sigma\). Subtracting them, the terms \(-\lambda^3\) cancel, and for every number \(\lambda\)
    \[c_2\lambda^2 + c_1\lambda + c_0 = 0 , \qquad c_2 = \bar I_1 - I_1 , \quad c_1 = I_2 - \bar I_2 , \quad c_0 = \bar I_3 - I_3 .\]
  6. At \(\lambda = 0\) we get \(c_0 = 0\). At \(\lambda = 1\) and \(\lambda = -1\) we get \(c_2 + c_1 = 0\) and \(c_2 - c_1 = 0\). Adding the two equations gives \(2c_2 = 0\), so \(c_2 = 0\) and then \(c_1 = 0\). The invariants are equal.

Source: SADD §1.6 (PDF p. 21) states that the invariants do not change under a change of the coordinate system, CM §4.3.1.1 (PDF p. 186). The proof is not from the sources.

The proof used only that \(\bar{\ten\sigma} = \mat Q\ten\sigma\mat Q^\T\) and that the determinant of a product is the product of the determinants. The lemma therefore holds for every matrix that changes like stress when the axes turn, for example for the small strain matrix. Now we can compute the characteristic equation in the principal axes, where the matrix is simplest.

Corollary 2.67 The principal stresses are the roots of the characteristic equation (glavne napetosti so rešitve karakteristične enačbe)

Let \(\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \sigma_3\) be the principal stresses at the point \(P\). Then for every number \(\lambda\)

\[\det(\ten\sigma - \lambda\mat I) = (\sigma_1 - \lambda)(\sigma_2 - \lambda)(\sigma_3 - \lambda) .\]

The solutions of the characteristic equation are therefore exactly the numbers \(\sigma_1\), \(\sigma_2\) and \(\sigma_3\), there are no other principal stresses, and

\[\begin{aligned} I_1 &= \sigma_1 + \sigma_2 + \sigma_3 , \\ I_2 &= \sigma_1\sigma_2 + \sigma_2\sigma_3 + \sigma_3\sigma_1 , \\ I_3 &= \sigma_1\sigma_2\sigma_3 . \end{aligned} \tag{2.65}\]
Proof

We compute the characteristic polynomial in the principal axes and use that it is the same in all axes.

  1. By item 1 of corollary 2.63, in the principal axes the matrix of components is diagonal, with \(\sigma_1\), \(\sigma_2\) and \(\sigma_3\) on the diagonal. The matrix \(\bar{\ten\sigma} - \lambda\mat I\) therefore has \(\sigma_1 - \lambda\), \(\sigma_2 - \lambda\) and \(\sigma_3 - \lambda\) on the diagonal and zeros elsewhere.
  2. In the formula for the determinant every term except the first contains at least one entry off the diagonal, which is zero. So \(\det(\bar{\ten\sigma} - \lambda\mat I) = (\sigma_1 - \lambda)(\sigma_2 - \lambda)(\sigma_3 - \lambda)\).
  3. By lemma 2.66, \(\det(\ten\sigma - \lambda\mat I) = \det(\bar{\ten\sigma} - \lambda\mat I)\), which proves the first equality.
  4. A product of three numbers is zero if and only if one of them is zero. The solutions of the characteristic equation are therefore \(\sigma_1\), \(\sigma_2\) and \(\sigma_3\). Every principal stress is, by lemma 2.64, a solution of this equation, so it equals one of these three numbers.
  5. Multiplying out the brackets we get \(-\lambda^3 + (\sigma_1 + \sigma_2 + \sigma_3)\lambda^2 - (\sigma_1\sigma_2 + \sigma_2\sigma_3 + \sigma_3\sigma_1)\lambda + \sigma_1\sigma_2\sigma_3\). By item 2 of lemma 2.64 the same polynomial equals \(-\lambda^3 + I_1\lambda^2 - I_2\lambda + I_3\). As in steps 5 and 6 of the proof of lemma 2.66, it follows that they have equal coefficients, which is (2.65).

Source: CM (2.5.44) (PDF p. 94), SADD (3.4.2) (PDF p. 69). The proof is not from the sources.

So the principal stresses depend only on the three invariants, which are the same in all axes. They are therefore a property of the stress at the point, not of the choice of axes, which is what we expect of a quantity that tells whether the material holds. Let us try it on the example with numbers, with the components \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 67\) MPa, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = 53\) MPa and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}} = 24\) MPa. The invariants are \(I_1 = 67 + 53 + 0 = 120\) MPa and \(I_2 = 67 \cdot 53 - 24^2 = 3551 - 576 = 2975\) MPa², and the third is \(I_3 = 0\), because every term of the formula for the determinant contains one entry of the third row, which are all zero. The characteristic equation is

\[\lambda^3 - 120\,\lambda^2 + 2975\,\lambda = \lambda\,(\lambda^2 - 120\,\lambda + 2975) = 0 .\]

One solution is \(\lambda = 0\), the other two we get from the quadratic equation with the school formula, \(\lambda = \tfrac12\big(120 \pm \sqrt{120^2 - 4 \cdot 2975}\big) = \tfrac12\,(120 \pm 50)\), that is, \(85\) and \(35\). These are the principal stresses we guessed from the graph, and indeed \(85 + 35 + 0 = 120\) and \(85 \cdot 35 = 2975\), as (2.65) requires.

So we compute the principal stresses and principal directions in three steps. First we compute the invariants by (2.64). Then we find the solutions of the characteristic equation (2.63), which are the principal stresses. Finally, for each principal stress \(\lambda\) we solve the system \((\ten\sigma - \lambda\mat I)\,\vek n = \vek 0\) and divide the solution by its length. Let us try the procedure on three examples.

Pure shear. The cube of section 2.6.6 has \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}} = \tau_0\), and all other components are zero. The invariants are \(I_1 = 0\), \(I_2 = -\sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}^2 = -\tau_0^2\) and \(I_3 = 0\), because each of the six terms of the formula for the determinant contains at least one entry that is zero. The characteristic equation is

\[\lambda^3 - \tau_0^2\,\lambda = \lambda\,(\lambda - \tau_0)(\lambda + \tau_0) = 0 ,\]

so the principal stresses are \(\sigma_1 = \tau_0\), \(\sigma_2 = 0\) and \(\sigma_3 = -\tau_0\). For \(\lambda = \tau_0\) the matrix \(\ten\sigma - \tau_0\mat I\) has the rows \((-\tau_0, 0, \tau_0)\), \((0, -\tau_0, 0)\) and \((\tau_0, 0, -\tau_0)\). The first row gives \(n_{\mathrm{x}} = n_{\mathrm{z}}\), the second \(n_{\mathrm{y}} = 0\), and the third is the first with the opposite sign. The unit solution is \(\vek n_1 = (1, 0, 1)/\sqrt2\), the direction of the diagonal \(AC\). For \(\lambda = 0\), \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \tau_0\,(n_{\mathrm{z}}, 0, n_{\mathrm{x}}) = \vek 0\) only for \(n_{\mathrm{x}} = n_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\), so \(\vek n_2 = \vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\). The third direction is \(\vek n_3 = \vek n_1\times\vek n_2 = (-1, 0, 1)/\sqrt2\), the direction of the diagonal \(BD\), and indeed \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n_3 = \tau_0\,(1, 0, -1)/\sqrt2 = -\tau_0\,\vek n_3\). At \(\tau_0 = 50\) MPa the principal stresses are \(50\), \(0\) and \(-50\) MPa (figure 2.69).

A bar and a fluid at rest. In a bar \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma\) is the only nonzero component, so \(I_1 = \sigma\), \(I_2 = 0\) and \(I_3 = 0\). The characteristic equation \(\lambda^3 - \sigma\lambda^2 = \lambda^2(\lambda - \sigma) = 0\) has the solution \(\sigma\) and the double solution \(0\). For \(\sigma > 0\) the principal stresses are \(\sigma_1 = \sigma\) and \(\sigma_2 = \sigma_3 = 0\), so for our steel bar with the diameter 10 mm under the force of 10 kN, \(127{,}3\) MPa, \(0\) and \(0\). Earlier we saw that for the principal stress \(0\) every unit vector perpendicular to the axis of the bar is a principal direction. This holds whenever two principal stresses are equal. Let, for example, \(\sigma_2 = \sigma_3\) and let \(\vek v\) be perpendicular to \(\vek n_1\). Its component along the principal axis \(1\) is \(\vek v\cdot\vek n_1 = 0\), so by lemma 1.21 on a vector as a sum of steps along the axes, used in the principal axes, \(\vek v = c_2\vek n_2 + c_3\vek n_3\) for some numbers \(c_2\) and \(c_3\), and by linearity \(\ten\sigma\,\vek v = c_2\sigma_2\vek n_2 + c_3\sigma_3\vek n_3 = \sigma_2\,\vek v\). The principal directions are then not unique, only the direction \(\vek n_1\) is determined. In a fluid at rest \(\ten\sigma = -p\,\mat I\), so \(I_1 = -3p\), \(I_2 = 3p^2\) and \(I_3 = -p^3\), and the characteristic equation is \(\lambda^3 + 3p\lambda^2 + 3p^2\lambda + p^3 = (\lambda + p)^3 = 0\). All three principal stresses are \(-p\), and every direction is a principal direction, as figure 2.27 shows. SADD distinguishes exactly these three cases: three different principal stresses, two equal and three equal (SADD §1.6, PDF p. 21).

An example with all components. At the point \(P\) let the matrix of stress components in MPa be

\[\ten\sigma = \begin{bmatrix} 16 & 32 & -4 \\ 32 & 28 & 28 \\ -4 & 28 & 46 \end{bmatrix} .\]

All six different components are nonzero. We chose the numbers ourselves, so that the results are whole numbers that we can check by hand. The invariants are

\[\begin{aligned} I_1 &= 16 + 28 + 46 = 90 , \\ I_2 &= 16 \cdot 28 + 28 \cdot 46 + 46 \cdot 16 - 32^2 - 28^2 - (-4)^2 = 2472 - 1824 = 648 , \end{aligned}\]

in MPa and MPa² respectively. The third invariant follows from the formula for the determinant, term by term:

\[\begin{aligned} I_3 &= 16 \cdot 28 \cdot 46 + 32 \cdot 28 \cdot (-4) + (-4) \cdot 32 \cdot 28 \\ &\quad - (-4) \cdot 28 \cdot (-4) - 32 \cdot 32 \cdot 46 - 16 \cdot 28 \cdot 28 = -46\,656 , \end{aligned}\]

in MPa³. The characteristic equation is

\[\lambda^3 - 90\,\lambda^2 + 648\,\lambda + 46\,656 = 0 .\]

We solved this equation of the third degree numerically with NumPy (the function numpy.roots). The solutions are \(72\), \(36\) and \(-18\), which we check by substitution. For \(\lambda = 72\), \(72^3 - 90 \cdot 72^2 + 648 \cdot 72 + 46\,656 = 373\,248 - 466\,560 + 46\,656 + 46\,656 = 0\). The principal stresses are \(\sigma_1 = 72\) MPa, \(\sigma_2 = 36\) MPa and \(\sigma_3 = -18\) MPa, and indeed \(72 + 36 - 18 = 90 = I_1\). The principal direction for \(\sigma_1 = 72\) follows from the system \((\ten\sigma - 72\,\mat I)\,\vek n = \vek 0\):

\[\begin{aligned} -56\,n_{\mathrm{x}} + 32\,n_{\mathrm{y}} - 4\,n_{\mathrm{z}} &= 0 , \\ 32\,n_{\mathrm{x}} - 44\,n_{\mathrm{y}} + 28\,n_{\mathrm{z}} &= 0 , \\ -4\,n_{\mathrm{x}} + 28\,n_{\mathrm{y}} - 26\,n_{\mathrm{z}} &= 0 . \end{aligned}\]

From the first equation \(n_{\mathrm{z}} = 8\,n_{\mathrm{y}} - 14\,n_{\mathrm{x}}\). Putting this into the third, we get \(-4\,n_{\mathrm{x}} + 28\,n_{\mathrm{y}} - 208\,n_{\mathrm{y}} + 364\,n_{\mathrm{x}} = 360\,n_{\mathrm{x}} - 180\,n_{\mathrm{y}} = 0\), so \(n_{\mathrm{y}} = 2\,n_{\mathrm{x}}\) and then \(n_{\mathrm{z}} = 2\,n_{\mathrm{x}}\). For \(n_{\mathrm{x}} = 1\) this is the vector \((1, 2, 2)\), which also satisfies the second equation, \(32 - 88 + 56 = 0\). Its length is \(3\), so \(\vek n_1 = (1, 2, 2)/3\). In the same way we get \(\vek n_2 = (2, 1, -2)/3\), and the third direction is \(\vek n_3 = \vek n_1\times\vek n_2 = (-2, 2, -1)/3\). Check: \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n_2 = (72, 36, -72)/3 = 36\,\vek n_2\) and \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n_3 = (36, -36, 18)/3 = -18\,\vek n_3\), and the directions are mutually perpendicular, for example \(\vek n_1\cdot\vek n_2 = (2 + 2 - 4)/9 = 0\). NumPy (the function numpy.linalg.eigh) gives the same directions, some with the opposite sign.

What does this tell us? On the faces of the cube perpendicular to the axes the normal stresses are \(16\), \(28\) and \(46\) MPa, all of them tension. But the largest normal stress through the point \(P\) is \(72\) MPa, more than half again as large as \(46\) MPa, and it acts on the face with the normal \((1, 2, 2)/3\), which is not along any axis. On the face with the normal \(\vek n_3\) the material is compressed by the stress \(18\) MPa, although all faces of the cube are in tension (figure 2.70). Anyone who judged the stress only by the faces of the cube would underestimate the largest stress and would not notice the compression at all.

2.11.4 Principal strains

Let us return to strain. In section 1.17 we derived that the strain of a short fibre leaving the point \(P\) in the direction of the unit vector \(\vek n\) is approximately

\[\varepsilon(\vek n) \approx \vek n^\T\ten\varepsilon\,\vek n = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}n_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}n_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}n_{\mathrm{z}}^2 + 2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}n_{\mathrm{x}}n_{\mathrm{y}} + 2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}n_{\mathrm{x}}n_{\mathrm{z}} + 2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}n_{\mathrm{y}}n_{\mathrm{z}}\]

((1.53)). Here \(\ten\varepsilon\) is the small strain matrix at \(P\) (definition 1.115). The approximation holds when the partial derivatives of the displacements are so small in size that we may neglect their products. The expression has the same form as the normal stress \(\vek n^\T\ten\sigma\,\vek n\). The matrix \(\ten\varepsilon\) is symmetric, since for example \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \tfrac12(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial y + \partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial x) = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yx}}\). Theorem 2.61 therefore holds for it too.

Definition 2.68 Principal strain and principal direction of strain (glavna deformacija in glavna smer deformacije)

Let \(\ten\varepsilon\) be the small strain matrix at the point \(P\) in a chosen rectangular Cartesian coordinate system. A unit vector \(\vek n\) is a principal direction of strain (glavna smer deformacije) at \(P\), and the number \(\lambda\) the corresponding principal strain (glavna deformacija), if

\[\ten\varepsilon\,\vek n = \lambda\,\vek n ,\]

that is, if \(\vek n\) is a unit eigenvector and \(\lambda\) an eigenvalue of the matrix \(\ten\varepsilon\).

Source: SADD §2.4, (2.4.1)-(2.4.3) (PDF pp. 48-49). CM §3.4.6 (PDF p. 138) defines principal strains in the same way for the Green-Lagrange tensor.

We denote the principal strains, ordered by size, by \(\varepsilon_1 \ge \varepsilon_2 \ge \varepsilon_3\). What they mean is told by a corollary that is for strains what corollary 2.63 is for stresses.

Corollary 2.69 Strain in principal axes (deformacija v glavnih oseh)

Let \(\varepsilon_1 \ge \varepsilon_2 \ge \varepsilon_3\) be the principal strains at the point \(P\) and \(\vek n_1\), \(\vek n_2\), \(\vek n_3 = \vek n_1\times\vek n_2\) the corresponding principal directions of strain of theorem 2.61 for \(\mat A = \ten\varepsilon\). Then:

  1. In the coordinate system with its axes in the directions \(\vek n_1\), \(\vek n_2\) and \(\vek n_3\) the small strain matrix is diagonal, with \(\varepsilon_1\), \(\varepsilon_2\) and \(\varepsilon_3\) on the diagonal. The shear strains between these directions are zero.
  2. For a unit vector \(\vek n\) with the components \(\bar n_{\mathrm{a}} = \vek n\cdot\vek n_{\mathrm{a}}\) in these axes
    \[\vek n^\T\ten\varepsilon\,\vek n = \varepsilon_1\,\bar n_1^2 + \varepsilon_2\,\bar n_2^2 + \varepsilon_3\,\bar n_3^2 ,\]
    and \(\varepsilon_3 \le \vek n^\T\ten\varepsilon\,\vek n \le \varepsilon_1\).
Proof

For the first item we use how the small strain matrix changes when the axes turn, and the second is the computation of the proof of corollary 2.63. The indices \(a\) and \(b\) run over \(1\), \(2\) and \(3\).

1. A diagonal matrix.

  1. By lemma 2.44 the small strain matrix in turned axes is \(\mat Q\ten\varepsilon\mat Q^\T\), where \(\mat Q\) is the rotation matrix with the rows \(\vek n_1\), \(\vek n_2\) and \(\vek n_3\) (definition 2.37).
  2. By the rule for the matrix product the entry in row \(a\) and column \(b\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} Q_{\mathrm{ai}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\,Q_{\mathrm{bj}}\). The row \(a\) of the matrix \(\mat Q\) is the vector \(\vek n_{\mathrm{a}}\), so by (2.59) this is the number \(\vek n_{\mathrm{a}}\cdot(\ten\varepsilon\,\vek n_{\mathrm{b}})\).
  3. By theorem 2.61, \(\ten\varepsilon\,\vek n_{\mathrm{b}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{b}}\vek n_{\mathrm{b}}\), so the entry equals \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{b}}\,(\vek n_{\mathrm{a}}\cdot\vek n_{\mathrm{b}})\). This is \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{b}}\) for \(a = b\) and \(0\) for \(a \neq b\).

2. The strain of a fibre.

  1. By (2.59), \(\vek n^\T\ten\varepsilon\,\vek n = \vek n\cdot(\ten\varepsilon\,\vek n)\). We repeat steps 4 to 10 of the proof of corollary 2.63 with \(\ten\varepsilon\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{a}}\) in place of \(\ten\sigma\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}\). There we used only that \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}} = \vek n\cdot(\ten\sigma\,\vek n)\) and that \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n_{\mathrm{a}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{a}}\vek n_{\mathrm{a}}\), which holds for \(\ten\varepsilon\) in the same way.

Source: SADD (2.4.3) (PDF p. 49), CM §3.4.6 (PDF p. 138). The proof is not from the sources.

What does this mean for the material? By item 2 the strain of the fibres through \(P\) is largest in the direction \(\vek n_1\) and smallest in the direction \(\vek n_3\), and all others lie between them. By item 1 the shear strains between the principal directions of strain are zero. A shear strain measures the change of the right angle between two fibres (definition 1.3 and section 2.9.5). A small cube with its edges along the principal directions of strain therefore stays a box with right angles, only its edges get longer or shorter (SADD, PDF p. 49). With this we have proved the claim that in section 1.1 we stated from a source: for every small deformation there are three mutually perpendicular directions in which there is no shear, only stretching or shortening. For large deformations the Green-Lagrange strain tensor (definition 1.112) takes the place of \(\ten\varepsilon\). It is symmetric too, so it also has three perpendicular principal directions of strain (CM §3.4.6, PDF p. 138).

The sheared cube. Let us return to the steel cube with the edge \(h = 10\) mm \(= 10\,000\) µm of section 1.6.2. It is glued to a table by its bottom face, and its top face is pushed sideways by \(s = 10\) µm. The origin is at the corner \(A\), the axis \(x\) points in the direction of the push, the axis \(z\) up, and the displacement field is \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = 0{,}001\,z\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\) ((1.16)). Of the nine partial derivatives only \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial z = 0{,}001\) is nonzero, so by (1.51)

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zx}} = \tfrac12\left(\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial z} + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial x}\right) = \tfrac12\,(0{,}001 + 0) = 0{,}0005 ,\]

and all other components are zero. This is the same form of the matrix as in pure shear, only with the number \(0{,}0005\) in place of \(\tau_0\). The computation of the previous section therefore gives the principal strains \(\varepsilon_1 = 0{,}0005\), \(\varepsilon_2 = 0\) and \(\varepsilon_3 = -0{,}0005\) in the directions \(\vek n_1 = (1, 0, 1)/\sqrt2\), \(\vek n_2 = \vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\vek n_3 = (-1, 0, 1)/\sqrt2\). The first is the direction of the diagonal \(AC\), the third the direction of the diagonal \(BD\). The number \(0{,}0005\) is \(s/(2h) = 10/(2 \cdot 10\,000)\), the strain of the diagonals that we computed from the lengths in section 1.6.2. There we got exactly \(+5{,}001 \cdot 10^{-4}\) and \(-4{,}999 \cdot 10^{-4}\), and the difference comes from the terms with the square of the shift, which the small strain tensor neglects. Now we know more than then. No short fibre through any point of the cube has a strain larger than about \(5 \cdot 10^{-4}\) or smaller than about \(-5 \cdot 10^{-4}\), and the fibres in the direction of the axis \(y\) keep their length.

What about the right angles? In section 2.9.5 we computed from the exact vectors of section 1.6.2 that after loading the cosine of the angle between the edges \(AB\) and \(AD\) is \(0{,}0009999995\), while the cosine of the angle between the diagonals \(AC\) and \(BD\) is only \(5 \cdot 10^{-7}\), two thousand times less. So the right angle between the edges changes, while the right angle between the principal directions of strain stays practically a right angle, as item 1 of corollary 2.69 says. Even this small remainder comes only from the terms with the square of the shift, which the small strain tensor leaves out. Figure 2.49 of section 2.9.4 shows this on two small squares on the front face of the cube.

The hanging bar. A steel bar of length \(L = 10\) m hangs under its own weight. As in section 1.7.1, it is fixed only at the centre \(O\) of its top face, and the top face is pulled, in an idealized way, by an evenly distributed force. The axis \(x\) points from \(O\) vertically down. The point \(P\) lies at the depth \(x_{\mathrm{P}} = 5\) m, \(y_{\mathrm{P}} = 4\) mm from the axis, in the plane \(z = 0\). From the partial derivatives at \(P\) computed in section 1.7.2 we get \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = -5{,}856 \cdot 10^{-7}\). The shear components are zero. The partial derivatives \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial y\) and \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial x\) are equal in size and opposite in sign, so \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \tfrac12\,(-4{,}685 \cdot 10^{-10} + 4{,}685 \cdot 10^{-10}) = 0\), and for \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}\) both derivatives are zero at \(P\). The matrix is already diagonal, so the principal directions of strain are the axes. The principal strain \(\varepsilon_1 = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6}\) is along the bar, and the principal strains \(\varepsilon_2 = \varepsilon_3 = -5{,}856 \cdot 10^{-7}\) are equal, so every direction perpendicular to the bar is a principal direction of strain, as for the stress in a bar. The ratio \(\varepsilon_2/\varepsilon_1 = -0{,}3\) is the thinning of steel of section 1.7.1.

The stress at the same point has a single nonzero component, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \rho g\,(L - x_{\mathrm{P}}) = 7800\ \text{kg/m}^3 \cdot 9{,}81\ \text{m/s}^2 \cdot 5\ \text{m} = 0{,}3826\) MPa (CM Example 7.3.2, PDF p. 305, with differently placed axes). The principal stresses are \(0{,}3826\) MPa, \(0\) and \(0\), in the same directions as the principal strains. It is similar for the cube. In pure shear the principal directions of stress are the diagonals \(AC\) and \(BD\) and the axis \(y\), and in the sheared cube the same directions are the principal directions of strain. What stress causes such a shift we will see in chapter 3. In the hanging bar the stress and the strain have the same principal directions, although the principal stresses across the bar are zero and the principal strains are not, because the bar gets thinner without anything pressing on it from the side. For materials that behave the same in all directions and in which the relation between stress and strain is linear, as for steel below the yield stress (figure 2.9), the stress and the strain always have the same principal directions (SADD §4.2, PDF p. 94). We will show this in chapter 3, where we will relate stress and strain.

Let us sum up how we reached the answer. We wrote the normal stress on the face with the normal \(\vek n\) as \(\vek n^\T\ten\sigma\,\vek n\). The expression is a polynomial in the components of the normal, so on the closed and bounded set of unit vectors it attains a largest value. The parabola argument showed that there is no shear on the face with the largest normal stress, and repeating this on a smaller set, among the unit vectors perpendicular to the first direction, gave the second principal direction. The third is perpendicular to both, and because the matrix is symmetric, it maps the third to a multiple of it. In the principal axes the normal stress is a weighted average of the principal stresses, so the largest is \(\sigma_1\) and the smallest \(\sigma_3\). We compute them with the characteristic equation, whose coefficients, the invariants, are the same in all axes. For strains everything holds in the same way, because the strain of a fibre is also an expression \(\vek n^\T\ten\varepsilon\,\vek n\) with a symmetric matrix. Now we know on which face the normal stress is largest. But on which face is the shear stress largest? In the bar it was on the face at \(45^\circ\) to the cross section (lemma 2.19), and in section 2.12 we will show that in general, too, it is determined by the principal stresses.
