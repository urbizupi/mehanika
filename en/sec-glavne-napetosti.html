Chapter 2 Stress and equilibrium

2.11 Principal stresses and principal strains

At the end of section 2.6.6 three questions remained. Two of them we have already answered. Section 2.9 tells us how the stress components change when we turn the axes. Theorem 2.41 on the symmetry of stress tells us that \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zy}}\). What remains is the question on which face through a point the normal stress is largest, and on which the shear stress. In design this is decisive. A material fails when the size of the normal or shear stress exceeds the limit the material can bear, its strength (trdnost), so we must know where the largest stress is and how large it is (CM §4.3.2, PDF p. 189). In this section we answer for the normal stress, in section 2.12 for the shear stress.

Two answers we know already. In a bar pulled by an axial force, among the faces tilted about the axis \(z\), the normal stress was largest on the cross section, where it equals \(\sigma\) and where there is no shear stress (cross section, section 2.4). In pure shear the two faces perpendicular to the diagonals of the cube had the normal stress \(\tau_0\) and \(-\tau_0\) respectively, and no shear stress (section 2.6.6). In both cases there was no shear on the face with the largest normal stress among the faces we examined. That in pure shear \(\tau_0\) is the largest at all is shown by corollary 2.85. We will show that this is no coincidence.

From history

Cauchy captured the normal stress in all directions with one picture. From a point he drew in every direction a vector whose length is inversely proportional to the square root of the normal stress on the face perpendicular to that direction (LOVE, PDF p. 99 and 100, TIMO, PDF p. 117). That the ends of these vectors lie on a surface with a definite equation is shown by calculation. Let \(\vek n\) be a unit vector and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}} = \vek n\cdot\ten\sigma\,\vek n\) the positive normal stress on the face with the normal \(\vek n\). Let us choose the scale so that the length of the vector is \(r = 1/\sqrt{\sigma_{\mathrm{n}}}\). For the end of the vector \(\vek x = r\,\vek n\) it then holds that \(\vek x\cdot\ten\sigma\,\vek x = r^2\sigma_{\mathrm{n}} = 1\), hence

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}x^2 + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}y^2 + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}z^2 + 2\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,xy + 2\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\,xz + 2\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}\,yz = 1 .\]

In the equation there are squares of the coordinates and their products, so the ends of the vectors lie on a surface of the second degree (ploskev druge stopnje) (LOVE, PDF p. 99 and 100, TIMO, PDF p. 117). A plane section of such a surface is shown in figure 2.170. In it the axes \(x\) and \(y\) are grey, the blackwhite vector of length \(r\) from the point \(P\) makes the angle \(\theta\) with the axis \(x\), the grey segment is the face through \(P\) perpendicular to it, with the normal stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}}\), and the blue curve is the section of the surface. The coefficients at the products \(xy\), \(xz\) and \(yz\) are twice the shear components of stress. If we describe the surface in axes that are its axes of symmetry (the shorter and the longer axis of the ellipse in the figure), these products no longer appear in the equation, so neither do the shear components (LOVE, PDF p. 99 and 100). The axes of symmetry are therefore the sought directions, and the corresponding stresses are the stresses in them (TIMO, PDF p. 117). He drew the same picture for strain (TIMO, PDF p. 118). If the surface has three mutually perpendicular axes, at every point there are three perpendicular faces without shear, and LOVE concludes this too (LOVE, PDF p. 100). Why a surface of the second degree has such axes, LOVE does not say, so we do not rely on it. We prove that these directions exist without the surface, by calculating with the stress matrix (theorem 2.83).

Let us return to the question on which face through a point the normal stress is the largest. The goal of the section is three claims and one procedure. First, at every point of a body there are three mutually perpendicular faces on which there is no shear stress. Second, on one of them the normal stress is the largest among all faces through the point, on another the smallest. Third, the stresses on these faces depend only on the stress at the point, not on how we placed the axes. The procedure tells us how to compute these faces and stresses. At the end we show the same for strains. With this we prove what in section 1.1 we only stated from a source: for every small deformation there are three mutually perpendicular directions in which the material is not sheared but only stretched or shortened.

The model is the same as in sections 2.6 to 2.9. The body is in equilibrium, \(P\) is a point in its interior, and the axes of a rectangular Cartesian coordinate system are chosen. The stress at \(P\) is described by nine stress components, which we write in the matrix \(\ten\sigma\) with the rows \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\), \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}})\) and \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}})\) (definition 2.48). By the theorem on the symmetry of stress this matrix is symmetric, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{ji}}\), where the indices \(i\) and \(j\) run over \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\). So by Cauchy's formula in matrix form (2.54) the traction vector on the face through \(P\) with the unit normal \(\vek n\) is

\[\vek t(\vek n) = \ten\sigma\,\vek n ,\]

where the vector \(\vek n\) is written as a column. How the body is loaded and what material it is made of does not matter for this section. Everything that follows we derive from nine numbers at one point, and it holds at every point separately. At another point of the same body the faces without shear are in general turned differently. For strains we will also assume that all partial derivatives of the displacements are smaller in size than \(m\), where \(m < \tfrac13\). Then \(\vek n^\T\ten\varepsilon\,\vek n\) differs from the strain of the fibre by at most \(4{,}5\,m^2\) (section 1.17), and the strain is described by the small strain tensor of section 1.17.

2.11.1 Faces without shear

Let us begin with an example with numbers. At the point \(P\) let the matrix of stress components in MPa be

\[\ten\sigma = \begin{bmatrix} 67 & 24 & 0 \\ 24 & 53 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} ,\]

so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 67\) MPa, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = 53\) MPa and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}} = 24\) MPa, and all other components are zero. We chose the numbers ourselves, so that the results come out round. On the faces of the piece perpendicular to the axes \(x\) and \(y\) acts a force that lies in the plane of the axes \(x\) and \(y\) and has a normal part and a shear part, and on the faces perpendicular to the axis \(z\) no force acts (figure 2.172, left).

Let us look at the faces through \(P\) tilted about the axis \(z\). As in section 2.4, such a face has the unit normal \(\vek n = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta, 0)\), where \(\theta\) is the angle between the normal and the axis \(x\). The components of the traction vector \(\vek t(\vek n) = \ten\sigma\,\vek n\) follow from the rule for the product of a matrix and a vector (1.39). Each is the sum of the products of one row of the matrix with the components of the vector \(\vek n\):

\[\vek t(\vek n) = (67\cos\theta + 24\sin\theta,\ \ 24\cos\theta + 53\sin\theta,\ \ 0) .\]

The normal stress is the projection of this vector onto the normal (definition 2.18), so by (2.6)

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{n}} = \vek t\cdot\vek n = 67\cos^2\theta + 48\sin\theta\cos\theta + 53\sin^2\theta ,\]

where we added the products \(24\sin\theta\cos\theta\) from the first and the second component into \(48\sin\theta\cos\theta\). The shear stress is, by item 3 of lemma 2.14 on splitting a vector, \(\tau = \sqrt{|\vek t|^2 - \sigma_{\mathrm{n}}^2}\). The table shows the values at a few angles, computed with NumPy, and figure 2.171 shows the normal stress and the shear stress at all angles from \(0^\circ\) to \(180^\circ\) on axes of angle and stress. At the angle \(\theta + 180^\circ\) the normal is the opposite one, \(-\vek n\), but the face is the same, so after that the values repeat.

The normal stress is smallest a quarter turn later, at about \(127^\circ\), and there too the shear stress is zero. Why exactly a quarter turn? In section 2.12.3 we will see that the normal stress on these faces depends on the cosine of an angle that changes twice as fast as \(\theta\). The cosine is largest and smallest at angles half a turn apart, and in the meantime \(\theta\) changes by a quarter turn. Let us try the normal for which \(\cos\theta = \tfrac45\) and \(\sin\theta = \tfrac35\). This is the angle \(\theta = 36{,}87^\circ\) in the right triangle with sides 3, 4 and 5, and the vector is a unit vector because \(\tfrac{16}{25} + \tfrac{9}{25} = 1\). For \(\vek n_1 = (\tfrac45, \tfrac35, 0)\)

\[\vek t(\vek n_1) = \left(\frac{67 \cdot 4 + 24 \cdot 3}{5},\ \frac{24 \cdot 4 + 53 \cdot 3}{5},\ 0\right) = (68,\ 51,\ 0) = 85\,\vek n_1 ,\]

since \(85 \cdot \tfrac45 = 68\) and \(85 \cdot \tfrac35 = 51\). The traction vector points exactly in the direction of the normal. The normal stress is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}} = 85\,(\vek n_1\cdot\vek n_1) = 85\) MPa, and the shear part \(\vek t - \sigma_{\mathrm{n}}\vek n_1\) is the zero vector. The normal \(\vek n_2 = (-\tfrac35, \tfrac45, 0)\) belongs to the angle \(126{,}87^\circ\) and is perpendicular to \(\vek n_1\), since \(\vek n_1\cdot\vek n_2 = -\tfrac{12}{25} + \tfrac{12}{25} = 0\). For it

\[\vek t(\vek n_2) = \left(\frac{-67 \cdot 3 + 24 \cdot 4}{5},\ \frac{-24 \cdot 3 + 53 \cdot 4}{5},\ 0\right) = (-21,\ 28,\ 0) = 35\,\vek n_2 ,\]

again only a normal stress, 35 MPa. One more face has this property, the one with the normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\). On it \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}) = \ten\sigma\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = \vek 0 = 0\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), so the normal and the shear stress are zero. We have found three mutually perpendicular faces on which there is no shear (figure 2.172, right).

For the bar, for pure shear and in this example we have therefore found faces on which the traction vector points in the direction of the normal, \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \lambda\,\vek n\) for some number \(\lambda\). The matrix \(\ten\sigma\) maps the vector \(\vek n\) to a multiple of it. For most directions this is not so. The vector \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n\) points elsewhere than \(\vek n\), and the traction vector is tilted relative to the face (figure 2.173). In the figure the traction vector on the blackwhite faces is tilted, and on the blue faces it points perpendicular to them, in the directions \(\vek n_1\) and \(\vek n_2\) from the point \(P\), in the axes \(x\) and \(y\). The directions that the matrix keeps have a name in mathematics.

Definition 2.81 Eigenvalue and eigenvector (lastna vrednost in lastni vektor)

Let \(\mat A\) be a square matrix, that is, a matrix with equal numbers of rows and columns, \(m\). A real number \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue (lastna vrednost) of the matrix \(\mat A\) if there is a nonzero vector \(\vek v\) with \(m\) components for which (as with the traction vector on the blue faces, which points along the normal)

\[\mat A\,\vek v = \lambda\,\vek v . \tag{2.77}\]

Every such vector \(\vek v\) is an eigenvector (lastni vektor) of the matrix \(\mat A\) belonging to the eigenvalue \(\lambda\).

Source: CM (2.5.31) (PDF p. 91), SADD (1.6.1) (PDF pp. 20-21), KREYSZIG §8.1 (1) (PDF p. 349).

Why does the definition ask for \(\vek v\) to be nonzero? The zero vector satisfies the equation for every number, since \(\mat A\,\vek 0 = \vek 0 = \lambda\,\vek 0\). Without this condition every number would be an eigenvalue of every matrix, and the notion would tell us nothing about the matrix. An eigenvector, though, is not unique. For every number \(c \neq 0\), by lemma 1.98 on the linearity of the product with a matrix,

\[\mat A\,(c\,\vek v) = c\,(\mat A\,\vek v) = c\,\lambda\,\vek v = \lambda\,(c\,\vek v) ,\]

so \(c\,\vek v\) is also an eigenvector, with the same eigenvalue. Only the direction matters, and among the eigenvectors we can always choose a unit one, \(\vek v/|\vek v|\) (lemma 1.19). In the example above the matrix \(\ten\sigma\) has the eigenvalues \(85\), \(35\) and \(0\) with the unit eigenvectors \(\vek n_1\), \(\vek n_2\) and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\).

Not every matrix has eigenvectors. The matrix with two rows and two columns

\[\mat A = \begin{bmatrix} \tfrac35 & -\tfrac45 \\ \tfrac45 & \tfrac35 \end{bmatrix}\]

turns every vector of the plane by the same angle, \(53{,}13^\circ\), about the origin \(O\), for example the vector \((1, 0)\) into the vector \((\tfrac35, \tfrac45)\) and the vector \((\tfrac45, \tfrac35)\) into the vector \((0, 1)\) (figure 2.174). So no direction stays the same. The equation \(\mat A\,\vek v = \lambda\,\vek v\) reads, component by component, \(\tfrac35 v_1 - \tfrac45 v_2 = \lambda v_1\) and \(\tfrac45 v_1 + \tfrac35 v_2 = \lambda v_2\). We multiply the first equation by \(v_2\), the second by \(v_1\), and subtract the second from the first. The terms with \(\lambda\) and the terms with \(\tfrac35\) cancel, and what is left is \(-\tfrac45\,(v_1^2 + v_2^2) = 0\), which holds only for \(v_1 = v_2 = 0\). So there is no eigenvector for any real number \(\lambda\). This matrix is not symmetric, since \(A_{12} = -\tfrac45\) and \(A_{21} = \tfrac45\). For such matrices books allow eigenvalues that are complex numbers (CM §2.5.6.1, PDF p. 91). Complex numbers are introduced in chapter 9, and complex eigenvalues of matrices we meet in chapter 10 (lemma 10.26). We will not need them, because we will look for eigenvalues only of symmetric matrices. More generally, eigenvalues are also defined for rules that assign a vector to a vector and preserve sums and multiples, not only for matrices. This is how CM introduces them for tensors (CM §2.5.6.1, PDF p. 91).

For stress the notion gets its own name.

Definition 2.82 Principal stress and principal direction (glavna napetost in glavna smer)

Let \(\ten\sigma\) be the matrix of stress components at the point \(P\) in a chosen rectangular Cartesian coordinate system. A unit vector \(\vek n\) is a principal direction (glavna smer) of the stress at \(P\), and the number \(\lambda\) the corresponding principal stress (glavna napetost), if

\[\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \lambda\,\vek n ,\]

that is, if \(\vek n\) is a unit eigenvector and \(\lambda\) an eigenvalue of the matrix \(\ten\sigma\).

Source: CM §4.3.2, (4.3.11) (PDF p. 189), SADD §3.4 (PDF p. 69).

From history

The names principal direction and principal stress were given by Cauchy to the directions of the axes of symmetry of his surface and to the stresses in them (TIMO, PDF p. 117).

What does the definition of the principal direction and principal stress mean on a face? By Cauchy's formula \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n\) is the traction vector \(\vek t(\vek n)\). A principal direction is therefore the normal of a face on which the traction vector points in the direction of the normal, \(\vek t(\vek n) = \lambda\,\vek n\). The normal stress on this face is \(\vek t\cdot\vek n = \lambda\,(\vek n\cdot\vek n) = \lambda\), the shear part \(\vek t - \lambda\,\vek n\) is the zero vector, and the shear stress is zero. The converse also holds. If the shear stress on the face with the normal \(\vek n\) is zero, the shear part is the zero vector, and \(\vek t(\vek n) = \sigma_{\mathrm{n}}\,\vek n\). Then \(\vek n\) is a principal direction with the principal stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}}\). The principal directions are therefore exactly the normals of the faces on which there is no shear stress. A positive principal stress means tension, a negative one compression. CM calls the faces whose normal is a principal direction principal planes (glavne ravnine) (CM, PDF p. 189). The normal of a plane is determined only up to its sign, but this does no harm: if \(\vek n\) is a principal direction, so is \(-\vek n\), with the same principal stress, since \(\ten\sigma\,(-\vek n) = -\lambda\,\vek n = \lambda\,(-\vek n)\).

Let us look at both examples we already know in the new words. In the bar \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma\) is the only nonzero component, so \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = (\sigma\,n_{\mathrm{x}}, 0, 0)\). Let \(\sigma \neq 0\). The equation \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \lambda\,\vek n\) reads, component by component, \(\sigma\,n_{\mathrm{x}} = \lambda\,n_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(0 = \lambda\,n_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(0 = \lambda\,n_{\mathrm{z}}\). If \(\lambda \neq 0\), the last two equations give \(n_{\mathrm{y}} = n_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\), so the unit vector is \(\vek n = \pm\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), and the first equation gives \(\lambda = \sigma\). If \(\lambda = 0\), the first equation gives \(n_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\), and every unit vector perpendicular to the axis of the bar is a principal direction. The principal stresses are therefore \(\sigma\) on the cross section and \(0\) on every lengthwise face. In pure shear \(\vek t(\vek n) = \tau_0\,(n_{\mathrm{z}}, 0, n_{\mathrm{x}})\). The normals \((1, 0, 1)/\sqrt2\) and \((-1, 0, 1)/\sqrt2\) of the faces perpendicular to the diagonals \(AC\) and \(BD\) point along these diagonals and are principal directions with the principal stresses \(\tau_0\) and \(-\tau_0\), which we checked in section 2.6.6. The third principal direction is \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) with the principal stress \(0\), since \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}) = \vek 0\).

In all three cases we found the principal directions, and each time there were three of them, mutually perpendicular. Is this so for every stress? And is the largest normal stress always on a face without shear? The next section shows that it is so for every symmetric matrix, which the matrix of stress components is.

2.11.2 Three perpendicular principal directions

The idea is to reverse the conclusion of the example. There we found the largest normal stress on a face without shear. Now we will first look for the face with the largest normal stress and then show that there is no shear on it. Why should this be so? Suppose a shear part acted on this face. It lies in the face and points along it. If we tilt the normal of the face a little toward the direction of the shear part, this part starts to contribute to the normal stress. The change of the stress vector that the tilt brings contributes too. Since the matrix is symmetric, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{ji}}\), this second contribution equals the first, so the normal stress increases. Without symmetry the two contributions could cancel. But that is impossible, because the normal stress was already the largest (figure 2.176 after the proof below shows the same tilt with the symbols of the proof). In the example with numbers, at \(\theta = 30^\circ\) the shear stress is \(5{,}94\) MPa, and the shear part points in the direction in which the normal tilts when we increase the angle \(\theta\). The normal stress indeed grows, from \(84{,}28\) MPa at \(30^\circ\) to \(85\) MPa at \(36{,}87^\circ\) (figure 2.171).

To be able to compute, let us write the normal stress with the components of the normal. We will need two rules for the scalar product with a matrix. Let \(\mat A\) be a matrix with three rows and three columns, and \(\vek v\) and \(\vek w\) vectors written as columns.

  1. By (1.39) the component \(i\) of the vector \(\mat A\,\vek w\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} A_{\mathrm{ij}} w_{\mathrm{j}}\).
  2. By (2.6) the scalar product is the sum of the products of corresponding components, so
    \[\vek v\cdot(\mat A\,\vek w) = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} v_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} A_{\mathrm{ij}} w_{\mathrm{j}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} A_{\mathrm{ij}}\,v_{\mathrm{i}}\,w_{\mathrm{j}} = \vek v^\T\mat A\,\vek w . \tag{2.78}\]
    The double sum has nine terms, one for each pair of indices, and the notation \(\vek v^\T\mat A\,\vek w\) for it was introduced in (1.42). For the stress we did the same computation in section 2.9.2.
  3. For \(\mat A = \ten\sigma\) and \(\vek v = \vek w = \vek n\) we get the normal stress of (2.55), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}} = \vek t\cdot\vek n = \vek n\cdot(\ten\sigma\,\vek n) = \vek n^\T\ten\sigma\,\vek n\). As there, because \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\), the terms \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}n_{\mathrm{x}}n_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}n_{\mathrm{y}}n_{\mathrm{x}}\) add up to \(2\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}n_{\mathrm{x}}n_{\mathrm{y}}\), and likewise for the other two pairs. Of the nine terms six remain:
    \[\sigma_{\mathrm{n}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}n_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}n_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}n_{\mathrm{z}}^2 + 2\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}n_{\mathrm{x}}n_{\mathrm{y}} + 2\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}n_{\mathrm{x}}n_{\mathrm{z}} + 2\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}n_{\mathrm{y}}n_{\mathrm{z}} .\]
    In the example with numbers, for \(\vek n = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta, 0)\) this is \(67\cos^2\theta + 53\sin^2\theta + 48\cos\theta\sin\theta\), as above.
  4. Let \(\mat A\) be symmetric. Then
    \[\vek v\cdot(\mat A\,\vek w) = \vek w\cdot(\mat A\,\vek v) . \tag{2.79}\]
    By (2.78) the right side is the sum of the terms \(A_{\mathrm{ij}}\,w_{\mathrm{i}}\,v_{\mathrm{j}}\). The term with the pair of indices \((i, j)\) on the right equals the term with the pair \((j, i)\) on the left, \(A_{\mathrm{ij}}\,w_{\mathrm{i}}\,v_{\mathrm{j}} = A_{\mathrm{ji}}\,v_{\mathrm{j}}\,w_{\mathrm{i}}\), because \(A_{\mathrm{ij}} = A_{\mathrm{ji}}\). For example, the term \(A_{\mathrm{yx}}\,w_{\mathrm{y}}\,v_{\mathrm{x}}\) of the right sum equals the term \(A_{\mathrm{xy}}\,v_{\mathrm{x}}\,w_{\mathrm{y}}\) of the left one. When the pair \((i, j)\) runs through all nine pairs, so does the pair \((j, i)\), so the two sums are equal.

Now we can state the theorem. It holds for every symmetric matrix, so we state it for a matrix \(\mat A\), and we will use it for stress and for strain.

Theorem 2.83 Principal directions of a symmetric matrix (glavne smeri simetrične matrike)

Let \(\mat A\) be a symmetric matrix with three rows and three columns and real entries \(A_{\mathrm{ij}} = A_{\mathrm{ji}}\). Then there are three mutually perpendicular unit vectors \(\vek n_1\), \(\vek n_2\) and \(\vek n_3 = \vek n_1\times\vek n_2\) and real numbers \(\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \lambda_3\) for which

\[\mat A\,\vek n_1 = \lambda_1\,\vek n_1 , \qquad \mat A\,\vek n_2 = \lambda_2\,\vek n_2 , \qquad \mat A\,\vek n_3 = \lambda_3\,\vek n_3 . \tag{2.80}\]

The number \(\lambda_1\) is the largest value of the expression \(\vek n^\T\mat A\,\vek n\) among all unit vectors \(\vek n\).

Proof

We find the directions one after the other. The first we obtain as the unit vector for which \(\vek n^\T\mat A\,\vek n\) is largest, and we show that it is an eigenvector. The second we find in the same way among the unit vectors perpendicular to the first, and the third is the vector product of the first two. For every vector \(\vek v\) we write \(q(\vek v) = \vek v^\T\mat A\,\vek v\).

1. The first direction.

  1. By (2.78), \(q(\vek v) = \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} A_{\mathrm{ij}}\,v_{\mathrm{i}}\,v_{\mathrm{j}}\). Each of the nine terms is the number \(A_{\mathrm{ij}}\) multiplied by the product of two components of the vector \(\vek v\). To the vector \(\vek v\) belongs the point \(O\)\( + \vek v\) with the coordinates \(v_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(v_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(v_{\mathrm{z}}\), so \(q\) is a polynomial in the coordinates of this point. By item 2 of corollary 1.70 on the continuity of sums, products and polynomials in space it is a continuous function of the point in all of space. So it is also continuous on every set of points, since the condition of continuity in definition 1.68 asks only for less on a smaller set.
  2. The set \(S\) of all unit vectors, that is, of the points at the distance \(1\) from \(O\), is the unit sphere of section 1.13, which is nonempty, closed and bounded. By theorem 1.79 on the largest value in space, \(q\) attains a largest value on \(S\). So there is a \(\vek n_1\) in \(S\) such that \(q(\vek n) \le q(\vek n_1)\) for every \(\vek n\) in \(S\). We write \(\lambda_1 = q(\vek n_1)\). This proves the last sentence of the theorem.
  3. Each term of the sum \(q(c\,\vek v)\) contains two components of the vector \(c\,\vek v\), each with the factor \(c\), so \(q(c\,\vek v) = c^2\,q(\vek v)\) for every number \(c\). Let \(\vek v \neq \vek 0\). The vector \(\vek v/|\vek v|\) is a unit vector (lemma 1.19), so by step 2
    \[q(\vek v) = |\vek v|^2\,q(\vek v/|\vek v|) \le \lambda_1\,|\vek v|^2 .\]
    For \(\vek v = \vek 0\) both sides are zero. So the inequality \(q(\vek v) \le \lambda_1|\vek v|^2\) holds for every vector.
  4. Let \(\mat B\) be the matrix obtained from \(-\mat A\) by adding \(\lambda_1\) to every diagonal entry: \(B_{\mathrm{xx}} = \lambda_1 - A_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(B_{\mathrm{xy}} = -A_{\mathrm{xy}}\), and so on. We write \(\mat B = \lambda_1\mat I - \mat A\). The matrix \(\mat B\) is symmetric because \(\mat A\) is symmetric. The component \(i\) of the vector \(\mat B\,\vek v\) is \(\lambda_1 v_{\mathrm{i}} - \sum_{\mathrm{j}} A_{\mathrm{ij}} v_{\mathrm{j}}\), so \(\mat B\,\vek v = \lambda_1\vek v - \mat A\,\vek v\). By (2.78), items 2 to 4 of the properties of the scalar product (lemma 2.12) and step 3,
    \[\vek v^\T\mat B\,\vek v = \vek v\cdot(\lambda_1\vek v - \mat A\,\vek v) = \lambda_1|\vek v|^2 - q(\vek v) \ge 0\]
    for every vector \(\vek v\), because \(q\) on the sphere does not exceed \(\lambda_1\). For \(\vek v =\) \(\vek n_1\) the value is \(\lambda_1 \cdot 1 - \lambda_1 = 0\).
  5. Let \(\vek m\) be any vector and \(t\) any number. Into step 4 we put \(\vek v = \vek n_1 + t\,\vek m\), that is, the vector \(\vek n_1\) tilted by \(t\,\vek m\). By lemma 1.98 on the linearity of the product with a matrix, \(\mat B\,(\vek n_1 + t\,\vek m) = \mat B\,\vek n_1 + t\,\mat B\,\vek m\), and by items 1, 2 and 3 of the properties of the scalar product
    \[(\vek n_1 + t\,\vek m)\cdot(\mat B\,\vek n_1 + t\,\mat B\,\vek m) = \vek n_1\cdot(\mat B\,\vek n_1) + t\,\vek n_1\cdot(\mat B\,\vek m) + t\,\vek m\cdot(\mat B\,\vek n_1) + t^2\,\vek m\cdot(\mat B\,\vek m) .\]
    The first term is zero by step 4. The matrix \(\mat B\) is symmetric, so by (2.79) \(\vek n_1\cdot(\mat B\,\vek m) = \vek m\cdot(\mat B\,\vek n_1)\), and the two middle terms are equal. We write \(a = \vek m\cdot(\mat B\,\vek n_1)\) and \(b = \vek m\cdot(\mat B\,\vek m)\). Step 4 then says that
    \[2t\,a + t^2 b \ge 0 \qquad \text{for every number } t .\]
    Here \(b \ge 0\), which is step 4 for \(\vek v = \vek m\).
  6. Let us show that \(a = 0\). Suppose that \(a \neq 0\), and take \(t = -a/(b + 1)\), which we may, because \(b + 1 \ge 1\). We substitute and bring the expression to the common denominator \((b + 1)^2\):
    \[2t\,a + t^2 b = -\frac{2a^2}{b + 1} + \frac{a^2 b}{(b + 1)^2} = \frac{-2a^2(b + 1) + a^2 b}{(b + 1)^2} = -\frac{a^2\,(b + 2)}{(b + 1)^2} .\]
    Because \(a^2 > 0\) and \(b + 2 > 0\), this is a negative number, which contradicts step 5 (figure 2.177). So \(a = \vek m\cdot(\mat B\,\vek n_1) = 0\) for every vector \(\vek m\).
  7. Take \(\vek m = \mat B\,\vek n_1\). By step 6, \((\mat B\,\vek n_1)\cdot(\mat B\,\vek n_1) = 0\), so by item 4 of the properties of the scalar product \(\mat B\,\vek n_1 = \vek 0\). By step 4, \(\mat B\,\vek n_1 = \lambda_1\vek n_1 - \mat A\,\vek n_1\), hence \(\mat A\,\vek n_1 = \lambda_1\vek n_1\). The vector \(\mat A\,\vek n_1\) therefore has no part perpendicular to \(\vek n_1\), the part that figure 2.176 shows.

2. The second direction.

  1. The matrix \(\mat A\) maps every vector perpendicular to \(\vek n_1\) to a vector perpendicular to \(\vek n_1\). Indeed, if \(\vek w\cdot\vek n_1 = 0\), then by (2.79) and step 7
    \[\vek n_1\cdot(\mat A\,\vek w) = \vek w\cdot(\mat A\,\vek n_1) = \vek w\cdot(\lambda_1\vek n_1) = \lambda_1\,(\vek w\cdot\vek n_1) = 0 .\]
  2. Let \(S_1\) be the set of unit vectors perpendicular to \(\vek n_1 = (n_{1\mathrm{x}}, n_{1\mathrm{y}}, n_{1\mathrm{z}})\). The set is not empty. If \(n_{1\mathrm{x}}\) and \(n_{1\mathrm{y}}\) are not both zero, the vector \((-n_{1\mathrm{y}}, n_{1\mathrm{x}}, 0)\) is nonzero and perpendicular to \(\vek n_1\), since the scalar product is \(-n_{1\mathrm{y}}n_{1\mathrm{x}} + n_{1\mathrm{x}}n_{1\mathrm{y}} + 0 = 0\). Divided by its length, it lies in \(S_1\). Otherwise \(\vek n_1 = \pm\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) lies in \(S_1\).
  3. The set \(S_1\) is bounded because it is part of the set \(S\). By definition 1.75 it is closed if the limit of every convergent sequence of vectors from \(S_1\) lies in \(S_1\) again. Let such a sequence converge to \(\vek w\). The vector \(\vek w\) lies in \(S\), because \(S\) is closed. The scalar products of the terms with \(\vek n_1\) are all zero. Each such product is the sum of three coordinates multiplied by the fixed numbers \(n_{1\mathrm{x}}\), \(n_{1\mathrm{y}}\) and \(n_{1\mathrm{z}}\). By lemma 1.67 the coordinates converge to the coordinates of the vector \(\vek w\), so by lemma 1.61 the products converge to \(\vek w\cdot\vek n_1\). The limit of a sequence of zeros is zero, so \(\vek w\cdot\vek n_1 = 0\), and \(\vek w\) lies in \(S_1\).
  4. By the theorem on the largest value in space, \(q\) attains on \(S_1\) a largest value \(\lambda_2\) at some \(\vek n_2\) in \(S_1\). Since \(S_1\) is part of the set \(S\), on which the largest value is \(\lambda_1\), \(\lambda_2 \le \lambda_1\).
  5. We repeat steps 3 to 6 with \(\lambda_2\), \(\vek n_2\) and \(\mat B_2 = \lambda_2\mat I - \mat A\) in place of \(\lambda_1\), \(\vek n_1\) and \(\mat B\), except that we take only vectors \(\vek v\) and \(\vek m\) perpendicular to \(\vek n_1\). For a nonzero \(\vek v\) perpendicular to \(\vek n_1\), \(\vek v/|\vek v|\) lies in \(S_1\), so \(q(\vek v) \le \lambda_2|\vek v|^2\) and \(\vek v^\T\mat B_2\,\vek v \ge 0\). If \(\vek m\) is perpendicular to \(\vek n_1\), so is \(\vek n_2 + t\,\vek m\), since \((\vek n_2 + t\,\vek m)\cdot\vek n_1 = 0 + t \cdot 0\). Steps 5 and 6 therefore give \(\vek m\cdot(\mat B_2\,\vek n_2) = 0\) for every \(\vek m\) perpendicular to \(\vek n_1\).
  6. The vector \(\mat B_2\,\vek n_2 = \lambda_2\vek n_2 - \mat A\,\vek n_2\) is perpendicular to \(\vek n_1\). Indeed, the vector \(\vek n_2\) is perpendicular to \(\vek n_1\) because it lies in \(S_1\), and the vector \(\mat A\,\vek n_2\) by step 8. So we may take it as \(\vek m\) in step 12, and as in step 7 we get \(\mat B_2\,\vek n_2 = \vek 0\), hence \(\mat A\,\vek n_2 = \lambda_2\vek n_2\).

3. The third direction.

  1. Let \(\vek n_3 = \vek n_1\times\vek n_2\). By lemma 2.34 on the properties of the vector product it is perpendicular to \(\vek n_1\) and to \(\vek n_2\), and \(|\vek n_3|^2 = |\vek n_1|^2|\vek n_2|^2 - (\vek n_1\cdot\vek n_2)^2 = 1 - 0 = 1\).
  2. The vector \(\mat A\,\vek n_3\) is perpendicular to \(\vek n_1\) by step 8, because \(\vek n_3\) is perpendicular to \(\vek n_1\). It is also perpendicular to \(\vek n_2\), since by (2.79) and step 13 \(\vek n_2\cdot(\mat A\,\vek n_3) = \vek n_3\cdot(\mat A\,\vek n_2) = \lambda_2\,(\vek n_3\cdot\vek n_2) = 0\).
  3. The vectors \(\vek n_1\), \(\vek n_2\) and \(\vek n_3\) are mutually perpendicular unit vectors, so they are the unit vectors of the axes of a turned coordinate system, as we saw at the end of section 2.9.1. By lemma 2.45 on the components of a vector in turned axes, the components of the vector \(\mat A\,\vek n_3\) in this system are its scalar products with \(\vek n_1\), \(\vek n_2\) and \(\vek n_3\). By step 15 these are the numbers \(0\), \(0\) and \(\lambda_3 = \vek n_3\cdot(\mat A\,\vek n_3)\).
  4. By lemma 1.21 on a vector as a sum of steps along the axes, used in this system, \(\mat A\,\vek n_3\) is the sum of steps along the axes, \(\mat A\,\vek n_3 = 0\,\vek n_1 + 0\,\vek n_2 + \lambda_3\,\vek n_3 = \lambda_3\vek n_3\).
  5. We have \(\lambda_3 = q(\vek n_3)\), and \(\vek n_3\) lies in \(S_1\), because it is a unit vector perpendicular to \(\vek n_1\). Since \(\lambda_2\) is the largest value of \(q\) on \(S_1\), \(\lambda_3 \le \lambda_2\).

Source: The theorem is stated in CM §2.5.6.3 (PDF p. 93) and SADD §1.6 (PDF pp. 21-22). CM shows that the eigenvalues of a symmetric matrix are real and that eigenvectors with different eigenvalues are perpendicular (PDF pp. 92-93). The proof through the largest value is not from the sources.

The proof has shown why principal directions exist. The expression \(\vek n^\T\mat A\,\vek n\) attains a largest value on the unit vectors, because it is a polynomial and because the unit vectors form a closed and bounded set. Steps 5 to 7 are the precise form of the idea from the beginning of the section, and the symmetry of the matrix enters in step 5, where the two middle terms are equal. If \(\mat A\,\vek n_1\) were not a multiple of the vector \(\vek n_1\), we could tilt \(\vek n_1\) toward the vector \(-\vek m = \mat A\,\vek n_1 - \lambda_1\vek n_1\) (figure 2.176), where \(\vek m = \mat B\,\vek n_1\) is the vector of step 7, so that the expression \(\vek n^\T\mat A\,\vek n\) on the unit vectors, which reach from the point \(O\) to the sphere, would grow. The vector \(-\vek m\) is the part of \(\mat A\,\vek n_1\) perpendicular to \(\vek n_1\) (lemma 2.14 on splitting a vector), for stress therefore the shear part, and the part along \(\vek n_1\) is \(\lambda_1\vek n_1\). In step 5 the value of the expression \(\vek v^\T\mat B\,\vek v\) at the tilted vector \(\vek v = \vek n_1 + t\,\vek m\) is \(2t\,a + t^2 b\). For \(t\) with the sign opposite to that of \(a\) and with \(|t| < 2|a|/b\) we have \(t^2 b < 2|t\,a|\) (for \(b = 0\) for every such \(t\)), so the sum is negative, as the blackwhite curve shows at \(t = -a/(b + 1)\) (figure 2.177). For \(a = 0\) the blue curve only touches the axis \(t\). For \(\vek m = \mat B\,\vek n_1\) we have \(a = |\vek m|^2 > 0\), so this happens for \(t < 0\), when we tilt \(\vek n_1\) toward \(-\vek m\). A negative value means that \(\vek v^\T\mat A\,\vek v > \lambda_1|\vek v|^2\) (step 4), so the expression at the unit vector \(\vek v/|\vek v|\) is larger than \(\lambda_1\) (step 3).

The second direction we obtained with the same move on a smaller set, the circle of unit vectors perpendicular to \(\vek n_1\) (figure 2.178). The third direction is the vector product of the first two. The matrix maps it to a vector that is perpendicular to \(\vek n_1\) (step 8) and to \(\vek n_2\) (step 15), and so to a multiple of it (steps 16 and 17).

We used the symmetry of the matrix in steps 5, 8 and 15. Without it the theorem fails. Take the matrix with three rows and three columns that has the matrix with two rows and two columns of the previous section, which turns every vector of the plane by the same angle (figure 2.174), in its upper left corner, the number \(1\) at the place \((z, z)\), and zeros elsewhere. It is not symmetric. The first two equations of the system \(\mat A\,\vek v = \lambda\,\vek v\) are for it the equations of the previous section for the components \(v_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(v_{\mathrm{y}}\), so these components are zero, and the third equation is \(v_{\mathrm{z}} = \lambda\,v_{\mathrm{z}}\). Its eigenvectors are therefore only the multiples of \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), with the eigenvalue \(1\), and this matrix does not have three perpendicular eigenvectors. The theorem also holds for symmetric matrices with \(m\) rows and \(m\) columns, which have \(m\) mutually perpendicular unit eigenvectors (CM §2.5.6.2, PDF p. 92). The proof would then repeat steps 8 to 13, each time on the unit vectors perpendicular to all directions found so far. The vector product, which exists only in a space with three dimensions, would not be needed.

The matrix of stress components is symmetric, so the theorem says that the stress at every point of every body in equilibrium has three mutually perpendicular principal directions. We denote the principal stresses, ordered by size, by \(\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \sigma_3\), as SADD does (SADD §3.4, PDF pp. 69-71). They are the numbers \(\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \lambda_3\) of the theorem for \(\mat A = \ten\sigma\). The indices \(1\), \(2\) and \(3\) do not mean the axes \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\) here, but the order by size. The directions \(\vek n_1\), \(\vek n_2\) and \(\vek n_3 = \vek n_1\times\vek n_2\) of the theorem are the unit vectors of the axes of a new rectangular Cartesian coordinate system with its origin at \(P\). Because \(\vek n_3 = \vek n_1\times\vek n_2\), the axes follow each other in the same order as the axes \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\), for which \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\). There are no other principal stresses. Indeed, let \(\ten\sigma\,\vek v = \mu\,\vek v\) for some nonzero vector \(\vek v\). By lemma 1.21 on a vector as a sum of steps along the axes, applied in these axes as in step 17 of the proof, \(\vek v = \bar v_1\vek n_1 + \bar v_2\vek n_2 + \bar v_3\vek n_3\), and by lemma 1.98 on the linearity of the product with a matrix, \(\vek 0 = \ten\sigma\,\vek v - \mu\,\vek v = (\sigma_1 - \mu)\,\bar v_1\vek n_1 + (\sigma_2 - \mu)\,\bar v_2\vek n_2 + (\sigma_3 - \mu)\,\bar v_3\vek n_3\). The scalar product with \(\vek n_{\mathrm{a}}\) gives \((\sigma_{\mathrm{a}} - \mu)\,\bar v_{\mathrm{a}} = 0\) for \(a = 1, 2, 3\), because the directions are unit vectors and mutually perpendicular. At least one of the numbers \(\bar v_{\mathrm{a}}\) is not zero, because \(\vek v\) is not the zero vector, so \(\mu\) is one of the numbers \(\sigma_1\), \(\sigma_2\), \(\sigma_3\). That the triple does not depend on which direction with the largest value of \(\vek n^\T\ten\sigma\,\vek n\) we choose in step 2 of the proof, we will show in corollary 2.89. We will use the system with its axes in the principal directions often, so we give it a name. The theorem holds for every symmetric matrix, so we define the name for every such matrix.

Definition 2.84 Principal axes (glavne osi)

Let \(\mat A\) be a symmetric matrix with three rows and three columns and \(P\) a point. The principal axes (glavne osi) of the matrix \(\mat A\) at \(P\) are a rectangular Cartesian coordinate system with its origin at \(P\) whose axes \(1\), \(2\) and \(3\) have the unit vectors \(\vek n_1\), \(\vek n_2\) and \(\vek n_3\), where \(\vek n_1\), \(\vek n_2\) and \(\vek n_3 = \vek n_1\times\vek n_2\) are mutually perpendicular unit eigenvectors of the matrix \(\mat A\) with eigenvalues \(\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \lambda_3\), as in theorem 2.83:

\[\mat A\,\vek n_1 = \lambda_1\,\vek n_1 , \qquad \mat A\,\vek n_2 = \lambda_2\,\vek n_2 , \qquad \mat A\,\vek n_3 = \lambda_3\,\vek n_3 .\]

When \(\mat A = \ten\sigma\) is the matrix of stress components at \(P\), these are the principal axes of the stress at \(P\). The directions of their axes are then principal directions, and the numbers \(\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \lambda_3\) are the principal stresses \(\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \sigma_3\). When \(\mat A = \ten\varepsilon\) is the small strain matrix at \(P\) (definition 1.121), these are the principal axes of the strain at \(P\).

Source: SADD §1.6 (PDF pp. 21-22), where the axes in the principal directions of a symmetric tensor are called the principal axes of the tensor, SADD §3.4 (PDF p. 69) for stress, where they are also called the principal coordinate system, and SADD §2.4 (PDF pp. 48-49) for strain. CM speaks only of principal directions and principal planes (CM §4.3.2, PDF p. 189).

Principal axes exist for every symmetric matrix, because the directions of theorem 2.83 give them. But they are not always determined uniquely. In a bar with the stress \(\sigma > 0\), a principal direction with the principal stress \(0\) is every unit vector perpendicular to the axis of the bar, as we saw in section 2.11. Axis \(1\) of the principal axes therefore lies along the axis of the bar, and axes \(2\) and \(3\) can be any two perpendicular directions in the cross section, also turned about the axis of the bar, as long as \(\vek n_3 = \vek n_1\times\vek n_2\) (figure 2.179). The definition allows every such choice, because it only requires that the unit vectors of the axes be eigenvectors with eigenvalues ordered by size. What does the stress look like, written in principal axes?

Corollary 2.85 Stress in principal axes (napetost v glavnih oseh)

Let \(\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \sigma_3\) be the principal stresses at the point \(P\), \(\vek n_1\), \(\vek n_2\), \(\vek n_3 = \vek n_1\times\vek n_2\) the corresponding principal directions of theorem 2.83 for \(\mat A = \ten\sigma\), and the principal axes of the stress at \(P\) the coordinate system with its axes in these directions (definition 2.84). Then:

  1. In the principal axes the matrix of stress components is diagonal, with \(\sigma_1\), \(\sigma_2\) and \(\sigma_3\) on the diagonal and zeros elsewhere.
  2. Let the unit normal \(\vek n\) have the components \(\bar n_1 = \vek n\cdot\vek n_1\), \(\bar n_2 = \vek n\cdot\vek n_2\) and \(\bar n_3 = \vek n\cdot\vek n_3\) in the principal axes. The normal stress on the face with the normal \(\vek n\) is
    \[\sigma_{\mathrm{n}} = \sigma_1\,\bar n_1^2 + \sigma_2\,\bar n_2^2 + \sigma_3\,\bar n_3^2 , \tag{2.81}\]
    and \(\sigma_3 \le \sigma_{\mathrm{n}} \le \sigma_1\). The normal stress is largest on the face with the normal \(\vek n_1\) and smallest on the face with the normal \(\vek n_3\).
Proof

We must show both items. For the first we use the definition of the stress components in the principal axes, for the second we split the normal into steps along the principal axes. The indices \(a\) and \(b\) run over \(1\), \(2\) and \(3\).

1. A diagonal matrix.

  1. By definition 2.27, used in the principal axes, the row \(a\) of the matrix of components is the traction vector \(\vek t(\vek n_{\mathrm{a}})\) on the face with the normal \(\vek n_{\mathrm{a}}\). Its components are, by lemma 2.45, the scalar products with \(\vek n_1\), \(\vek n_2\) and \(\vek n_3\). The entry in row \(a\) and column \(b\) is therefore \(\bar\sigma_{\mathrm{ab}} = \vek t(\vek n_{\mathrm{a}})\cdot\vek n_{\mathrm{b}}\).
  2. By Cauchy's formula and theorem 2.83, \(\vek t(\vek n_{\mathrm{a}}) = \ten\sigma\,\vek n_{\mathrm{a}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{a}}\vek n_{\mathrm{a}}\).
  3. So \(\bar\sigma_{\mathrm{ab}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{a}}\,(\vek n_{\mathrm{a}}\cdot\vek n_{\mathrm{b}})\). This is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}\) for \(a = b\), because \(\vek n_{\mathrm{a}}\) is a unit vector, and \(0\) for \(a \neq b\), because \(\vek n_{\mathrm{a}}\) and \(\vek n_{\mathrm{b}}\) are perpendicular.

2. The normal stress.

  1. By lemma 1.21 on a vector as a sum of steps along the axes, used in the principal axes, \(\vek n = \bar n_1\vek n_1 + \bar n_2\vek n_2 + \bar n_3\vek n_3\). Figure 2.181 shows this for the normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) of the example with numbers, with the steps along \(\vek n_1\) and along \(\vek n_2\).
  2. By lemma 1.98 on the linearity of the product with a matrix and by theorem 2.83, \(\vek t(\vek n) = \ten\sigma\,\vek n = \bar n_1\sigma_1\vek n_1 + \bar n_2\sigma_2\vek n_2 + \bar n_3\sigma_3\vek n_3\). Each step along a principal axis is thus only multiplied by the principal stress of that axis.
  3. The normal stress is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}} = \vek t(\vek n)\cdot\vek n\). We substitute steps 4 and 5 and multiply out by the properties of the scalar product. We get nine terms of the form \(\bar n_{\mathrm{a}}\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}\bar n_{\mathrm{b}}\,(\vek n_{\mathrm{a}}\cdot\vek n_{\mathrm{b}})\). The terms with \(a \neq b\) are zero, because the directions are perpendicular, and in the terms with \(a = b\), \(\vek n_{\mathrm{a}}\cdot\vek n_{\mathrm{a}} = 1\). What remains is (2.81). In the example with numbers this is the point \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}}\) on the number line.
  4. In the same way \(1 = \vek n\cdot\vek n = \bar n_1^2 + \bar n_2^2 + \bar n_3^2\). In the example with numbers the weights are \(\tfrac{16}{25}\), \(\tfrac{9}{25}\) and \(0\), with sum \(1\).
  5. From (2.81) we subtract \(\sigma_3 = \sigma_3\,(\bar n_1^2 + \bar n_2^2 + \bar n_3^2)\) and get \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}} - \sigma_3 = (\sigma_1 - \sigma_3)\,\bar n_1^2 + (\sigma_2 - \sigma_3)\,\bar n_2^2\). Both brackets are nonnegative, because \(\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \sigma_3\), and so are the squares. Hence \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}} \ge \sigma_3\): on the number line \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}}\) does not lie left of \(\sigma_3\).
  6. Likewise \(\sigma_1 - \sigma_{\mathrm{n}} = (\sigma_1 - \sigma_2)\,\bar n_2^2 + (\sigma_1 - \sigma_3)\,\bar n_3^2 \ge 0\). On the number line \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}}\) does not lie right of \(\sigma_1\).
  7. For \(\vek n = \vek n_1\), \(\bar n_1 = 1\) and \(\bar n_2 = \bar n_3 = 0\), so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}} = \sigma_1\). For \(\vek n = \vek n_3\) likewise \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}} = \sigma_3\). The point \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}}\) is then at an end of the line, at \(\sigma_1\) or at \(\sigma_3\).

Source: SADD (3.4.3), (3.4.5) (PDF pp. 69-70). SADD §1.6 (PDF p. 22) states that the largest eigenvalue is the largest diagonal entry the matrix can have in any axes, and the smallest the smallest. The proof is not from the sources.

The formula (2.81) has a simple picture. The numbers \(\bar n_1^2\), \(\bar n_2^2\) and \(\bar n_3^2\) are nonnegative and add up to \(1\), so the normal stress on every face is a weighted average of the three principal stresses, with the weights \(\bar n_1^2\), \(\bar n_2^2\) and \(\bar n_3^2\). An average cannot be larger than the largest value nor smaller than the smallest (figure 2.181, right). The principal axes show something more. Every stress at a point is, seen in the right axes, only tension or compression in three mutually perpendicular directions, without any shear (SADD, figure 3.5, PDF p. 69).

In the example with numbers, where \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 67\) MPa, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = 53\) MPa and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}} = 24\) MPa and the other components are zero, the principal stresses are \(\sigma_1 = 85\) MPa, \(\sigma_2 = 35\) MPa and \(\sigma_3 = 0\), with the principal directions \(\vek n_1 = (\tfrac45, \tfrac35, 0)\), \(\vek n_2 = (-\tfrac35, \tfrac45, 0)\) and \(\vek n_3 = \vek n_1\times\vek n_2 = (0, 0, \tfrac{16}{25} + \tfrac{9}{25}) = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\). The largest normal stress among all faces through \(P\), not only among the faces tilted about the axis \(z\), is therefore 85 MPa. The smallest, though, is not 35 MPa, the smallest value in figure 2.171, but \(0\) on the face perpendicular to the axis \(z\), which is not among the faces of that figure. Let us also try the formula (2.81) at \(\theta = 0^\circ\), when the normal points along the axis \(x\), that is, for \(\vek n = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). Then \(\bar n_1 = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\cdot\vek n_1 = \tfrac45\), \(\bar n_2 = -\tfrac35\) and \(\bar n_3 = 0\), and

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{n}} = 85 \cdot \frac{16}{25} + 35 \cdot \frac{9}{25} = 54{,}4 + 12{,}6 = 67\ \text{MPa} ,\]

which is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), as it must be. On the number line, 67 MPa lies between 35 MPa and 85 MPa, namely \(\tfrac{16}{25}\) of the way from 35 to 85, because the weight of \(\sigma_1\) is \(\bar n_1^2 = \tfrac{16}{25}\) (figure 2.181).

2.11.3 The characteristic equation and the invariants

Theorem 2.83 tells us that principal directions exist, but not how to compute them. We cannot simply find the largest value of the expression \(\vek n^\T\ten\sigma\,\vek n\) over all unit vectors, and in the example with numbers we guessed the principal direction from the graph, from the angle of its peak. We need an equation from which to compute the principal stresses. The condition \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \lambda\,\vek n\) is a system of three equations for the three unknown components of the vector \(\vek n\), in which the unknown number \(\lambda\) also appears. We eliminate the components with the determinant, which tells us when such a system has a nonzero solution (lemma 2.75 on a zero determinant). Recall the formula for the determinant of a matrix \(\mat M\) with three rows and three columns (equation (2.70) in definition 2.72):

\[\begin{aligned} \det\mat M &= M_{\mathrm{xx}}M_{\mathrm{yy}}M_{\mathrm{zz}} + M_{\mathrm{xy}}M_{\mathrm{yz}}M_{\mathrm{zx}} + M_{\mathrm{xz}}M_{\mathrm{yx}}M_{\mathrm{zy}} \\ &\quad - M_{\mathrm{xz}}M_{\mathrm{yy}}M_{\mathrm{zx}} - M_{\mathrm{xy}}M_{\mathrm{yx}}M_{\mathrm{zz}} - M_{\mathrm{xx}}M_{\mathrm{yz}}M_{\mathrm{zy}} . \end{aligned}\]

Each of the six terms is a product of three entries, one from each row and each column.

Figure 2.182 shows these six terms. Each small grid has nine places, like the matrix. The three filled circles are the entries the term multiplies, and the sign above the grid is the sign of the term. The first term takes all three entries on the diagonal, the second and the third none, the fourth, the fifth and the sixth one each. We draw the diagonal blue because we shall compute the determinant of a matrix in which the unknown number \(\lambda\) stands only on the diagonal. Below each grid are the powers of the number \(\lambda\) the term then contributes: the terms without a diagonal entry do not contain \(\lambda\), the terms with one contain it only to the first power. We derive them in the proof of lemma 2.86.

From history

The equation for \(\lambda\) was needed already in celestial mechanics. The planets disturb one another (CAJORI, PDF p. 256), so the elements of their orbits, for example the eccentricity of an orbit (izsrednost), change. For two planets Euler explained the secular variations (sekularne spremembe) of these elements (CAJORI, PDF p. 256), and Laplace showed that the eccentricity of the earth's orbit changes secularly and that this explains the acceleration of the moon (CAJORI, PDF p. 277). Changes of the orbital elements caused by mutual attraction are perturbations (motnje). The secular ones among them do not oscillate periodically but accumulate slowly (CAJORI, PDF p. 277 sets them apart from the ordinary periodic perturbations). They were tied to the question whether the solar system is stable. Newton and Euler doubted that so many forces in it could keep a state of equilibrium permanently (CAJORI, PDF p. 276), and Lagrange and Laplace investigated the limits within which the orbital elements change (CAJORI, PDF p. 276 and 277). The equation that arose in computing these changes MUIR calls Lagrange's determinantal equation, and the time called it the equation of the secular inequalities (enačba sekularnih neenakosti) (MUIR, PDF p. 652). In 1829, in a memoir on the equation by which the secular inequalities of the motion of the planets are determined, Cauchy treated the extreme values of a sum of terms, each of which is a product of two variables with a real number, when the sum of the squares of the variables equals \(1\) (MUIR, PDF p. 451). MUIR says that the solutions of the equation are the extreme values of this sum (MUIR, PDF p. 451). The largest solution is the largest value of the sum, the smallest solution the smallest, and the middle one is neither. He arrived at the equation from the known condition for extreme values, that the derivatives of this sum with respect to the variables are proportional to the variables (MUIR, PDF p. 451).

Cauchy's sum is exactly our expression \(\vek n^\T\ten\sigma\,\vek n\) on unit vectors, for which we looked for the largest value. From the formula with six terms the partial derivative (section 1.7.2) with respect to \(n_{\mathrm{x}}\) is \(2\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}n_{\mathrm{x}} + 2\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}n_{\mathrm{y}} + 2\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}n_{\mathrm{z}}\), which is the component \(x\) of the vector \(2\,\ten\sigma\,\vek n\), and the same holds for \(n_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(n_{\mathrm{z}}\). The derivatives with respect to the components of the vector \(\vek n\) are therefore the components of the vector \(2\,\ten\sigma\,\vek n\), so the condition of proportionality is exactly \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \lambda\,\vek n\).

A second view: Lagrange multipliers. Cauchy's condition of proportionality is a special case of a procedure that is today called the method of Lagrange multipliers (metoda Lagrangeevih množiteljev). We use it to find the largest or smallest value of a function when its variables are not free but tied by an equation. Here that equation is \(|\vek n| = 1\). So let us look at the principal stresses from this side as well. Theorem 2.83 told us that the largest value exists, and built the principal directions from it one after another. This view adds three things: a picture in which we see the condition \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \lambda\,\vek n\), a calculation by differentiation that gives all three principal directions at once, and the meaning of the number \(\lambda\). It does not replace the proof. We shall prove the general theorem on multipliers only in chapter 8 (theorem 8.37), so here we check every step against what we already have.

Let us first draw the condition in the plane, for the example with numbers \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 67\) MPa, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = 53\) MPa and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}} = 24\) MPa. Let the normals lie in the plane \(xy\). We compute the expression \(\vek n^\T\ten\sigma\,\vek n\) for every vector \(\vek v = (v_{\mathrm{x}}, v_{\mathrm{y}})\) of this plane, not only for unit vectors, and call it

\[q(\vek v) = 67\,v_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + 48\,v_{\mathrm{x}} v_{\mathrm{y}} + 53\,v_{\mathrm{y}}^2 .\]

The number \(48\) is \(2\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\), because the terms \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}v_{\mathrm{x}}v_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}v_{\mathrm{y}}v_{\mathrm{x}}\) add up. We draw the vector \(\vek v\) from the origin \(O\) and assign the value \(q\) to the point where it ends. As with the shaft in section 2.9.3 we join the points with the same value. We get curves that correspond to the contour lines of a map: \(q = 35\), \(q = 67\) and \(q = 85\). A larger value gives a larger curve, because \(q\) is four times larger for a vector twice as long. Unit vectors end on the circle of radius \(1\). This circle is the constraint: the normal stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}}\) is the value of \(q\) at a point of the circle, and we may not choose other points. Without the constraint there is no largest value, since already at the point \((2, 0)\) outside the circle \(q = 268\).

Why do the contour lines have this shape? In section 2.11 we computed \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n_1 = 85\,\vek n_1\) for \(\vek n_1 = (\tfrac45, \tfrac35)\) and \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n_2 = 35\,\vek n_2\) for \(\vek n_2 = (-\tfrac35, \tfrac45)\). We write the vector \(\vek v\) with steps along these two directions, \(\vek v = a\,\vek n_1 + b\,\vek n_2\). Then \(\ten\sigma\,\vek v = 85\,a\,\vek n_1 + 35\,b\,\vek n_2\), and since \(\vek n_1\) and \(\vek n_2\) are unit vectors and perpendicular, \(q = \vek v\cdot\ten\sigma\,\vek v = 85\,a^2 + 35\,b^2\). The contour line \(q = c\) therefore cuts the line along \(\vek n_1\) at \(a = \pm\sqrt{c/85}\) and the line along \(\vek n_2\) at \(b = \pm\sqrt{c/35}\):

contour line along \(\vek n_1\) along \(\vek n_2\)
\(q = 35\) \(0{,}642\) \(1\)
\(q = 67\) \(0{,}888\) \(1{,}384\)
\(q = 85\) \(1\) \(1{,}558\)

Curves with an equation of this form are ellipses (KREYSZIG §8.4, PDF p. 369). All of them are elongated in the direction \(\vek n_2\), because \(q\) grows more slowly there, with the factor \(35\) instead of \(85\).

Let us walk along the circle and watch the value of \(q\). At the point \(T = (1, 0)\), which belongs to the normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(q = 67\), which is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\). There the circle crosses the contour line \(q = 67\): above the point \(T\) we are outside this ellipse, where \(q > 67\), below it inside, where \(q < 67\). A point where the circle crosses a contour line therefore gives neither the largest nor the smallest value. We get the largest value where the circle only touches a contour line. These are the points \(\pm\vek n_1\) on the contour line \(q = 85\): the whole rest of the circle lies inside this ellipse, where \(q < 85\). At the points \(\pm\vek n_2\) the circle touches the contour line \(q = 35\), which lies wholly inside the circle, so \(q\) is larger than \(35\) elsewhere on the circle. These are the top and the bottom of the graph of the normal stress that we read off earlier.

We write the touching with the gradient. The circle is the contour line of the function \(g(\vek v) = v_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + v_{\mathrm{y}}^2\) at the value \(1\). The functions \(q\) and \(g\) do not depend on \(v_{\mathrm{z}}\), just as the function \(f\) of the shaft did not depend on \(z\). Their level surfaces are therefore cylinders that we look at along the axis, and in the figure we see their traces, the circle and the ellipses. By theorem 2.52 the gradient of a function is perpendicular to its level surface. Where two curves touch they have a common tangent, and both gradients are perpendicular to it. In the plane this means that they are parallel, one is a multiple of the other:

\[\nabla q = \lambda\,\nabla g . \tag{2.82}\]

The number \(\lambda\) is the Lagrange multiplier (Lagrangeev množitelj). The name says what it does: it multiplies the gradient of the constraint. Let us check with numbers. By definition 2.50, \(\nabla q = (134\,v_{\mathrm{x}} + 48\,v_{\mathrm{y}},\ 48\,v_{\mathrm{x}} + 106\,v_{\mathrm{y}})\), which is \(2\,\ten\sigma\,\vek v\), and \(\nabla g = (2\,v_{\mathrm{x}},\ 2\,v_{\mathrm{y}}) = 2\,\vek v\).

  • At the point \(\vek n_1\), \(\nabla q = (136, 102)\) and \(\nabla g = (1{,}6;\ 1{,}2)\). The quotients of the components are equal, \(136/1{,}6 = 102/1{,}2 = 85\), so the gradients are parallel and \(\lambda = 85\).
  • At the point \(\vek n_2\), \(\nabla q = (-42, 56)\) and \(\nabla g = (-1{,}2;\ 1{,}6)\), the quotients are \(-42/(-1{,}2) = 56/1{,}6 = 35\), so \(\lambda = 35\).
  • At the point \(T\), \(\nabla q = (134, 48)\) and \(\nabla g = (2, 0)\), which points along the axis \(v_{\mathrm{x}}\). The gradients are not parallel: besides the part \(134\) along the radius, \(\nabla q\) has a part \(48\) along the circle.

At both points of touching the multiplier is exactly the value of the contour line, that is, the normal stress. And what does the part of the gradient along the circle mean? By theorem 1.89 the change of \(q\) over a short step is the scalar product of the gradient with the step, up to a remainder that is small compared with the step. The part along the circle therefore tells how fast \(q\) changes as we turn the normal. At the point \(T\) it is \(48 = 2 \cdot 24 = 2\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\), twice the shear stress on the face with the normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). This is no coincidence. Since \(\nabla q = 2\,\ten\sigma\,\vek n = 2\,\vek t(\vek n)\), its scalar product with the unit vector \(\vek d\) along the circle is \(2\,\vek t(\vek n)\cdot\vek d\), twice the shear component of the traction vector. As the face turns, the normal stress stops changing exactly where there is no shear on the face. So we have said the same condition in three ways: the circle touches a contour line, the gradients are parallel, the face is free of shear.

Equation (2.82) has an assumption: the gradient of the constraint is not zero at the point of touching. Here it holds, because \(\nabla g = 2\,\vek n\) has length \(2\) at every point of the circle. When does it fail? The same circle is also described by the equation \((v_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + v_{\mathrm{y}}^2 - 1)^2 = 0\), but the gradient of its left side is zero everywhere on the circle. Equation (2.82) would then demand \(\nabla q = \vek 0\), although at the point \(\vek n_1\) in truth \(\nabla q = (136, 102)\), and it would find no principal direction. This is why theorem 8.37 demands this assumption explicitly.

In space the circle is replaced by the unit sphere and the contour lines are surfaces, but the condition of touching stays the same, \(\nabla q = \lambda\,\nabla g\) with \(q(\vek n) = \vek n^\T\ten\sigma\,\vek n\) and \(g(\vek n) = \vek n\cdot\vek n\). We put both conditions, the touching and the unit length, into one function of four variables, the components \(n_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(n_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(n_{\mathrm{z}}\) and the number \(\lambda\):

\[F(n_{\mathrm{x}}, n_{\mathrm{y}}, n_{\mathrm{z}}, \lambda) = \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,n_{\mathrm{j}}\,n_{\mathrm{i}} + \lambda\,\Big(1 - \sum_{\mathrm{i}} n_{\mathrm{i}}\,n_{\mathrm{i}}\Big) . \tag{2.83}\]
\[F(n_{\mathrm{k}}, \lambda) = \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,n_{\mathrm{j}}\,n_{\mathrm{i}} + \lambda\,(1 - n_{\mathrm{i}}\,n_{\mathrm{i}}) . \tag{2.83}\]

The first term is \(q\), the double sum of nine terms of (2.78), and the bracket is \(1 - g\). We demand that all four partial derivatives of the function \(F\) are zero. The derivative with respect to \(\lambda\) is the bracket, so it gives the condition \(|\vek n| = 1\), the circle in the figure. The derivatives with respect to the components of the normal are the components of the vector \(\nabla q - \lambda\,\nabla g\), so they give equation (2.82), the parallel gradients in the figure.

Let us compute these derivatives term by term. Let the index \(k\) be \(x\), \(y\) or \(z\). We arrange the nine terms of the first sum in a grid (figure 2.184), the term \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,n_{\mathrm{i}}\,n_{\mathrm{j}}\) in row \(i\) and column \(j\). For \(k = x\) the component \(n_{\mathrm{x}}\) is contained only in the terms of the row \(x\), where \(n_{\mathrm{x}}\) is the factor \(n_{\mathrm{i}}\), and in the terms of the column \(x\), where it is the factor \(n_{\mathrm{j}}\). The corner term \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}}\) lies in both. The other four terms do not contain the component \(n_{\mathrm{x}}\) and their derivative with respect to it is zero. The toolbar button Index notation shows every line of the calculation in index notation as well, in which the summation signs are left out.

1. Derivative of a product. The numbers \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\) and \(\lambda\) do not depend on the normal, so we differentiate only the products of components. By the product rule each product \(n_{\mathrm{j}}\,n_{\mathrm{i}}\) gives two terms, one from the column and one from the row of the grid, and \(n_{\mathrm{i}}\,n_{\mathrm{i}}\) two equal ones:

\[\frac{\partial F}{\partial n_{\mathrm{k}}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\frac{\partial n_{\mathrm{j}}}{\partial n_{\mathrm{k}}}\,n_{\mathrm{i}} + \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,n_{\mathrm{j}}\,\frac{\partial n_{\mathrm{i}}}{\partial n_{\mathrm{k}}} - 2\lambda\sum_{\mathrm{i}} n_{\mathrm{i}}\,\frac{\partial n_{\mathrm{i}}}{\partial n_{\mathrm{k}}} .\]
\[\frac{\partial F}{\partial n_{\mathrm{k}}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\frac{\partial n_{\mathrm{j}}}{\partial n_{\mathrm{k}}}\,n_{\mathrm{i}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,n_{\mathrm{j}}\,\frac{\partial n_{\mathrm{i}}}{\partial n_{\mathrm{k}}} - \lambda\,\Big(\frac{\partial n_{\mathrm{i}}}{\partial n_{\mathrm{k}}}\,n_{\mathrm{i}} + n_{\mathrm{i}}\,\frac{\partial n_{\mathrm{i}}}{\partial n_{\mathrm{k}}}\Big) .\]

2. Derivative of a component with respect to a component. The components \(n_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(n_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(n_{\mathrm{z}}\) are three independent variables of the function \(F\). In the partial derivative with respect to \(n_{\mathrm{k}}\) we hold the other two fixed (definition 1.26), just as a step along the axis \(v_{\mathrm{x}}\) does not change \(v_{\mathrm{y}}\). So \(\partial n_{\mathrm{j}}/\partial n_{\mathrm{k}}\) equals \(1\) for \(j = k\) and \(0\) for \(j \neq k\). This is the entry of the unit matrix \(\mat I\) in row \(j\) and column \(k\). This is the Kronecker delta \(\delta_{\mathrm{jk}}\) of definition 2.58.

In the sum over \(j\) only the term with \(j = k\) therefore remains,

\[\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\frac{\partial n_{\mathrm{j}}}{\partial n_{\mathrm{k}}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{ix}}\,\frac{\partial n_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial n_{\mathrm{k}}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{iy}}\,\frac{\partial n_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial n_{\mathrm{k}}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{iz}}\,\frac{\partial n_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial n_{\mathrm{k}}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{ik}} ,\]

and likewise in the other two sums only the term with \(i = k\) (lemma 2.59, the substitution rule):

\[\frac{\partial F}{\partial n_{\mathrm{k}}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ik}}\,n_{\mathrm{i}} + \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{kj}}\,n_{\mathrm{j}} - 2\lambda\,n_{\mathrm{k}} .\]
\[\frac{\partial F}{\partial n_{\mathrm{k}}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\delta_{\mathrm{jk}}\,n_{\mathrm{i}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,n_{\mathrm{j}}\,\delta_{\mathrm{ik}} - 2\lambda\,\delta_{\mathrm{ik}}\,n_{\mathrm{i}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{ik}}\,n_{\mathrm{i}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{kj}}\,n_{\mathrm{j}} - 2\lambda\,n_{\mathrm{k}} .\]

For \(k = x\) this is \((\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}n_{\mathrm{x}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}n_{\mathrm{y}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}n_{\mathrm{z}}) + (\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}n_{\mathrm{x}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}n_{\mathrm{y}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}n_{\mathrm{z}}) - 2\lambda\,n_{\mathrm{x}}\). The first bracket is the sum below the grid, which sums down the column \(x\) of the matrix, the second the sum to the right of the grid, which sums along its row \(x\).

3. Symmetry. In the second sum \(j\) is only the name of the index we sum over, so we may rename it \(i\). Since the stress is symmetric, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ki}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{ik}}\) (theorem 2.41), in the figure \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\), the two sums are equal and add up:

\[\frac{\partial F}{\partial n_{\mathrm{k}}} = 2\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ki}}\,n_{\mathrm{i}} - 2\lambda\,n_{\mathrm{k}} = 0 .\]
\[\frac{\partial F}{\partial n_{\mathrm{k}}} = 2\,\sigma_{\mathrm{ik}}\,n_{\mathrm{i}} - 2\lambda\,n_{\mathrm{k}} = 0 .\]

For \(k = x\) this is \(2\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}n_{\mathrm{x}} + 2\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}n_{\mathrm{y}} + 2\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}n_{\mathrm{z}} - 2\lambda\,n_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\), the derivative that we wrote above at Cauchy's sum.

What does the assumption of symmetry do? Without it the pair \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ik}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{ki}}\) would remain in the sum and the calculation would give the eigenvectors of the symmetric part of the matrix. For the stress we lose nothing, because the symmetry follows from the balance of moments: the shear components on neighbouring faces of the cube are equal, for example both \(32\) MPa.

4. Eigenproblem. We divide the equation by \(2\):

\[\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ki}}\,n_{\mathrm{i}} - \lambda\,n_{\mathrm{k}} = 0 , \qquad k = x, y, z . \tag{2.84}\]
\[(\sigma_{\mathrm{ik}} - \lambda\,\delta_{\mathrm{ik}})\,n_{\mathrm{i}} = 0 . \tag{2.84}\]

The sum on the left is the component \(k\) of the vector \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n\). The three equations together are therefore \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \lambda\,\vek n\), equation (2.77), or \((\ten\sigma - \lambda\mat I)\,\vek n = \vek 0\). A test in the plane, for \(k = x\) at the point \(\vek n_1\): \(67 \cdot 0{,}8 + 24 \cdot 0{,}6 = 68\) and \(85 \cdot 0{,}8 = 68\), half of the first component of the gradient \((136, 102)\).

So what is the number \(\lambda\)? We take the scalar product of the equation \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \lambda\,\vek n\) with \(\vek n\). On the left we get the normal stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}} = \vek n\cdot\ten\sigma\,\vek n\), on the right \(\lambda\,(\vek n\cdot\vek n) = \lambda\). The Lagrange multiplier is therefore the normal stress on the face that the calculation found, that is, a principal stress. In the figure these are the values \(85\) and \(35\) of the contour lines that the circle touches. The sign in front of \(\lambda\) is a convention: with the function \(q + \lambda\,(g - 1)\) we would get \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = -\lambda\,\vek n\) and the multiplier would be \(-\sigma_{\mathrm{n}}\). We choose the bracket \(1 - \sum_{\mathrm{i}} n_{\mathrm{i}}n_{\mathrm{i}}\) so that the multiplier is the principal stress itself.

The calculation does not tell which solution is the largest. The derivatives are zero in all three principal directions, also in the middle one, which in the example with all components below has the principal stress \(36\) MPa between \(-18\) MPa and \(72\) MPa. There the normal stress is neither the largest nor the smallest: in the plane through \(\vek n_2\) and \(\vek n_1\) it is the smallest there, and in the plane through \(\vek n_2\) and \(\vek n_3\) the largest, as (2.81) shows. Zero derivatives are a necessary condition, not a sufficient one, just as for a function of one variable (lemma 1.83). Nor does the calculation tell that a solution exists and that the three directions are perpendicular. Theorem 2.83 tells that. The method of multipliers gives the equations, and the theorem the guarantee that they have a solution. SADD only states that the eigenvalues have this extremal property (SADD §1.6, PDF p. 22) and gives no derivation with multipliers.

In this book \(\vek n_1\), \(\vek n_2\), \(\vek n_3\) are the principal directions and the components of the normal are \(n_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(n_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(n_{\mathrm{z}}\).

Lemma 2.86 Characteristic equation (karakteristična enačba)

Let \(\mat A\) be a matrix with three rows and three columns and \(\lambda\) a number. By \(\mat A - \lambda\mat I\) we denote the matrix obtained from \(\mat A\) by subtracting \(\lambda\) from every diagonal entry. Then:

  1. The number \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue of the matrix \(\mat A\) if and only if \(\det(\mat A - \lambda\mat I) = 0\).
  2. For every number \(\lambda\)
    \[\det(\mat A - \lambda\mat I) = -\lambda^3 + I_1\lambda^2 - I_2\lambda + I_3 ,\]
    where
    \[\begin{aligned} I_1 &= A_{\mathrm{xx}} + A_{\mathrm{yy}} + A_{\mathrm{zz}} , \\ I_2 &= A_{\mathrm{xx}}A_{\mathrm{yy}} + A_{\mathrm{yy}}A_{\mathrm{zz}} + A_{\mathrm{zz}}A_{\mathrm{xx}} - A_{\mathrm{xy}}A_{\mathrm{yx}} - A_{\mathrm{yz}}A_{\mathrm{zy}} - A_{\mathrm{zx}}A_{\mathrm{xz}} , \\ I_3 &= \det\mat A . \end{aligned}\]

The eigenvalues of the matrix \(\mat A\) are therefore the real solutions of the equation

\[\lambda^3 - I_1\lambda^2 + I_2\lambda - I_3 = 0 . \tag{2.85}\]
Proof

We must show both items. The first we reduce to the lemma on a zero determinant, the second we get by writing out the formula for the determinant.

1. The eigenvalues.

  1. The matrix \(\mat A - \lambda\mat I\) differs from \(\mat A\) only in the diagonal entry of each row, so the component \(i\) of the vector \((\mat A - \lambda\mat I)\,\vek v\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} A_{\mathrm{ij}}v_{\mathrm{j}} - \lambda v_{\mathrm{i}}\). This is the component \(i\) of the vector \(\mat A\,\vek v - \lambda\,\vek v\).
  2. The equation \(\mat A\,\vek v = \lambda\,\vek v\) is therefore equivalent to the equation \((\mat A - \lambda\mat I)\,\vek v = \vek 0\).
  3. By lemma 2.75 on a zero determinant, this equation has a nonzero solution \(\vek v\) if and only if \(\det(\mat A - \lambda\mat I) = 0\). By definition 2.81 this happens exactly when \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue.

2. The polynomial.

  1. Into the formula for the determinant we put \(M_{\mathrm{xx}} = A_{\mathrm{xx}} - \lambda\), \(M_{\mathrm{yy}} = A_{\mathrm{yy}} - \lambda\), \(M_{\mathrm{zz}} = A_{\mathrm{zz}} - \lambda\) (the blue diagonal), and leave the other entries, \(M_{\mathrm{xy}} = A_{\mathrm{xy}}\), and so on.
  2. The first term contains all three diagonal entries. Multiplying out the brackets,
    \[(A_{\mathrm{xx}} - \lambda)(A_{\mathrm{yy}} - \lambda)(A_{\mathrm{zz}} - \lambda) = -\lambda^3 + (A_{\mathrm{xx}} + A_{\mathrm{yy}} + A_{\mathrm{zz}})\,\lambda^2 - (A_{\mathrm{xx}}A_{\mathrm{yy}} + A_{\mathrm{yy}}A_{\mathrm{zz}} + A_{\mathrm{zz}}A_{\mathrm{xx}})\,\lambda + A_{\mathrm{xx}}A_{\mathrm{yy}}A_{\mathrm{zz}} .\]
  3. The last three terms contain one diagonal entry each. For example, \(-(A_{\mathrm{xx}} - \lambda)\,A_{\mathrm{yz}}A_{\mathrm{zy}} = -A_{\mathrm{xx}}A_{\mathrm{yz}}A_{\mathrm{zy}} + \lambda\,A_{\mathrm{yz}}A_{\mathrm{zy}}\). In the same way \(-A_{\mathrm{xy}}A_{\mathrm{yx}}(A_{\mathrm{zz}} - \lambda)\) and \(-A_{\mathrm{xz}}(A_{\mathrm{yy}} - \lambda)A_{\mathrm{zx}}\) give the terms \(\lambda\,A_{\mathrm{xy}}A_{\mathrm{yx}}\) and \(\lambda\,A_{\mathrm{xz}}A_{\mathrm{zx}}\).
  4. The second and the third term, \(A_{\mathrm{xy}}A_{\mathrm{yz}}A_{\mathrm{zx}}\) and \(A_{\mathrm{xz}}A_{\mathrm{yx}}A_{\mathrm{zy}}\), contain no diagonal entries and therefore no \(\lambda\).
  5. We collect the terms by powers of the number \(\lambda\). At \(\lambda^3\) the coefficient is \(-1\), at \(\lambda^2\) it is \(I_1\). At \(\lambda\) it is \(-(A_{\mathrm{xx}}A_{\mathrm{yy}} + A_{\mathrm{yy}}A_{\mathrm{zz}} + A_{\mathrm{zz}}A_{\mathrm{xx}}) + A_{\mathrm{xy}}A_{\mathrm{yx}} + A_{\mathrm{yz}}A_{\mathrm{zy}} + A_{\mathrm{zx}}A_{\mathrm{xz}}\), which is \(-I_2\). The terms without \(\lambda\) are the six terms of the formula for the determinant with \(\lambda = 0\) put in, that is, \(\det\mat A = I_3\). We checked the algebra with SymPy.
  6. By item 1 the eigenvalues are the solutions of the equation \(-\lambda^3 + I_1\lambda^2 - I_2\lambda + I_3 = 0\). Multiplying it by \(-1\) we get (2.85).

Source: CM (2.5.33), (2.5.42), (2.5.43) (PDF pp. 91, 94), CM (4.3.11), (4.3.12) (PDF p. 189), SADD (1.6.2), (1.6.3) (PDF p. 21). The sources write \(-\lambda^3 + I_1\lambda^2 - I_2\lambda + I_3 = 0\), which is the same equation multiplied by \(-1\). CM (2.5.43) and SADD (1.6.3) write \(I_2\) with squares, which holds for symmetric matrices. For a general matrix the formula with the products \(A_{\mathrm{xy}}A_{\mathrm{yx}}\) holds (checked with SymPy, CM (4.3.2), PDF p. 186). The expansion of the determinant is not from the sources.

We have reduced the search for an eigenvector, three unknown components together with an unknown number, to one equation for a single unknown \(\lambda\). The equation (2.85) is called the characteristic equation (karakteristična enačba) of the matrix (CM, PDF p. 91), and the expression \(\det(\mat A - \lambda\mat I)\) as a function of the number \(\lambda\) its characteristic polynomial (karakteristični polinom) (KREYSZIG §8.1, PDF p. 352). The solutions of the equation are also called the roots of the polynomial, because at them the value of the polynomial is zero. Once we know an eigenvalue \(\lambda\), we get the eigenvector from the system \((\mat A - \lambda\mat I)\,\vek v = \vek 0\).

The example with numbers shows what the characteristic polynomial looks like, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 67\) MPa, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = 53\) MPa and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}} = 24\) MPa, the other components zero. For it \(\det(\ten\sigma - \lambda\mat I) = -\lambda^3 + 120\,\lambda^2 - 2975\,\lambda\), and we compute the coefficients below. In figure 2.185 we put the number \(\lambda\) in MPa on the horizontal axis and the value of the polynomial on the vertical axis, in MPa³, and draw the curve. By point 1 of the lemma an eigenvalue is every number \(\lambda\) at which the value of the polynomial is zero, that is, every point where the curve crosses the horizontal axis. On the part we drew there are three such points. That there are no others we shall prove in corollary 2.89.

In the lectures the characteristic polynomial has one more name, the Cayley-Hamilton polynomial. It borrowed the name from a theorem that in mathematics carries the names of Cayley and Hamilton and says that a matrix satisfies its own characteristic equation: if in (2.85) we put the matrix itself in place of the number \(\lambda\), we get the matrix of zeros,

\[\mat A\mat A\mat A - I_1\,\mat A\mat A + I_2\,\mat A - I_3\,\mat I = \mat 0 . \tag{2.86}\]

For a symmetric matrix this follows from what we already have. Let \(\vek n_{\mathrm{a}}\) be one of the three principal directions of theorem 2.83 and \(\lambda_{\mathrm{a}}\) its eigenvalue. Every multiplication by \(\mat A\) multiplies the vector \(\vek n_{\mathrm{a}}\) by \(\lambda_{\mathrm{a}}\), so the left side, multiplied by \(\vek n_{\mathrm{a}}\), gives \((\lambda_{\mathrm{a}}^3 - I_1\lambda_{\mathrm{a}}^2 + I_2\lambda_{\mathrm{a}} - I_3)\,\vek n_{\mathrm{a}}\). The bracket is zero, because by lemma 2.86 \(\lambda_{\mathrm{a}}\) is a solution of the characteristic equation, one of the points where the curve crosses the axis. Every vector is a sum of steps along the three principal directions (lemma 1.21), so the left side sends every vector to \(\vek 0\), also \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\). Its columns are therefore zero. For the example with numbers we checked with SymPy that \(\ten\sigma\ten\sigma\ten\sigma - 120\,\ten\sigma\ten\sigma + 2975\,\ten\sigma\) is the matrix of zeros. We shall not need the theorem in this book, and the name only says that the polynomial is the same one.

The coefficients of the equation for stress get a name.

Definition 2.87 Stress invariants (invariante napetosti)

Let \(\ten\sigma\) be the symmetric matrix of stress components at the point \(P\). The stress invariants (invariante napetosti) at \(P\) are the numbers

\[\begin{aligned} I_1 &= \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} , \\ I_2 &= \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} - \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}^2 - \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}^2 - \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}^2 , \\ I_3 &= \det\ten\sigma , \end{aligned} \tag{2.87}\]

the coefficients of the characteristic equation of the matrix \(\ten\sigma\). The number \(I_1\) is the trace of the matrix \(\ten\sigma\), the sum of its diagonal entries, \(\tr\ten\sigma\) (section 1.17.4).

Source: CM (4.3.1), (4.3.2) (PDF p. 186), SADD (3.4.1), (3.4.2) (PDF p. 69).

The formula for \(I_2\) is the formula of lemma 2.86 in which we wrote the products \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\) as squares because of the symmetry. The invariants have units: \(I_1\) is in MPa, \(I_2\) in MPa², \(I_3\) in MPa³. CM writes the second invariant with the trace, \(I_2 = \tfrac12\big((\tr\ten\sigma)^2 - \tr(\ten\sigma\ten\sigma)\big)\) (CM (2.5.16), PDF p. 85, and CM (4.3.1), PDF p. 186). This is the same number. By the rule for the matrix product the diagonal entry \(i\) of the matrix \(\ten\sigma\ten\sigma\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}}\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\sigma_{\mathrm{ji}}\), so

\[\begin{aligned} \tr(\ten\sigma\ten\sigma) &= \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}^2 + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}^2 + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}^2 + 2\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}^2 + 2\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}^2 + 2\sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}^2 , \\ (\tr\ten\sigma)^2 &= \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}^2 + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}^2 + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}^2 + 2\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}) . \end{aligned}\]

The squares of the diagonal entries cancel in the subtraction, and half of what remains is \(I_2\) of (2.87). SADD uses the same signs (SADD (1.6.3), PDF p. 21).

The second invariant can also be put together from three smaller determinants (figure 2.186). In the matrix we strike out the row \(z\) and the column \(z\). Four components remain, which describe the stress in the plane \(xy\), and by definition 2.72 their determinant of size \(2 \times 2\) equals \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} - \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} - \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}^2\). In the same way we strike out the row and column \(y\) and the row and column \(x\). A determinant that remains when we strike out rows and columns of a matrix is called a minor (poddeterminanta). The sum of the three minors is

\[I_2 = \begin{vmatrix} \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} & \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} \\ \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}} & \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} \end{vmatrix} + \begin{vmatrix} \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} & \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} \\ \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}} & \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} \end{vmatrix} + \begin{vmatrix} \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} & \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} \\ \sigma_{\mathrm{zy}} & \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} \end{vmatrix} , \tag{2.88}\]

where the vertical bars mean the determinant. When we write out the three determinants we get the six terms of the second line of (2.87). This is how SADD writes the second invariant (SADD (1.6.3), PDF p. 21). In the example with numbers the first minor is \(67 \cdot 53 - 24^2 = 2975\), and the other two are zero, because they contain the row \(z\) of zeros.

The formula for the third invariant also shortens for a symmetric matrix. The second and third term of the determinant, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}\sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\sigma_{\mathrm{zy}}\), are equal because of the symmetry. The fourth, fifth and sixth term each contain one diagonal entry and two entries that are symmetric to each other, that is, a square. So

\[I_3 = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} + 2\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}\sigma_{\mathrm{zx}} - \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}^2 - \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}^2 - \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}^2 . \tag{2.89}\]

We checked both formulas with SymPy. In the lectures the invariants are denoted \(\sigma_{\mathrm{I}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{II}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{III}}\), and the components carry the indices \(1\), \(2\), \(3\) instead of \(x\), \(y\), \(z\).

The word invariant (invarianta) means something that does not change. The next lemma justifies the name.

Lemma 2.88 The invariants do not change when the axes turn (invariante se ne spremenijo, ko zasučemo osi)

Let \(\ten\sigma\) be the matrix of stress components at the point \(P\) in the chosen axes and \(\bar{\ten\sigma}\) the matrix of components of the same stress in turned axes. Then \(\det(\bar{\ten\sigma} - \lambda\mat I) = \det(\ten\sigma - \lambda\mat I)\) for every number \(\lambda\), and the stress invariants computed from \(\bar{\ten\sigma}\) are equal to the invariants computed from \(\ten\sigma\).

Proof

We must show that the two matrices have the same characteristic polynomial, and from this that they have the same coefficients.

  1. By theorem 2.53, \(\bar{\ten\sigma} = \mat Q\ten\sigma\mat Q^\T\), where \(\mat Q\) is the rotation matrix whose rows are the unit vectors of the turned axes. By lemma 2.47 on the orthogonality of the rotation matrix, \(\mat Q\mat Q^\T = \mat I\).
  2. The matrix \(\lambda\mat I\) has the number \(\lambda\) on its diagonal and zeros elsewhere. By the rule for the matrix product, therefore, \(\mat Q\,(\lambda\mat I) = \lambda\mat Q\) and \(\mat Q\,(\lambda\mat I)\,\mat Q^\T = \lambda\,\mat Q\mat Q^\T = \lambda\mat I\). By the distributivity of the matrix product (lemma 1.100) therefore
    \[\mat Q\,(\ten\sigma - \lambda\mat I)\,\mat Q^\T = \mat Q\ten\sigma\mat Q^\T - \lambda\mat I = \bar{\ten\sigma} - \lambda\mat I .\]
  3. By theorem 2.74 on the determinant of a product, used twice, \(\det\big(\mat Q\,(\ten\sigma - \lambda\mat I)\,\mat Q^\T\big) = \det\mat Q\,\det(\ten\sigma - \lambda\mat I)\,\det\mat Q^\T\).
  4. By the same theorem and lemma 2.73, \(\det\mat Q\,\det\mat Q^\T = \det(\mat Q\mat Q^\T) = \det\mat I = 1\). Steps 2 and 3 give \(\det(\bar{\ten\sigma} - \lambda\mat I) = \det(\ten\sigma - \lambda\mat I)\) for every number \(\lambda\). The curve of the characteristic polynomial is therefore the same in the turned axes, point for point.
  5. By lemma 2.86 both sides are polynomials of the form \(-\lambda^3 + I_1\lambda^2 - I_2\lambda + I_3\), one with the invariants \(\bar I_1\), \(\bar I_2\), \(\bar I_3\) of the matrix \(\bar{\ten\sigma}\), the other with the invariants \(I_1\), \(I_2\), \(I_3\) of the matrix \(\ten\sigma\). Subtracting them, the terms \(-\lambda^3\) cancel, and for every number \(\lambda\)
    \[c_2\lambda^2 + c_1\lambda + c_0 = 0 , \qquad c_2 = \bar I_1 - I_1 , \quad c_1 = I_2 - \bar I_2 , \quad c_0 = \bar I_3 - I_3 .\]
  6. Two curves that agree everywhere also agree at three chosen numbers \(\lambda\). At \(\lambda = 0\) we get \(c_0 = 0\). At \(\lambda = 1\) and \(\lambda = -1\) we get \(c_2 + c_1 = 0\) and \(c_2 - c_1 = 0\). Adding the two equations gives \(2c_2 = 0\), so \(c_2 = 0\) and then \(c_1 = 0\). The invariants are equal.

Source: SADD §1.6 (PDF p. 21) states that the invariants do not change under a change of the coordinate system, CM §4.3.1.1 (PDF p. 186). The proof is not from the sources.

The proof used only that \(\bar{\ten\sigma} = \mat Q\ten\sigma\mat Q^\T\) and that the determinant of a product is the product of the determinants. The lemma therefore holds for every matrix that changes like stress when the axes turn, for example for the small strain matrix. Now we can compute the characteristic equation in the principal axes, where the matrix is simplest.

Corollary 2.89 The principal stresses are the roots of the characteristic equation (glavne napetosti so rešitve karakteristične enačbe)

Let \(\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \sigma_3\) be the principal stresses at the point \(P\). Then for every number \(\lambda\)

\[\det(\ten\sigma - \lambda\mat I) = (\sigma_1 - \lambda)(\sigma_2 - \lambda)(\sigma_3 - \lambda) .\]

The solutions of the characteristic equation are therefore exactly the numbers \(\sigma_1\), \(\sigma_2\) and \(\sigma_3\), there are no other principal stresses, and

\[\begin{aligned} I_1 &= \sigma_1 + \sigma_2 + \sigma_3 , \\ I_2 &= \sigma_1\sigma_2 + \sigma_2\sigma_3 + \sigma_3\sigma_1 , \\ I_3 &= \sigma_1\sigma_2\sigma_3 . \end{aligned} \tag{2.90}\]
Proof

We compute the characteristic polynomial in the principal axes and use that it is the same in all axes.

  1. By item 1 of corollary 2.85, in the principal axes the matrix of components is diagonal, with \(\sigma_1\), \(\sigma_2\) and \(\sigma_3\) on the diagonal. The matrix \(\bar{\ten\sigma} - \lambda\mat I\) therefore has \(\sigma_1 - \lambda\), \(\sigma_2 - \lambda\) and \(\sigma_3 - \lambda\) on the diagonal and zeros elsewhere.
  2. In the formula for the determinant every term except the first contains at least one entry off the diagonal (a blackwhite circle), which is zero. So \(\det(\bar{\ten\sigma} - \lambda\mat I) = (\sigma_1 - \lambda)(\sigma_2 - \lambda)(\sigma_3 - \lambda)\).
  3. By lemma @lem-invariante, \(\det(\ten\sigma - \lambda\mat I) = \det(\bar{\ten\sigma} - \lambda\mat I)\), which proves the first equality.
  4. A product of three numbers is zero if and only if one of them is zero. The solutions of the characteristic equation, the points where the curve crosses the axis, are therefore \(\sigma_1\), \(\sigma_2\) and \(\sigma_3\). Every principal stress is, by lemma 2.86, a solution of this equation, so it equals one of these three numbers.
  5. Multiplying out the brackets we get \(-\lambda^3 + (\sigma_1 + \sigma_2 + \sigma_3)\lambda^2 - (\sigma_1\sigma_2 + \sigma_2\sigma_3 + \sigma_3\sigma_1)\lambda + \sigma_1\sigma_2\sigma_3\). By item 2 of lemma 2.86 the same polynomial equals \(-\lambda^3 + I_1\lambda^2 - I_2\lambda + I_3\). As in steps 5 and 6 of the proof of lemma 2.88, it follows that they have equal coefficients, which is (2.90).

Source: CM (2.5.44) (PDF p. 94), SADD (3.4.2) (PDF p. 69). The proof is not from the sources.

So the principal stresses depend only on the three invariants, which are the same in all axes. They are therefore a property of the stress at the point, not of the choice of axes, which is what we expect of a quantity that tells whether the material holds. Let us try it on the example with numbers, with the components \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 67\) MPa, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = 53\) MPa and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}} = 24\) MPa. The invariants are \(I_1 = 67 + 53 + 0 = 120\) MPa and \(I_2 = 67 \cdot 53 - 24^2 = 3551 - 576 = 2975\) MPa², and the third is \(I_3 = 0\), because every term of the formula for the determinant contains one entry of the third row (the circle in the bottom row of the grid), which are all zero. The characteristic equation is

\[\lambda^3 - 120\,\lambda^2 + 2975\,\lambda = \lambda\,(\lambda^2 - 120\,\lambda + 2975) = 0 .\]

One solution is \(\lambda = 0\): at \(\lambda = 0\) the polynomial equals \(I_3\), which is zero, so the curve passes through the origin. The other two we get from the quadratic equation with the school formula, \(\lambda = \tfrac12\big(120 \pm \sqrt{120^2 - 4 \cdot 2975}\big) = \tfrac12\,(120 \pm 50)\), that is, \(85\) and \(35\). These are the principal stresses we guessed from the graph, the highest point and the lowest point of the normal stress, and indeed \(85 + 35 + 0 = 120\) and \(85 \cdot 35 = 2975\), as (2.90) requires.

So we compute the principal stresses and principal directions in three steps. First we compute the invariants by (2.87). Then we find the solutions of the characteristic equation (2.85), which are the principal stresses. Finally, for each principal stress \(\lambda\) we solve the system \((\ten\sigma - \lambda\mat I)\,\vek n = \vek 0\) and divide the solution by its length. Let us try the procedure on three examples.

Pure shear. The cube of section 2.6.6 has \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}} = \tau_0\), and all other components are zero. The invariants are \(I_1 = 0\), \(I_2 = -\sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}^2 = -\tau_0^2\) and \(I_3 = 0\), because each of the six terms of the formula for the determinant contains at least one entry that is zero. The characteristic equation is

\[\lambda^3 - \tau_0^2\,\lambda = \lambda\,(\lambda - \tau_0)(\lambda + \tau_0) = 0 ,\]

so the principal stresses are \(\sigma_1 = \tau_0\), \(\sigma_2 = 0\) and \(\sigma_3 = -\tau_0\). For \(\lambda = \tau_0\) the matrix \(\ten\sigma - \tau_0\mat I\) has the rows \((-\tau_0, 0, \tau_0)\), \((0, -\tau_0, 0)\) and \((\tau_0, 0, -\tau_0)\). The first row gives \(n_{\mathrm{x}} = n_{\mathrm{z}}\), the second \(n_{\mathrm{y}} = 0\), and the third is the first with the opposite sign. The unit solution is \(\vek n_1 = (1, 0, 1)/\sqrt2\), the direction of the diagonal \(AC\). For \(\lambda = 0\), \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \tau_0\,(n_{\mathrm{z}}, 0, n_{\mathrm{x}}) = \vek 0\) only for \(n_{\mathrm{x}} = n_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\), so \(\vek n_2 = \vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\). The third direction is \(\vek n_3 = \vek n_1\times\vek n_2 = (-1, 0, 1)/\sqrt2\), the direction of the diagonal \(BD\), and indeed \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n_3 = \tau_0\,(1, 0, -1)/\sqrt2 = -\tau_0\,\vek n_3\). At \(\tau_0 = 50\) MPa the principal stresses are \(50\), \(0\) and \(-50\) MPa (figure 2.187). On the left of the figure are the corners, the forces \(\tau_0\) along the faces of the cube and the axes \(x\) and \(z\). On the right is a piece with faces perpendicular to the diagonals, with tension on the faces perpendicular to \(AC\) and compression on the faces perpendicular to \(BD\).

A bar and a fluid at rest. In a bar \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma\) is the only nonzero component, so \(I_1 = \sigma\), \(I_2 = 0\) and \(I_3 = 0\). The characteristic equation \(\lambda^3 - \sigma\lambda^2 = \lambda^2(\lambda - \sigma) = 0\) has the solution \(\sigma\) and the double solution \(0\). For \(\sigma > 0\) the principal stresses are \(\sigma_1 = \sigma\) and \(\sigma_2 = \sigma_3 = 0\), so for our steel bar with the diameter 10 mm under the force of 10 kN, \(127{,}3\) MPa, \(0\) and \(0\). Earlier we saw that for the principal stress \(0\) every unit vector perpendicular to the axis of the bar is a principal direction. This holds whenever two principal stresses are equal. Let, for example, \(\sigma_1 > \sigma_2 = \sigma_3\) and let \(\vek v\) be perpendicular to \(\vek n_1\). Its component along the principal axis \(1\) is \(\vek v\cdot\vek n_1 = 0\), so by lemma 1.21 on a vector as a sum of steps along the axes, used in the principal axes, \(\vek v = c_2\vek n_2 + c_3\vek n_3\) for some numbers \(c_2\) and \(c_3\), and by linearity \(\ten\sigma\,\vek v = c_2\sigma_2\vek n_2 + c_3\sigma_3\vek n_3 = \sigma_2\,\vek v\). The principal directions are then not unique, only the direction \(\vek n_1\) is determined. In a fluid at rest \(\ten\sigma = -p\,\mat I\), so \(I_1 = -3p\), \(I_2 = 3p^2\) and \(I_3 = -p^3\), and the characteristic equation is \(\lambda^3 + 3p\lambda^2 + 3p^2\lambda + p^3 = (\lambda + p)^3 = 0\). All three principal stresses are \(-p\), and every direction is a principal direction, as figure 2.57 shows. SADD distinguishes exactly these three cases: three different principal stresses, two equal and three equal (SADD §1.6, PDF p. 21).

An example with all components. At the point \(P\) let the matrix of stress components in MPa be

\[\ten\sigma = \begin{bmatrix} 16 & 32 & -4 \\ 32 & 28 & 28 \\ -4 & 28 & 46 \end{bmatrix} .\]

All six different components are nonzero. We chose the numbers ourselves, so that the results are whole numbers that we can check by hand. The invariants are

\[\begin{aligned} I_1 &= 16 + 28 + 46 = 90 , \\ I_2 &= 16 \cdot 28 + 28 \cdot 46 + 46 \cdot 16 - 32^2 - 28^2 - (-4)^2 = 2472 - 1824 = 648 , \end{aligned}\]

in MPa and MPa² respectively. The three minors of (2.88) give the same: without \(z\) it is \(16 \cdot 28 - 32^2 = -576\), without \(y\) it is \(16 \cdot 46 - (-4)^2 = 720\), without \(x\) it is \(28 \cdot 46 - 28^2 = 504\), and \(-576 + 720 + 504 = 648\). The third invariant follows from the formula for the determinant, term by term:

\[\begin{aligned} I_3 &= 16 \cdot 28 \cdot 46 + 32 \cdot 28 \cdot (-4) + (-4) \cdot 32 \cdot 28 \\ &\quad - (-4) \cdot 28 \cdot (-4) - 32 \cdot 32 \cdot 46 - 16 \cdot 28 \cdot 28 = -46\,656 , \end{aligned}\]

in MPa³. The characteristic equation is

\[\lambda^3 - 90\,\lambda^2 + 648\,\lambda + 46\,656 = 0 .\]

An equation of the third degree with integer coefficients can be solved by hand when it has an integer solution. The numbers of this example are chosen so that it has one. We write the equation as \(\lambda\,(\lambda^2 - 90\,\lambda + 648) = -46\,656\). If \(\lambda\) is an integer, the number in the bracket is an integer too, so \(\lambda\) divides \(46\,656\) without remainder. The integer solutions are therefore among the divisors of the third invariant, with the sign plus or minus. Since \(46\,656 = 2^6 \cdot 3^6\), there are \(49\) divisors, so we first narrow the choice with two bounds for the largest solution \(\sigma_1\):

  • By item 2 of corollary 2.85, \(\sigma_1\) is at least as large as every normal stress, so also as the largest diagonal entry \(46\).
  • By (2.90), \(I_1^2 - 2I_2 = (\sigma_1 + \sigma_2 + \sigma_3)^2 - 2\,(\sigma_1\sigma_2 + \sigma_2\sigma_3 + \sigma_3\sigma_1) = \sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 + \sigma_3^2\). So \(\sigma_1^2 \le 90^2 - 2 \cdot 648 = 6804\) and \(\sigma_1 \le 82{,}5\).

Five divisors lie between \(46\) and \(82{,}5\). At them the left side of the equation has the values

\(\lambda\) \(48\) \(54\) \(64\) \(72\) \(81\)
\(\lambda^3 - 90\,\lambda^2 + 648\,\lambda + 46\,656\) \(-19\,008\) \(-23\,328\) \(-18\,368\) \(0\) \(40\,095\)

The solution is \(72\). So we can divide the polynomial by \(\lambda - 72\):

\[\lambda^3 - 90\,\lambda^2 + 648\,\lambda + 46\,656 = (\lambda - 72)(\lambda^2 - 18\,\lambda - 648) .\]

We check by multiplying the brackets: at \(\lambda^2\) we get \(-18 - 72 = -90\), at \(\lambda\) we get \(-648 + 72 \cdot 18 = 648\), and the term without \(\lambda\) is \(72 \cdot 648 = 46\,656\). By the school formula the quadratic equation \(\lambda^2 - 18\,\lambda - 648 = 0\) gives \(\lambda = 9 \pm \sqrt{81 + 648} = 9 \pm 27\), that is, \(36\) and \(-18\). This route works only when a solution is an integer, which is an exception for measured stresses. If we replace the component \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 46\) by \(47\), the equation is \(\lambda^3 - 91\,\lambda^2 + 692\,\lambda + 47\,232 = 0\). No divisor of the number \(47\,232\) solves it, and the solutions \(72{,}45\), \(36{,}44\) and \(-17{,}89\) can only be found numerically.

As a check we also solved the original equation numerically with NumPy (the function numpy.roots). The solutions are \(72\), \(36\) and \(-18\), which we check by substitution. For \(\lambda = 72\), \(72^3 - 90 \cdot 72^2 + 648 \cdot 72 + 46\,656 = 373\,248 - 466\,560 + 46\,656 + 46\,656 = 0\). The principal stresses are \(\sigma_1 = 72\) MPa, \(\sigma_2 = 36\) MPa and \(\sigma_3 = -18\) MPa, and indeed \(72 + 36 - 18 = 90 = I_1\). The principal direction for \(\sigma_1 = 72\) follows from the system \((\ten\sigma - 72\,\mat I)\,\vek n = \vek 0\):

\[\begin{aligned} -56\,n_{\mathrm{x}} + 32\,n_{\mathrm{y}} - 4\,n_{\mathrm{z}} &= 0 , \\ 32\,n_{\mathrm{x}} - 44\,n_{\mathrm{y}} + 28\,n_{\mathrm{z}} &= 0 , \\ -4\,n_{\mathrm{x}} + 28\,n_{\mathrm{y}} - 26\,n_{\mathrm{z}} &= 0 . \end{aligned}\]

From the first equation \(n_{\mathrm{z}} = 8\,n_{\mathrm{y}} - 14\,n_{\mathrm{x}}\). Putting this into the third, we get \(-4\,n_{\mathrm{x}} + 28\,n_{\mathrm{y}} - 208\,n_{\mathrm{y}} + 364\,n_{\mathrm{x}} = 360\,n_{\mathrm{x}} - 180\,n_{\mathrm{y}} = 0\), so \(n_{\mathrm{y}} = 2\,n_{\mathrm{x}}\) and then \(n_{\mathrm{z}} = 2\,n_{\mathrm{x}}\). For \(n_{\mathrm{x}} = 1\) this is the vector \((1, 2, 2)\), which also satisfies the second equation, \(32 - 88 + 56 = 0\). Its length is \(3\), so \(\vek n_1 = (1, 2, 2)/3\). In the same way we get \(\vek n_2 = (2, 1, -2)/3\), and the third direction is \(\vek n_3 = \vek n_1\times\vek n_2 = (-2, 2, -1)/3\). Check: \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n_2 = (72, 36, -72)/3 = 36\,\vek n_2\) and \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n_3 = (36, -36, 18)/3 = -18\,\vek n_3\), and the directions are mutually perpendicular, for example \(\vek n_1\cdot\vek n_2 = (2 + 2 - 4)/9 = 0\). NumPy (the function numpy.linalg.eigh) gives the same directions, some with the opposite sign.

Let us draw these three directions (figure 2.188). From the point \(P\) we draw the axes \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\) and write at each the normal stress on the face perpendicular to it, in MPa. We reach the end of the vector \(\vek n_1\) in three steps: \(\tfrac13\) along the axis \(x\), \(\tfrac23\) along the axis \(y\) and \(\tfrac23\) along the axis \(z\). The vector \(\vek n_1\) therefore points obliquely between all three axes. In the same way we draw \(\vek n_2\), which goes by the steps \(\tfrac23\), \(\tfrac13\) and \(-\tfrac23\), so the last step is downwards, and \(\vek n_3\) with the steps \(-\tfrac23\), \(\tfrac23\) and \(-\tfrac13\). At each principal direction we write its principal stress.

What does this tell us? On the faces of the cube perpendicular to the axes the normal stresses are \(16\), \(28\) and \(46\) MPa, all of them tension. But the largest normal stress through the point \(P\) is \(72\) MPa, more than half again as large as \(46\) MPa, and it acts on the face with the normal \((1, 2, 2)/3\), which is not along any axis. On the face with the normal \(\vek n_3\) the material is compressed by the stress \(18\) MPa, although all faces of the cube are in tension (figure 2.189). On the number line the normal stresses on the faces of the cube are blackwhite and the principal stresses \(-18\), \(36\) and \(72\) MPa are blue. The normal stress on every face through \(P\) lies between \(\sigma_3\) and \(\sigma_1\). Anyone who judged the stress only by the faces of the cube would underestimate the largest stress and would not notice the compression at all.

Finding the principal axes is also called diagonalization, because it turns the matrix of components into a diagonal one.

Definition 2.90 Diagonalization of a symmetric matrix (diagonalizacija simetrične matrike)

Let \(\mat A\) be a symmetric matrix with three rows and three columns. Diagonalization (diagonalizacija) of the matrix \(\mat A\) is the search for a rotation matrix \(\mat Q\) (definition 2.44) for which the matrix \(\mat Q\mat A\mat Q^\T\) is diagonal, that is, has only zeros off the diagonal.

Source: KREYSZIG §8.4, theorem 4 (PDF p. 367) for a general matrix with a basis of eigenvectors, which it puts into the columns of a matrix \(\mat X\) and writes \(\mat X^{-1}\mat A\mat X\). Here the eigenvectors are the rows of the matrix \(\mat Q\), so \(\mat Q = \mat X^\T\). SADD (1.6.4) (PDF p. 22) for a symmetric tensor in the principal axes.

Such a matrix \(\mat Q\) exists for every symmetric matrix: into its rows we write the principal directions \(\vek n_1\), \(\vek n_2\) and \(\vek n_3 = \vek n_1\times\vek n_2\) of theorem 2.83. By theorem 2.53, \(\mat Q\ten\sigma\mat Q^\T\) is the matrix of components of the same stress in the axes along these directions, and by item 1 of corollary 2.85 that matrix is diagonal. For the matrix of the example with all components,

\[\mat Q = \frac13\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 & -2 \\ -2 & 2 & -1 \end{bmatrix} , \qquad \mat Q\,\ten\sigma = \begin{bmatrix} 24 & 48 & 48 \\ 24 & 12 & -24 \\ 12 & -12 & 6 \end{bmatrix} , \qquad \mat Q\,\ten\sigma\,\mat Q^\T = \begin{bmatrix} 72 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 36 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -18 \end{bmatrix} .\]

The rows of the matrix \(\mat Q\,\ten\sigma\) are the traction vectors on the three principal faces, \(72\,\vek n_1 = (24, 48, 48)\), \(36\,\vek n_2 = (24, 12, -24)\) and \(-18\,\vek n_3 = (12, -12, 6)\). Multiplication by \(\mat Q^\T\) takes the scalar product of each with the three principal directions. Since these are pairwise perpendicular, only the product of a vector with its own direction remains, which is the diagonal.

Let us draw both matrices (figure 2.190). Around the point \(P\) we cut out a cube with faces perpendicular to the axes \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\). On each visible face we draw the traction vector by its components, which are a row of the first matrix. The normal components \(16\), \(28\) and \(46\) MPa point out of the faces. The shear components lie in the faces: \(32\) and \(28\) MPa in the direction of an axis, and the component \(-4\) MPa we draw as an arrow labelled \(4\) against the direction of the axis. Then around the same point we cut out a cube of the same size with faces perpendicular to the principal directions. On its faces there is no blackwhite arrow, because there are no shear components. The face with the normal \(\vek n_1\) is pulled by a tension of \(72\) MPa, the face with the normal \(\vek n_2\) by a tension of \(36\) MPa, and on the third face presses a compression of \(18\) MPa, so the arrow points into the cube. Of the third pair of faces we see the one with the outward normal \(-\vek n_3\). Below the cube is the diagonal matrix.

The matrices are different, but the stress is the same: the same point, the same material, the same forces between its parts. Only the faces on which we measure these forces differ. So all the quantities that do not depend on the choice of axes are equal. By (2.90) the invariants of the diagonal matrix are \(72 + 36 - 18 = 90\), \(72 \cdot 36 + 36 \cdot (-18) + (-18) \cdot 72 = 648\) and \(72 \cdot 36 \cdot (-18) = -46\,656\), exactly those that we computed from the first matrix. Diagonalization therefore does not change the stress, but shows it in the axes in which three numbers describe it instead of six. The other three numbers have not vanished: they tell how the principal axes are turned, and are stored in the matrix \(\mat Q\).

2.11.4 Principal strains

Let us return to strain. In section 1.17 we derived that the strain of a short fibre leaving the point \(P\) in the direction of the unit vector \(\vek n\) is approximately

\[\varepsilon(\vek n) \approx \vek n^\T\ten\varepsilon\,\vek n = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}n_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}n_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}n_{\mathrm{z}}^2 + 2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}n_{\mathrm{x}}n_{\mathrm{y}} + 2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}n_{\mathrm{x}}n_{\mathrm{z}} + 2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}n_{\mathrm{y}}n_{\mathrm{z}}\]

((1.61)). Here \(\ten\varepsilon\) is the small strain matrix at \(P\) (definition 1.121). The approximation differs from the strain of the fibre by at most \(4{,}5\,m^2\) if all partial derivatives of the displacements are smaller in size than \(m < \tfrac13\) (section 1.17). For the sheared cube below the largest partial derivative is \(0{,}001\), so the bound on the error is about \(4{,}5 \cdot 10^{-6}\), and we shall see that the actual error is only \(10^{-7}\). The expression has the same form as the normal stress \(\vek n^\T\ten\sigma\,\vek n\). The matrix \(\ten\varepsilon\) is symmetric, since for example \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \tfrac12(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial y + \partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial x) = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yx}}\). Theorem 2.83 therefore holds for it too.

Definition 2.91 Principal strain and principal direction of strain (glavna deformacija in glavna smer deformacije)

Let \(\ten\varepsilon\) be the small strain matrix at the point \(P\) in a chosen rectangular Cartesian coordinate system. A unit vector \(\vek n\) is a principal direction of strain (glavna smer deformacije) at \(P\), and the number \(\lambda\) the corresponding principal strain (glavna deformacija), if

\[\ten\varepsilon\,\vek n = \lambda\,\vek n ,\]

that is, if \(\vek n\) is a unit eigenvector and \(\lambda\) an eigenvalue of the matrix \(\ten\varepsilon\).

Source: SADD §2.4, (2.4.1)-(2.4.3) (PDF pp. 48-49). CM §3.4.6 (PDF p. 138) defines principal strains in the same way for the Green-Lagrange tensor.

We denote the principal strains, ordered by size, by \(\varepsilon_1 \ge \varepsilon_2 \ge \varepsilon_3\). What they mean is told by a corollary that is for strains what corollary 2.85 is for stresses.

Let us first draw the corollary on the front face of the sheared cube of section 1.6.2. There we computed from the lengths that the diagonal \(AC\) gets longer by about \(5 \cdot 10^{-4}\) of its length, and the diagonal \(BD\) shorter by as much. In the example below we shall check that these are the principal directions of strain \(\vek n_1\) and \(\vek n_3\). On the left, from the point \(P\), we draw the axes \(x\) and \(z\) and, turned by \(45^\circ\), the axes in the principal directions \(\vek n_1\) and \(\vek n_3\). Along them we lay a small square with the side \(a\), grey and dashed, because this is the state before loading. In orange is the same piece after loading. The side along \(\vek n_1\) gets longer by \(\varepsilon_1 a\), the side along \(\vek n_3\) gets shorter by \(|\varepsilon_3|\,a\), and the angle between them at \(P\) stays right. This will be item 1 of the corollary. The changes of length are drawn as a quarter of the side so that we see them, while in the cube they are only \(0{,}0005\,a\). On the right, from the same point \(P\) with the same axes, we draw a unit fibre in the direction of the vertical edge of the cube, \(\vek n = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\). Its perpendicular projections onto the axes are the components \(\bar n_1 = 1/\sqrt2\) and \(\bar n_3 = 1/\sqrt2\), and the component \(\bar n_2\) along the axis \(y\) is zero. Below the fibre is the number line of strains with the principal strains of the cube \(\varepsilon_3 = -5 \cdot 10^{-4}\) and \(\varepsilon_1 = 5 \cdot 10^{-4}\). Item 2 of the corollary will say that the strain of every fibre through \(P\) lies on the blue segment between them. For the vertical edge it will give \(\varepsilon_1 \cdot \tfrac12 + \varepsilon_3 \cdot \tfrac12 = 0\), the point in the middle of the segment: within small strains the vertical edge of the cube does not get longer. In the cube the small square also turns as a whole (figure 2.121, right). We did not draw this turning here, because it changes neither the lengths of the sides nor the angle between them.

Corollary 2.92 Strain in principal axes (deformacija v glavnih oseh)

Let \(\varepsilon_1 \ge \varepsilon_2 \ge \varepsilon_3\) be the principal strains at the point \(P\) and \(\vek n_1\), \(\vek n_2\), \(\vek n_3 = \vek n_1\times\vek n_2\) the corresponding principal directions of strain of theorem 2.83 for \(\mat A = \ten\varepsilon\). Then:

  1. In the coordinate system with its axes in the directions \(\vek n_1\), \(\vek n_2\) and \(\vek n_3\) the small strain matrix is diagonal, with \(\varepsilon_1\), \(\varepsilon_2\) and \(\varepsilon_3\) on the diagonal. The shear strains between these directions are zero.
  2. For a unit vector \(\vek n\) with the components \(\bar n_{\mathrm{a}} = \vek n\cdot\vek n_{\mathrm{a}}\) in these axes
    \[\vek n^\T\ten\varepsilon\,\vek n = \varepsilon_1\,\bar n_1^2 + \varepsilon_2\,\bar n_2^2 + \varepsilon_3\,\bar n_3^2 ,\]
    and \(\varepsilon_3 \le \vek n^\T\ten\varepsilon\,\vek n \le \varepsilon_1\).
Proof

For the first item we use how the small strain matrix changes when the axes turn, and the second is the computation of the proof of corollary 2.85. The indices \(a\) and \(b\) run over \(1\), \(2\) and \(3\).

1. A diagonal matrix.

  1. By lemma 2.55 the small strain matrix in turned axes is \(\mat Q\ten\varepsilon\mat Q^\T\), where \(\mat Q\) is the rotation matrix with the rows \(\vek n_1\), \(\vek n_2\) and \(\vek n_3\) (definition 2.44).
  2. By the rule for the matrix product the entry in row \(a\) and column \(b\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} Q_{\mathrm{ai}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\,Q_{\mathrm{bj}}\). The row \(a\) of the matrix \(\mat Q\) is the vector \(\vek n_{\mathrm{a}}\), so by (2.78) this is the number \(\vek n_{\mathrm{a}}\cdot(\ten\varepsilon\,\vek n_{\mathrm{b}})\).
  3. By theorem 2.83, \(\ten\varepsilon\,\vek n_{\mathrm{b}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{b}}\vek n_{\mathrm{b}}\), so the entry equals \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{b}}\,(\vek n_{\mathrm{a}}\cdot\vek n_{\mathrm{b}})\). This is \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{b}}\) for \(a = b\), the strain of the side along \(\vek n_{\mathrm{b}}\), and \(0\) for \(a \neq b\), so the angle between the sides stays right.

2. The strain of a fibre.

  1. By (2.78), \(\vek n^\T\ten\varepsilon\,\vek n = \vek n\cdot(\ten\varepsilon\,\vek n)\). The fibre \(\vek n\) with the components \(\bar n_1\) and \(\bar n_3\) and the range from \(\varepsilon_3\) to \(\varepsilon_1\) are shown in figure 2.191 on the right. We repeat steps 4 to 10 of the proof of corollary 2.85 with \(\ten\varepsilon\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{a}}\) in place of \(\ten\sigma\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}\). There we used only that \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}} = \vek n\cdot(\ten\sigma\,\vek n)\) and that \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n_{\mathrm{a}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{a}}\vek n_{\mathrm{a}}\), which holds for \(\ten\varepsilon\) in the same way.

Source: SADD (2.4.3) (PDF p. 49), CM §3.4.6 (PDF p. 138). The proof is not from the sources.

What does this mean for the material? By item 2, within the small strain tensor (up to an error of \(4{,}5\,m^2\)), the strain of the fibres through \(P\) is largest in the direction \(\vek n_1\) and smallest in the direction \(\vek n_3\), and all others lie between them. By item 1 the shear strains between the principal directions of strain are zero. A shear strain measures the change of the right angle between two fibres (definition 1.3 and section 2.9.6). A cube with its edges along the principal directions of strain therefore stays, within the linear theory, a box with right angles, only its edges get longer or shorter (SADD, PDF p. 49). On the front face of the sheared cube this is shown by the square along the diagonals, which stays nearly a rectangle, longer along \(AC\) and shorter along \(BD\) in figure 2.121. The right angles are kept only up to terms with squares of the partial derivatives, as the example below shows (cosine \(5 \cdot 10^{-7}\)). With this we have proved the claim that in section 1.1 we stated from a source: for every small deformation there are three mutually perpendicular directions in which there is no shear, only stretching or shortening. For large deformations the Green-Lagrange strain tensor (definition 1.118) takes the place of \(\ten\varepsilon\). It is symmetric too, so it also has three perpendicular principal directions of strain (CM §3.4.6, PDF p. 138).

The sheared cube. Let us return to the steel cube with the edge \(h = 10\) mm \(= 10\,000\) µm of section 1.6.2. It is glued to a table by its bottom face, and its top face is pushed sideways by \(s = 10\) µm (figure 1.95). The origin is at the corner \(A\), the axis \(x\) points in the direction of the push, the axis \(z\) up, and the displacement field is \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = 0{,}001\,z\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\) ((1.17)). Of the nine partial derivatives only \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial z = 0{,}001\) is nonzero, so by (1.59)

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zx}} = \tfrac12\left(\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial z} + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial x}\right) = \tfrac12\,(0{,}001 + 0) = 0{,}0005 ,\]

and all other components are zero. This is the same form of the matrix as in pure shear, only with the number \(0{,}0005\) in place of \(\tau_0\). The computation of the previous section therefore gives the principal strains \(\varepsilon_1 = 0{,}0005\), \(\varepsilon_2 = 0\) and \(\varepsilon_3 = -0{,}0005\) in the directions \(\vek n_1 = (1, 0, 1)/\sqrt2\), \(\vek n_2 = \vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\vek n_3 = (-1, 0, 1)/\sqrt2\). The first is the direction of the diagonal \(AC\), the third the direction of the diagonal \(BD\). The number \(0{,}0005\) is \(s/(2h) = 10/(2 \cdot 10\,000)\), the strain of the diagonals that we computed from the lengths in section 1.6.2. There we got exactly \(+5{,}001 \cdot 10^{-4}\) and \(-4{,}999 \cdot 10^{-4}\), and the difference comes from the terms with the square of the shift, which the small strain tensor neglects. Now we know more than then. No short fibre through any point of the cube has a strain larger than about \(5 \cdot 10^{-4}\) or smaller than about \(-5 \cdot 10^{-4}\), and the fibres in the direction of the axis \(y\) keep their length. So does the vertical edge, which has the components \(1/\sqrt2\) and \(1/\sqrt2\) in the principal axes: by item 2 of the corollary its strain is \(0{,}0005 \cdot \tfrac12 - 0{,}0005 \cdot \tfrac12 = 0\).

What about the right angles? In section 2.9.6 we computed from the exact vectors of section 1.6.2 that after loading the cosine of the angle between the edges \(AB\) and \(AD\) is \(0{,}0009999995\), while the cosine of the angle between the diagonals \(AC\) and \(BD\) is only \(5 \cdot 10^{-7}\), two thousand times less. So the right angle between the edges changes, while the right angle between the principal directions of strain stays practically a right angle, as item 1 of corollary 2.92 says. Even this small remainder comes only from the terms with the square of the shift, which the small strain tensor leaves out. Figure 2.121 of section 2.9.5 shows this on two small squares on the front face of the cube: the square along the axes becomes a parallelogram, the square along the diagonals stays nearly a rectangle.

The hanging bar. A steel bar of length \(L = 10\) m hangs under its own weight. As in section 1.7.1, it is fixed only at the centre \(O\) of its top face, and the top face is pulled, in an idealized way, by an evenly distributed force. The axis \(x\) points from \(O\) vertically down. The point \(P\) (figure 1.104) lies at the depth \(x_{\mathrm{P}} = 5\) m, \(y_{\mathrm{P}} = 4\) mm from the axis, in the plane \(z = 0\). From the partial derivatives at \(P\) computed in section 1.7.2 we get \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6}\), the fraction by which the small piece of the bar at \(P\) gets longer in height, and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = -5{,}856 \cdot 10^{-7}\), the fraction by which it gets narrower in width. The shear components are zero. The partial derivatives \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial y\) and \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial x\) are equal in size and opposite in sign, so \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \tfrac12\,(-4{,}685 \cdot 10^{-10} + 4{,}685 \cdot 10^{-10}) = 0\), and for \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}\) both derivatives are zero at \(P\). The matrix is already diagonal, so the principal directions of strain are the axes. The principal strain \(\varepsilon_1 = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6}\) is along the bar, and the principal strains \(\varepsilon_2 = \varepsilon_3 = -5{,}856 \cdot 10^{-7}\) are equal, so every direction perpendicular to the bar is a principal direction of strain, as for the stress in a bar. The ratio \(\varepsilon_2/\varepsilon_1 = -0{,}3\) is the thinning of steel of section 1.7.1.

The stress at the same point has a single nonzero component, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \rho g\,(L - x_{\mathrm{P}}) = 7800\ \text{kg/m}^3 \cdot 9{,}81\ \text{m/s}^2 \cdot 5\ \text{m} = 0{,}3826\) MPa (CM Example 7.3.2, PDF p. 305, with differently placed axes). The principal stresses are \(0{,}3826\) MPa, \(0\) and \(0\), in the same directions as the principal strains. It is similar for the cube. In pure shear the principal directions of stress are the diagonals \(AC\) and \(BD\) and the axis \(y\), and in the sheared cube the same directions are the principal directions of strain. What stress causes such a shift we will see in chapter 3. In the hanging bar the stress and the strain have the same principal directions, although the principal stresses across the bar are zero and the principal strains are not, because the bar gets thinner without anything pressing on it from the side (figure 1.107). For materials that behave the same in all directions and in which the relation between stress and strain is linear, as for steel below the yield stress (figure 2.18), the stress and the strain always have the same principal directions (SADD §4.2, PDF p. 94). We will show this in chapter 3, where we will relate stress and strain.

Let us sum up how we reached the answer. We wrote the normal stress on the face with the normal \(\vek n\) as \(\vek n^\T\ten\sigma\,\vek n\). The expression is a polynomial in the components of the normal, so on the closed and bounded set of unit vectors it attains a largest value. The parabola argument showed that there is no shear on the face with the largest normal stress, and repeating this on a smaller set, among the unit vectors perpendicular to the first direction, gave the second principal direction. The third is perpendicular to both, and because the matrix is symmetric, it maps the third to a multiple of it. In the principal axes the normal stress is a weighted average of the principal stresses, so the largest is \(\sigma_1\) and the smallest \(\sigma_3\). We compute them with the characteristic equation, whose coefficients, the invariants, are the same in all axes. For strains everything holds in the same way, because the strain of a fibre is also an expression \(\vek n^\T\ten\varepsilon\,\vek n\) with a symmetric matrix. Now we know on which face the normal stress is largest. But on which face is the shear stress largest? In the bar it was on the face at \(45^\circ\) to the cross section (lemma 2.19), and in section 2.12 we will show that in general, too, it is determined by the principal stresses.
