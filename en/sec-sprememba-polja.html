Chapter 1 Strain

1.15 The change of a field over a short step

Now we return to the fibre of section 1.7.3 and finish the work there. Let us recall where we stopped. A fibre between the points \(P\) and \(Q\) of a body changes under loading only by the difference of the displacements of its ends, \(\vek u(Q) - \vek u(P)\) ((1.15)). Let \(P = (x_{\mathrm{P}}, y_{\mathrm{P}}, z_{\mathrm{P}})\) and \(Q = (x_{\mathrm{P}} + \Delta x,\ y_{\mathrm{P}} + \Delta y,\ z_{\mathrm{P}} + \Delta z)\). We wrote the component \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) of the difference of displacements as the sum of three products of the partial derivatives at the point \(P\) with the components of the step \(\Delta x\), \(\Delta y\), \(\Delta z\), and a remainder (ostanek) \(R_{\mathrm{x}}\), which is everything that is left over:

\[u_{\mathrm{x}}(Q) - u_{\mathrm{x}}(P) = \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial x}(P)\,\Delta x + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial y}(P)\,\Delta y + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial z}(P)\,\Delta z + R_{\mathrm{x}}\]

((1.24)). For the hanging bar we computed the remainder exactly. It was a number times the square of the length of the fibre, so the remainder divided by the length of the fibre went to zero as we shortened the fibre. For a general field two facts were missing. The first is the mean value theorem 1.84 of section 1.14: if a function of one variable is continuous on a closed interval and differentiable at every point between its end points, its change between the end points equals the derivative at some point in between times the length of the interval. The second is an assumption about the field, which we can now state precisely: the partial derivatives exist at all points near \(P\) and are continuous at \(P\) (definition 1.67), so at points close enough to \(P\) they differ only a little from those at \(P\).

Figure 1.290 shows the change of the field as one length.

The horizontal line with ticks at its ends at the top is the change of the field \(f(Q) - f(P)\), drawn as one length. Below it we split it into pieces. The three blue lines are the products of the split: the first is \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(P)\,\Delta x\), the second \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(P)\,\Delta y\) and the third \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}(P)\,\Delta z\). They are drawn one after another and each one step higher than the previous one, so that they do not overlap. The orange line at the end is the remainder \(R\), which is all that the three products do not explain. The remainder is drawn in the figure much larger than it really is, so that it can be seen. The grey dashed lines join the end of each line to the start of the next one, and the start of the first blue line and the end of the orange line to the top line, so we see that the pieces together give the whole length. For a fibre of the hanging bar of length 1 mm the remainder is about \(10^{-4}\) of the part with the partial derivatives, as the table below shows.

The goal of this section is to put both facts together into a theorem: for every field with such partial derivatives the remainder is small compared with the length of the step. Then three partial derivatives at the point \(P\) will determine how the field changes over a step in any direction, and the fibre in every direction will be determined by nine numbers: three partial derivatives for each of the three components of the displacement. The route is this. First we say precisely what "small compared with the length of the step" means. Then we carry the mean value theorem over to a step along one axis and use it to prove the theorem. We test the theorem on the hanging bar, where we know the remainder, and on a field with kinks, where the assumptions fail and so does the conclusion. From this we get the assumption that we will make for displacement fields from now on, and we say what it means physically and where it fails. Finally we use the theorem for all three components of the displacement and for a fibre in any direction. Strain in space will be built on this result.

1.15.1 When the remainder is small

What should it mean that the remainder is small? It is not enough that it goes to zero as we shorten the step. The remainder is the difference

\[R_{\mathrm{x}} = \big(u_{\mathrm{x}}(Q) - u_{\mathrm{x}}(P)\big) - \Big(\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial x}(P)\,\Delta x + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial y}(P)\,\Delta y + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial z}(P)\,\Delta z\Big) .\]

If \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) is continuous at \(P\), the first bracket already goes to zero (definition 1.67), and so does the second, because every component of the step goes to zero. So the remainder of every such field goes to zero, and that tells us nothing. What matters is how fast it goes to zero compared with the step. The three products in the second bracket are proportional to the length of the step: for a step half as long in the same direction they are half as large. If the remainder were also proportional to the length of the step, it would be comparable with them for every step, and the sum of the three products would not be a good approximation of the change, not even for a very short step. So the remainder must shrink faster than the step: divided by the length of the step, it must go to zero. This is exactly what we need for strain, which is a difference of displacements divided by the length of the fibre. For the hanging bar we saw in section 1.7.3 how this works: for a fibre ten times shorter the part with the partial derivatives was ten times smaller, but the remainder a hundred times smaller. The numbers for the component \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) of the fibre from the point \(P\) in the direction \(\vek n = (\tfrac13, \tfrac23, \tfrac23)\), the first two rows from section 1.7.3, the third by the same proportionality:

Let us draw the table. The horizontal axis is the fibre length \(h\) in millimetres with the marks \(0{,}01\), \(0{,}1\) and \(1\) at equal spacing. The spacing is equal because the axis is logarithmic: each step means a ten times longer fibre. The vertical axis is the size in metres, also logarithmic, with the marks from \(10^{-18}\) to \(10^{-9}\). The three blue dots are the values of the second column of the table (\(6{,}504 \cdot 10^{-12}\), \(6{,}504 \cdot 10^{-11}\) and \(6{,}504 \cdot 10^{-10}\) m). They are joined by a blue line with the label \(\approx h\): for a ten times longer fibre the value is ten times larger. The three orange dots are the sizes of the remainders from the third column (\(7{,}374 \cdot 10^{-18}\), \(7{,}374 \cdot 10^{-16}\) and \(7{,}374 \cdot 10^{-14}\) m), and the orange line with the label \(\approx h^2\) is a hundred times larger for a ten times longer fibre, so it is twice as steep. The remainder is negative in the table, and its size is drawn. Two dimension lines show how many times the blue value is larger than the orange one, at \(1\) mm and at \(0{,}01\) mm.

On a logarithmic axis the length of a dimension line is not the difference of the values, but the number of decades between them. At the 1 mm fibre the blue value is about \(9 \cdot 10^{3}\) times larger than the orange one, which is almost four decades. Since the blue part falls by a factor of ten for a fibre ten times shorter, and the orange remainder by a factor of a hundred, the ratio grows tenfold for every fibre ten times shorter and at the fibre of \(0{,}01\) mm is about \(9 \cdot 10^{5}\), which is almost six decades.

Let us write this precisely for any function. Let \(f\) be a function of three variables, that is, a function whose input is the three coordinates of a point and whose output is one number, for example one of the components of the displacement. A function that assigns a number or a vector to every point of some part of space is also called a field (polje), just as we called the rule that assigns to every point of a body its displacement vector the displacement field (definition 1.23). The step from \(P = (x_{\mathrm{P}}, y_{\mathrm{P}}, z_{\mathrm{P}})\) to \(Q = (x_{\mathrm{P}} + \Delta x,\ y_{\mathrm{P}} + \Delta y,\ z_{\mathrm{P}} + \Delta z)\) has the length

\[|PQ| = \sqrt{\Delta x^2 + \Delta y^2 + \Delta z^2} ,\]

the distance between the points from definition 1.17. The remainder \(R(Q)\) is the number that is left when we subtract from the change \(f(Q) - f(P)\) the three products of the partial derivatives at \(P\) with the components of the step. It depends on the point \(Q\), that is, on the three numbers \(\Delta x\), \(\Delta y\), \(\Delta z\).

We would like to say that the quotient \(R(Q)/|PQ|\) goes to zero as \(Q\) goes to \(P\). But we defined the limit only for functions of one number \(h\) (definition 1.5), while the point \(Q\) can approach \(P\) from all directions. So we write the requirement directly with an error \(\eta\) and a band \(\delta\), as in the definition of the limit: for every \(\eta > 0\) there is a \(\delta > 0\) such that

\[|R(Q)| \le \eta\,|PQ| \quad \text{for every point } Q \text{ with } 0 < |PQ| < \delta . \tag{1.32}\]

The number \(\eta\) is the allowed error per unit length of the step. With \(\eta = 10^{-10}\) and a step of length 1 mm the remainder may be at most \(10^{-10} \cdot 10^{-3}\ \text{m} = 10^{-13}\) m. We divide the inequality by the positive number \(|PQ|\), which does not reverse it, and get \(|R(Q)|/|PQ| \le \eta\). So the quotient differs from zero by at most \(\eta\) at every point \(Q\) whose distance from \(P\) is less than \(\delta\) but which is not \(P\) itself. This is the pattern of the definition of the limit, where \(g(h)\) differed from \(d\) by less than \(\eta\) for every \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta\) (figure 1.292 below shows the requirement). That we write \(\le\) instead of \(<\) does not matter: if we satisfy the requirement with the error \(\eta/2\), then \(|R(Q)|/|PQ| \le \eta/2 < \eta\).

Figure 1.292 shows what the requirement means. The example is invented, so the figure is schematic. In the left part there are the axes: the horizontal one for the length of the step \(|PQ|\) and the vertical one for the size of the remainder \(|R|\). For every point \(Q\) near \(P\) let us draw a dot whose horizontal coordinate is the length of the step \(|PQ|\) and whose vertical coordinate is the size of the remainder \(|R(Q)|\). The line \(\eta_1\,|PQ|\) passes through the origin, and the part of the plane below it gets lower the closer we are to the origin. The requirement says that the dots of all points \(Q\) closer to \(P\) than \(\delta_1\) lie below this line. A smaller error \(\eta_2\) gives a flatter line. The allowed error is smaller, so as a rule we must shorten the step more, and \(\delta_2\) is smaller. The two highest dots to the right of \(\delta_1\) are above the line \(\eta_1\,|PQ|\), because the requirement holds only for \(|PQ| < \delta_1\). That is why we need the bound \(\delta_1\) at all. The right half of the figure shows the same as the quotient \(|R(Q)|/|PQ|\), enlarged three times vertically. It has its own axes and the same dots, among them again the two highest. The bounds are \(\delta_1\) and \(\delta_2\), and instead of lines there are the horizontal lines \(\eta_1\) and \(\eta_2\). The line through the origin has become horizontal, because the quotient is the size divided by \(|PQ|\). The requirement says the same in both parts: the dots are below the bound \(\eta_1\) when \(|PQ| < \delta_1\), and below the bound \(\eta_2\) when \(|PQ| < \delta_2\).

1.15.2 A step along one axis

Let us draw the step. In the top left corner are the axes: \(x\) points down, \(y\) to the right and \(z\) towards the reader, as for the hanging bar. The point \(P\) is the start of the step and the point \(Q\) its end. We do not go from \(P\) to \(Q\) straight, but along the axes. First from \(P\) we go down by \(\Delta x\) to the point \(A\), then to the right by \(\Delta y\) to the point \(B\), and finally towards the reader by \(\Delta z\) to \(Q\). The blackwhite arrow for \(\Delta y\) has a short gap, because the grey arrow from \(P\) to \(Q\), which is the whole step, runs in front of it. The grey dashed edges are four edges of the box whose opposite corners are \(P\) and \(Q\), and they show that \(Q\) is opposite \(P\) in space. The diagonal of the box, the grey arrow \(PQ\), is longer than each of its sides \(\Delta x\), \(\Delta y\) and \(\Delta z\), which we will need in step 4 of the proof below. On each of the three steps there is one blue dot: \(T_{\mathrm{x}}\) on the first, \(T_{\mathrm{y}}\) on the second and \(T_{\mathrm{z}}\) on the third. By the mean value theorem 1.84 there is on each step a point where the partial derivative in the direction of the step equals the change of the field divided by the component of the step. Where on the step it lies, the theorem does not say, so the dots are drawn somewhere in between, not in the middle.

We will estimate the remainder as in section 1.7.3: we split the step from \(P\) to \(Q\) into three steps along the axes. Each changes only one coordinate, so along it the field is a function of one variable, for which the mean value theorem holds. We write a step with a point to which we add a vector (section 1.5.3). For a point \(S = (x_{\mathrm{S}}, y_{\mathrm{S}}, z_{\mathrm{S}})\) and a number \(t\),

\[S + t\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} = (x_{\mathrm{S}},\ y_{\mathrm{S}} + t,\ z_{\mathrm{S}})\]

is the point we reach from \(S\) by a step \(t\) in the direction of the axis \(y\), where \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} = (0, 1, 0)\) is the unit vector of the axis \(y\) (definition 1.20). For \(t < 0\) we step the opposite way.

What does the mean value theorem say about such a step? The values of the field at the points of the step are described by the function \(g(t) = f(S + t\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}})\). Its input is one number \(t\), how far we have stepped from \(S\) in the direction of the axis \(y\), and its output is the value of the field at the point we have reached. Along the step only the coordinate \(y\) changes, so the derivative of the function \(g\) is the partial derivative of \(f\) with respect to \(y\). The graph of the function \(g\) is shown in figure 1.294.

Let us draw the graph. The axes show \(t\), the step in the direction of the axis \(y\), horizontally and the value \(g\) vertically. The blackwhite curve is the graph of the function \(g\) for \(t\) from \(0\) to \(\Delta y\). Its end points are blackwhite dots: \(g(0) = f(S)\) at \(t = 0\) and \(g(\Delta y) = f(S + \Delta y\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}})\) at \(t = \Delta y\), with a vertical grey dashed line to the axis. The grey dashed line passes through both end points and is extended a little beyond them so that its direction can be seen. Its slope is the change divided by \(\Delta y\). The blue tangent is parallel to it and touches the graph at the blue dot at \(\xi\). For the graph in the figure, \(g(t) = 0{,}3 + 0{,}2\,t + 0{,}1\,t^3\) up to \(\Delta y = 2{,}8\), the slope of the line is \(0{,}984\) and \(\xi \approx 1{,}62\). The point is unique, because \(g'(t) = 0{,}2 + 0{,}3\,t^2\) increases, so the slope \(0{,}984\) occurs only once.

The mean value theorem therefore says that the change of the field over the step equals the partial derivative with respect to \(y\) at some point of the step times the component of the step \(\Delta y\). The next lemma states this precisely.

Let us draw the intervals from the proof, each on its own axis \(t\). On the top axis is the closed interval \(I\) from \(0\) to \(\Delta y = 4\) with filled dots at the end points, because the end points belong to it. The blue dot is the chosen \(t\) from \(I\), near the left end point. The blue measure above it is the interval \((t - r_{\mathrm{t}},\ t + r_{\mathrm{t}})\) in which the function \(g\) is defined. For \(r_{\mathrm{t}} = 1{,}2\) it reaches beyond the left end point, where \(g\) need not be defined. On the second axis the part of \(I\) that lies inside this interval is blue, the numbers from \(0\) to \(t + r_{\mathrm{t}}\). On the third axis is the interval without its end points, which the empty circles show. On the fourth axis \(\Delta y = -3 < 0\), so \(I\) lies to the left of \(0\) and its left end point is \(\Delta y\).

Lemma 1.87 Change over a step along an axis (sprememba na koraku vzdolž osi)

Let \(f\) be a function of three variables, \(S = (x_{\mathrm{S}}, y_{\mathrm{S}}, z_{\mathrm{S}})\) a point and \(\Delta y \neq 0\) a number. Suppose that the partial derivative \(\partial f/\partial y\) exists at every point \(S + t\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} = (x_{\mathrm{S}},\ y_{\mathrm{S}} + t,\ z_{\mathrm{S}})\) with \(t\) in the closed interval with the end points \(0\) and \(\Delta y\), that is, in \([0, \Delta y]\) for \(\Delta y > 0\) and in \([\Delta y, 0]\) for \(\Delta y < 0\). Then there is a number \(\xi\) strictly between \(0\) and \(\Delta y\) such that

\[f(S + \Delta y\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}) - f(S) = \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(S + \xi\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}})\,\Delta y . \tag{1.33}\]

The same holds for a step \(\Delta x \neq 0\) in the direction of the axis \(x\) with the partial derivative with respect to \(x\), and for a step \(\Delta z \neq 0\) in the direction of the axis \(z\) with the partial derivative with respect to \(z\).

Proof

We must show that a number \(\xi\) with this property exists. We denote the closed interval with the end points \(0\) and \(\Delta y\) by \(I\).

  1. For every \(t\) for which the point \(S + t\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) is in the domain of the function \(f\), let \(g(t) = f(S + t\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}) = f(x_{\mathrm{S}},\ y_{\mathrm{S}} + t,\ z_{\mathrm{S}})\). This is a function of one variable.
  2. Let \(t\) be in \(I\). By assumption the partial derivative \(\partial f/\partial y\) exists at the point \(S + t\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\). By the definition of the partial derivative 1.25, \(f\) is therefore defined at all points whose distance from this point is less than some number \(r_{\mathrm{t}} > 0\). Among them are the points \(S + (t + s)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) with \(|s| < r_{\mathrm{t}}\), since their distance from \(S + t\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) is \(|s|\). So the function \(g\) is defined at all numbers \(t + s\) with \(|s| < r_{\mathrm{t}}\).
  3. The difference quotient of the function \(g\) at \(t\) is
    \[\frac{g(t + s) - g(t)}{s} = \frac{f(x_{\mathrm{S}},\ y_{\mathrm{S}} + t + s,\ z_{\mathrm{S}}) - f(x_{\mathrm{S}},\ y_{\mathrm{S}} + t,\ z_{\mathrm{S}})}{s} .\]
    This is the quotient from the second line of (1.19) at the point \(S + t\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\), except that we write the change as \(s\) instead of \(\Delta y\). So its limit as \(s\) goes to zero exists and is the partial derivative at this point. By the definition of the derivative 1.9, \(g\) is differentiable at \(t\) and
    \[g'(t) = \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(S + t\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}) .\]
  4. The mean value theorem requires \(g\) to be continuous on the whole interval \(I\), not only differentiable between the end points, so we check this. Let \(t\) be in \(I\). By step 2, \(g\) is defined on the open interval \((t - r_{\mathrm{t}}, t + r_{\mathrm{t}})\), and by step 3 it is differentiable at \(t\). By lemma 1.79, that a differentiable function is continuous, it is therefore continuous at \(t\) as a function on this interval. The condition of the definition of continuity 1.58 for the function on the set \(I\) asks for less, since we check it only at the numbers in \(I\), and those among them whose distance from \(t\) is less than \(r_{\mathrm{t}}\) all lie in the interval \((t - r_{\mathrm{t}}, t + r_{\mathrm{t}})\). If we reduce the band \(\delta\) from the continuity on this interval to \(r_{\mathrm{t}}\) where needed, the condition also holds on \(I\). So \(g\) is continuous at every point of \(I\), that is, continuous on \(I\).
  5. By step 3, \(g\) is differentiable at every point strictly between the end points of the interval \(I\).
  6. Let \(\Delta y > 0\), so \(I = [0, \Delta y]\). By the mean value theorem 1.84 there is a \(\xi\) strictly between \(0\) and \(\Delta y\) such that \(g(\Delta y) - g(0) = g'(\xi)\,(\Delta y - 0) = g'(\xi)\,\Delta y\).
  7. Let \(\Delta y < 0\), so \(I = [\Delta y, 0]\). By the same theorem there is a \(\xi\) strictly between \(\Delta y\) and \(0\) such that \(g(0) - g(\Delta y) = g'(\xi)\,(0 - \Delta y)\). We multiply both sides by \(-1\) and get \(g(\Delta y) - g(0) = g'(\xi)\,\Delta y\), the same equality as in step 6.
  8. Into the equality of step 6 or 7 we put \(g(\Delta y) = f(S + \Delta y\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}})\) and \(g(0) = f(S)\) and \(g'(\xi)\) from step 3. We get (1.33).
  9. For a step in the direction of the axis \(x\) we take \(g(t) = f(x_{\mathrm{S}} + t,\ y_{\mathrm{S}},\ z_{\mathrm{S}})\), for a step in the direction of the axis \(z\) we take \(g(t) = f(x_{\mathrm{S}},\ y_{\mathrm{S}},\ z_{\mathrm{S}} + t)\), and we repeat steps 1 to 8 with the first or the third line of (1.19).

Source: LEBL2 Prop. 8.4.6 (PDF p. 49) uses the mean value theorem on a step along one axis in the same way. As a separate statement the lemma is not from the source.

The lemma does not say where on the step the point \(S + \xi\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) lies, only that it exists and that it lies on the step. For us this will be enough, because every point of a short step will be close to the point \(P\).

1.15.3 The theorem on the change of a field

Now we can write the theorem. Before it, let us state the idea of the proof. We go from \(P\) to \(Q\) in three steps along the axes, through the points \(A = P + \Delta x\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(B = A + \Delta y\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\), as in section 1.7.3 (figure 1.293).

By lemma 1.87 on the change over a step along an axis, the change of the field over the first step equals the partial derivative \(\partial f/\partial x\) at some point \(T_{\mathrm{x}}\) of this step times \(\Delta x\), and likewise over the second and third step with the points \(T_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(T_{\mathrm{z}}\). So three differences are left in the remainder, each multiplied by a component of the step:

\[\begin{aligned} R(Q) &= \Big(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(T_{\mathrm{x}}) - \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(P)\Big)\Delta x + \Big(\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(T_{\mathrm{y}}) - \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(P)\Big)\Delta y \\ &\quad + \Big(\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}(T_{\mathrm{z}}) - \frac{\partial f}{\partial z}(P)\Big)\Delta z . \end{aligned}\]

In the brackets is the partial derivative at the intermediate point minus the partial derivative at \(P\). We will bound each bracket by \(\eta/3\), because we will add three of them. The intermediate points lie on the path from \(P\) to \(Q\), so they are not farther from \(P\) than \(Q\). For a short step they are close to \(P\), and the continuity of the partial derivatives at \(P\) makes the differences small. Each difference is multiplied by a component of the step, which is not longer than the step, so the remainder is small compared with the step.

In the proof of the theorem, steps 5 and 11 use figure 1.296 for a step in the plane \(z = z_{\mathrm{P}}\), where \(\Delta z = 0\) and the path consists of two steps.

Let us draw the step in the plane. The axes in the corner show \(x\) down and \(y\) to the right. The point \(P\) is the start, the point \(Q\) the end of the step. The example has \(\Delta x = 1{,}8\) and \(\Delta y = 2{,}4\), so \(|PQ| = \sqrt{1{,}8^2 + 2{,}4^2} = 3\). The path has two steps: first down by \(\Delta x\) to the point \(A\), then to the right by \(\Delta y\) to \(Q\). The grey dashed circle around \(P\) has the radius \(|PQ|\), which is drawn as a grey dimension line from \(P\) towards the upper right edge. The path stays in the disc bounded by this circle, because no piece of it is farther from \(P\) than \(Q\), as step 5 of the proof below shows. On the first step is the blue dot \(T_{\mathrm{x}}\), on the second the blue dot \(T_{\mathrm{y}}\). The grey dashed segment from \(P\) to \(T_{\mathrm{y}}\) has the length \(\sqrt{1{,}8^2 + 1{,}4^2} \approx 2{,}28\), which is less than \(|PQ| = 3\). The blue circle has the larger radius \(\delta = 3{,}6\), drawn as a blue dimension line. It is larger because \(|PQ| < \delta\) must hold. At the points inside this circle the partial derivatives differ from those at \(P\) by less than \(\eta/3\). The points \(T_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(T_{\mathrm{y}}\) are therefore in it.

Let us draw the bands. The top line is the radius \(r\), the next is \(\delta_{\mathrm{x}}\) and the third is \(\delta_{\mathrm{z}}\). The fourth is \(\delta_{\mathrm{y}}\), the shortest. The blue line is the smallest of the four numbers, so it is as long as \(\delta_{\mathrm{y}}\), as the grey dashed line shows. The bottom line is the step \(|PQ| = 1{,}4\), which is shorter than \(\delta\).

Let us draw the change by steps. The top line is the change of the field \(f(Q) - f(P)\) as one length, in made-up numbers \(5{,}0\). Below it are the three steps one after another, each one stair lower: the first \(f(A) - f(P)\), the second \(f(B) - f(A)\) and the third \(f(Q) - f(B)\), with lengths \(2{,}5\), \(1{,}5\) and \(1{,}0\). The dashed lines show that together they give the whole length. The lower part shows the same three steps, each along its axis, starting at the same height. The blackwhite line is the partial derivative at the intermediate point times the component of the step, as long as the step above. The blue line below it is the partial derivative at \(P\) times the same component, in the example \(2{,}2\), \(1{,}4\) and \(0{,}8\). The orange piece at the end of the blue line is the difference between them: \(0{,}3\), \(0{,}1\) and \(0{,}2\). The orange segments placed one after another are the sum of these differences, the remainder \(R = 0{,}6\).

Theorem 1.88 Change of a field over a short step (sprememba polja na kratkem koraku)

Let \(f\) be a function of three variables, \(P = (x_{\mathrm{P}}, y_{\mathrm{P}}, z_{\mathrm{P}})\) a point and \(r > 0\) a number such that \(f\) is defined at all points whose distance from \(P\) is less than \(r\). Suppose that at each of these points all three partial derivatives \(\partial f/\partial x\), \(\partial f/\partial y\), \(\partial f/\partial z\) exist (definition 1.25) and that all three are continuous at the point \(P\) (definition 1.67). For a point \(Q = (x_{\mathrm{P}} + \Delta x,\ y_{\mathrm{P}} + \Delta y,\ z_{\mathrm{P}} + \Delta z)\) with \(|PQ| < r\) let the remainder \(R(Q)\) be the number determined by the equality

\[f(Q) - f(P) = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(P)\,\Delta x + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(P)\,\Delta y + \frac{\partial f}{\partial z}(P)\,\Delta z + R(Q) . \tag{1.34}\]

Then for every \(\eta > 0\) there is a \(\delta\) with \(0 < \delta \le r\) such that \(|R(Q)| \le \eta\,|PQ|\) for every point \(Q\) with \(0 < |PQ| < \delta\).

Proof

We must show: for every \(\eta > 0\) there is a \(\delta\) with \(0 < \delta \le r\) such that \(|R(Q)| \le \eta\,|PQ|\) for every point \(Q\) with \(0 < |PQ| < \delta\).

  1. We choose any error \(\eta > 0\). For it we must find a band \(\delta\).
  2. The partial derivative \(\partial f/\partial x\) is a function that is defined at all points whose distance from \(P\) is less than \(r\), and it is continuous at \(P\). We write the definition of continuity 1.67 for it with the error \(\eta/3\): there is a \(\delta_{\mathrm{x}} > 0\) such that
    \[\Big|\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(T) - \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(P)\Big| < \frac{\eta}{3}\]
    for every point \(T\) whose distance from \(P\) is less than \(r\) and less than \(\delta_{\mathrm{x}}\). We take a third of the error because in step 12 we will add three such errors. In the same way we get a band \(\delta_{\mathrm{y}}\) for \(\partial f/\partial y\) and a band \(\delta_{\mathrm{z}}\) for \(\partial f/\partial z\).
  3. As the band \(\delta\) we take the smallest of the four positive numbers \(r\), \(\delta_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\delta_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(\delta_{\mathrm{z}}\), which is positive and not larger than \(r\). Let \(Q = (x_{\mathrm{P}} + \Delta x,\ y_{\mathrm{P}} + \Delta y,\ z_{\mathrm{P}} + \Delta z)\) be any point with \(0 < |PQ| < \delta\). We must show that \(|R(Q)| \le \eta\,|PQ|\).
  4. No component of the step is longer than the step, since the step \(PQ\) is the diagonal of the box and the sides are the components. We have \(\Delta x^2 \le \Delta x^2 + \Delta y^2 + \Delta z^2 = |PQ|^2\), so by lemma 1.28, that a larger nonnegative number has a larger square, \(|\Delta x| \le |PQ|\). Likewise \(|\Delta y| \le |PQ|\) and \(|\Delta z| \le |PQ|\).
  5. We go from \(P\) to \(Q\) in three steps along the axes (figure 1.296), through the points \(A = P + \Delta x\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(B = A + \Delta y\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\), and the last step takes us to \(Q = B + \Delta z\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\). No point of this path is farther from \(P\) than \(Q\). The point \(P + t\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) with \(t\) between \(0\) and \(\Delta x\) has the distance \(|t| \le |\Delta x| \le |PQ|\) from \(P\). The point \(A + t\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) with \(t\) between \(0\) and \(\Delta y\) has the distance \(\sqrt{\Delta x^2 + t^2}\) from \(P\). Since \(|t| \le |\Delta y|\), by the same lemma \(t^2 \le \Delta y^2\), so \(\Delta x^2 + t^2 \le |PQ|^2\) and the distance is at most \(|PQ|\). Likewise the point \(B + t\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) with \(t\) between \(0\) and \(\Delta z\) has the distance \(\sqrt{\Delta x^2 + \Delta y^2 + t^2} \le |PQ|\) from \(P\). Since \(|PQ| < \delta \le r\), all points of the path are at a distance less than \(r\) and less than \(\delta\) from \(P\). At them \(f\) is defined and all three partial derivatives exist.
  6. Equality (1.22) holds for every function defined at the points \(P\), \(A\), \(B\) and \(Q\). For \(f\) it reads
    \[f(Q) - f(P) = \big(f(A) - f(P)\big) + \big(f(B) - f(A)\big) + \big(f(Q) - f(B)\big) .\]
  7. The first bracket is the change over the step \(\Delta x\) in the direction of the axis \(x\) from the point \(P\). Let \(\Delta x \neq 0\). By step 5 the partial derivative with respect to \(x\) exists at every point \(P + t\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) with \(t\) in the closed interval with the end points \(0\) and \(\Delta x\). By lemma 1.87 on the change over a step along an axis there is therefore a number \(\xi_{\mathrm{x}}\) strictly between \(0\) and \(\Delta x\) such that for the point \(T_{\mathrm{x}} = P + \xi_{\mathrm{x}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\)
    \[f(A) - f(P) = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(T_{\mathrm{x}})\,\Delta x .\]
    If \(\Delta x = 0\), then \(A = P\) and both sides are zero, so the equality holds with \(T_{\mathrm{x}} = P\).
  8. In the same way we get the second bracket, the step \(\Delta y\) from the point \(A\), and the third, the step \(\Delta z\) from the point \(B\). There are points \(T_{\mathrm{y}} = A + \xi_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(T_{\mathrm{z}} = B + \xi_{\mathrm{z}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) on the path such that
    \[f(B) - f(A) = \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(T_{\mathrm{y}})\,\Delta y , \qquad f(Q) - f(B) = \frac{\partial f}{\partial z}(T_{\mathrm{z}})\,\Delta z .\]
  9. From (1.34) we express the remainder by moving the three products to the left side:
    \[R(Q) = f(Q) - f(P) - \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(P)\,\Delta x - \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(P)\,\Delta y - \frac{\partial f}{\partial z}(P)\,\Delta z .\]
  10. In place of \(f(Q) - f(P)\) we put the sum of step 6, with the brackets written as in steps 7 and 8, and we group the terms with the same component of the step:
    \[\begin{aligned} R(Q) &= \Big(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(T_{\mathrm{x}}) - \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(P)\Big)\Delta x + \Big(\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(T_{\mathrm{y}}) - \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(P)\Big)\Delta y \\ &\quad + \Big(\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}(T_{\mathrm{z}}) - \frac{\partial f}{\partial z}(P)\Big)\Delta z . \end{aligned}\]
  11. The points \(T_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(T_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(T_{\mathrm{z}}\) lie on the path, so by step 5 their distance from \(P\) is less than \(r\) and less than \(\delta\). By step 3 the band \(\delta\) is the smallest of the numbers \(r\), \(\delta_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\delta_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(\delta_{\mathrm{z}}\), so it is not larger than any of the bands \(\delta_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\delta_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(\delta_{\mathrm{z}}\). By step 2 each of the three brackets in step 10 is therefore less than \(\eta/3\) in absolute value.
  12. By the triangle inequality for numbers, \(|a + b| \le |a| + |b|\), used twice, and because the absolute value of a product is the product of the absolute values (lemma 1.6, items 1 and 2),
    \[|R(Q)| \le \frac{\eta}{3}\,|\Delta x| + \frac{\eta}{3}\,|\Delta y| + \frac{\eta}{3}\,|\Delta z| .\]
    We write a non-strict inequality because one of the components of the step may be zero.
  13. By step 4 none of the numbers \(|\Delta x|\), \(|\Delta y|\), \(|\Delta z|\) is larger than \(|PQ|\), so
    \[|R(Q)| \le 3 \cdot \frac{\eta}{3}\,|PQ| = \eta\,|PQ| .\]
  14. We chose the error \(\eta\) arbitrarily and found for it a band \(\delta\) that works for every point \(Q\) with \(0 < |PQ| < \delta\). This is what we had to show.

Source: LEBL2 Prop. 8.4.6 (PDF pp. 48-49), for functions of \(n\) variables on an open set, where the partial derivatives are continuous at every point. The source adds the steps along the axes by induction on the number of variables and on each one uses the mean value theorem and the continuity of the partial derivatives at the point, as we do. Since continuity is used only at the point \(P\), continuity at \(P\) is enough. The form with \(\eta\) and \(\delta\) instead of a limit is not from the source.

As we go along the path from \(P\) toward \(Q\), the distance from \(P\) only grows. On the first step it is \(|t|\), on the second \(\sqrt{\Delta x^2 + t^2}\), where \(|t|\) grows from \(0\) to \(|\Delta y|\), and at the end it is \(|PQ|\). So the whole path lies in the disc with the centre \(P\) and the radius \(|PQ|\). If \(|PQ|\) is smaller than the band \(\delta\), the points \(T_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(T_{\mathrm{y}}\) are also closer to \(P\) than \(\delta\), and the partial derivatives at them differ from those at \(P\) by less than \(\eta/3\).

The proof showed where we need which assumption. We needed the existence of the partial derivatives at all points near \(P\) in steps 7 and 8, so that we could use the lemma on the change over a step along an axis, and with it the mean value theorem, on each step. We needed continuity at \(P\) in step 11, so that the partial derivatives at the intermediate points are close to those at \(P\). The proof needs no other derivatives. We will meet both moves again several times: the mean value theorem on a step along an axis, and continuity at a point.

What does the theorem mean? For a short step the change of the field equals the sum of the three products of the partial derivatives at \(P\) with the components of the step, up to an error that is only a small part of the length of the step. So three partial derivatives at a single point \(P\) determine how the field changes over every short step in any direction, even though we computed them only along the three axes. The theorem does not say, though, how short the step must be. The band \(\delta\) depends on how fast the partial derivatives change around \(P\): where they change fast, the step must be very short. The theorem cannot be used at points around which not all points closer than some number \(r\) are available, for example on the surface of a body, nor at points where the partial derivatives are not continuous. What goes wrong there we show in section 1.15.5.

The theorem and its proof hold for functions of any number of variables, only there are as many steps along the axes as there are variables (LEBL2 Prop. 8.4.6, PDF pp. 48-49). The property that the theorem proves has its own name in the books. A function is differentiable (odvedljiva) at the point \(P\) if there are three numbers which, used in (1.34) in place of the partial derivatives, give a remainder that is small in the sense of (1.32) (LEBL2 Def. 8.3.1, PDF p. 35). For a function of one variable this is the same as differentiability in the sense of definition 1.9, as LEBL2 shows on the same page. So the theorem says that a function with continuous partial derivatives is differentiable. The mere existence of the partial derivatives is not enough for this (LEBL2, PDF p. 48), as we will see in an example. We do not need the name in what follows, it is given so that the reader recognizes the property in the books.

1.15.4 A test on the hanging bar

Figure 1.299 shows the bar in cross section and the point at which we will test the theorem.

Let us draw the bar. The rectangle is the section of the bar by the plane \(z = 0\), seen from the side. It is drawn much thicker and shorter than the bar really is: the diameter of 10 mm is drawn as \(5\) units, the length of 10 m as \(6\), so that everything can be seen at once. The thick right line is the surface of the bar. The dot \(O\) is the origin, at the centre of the top end. The axis \(x\) goes from it downward. It is drawn dashed through the bar, because it runs along its middle, and continues below the lower edge with an arrow that shows the direction downward. The axis \(y\) points to the right. The dot \(P\) is the point at which we will test the theorem. The vertical dimension line shows its depth \(x_{\mathrm{P}} = 5\) m, from the height of \(O\) to the height of \(P\), half the length, so it is drawn at half height. It is offset to the left of the bar so that it does not run across the section. The horizontal dimension line shows the distance of the point \(P\) from the axis \(x\), \(y_{\mathrm{P}} = 4\) mm. It is drawn above \(P\) so that it does not cover the dot, and it is horizontal, so it does not matter at what height it stands. The blue circle around \(P\) has the radius \(r = 1\) mm. It touches the surface, because \(4 + 1 = 5\) mm is the radius of the bar. Inside it all points are still in the bar.

Let us test the theorem on a field whose remainder we know exactly. Take the steel hanging bar of section 1.7.1. It is \(L = 10\) m long, has a round cross section with a diameter of 10 mm and stretches under its own weight. In the model its field comes from, only the point \(O\) at the centre of the top face is fixed, and the weight is carried by a force spread evenly over the top face, which is an idealization. The axis \(x\) points from \(O\) vertically down, the axes \(y\) and \(z\) lie horizontally. The displacement field is

\[\begin{aligned} u_{\mathrm{x}} &= a\,(2Lx - x^2) - 0{,}3\,a\,(y^2 + z^2) , \\ u_{\mathrm{y}} &= -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\,y , \\ u_{\mathrm{z}} &= -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\,z , \end{aligned}\]

where \(a = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1}\) as in section 1.7.1, and the number \(0{,}3\) is the ratio of thinning to stretching of steel.

First we check the assumptions of the theorem at the point \(P\) with \(x_{\mathrm{P}} = 5\) m, \(y_{\mathrm{P}} = 4\) mm and \(z_{\mathrm{P}} = 0\). The point is an interior point: its distance from the surface of the bar is 1 mm, so all points whose distance from \(P\) is less than \(r = 1\) mm are in the bar (section 1.7.2). The formulas of the field are sums of products of numbers and coordinates, so we can evaluate them at every point. The computations of the partial derivatives in section 1.7.2 used only these formulas, so they hold at every point where we use the formulas, also at all points whose distance from \(P\) is less than \(r\). We got:

w.r.t. \(x\) w.r.t. \(y\) w.r.t. \(z\)
\(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) \(2a\,(L - x)\) \(-0{,}6\,a\,y\) \(-0{,}6\,a\,z\)
\(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) \(0{,}6\,a\,y\) \(-0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\) \(0\)
\(u_{\mathrm{z}}\) \(0{,}6\,a\,z\) \(0\) \(-0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\)

So all nine exist at all points whose distance from \(P\) is less than \(r\). All of them are polynomials in \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\), that is, sums of products of numbers and coordinates. By corollary 1.69 on the continuity of sums, products and polynomials in space, item 2, they are therefore continuous at every point, also at \(P\). Of course the formulas have a physical meaning only in the bar. Theorem 1.88 on the change of a field over a short step therefore holds for all three components: for every \(\eta > 0\) there is a \(\delta > 0\) such that the remainder of every step shorter than \(\delta\) is at most \(\eta\) times its length.

The theorem only says that such a \(\delta\) exists. For the hanging bar we can compute it, because we know the remainders from section 1.7.3:

\[R_{\mathrm{x}} = -a\,\Delta x^2 - 0{,}3\,a\,\Delta y^2 - 0{,}3\,a\,\Delta z^2 , \qquad R_{\mathrm{y}} = 0{,}6\,a\,\Delta x\,\Delta y , \qquad R_{\mathrm{z}} = 0{,}6\,a\,\Delta x\,\Delta z .\]

Let us estimate them by the length of the step \(|PQ|\). The result is shown in figure 1.301 below.

Let us draw the estimate for a step with \(|PQ| = 3\) and components \(1\), \(2\), \(2\) (the numbers are chosen). The top line is \(|PQ|^2 = 9\) as one length, split into the squares of the components \(\Delta x^2 = 1\), \(\Delta y^2 = 4\) and \(\Delta z^2 = 4\). The blue lines below it are the terms of the remainder \(|R_{\mathrm{x}}|/a\): \(\Delta x^2\), \(0{,}3\,\Delta y^2\) and \(0{,}3\,\Delta z^2\), each under its own square and shorter than it. At the bottom is the square with side \(|PQ|\) and in it the blue rectangle with sides \(|\Delta x|\) and \(|\Delta y|\). Their products are the areas, and the rectangle is smaller than the square, because no side is longer than \(|PQ|\).

  1. The number \(a\) is positive, so all three terms of \(R_{\mathrm{x}}\) have the sign minus, and \(|R_{\mathrm{x}}| = a\,(\Delta x^2 + 0{,}3\,\Delta y^2 + 0{,}3\,\Delta z^2)\).
  2. Squares are not negative and \(0{,}3 < 1\), so \(0{,}3\,\Delta y^2 \le \Delta y^2\) and \(0{,}3\,\Delta z^2 \le \Delta z^2\). Hence \(|R_{\mathrm{x}}| \le a\,(\Delta x^2 + \Delta y^2 + \Delta z^2) = a\,|PQ|^2\).
  3. No component of the step is longer than the step (step 4 of the proof of theorem 1.88), so \(|R_{\mathrm{y}}| = 0{,}6\,a\,|\Delta x|\,|\Delta y| \le 0{,}6\,a\,|PQ|^2 \le a\,|PQ|^2\). Likewise \(|R_{\mathrm{z}}| \le a\,|PQ|^2\).
  4. So all three remainders are at most \(a\,|PQ| \cdot |PQ|\). If \(|PQ| < \eta/a\), then \(a\,|PQ| < \eta\), and the remainders are at most \(\eta\,|PQ|\).
  5. As the band \(\delta\) we therefore take the smaller of the numbers \(\eta/a\) and \(r\). For every step shorter than \(\delta\) each of the three remainders is at most \(\eta\,|PQ|\).

For \(\eta = 10^{-10}\),

\[\frac{\eta}{a} = \frac{10^{-10}}{1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1}} = 5{,}123 \cdot 10^{-4}\ \text{m} ,\]

about \(0{,}51\) mm, which is less than \(r = 1\) mm. So every fibre from \(P\) shorter than \(0{,}51\) mm has, in any direction, remainders that are at most a ten-billionth of its length.

Let us check this (figure 1.301) on the fibre from the table of section 1.7.3, which points from \(P\) in the direction of the unit vector \(\vek n = (\tfrac13, \tfrac23, \tfrac23)\). We compare the remainder \(R_{\mathrm{x}}\) with the limit \(\eta\,|PQ| = \eta\,h\) at \(\eta = 10^{-10}\):

fibre length \(h\) remainder \(R_{\mathrm{x}}\) limit \(\eta\,h\)
0.1 mm \(-7{,}374 \cdot 10^{-16}\) m \(10^{-14}\) m
1 mm \(-7{,}374 \cdot 10^{-14}\) m \(10^{-13}\) m

For the length 0.1 mm the remainder is more than ten times below the limit. For the length 1 mm it is below the limit too, although it is longer than \(0{,}51\) mm (figure 1.301). So the band we got from the estimate \(a\,|PQ|^2\) is not the largest possible one. The estimate holds for all directions at once, while in the direction \(\vek n\) the remainder is smaller. A fibre of length \(h\) has the components \(\Delta x = h\,n_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\Delta y = h\,n_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(\Delta z = h\,n_{\mathrm{z}}\), so

\[\begin{aligned} |R_{\mathrm{x}}| &= a\,\big(n_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + 0{,}3\,n_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + 0{,}3\,n_{\mathrm{z}}^2\big)\,h^2 \\ &= a\,\Big(\frac19 + 0{,}3 \cdot \frac49 + 0{,}3 \cdot \frac49\Big)\,h^2 = \frac{3{,}4}{9}\,a\,h^2 = 0{,}3778\,a\,h^2 . \end{aligned}\]

Requirement (1.32) does not look for the largest band, one band that works is enough.

Let us draw the comparison. The horizontal axis is the length of the fibre \(h\) in millimetres, from \(0\) to \(1\). The vertical axis is the size of the remainder in units of \(10^{-13}\) m. The blackwhite curve is \(|R_{\mathrm{x}}| = 0{,}3778\,a\,h^2\), in these units \(0{,}7374\,h^2\). The two blackwhite dots on it are the fibres of \(0{,}1\) mm and \(1\) mm from the table, with the values \(0{,}0074\) and \(0{,}7374\). The grey dashed curve is the estimate \(a\,h^2 = 1{,}952\,h^2\), which holds in every direction, so it is above the blackwhite one. The blue line is the limit \(\eta\,h\) at \(\eta = 10^{-10}\), in these units \(1{,}0\,h\). The estimate meets the limit at \(h = \delta = \eta/a = 0{,}512\) mm, because there \(a\,h^2 = \eta\,h\). To the left of it the estimate is below the limit, so the remainder is below it too in every direction. The blackwhite curve is below the limit over the whole drawn part, also to the right of \(\delta\), because in the direction \(\vek n\) the remainder is smaller than the estimate.

For the bar the remainder is at most \(a\,|PQ|^2\), so it is proportional to the square of the length of the step. This is more than the theorem promises. For a general field the theorem only guarantees that the remainder is smaller than every fraction \(\eta\) of the length of the step if the step is short enough, not that it is proportional to the square. For the bar the estimate with the square held because every term of the remainder, for example \(-a\,\Delta x^2\) or \(0{,}6\,a\,\Delta x\,\Delta y\), is a product of a number and two components of the step. That is because the components of its field are sums of terms with at most two coordinate factors, as we saw in section 1.7.3.

1.15.5 A field with kinks

Are the two assumptions of the theorem really needed? Perhaps it would be enough for the partial derivatives to exist only at the point \(P\) itself, since only values at \(P\) appear in equality (1.34). The next example shows that it is not. In section 1.12 we already saw the function \(xy/(x^2 + y^2)\), which has all three partial derivatives at \(O\) but is not even continuous at \(O\). The field we take now is continuous at \(O\), and still the remainder is not small. We built the example ourselves, while LEBL2 (PDF p. 48) states that the existence of the partial derivatives without their continuity is not enough. Take the field

\[f(x, y, z) = |x + y| - |x - y| \tag{1.35}\]

and the point \(P = O = (0, 0, 0)\). The field is continuous at \(O\), since \(f(O) = 0\) and by the triangle inequality for numbers \(|f(Q)| \le |x + y| + |x - y| \le 2\,|x| + 2\,|y| \le 4\,|OQ|\), which is less than \(\eta\) whenever \(|OQ| < \eta/4\). The field does not depend on \(z\), so it is enough to look at it in the plane \(z = 0\) (figure 1.302). The lines \(y = x\) and \(y = -x\) divide it into four parts.

Let us draw the plane. The axes show \(x\) to the right and \(y\) up, the dot \(O\) is the origin, where we look for the remainder. The two thick blackwhite lines, \(y = x\) and \(y = -x\), are the kinks where the graph of the field breaks, and they divide the plane into four parts. Each part has its formula written in it. The right part, coloured blue, has \(f = 2y\). The upper part, coloured orange, has \(f = 2x\). The left part, coloured grey, has \(f = -2y\). The lower part, coloured lighter orange, has \(f = -2x\). The colours are only a help so that the parts can be told apart. The blue path goes from \(O\) to the right to the point \(A\) and then up to \(Q\). This is the path along the axes to the end of the step \(h\,\vek n\) in the direction \(\vek n = (\tfrac35, \tfrac45)\), about which more below. It is drawn at \(h = 2{,}5\), so \(A\) is \(\tfrac35 \cdot 2{,}5 = 1{,}5\) to the right of \(O\) and \(Q\) is a further \(\tfrac45 \cdot 2{,}5 = 2\) higher than \(A\). The second step crosses the line \(y = x\) at \(y = 1{,}5\), so from the right part into the upper one. The point \((t, t)\) is on the kink \(y = x\), drawn at \(t = 0{,}7\). From it two steps in the direction of the axis \(y\) lead: \(s > 0\) up into the upper part and \(s < 0\) down into the right part. There the formula of the field changes, so the quotient with respect to \(y\) has no limit at this point.

Let us see what the field is in the right part, where \(x > |y|\), that is, between the lines \(y = x\) and \(y = -x\) on the side where \(x\) is positive. There \(x + y\) and \(x - y\) are positive, because \(x\) is larger than \(y\) and than \(-y\). So \(|x + y| = x + y\) and \(|x - y| = x - y\), and

\[f = (x + y) - (x - y) = 2y .\]

In the same way we get the formulas in the upper, left and lower part:

\[f = \begin{cases} 2y & \text{pri } x > |y| , \\ 2x & \text{pri } y > |x| , \\ -2y & \text{pri } x < -|y| , \\ -2x & \text{pri } y < -|x| . \end{cases}\]

In each of the four parts the graph of the field is a plane. Together they form a roof of four flat triangles that meet along the lines \(y = x\) and \(y = -x\) (figure 1.303). There the graph breaks, just as two sides of a roof meet at the ridge or in the valley. Such a place is called a kink (pregib).

Let us draw the graph. The small triple of axes in the corner shows the directions \(x\), \(y\) and \(f\), with \(f\) up. The grey dashed square lies in the plane \(f = 0\), over the square \(-1 \le x, y \le 1\). From its corners verticals lead to the graph, which has the height \(2\) over the corners \((1, 1)\) and \((-1, -1)\) and the height \(-2\) over \((-1, 1)\) and \((1, -1)\), as shown by the numbers \(2\) and \(-2\) at the four corners of the graph. The grey edges of the graph join these four points. The point \(O\) is the common corner of all four triangles, and the graph is at height \(0\) there. The grey lines are a grid on the graph over lines parallel to the axes. Each is first at a constant height \(f\) (in the oblique view such a line is tilted), then rises or falls evenly and is at a constant height again, because in each of the four parts one formula holds, and it breaks where it crosses one of the lines \(y = x\) and \(y = -x\). The blue lines are the graph over the axes \(x\) and \(y\), where \(f = 0\). The thick blackwhite lines are the kinks over the lines \(y = x\) and \(y = -x\), and we tell them apart in the next paragraph.

The kink above the line \(y = x\) is a ridge: both thick lines that go from \(O\) to the corners with height \(2\) rise, so the graph rises along it away from \(O\). The kink above the line \(y = -x\) is a valley: both thick lines that go from \(O\) to the corners with height \(-2\) fall, so the graph falls along it away from \(O\). Above the axes the field is zero.

The partial derivatives at \(O\) exist. On the axis \(x\) we have \(f(\Delta x, 0, 0) = |\Delta x| - |\Delta x| = 0\) for every \(\Delta x\). So the difference quotient with respect to \(x\) is zero for every \(\Delta x \neq 0\), and by lemma 1.26 on the limit of a number plus a multiple with \(d = 0\) and \(c = 0\), \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(O) = 0\). Likewise \(f(0, \Delta y, 0) = |\Delta y| - |{-\Delta y}| = 0\), so \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(O) = 0\), and the field does not depend on \(z\), so \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}(O) = 0\). Along all three axes the field is zero. Since also \(f(O) = 0\), equality (1.34) at the point \(O\) says

\[f(Q) - 0 = 0 \cdot \Delta x + 0 \cdot \Delta y + 0 \cdot \Delta z + R(Q) , \qquad \text{so} \qquad R(Q) = f(Q) .\]

Now let us step from \(O\) in the direction of the unit vector \(\vek n = (\tfrac35, \tfrac45, 0)\), which has the length \(\sqrt{9/25 + 16/25 + 0} = 1\). The point \(Q = O + h\,\vek n = (\tfrac35 h,\ \tfrac45 h,\ 0)\) has the distance \(|h|\) from \(O\), and

\[f(Q) = \Big|\tfrac35 h + \tfrac45 h\Big| - \Big|\tfrac35 h - \tfrac45 h\Big| = \tfrac75\,|h| - \tfrac15\,|h| = \tfrac65\,|h| .\]

So the remainder is \(R(Q) = \tfrac65\,|OQ|\) for every \(h \neq 0\), no matter how short the step is. The quotient \(R(Q)/|OQ| = \tfrac65\) does not go to zero. Requirement (1.32) fails for every \(\eta < \tfrac65\): in every band \(\delta\) there are points \(Q\) on this line with \(|R(Q)| = \tfrac65\,|OQ| > \eta\,|OQ|\) (figure 1.304). The sum of the three products predicts that the field does not change over a step from \(O\), but in fact over a step in the direction \(\vek n\) it changes by \(\tfrac65\) of the length of the step.

Let us draw both sections. In the left part there are the axes: \(h\) horizontally, the field \(f\) vertically. The thick blackwhite line lies on the horizontal axis, because along the axis \(x\) the field is \(f = 0\) for every \(h\), and likewise along the axis \(y\). In the right part there are other axes. The thick blue line on the horizontal axis is the prediction of the sum of the three products with the partial derivatives at \(O\), which are all zero. The blackwhite letter V is the actual field along the direction \(\vek n\), \(f = \tfrac65\,|h|\). It has the shape of the letter V, because \(|h|\) equals \(-h\) for negative \(h\). The grey dimension line at \(h = 1\) has the length \(\tfrac65\) and shows the difference between the field and the prediction, that is the remainder \(R\). The blue shaded part between the lines \(\eta\,|h|\) and \(-\eta\,|h|\) is the part in which the remainder would have to lie for \(\eta = \tfrac35\). At \(|h| = 1{,}5\) the edges are at heights \(\pm 0{,}9\). The blackwhite letter V is above it at every \(h \neq 0\), because \(\tfrac65 > \tfrac35\).

Which assumption of the theorem fails? Let us look at the partial derivative with respect to \(y\) near \(O\).

Let us draw the derivative. The axes show \(t\), which is the coordinate \(x\) of the point \((t, 0, 0)\), and the value of the partial derivative with respect to \(y\) there. The thick blackwhite line is this value, \(2\) at every \(t > 0\). The empty circle and the filled dot at \(t = 0\) show that at \(O\) itself the value is not \(2\) but \(0\). The grey dashed horizontals are the levels \(\eta\) and \(-\eta\) for \(\eta = 1\): a band around the value \(0\) at \(O\). The blue measure below the axis is the chosen band \(\delta = 1{,}6\). The blue dots are the points at \(t = 0{,}5\) and \(t = 1{,}1\), which are closer to \(O\) than \(\delta\), and their value \(2\) is outside the band.

  1. Let \(t > 0\) and take the point \((t, 0, 0)\) on the axis \(x\). For every \(s\) with \(0 < |s| < t\) the point \((t, s, 0)\) lies in the part where \(x > |y|\), because \(t > |s|\). There \(f = 2y\), so \(f(t, s, 0) = 2s\), while \(f(t, 0, 0) = 0\).
  2. So the difference quotient with respect to \(y\) at the point \((t, 0, 0)\) is \((2s - 0)/s = 2\) for every such \(s\). By lemma 1.26 on the limit of a number plus a multiple with \(d = 2\) and \(c = 0\), \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(t, 0, 0) = 2\).
  3. The point \((t, 0, 0)\) has the distance \(t\) from \(O\). So for every \(\delta > 0\) there are points \((t, 0, 0)\) with \(0 < t < \delta\), whose distance from \(O\) is less than \(\delta\), and the partial derivative with respect to \(y\) is \(2\) at them but \(0\) at \(O\). For the error \(\eta = 1\) the condition of the definition of continuity 1.67 fails for every \(\delta\), and the partial derivative with respect to \(y\) is not continuous at \(O\).

Existence also fails at some points near \(O\). Take the point \((t, t, 0)\) with \(t > 0\) on the line \(y = x\), where \(f = 2t\). A step \(s > 0\) in the direction of the axis \(y\) takes us into the part where \(y > |x|\) and \(f = 2x = 2t\), so the quotient with respect to \(y\) is \((2t - 2t)/s = 0\). A step \(s < 0\) with \(|s| < 2t\) takes us into the part where \(x > |y|\), because then \(-t < t + s < t\), and \(f = 2y = 2(t + s)\), so the quotient is \((2t + 2s - 2t)/s = 2\). If the quotient approached some number \(d\), then for the error \(1\) and small \(s\) we would have \(|0 - d| < 1\) and \(|2 - d| < 1\), and by the triangle inequality for numbers (lemma 1.6, item 1) \(2 = |2 - 0| \le |2 - d| + |d - 0| < 2\), which is impossible. So the partial derivative with respect to \(y\) does not exist at the point \((t, t, 0)\). This is the kink.

This is exactly where the proof of the theorem would break down. For the step from \(O\) to \(Q = (\tfrac35 h,\ \tfrac45 h,\ 0)\) with \(h > 0\) the path goes through the point \(A = (\tfrac35 h, 0, 0)\), and the second step, from \(A\) to \(Q\), changes \(y\) from \(0\) to \(\tfrac45 h\). Up to the line \(y = x\), at \(y = \tfrac35 h\), it lies in the part where \(f = 2y\) and the partial derivative with respect to \(y\) is \(2\). Beyond it, it lies in the part where \(f = 2x = \tfrac65 h\) is constant. So the change over this step is \(2 \cdot \tfrac35 h = \tfrac65 h\), and all of it comes from the partial derivative \(2\), which differs from the value \(0\) at \(O\) however short the step is. This is the difference that continuity at \(P\) bounded in step 11 of the proof.

The example shows why the theorem needs its assumptions. The partial derivatives at a point \(P\) look only along the three axes. The theorem uses them to predict the change in all other directions, and this works only if the field does not break anywhere around \(P\). For a displacement field a kink means that the rate at which the displacement changes, from which we will compute the strain, jumps from one value on one side of a line or a surface to another value on the other side. Where this happens in bodies, for example at the contact of steel and rubber, we look at in the next part.

1.15.6 Continuously differentiable fields

For bodies we will make the assumptions of the theorem in advance, for all interior points at once. Recall from section 1.7.2: a point is an interior point of a body if there is still some material around it in all directions, that is, if there is a number \(r > 0\) such that all points whose distance from it is less than \(r\) are in the body. We speak in the same way of the interior points of any set of points (figure 1.306).

Let us draw the body. The closed blackwhite curve is the boundary of the body in the section, and inside it there is material. The blackwhite dot \(P\) is the chosen interior point. The grey dashed circle around it has the radius \(r\), which is drawn as a grey dimension line from \(P\) towards the lower left edge. It is all inside the body, because \(P\) is an interior point. The blue dot \(S\) is a point near \(P\), in the figure \(0{,}5\) from it, which is less than \(r/4 = 0{,}55\). The blue circle around \(S\) has the radius \(r/4\), drawn as a blue dimension line. \(P\) lies in it, because \(|PS| < r/4\). The disc around \(S\) lies inside the body, which lemma 1.89 below proves. On the right at the edge there is a blackwhite dot on the surface with a grey dashed circle that reaches out of the body, so this point is not an interior point.

In the hanging bar the point \(P\) was an interior point with \(r = 1\) mm, while a point on the surface is not an interior point, because every ball around it, however small, reaches out of the body.

We will make the requirement on the displacement field on the set of all interior points of the body. For the theorem to be usable at each of them we need one more property: all points close enough to an interior point are interior points too. For a point with \(r = 1\) mm we expect this, since a point only \(0{,}1\) mm away from it is still quite far from the surface. Let us show that this holds in general.

Let us draw the proof. On the upper axis are the coordinates \(x\) of the points \(P\), \(S\) and \(T\). The difference \(|x_{\mathrm{S}} - x_{\mathrm{P}}|\) is \(0{,}8\) in the example, the difference \(|x_{\mathrm{T}} - x_{\mathrm{S}}|\) is \(0{,}9\), both less than \(r/4 = 1\). Their sum \(|x_{\mathrm{T}} - x_{\mathrm{P}}|\) is \(1{,}7\), less than \(r/2 = 2\). Below is the plane with the same points. The grey dashed circle around \(P\) has radius \(r\), drawn as the blackwhite measure. The blue square has side \(r\), since its points differ from \(P\) in each coordinate by less than \(r/2\). It lies inside the circle. The dot \(S\) is at distance \(0{,}78\) from \(P\), the blackwhite circle around it has radius \(r/4\) and \(P\) is inside it. The dot \(T\) is at distance \(0{,}86\) from \(S\), so it is inside this circle.

Lemma 1.89 Neighbourhood of an interior point (okolica notranje točke)

Let \(M\) be a set of points and \(P\) an interior point of it, such that all points whose distance from \(P\) is less than \(r > 0\) are in \(M\). Then every point \(S\) whose distance from \(P\) is less than \(r/4\) is also an interior point of the set \(M\): all points whose distance from \(S\) is less than \(r/4\) are in \(M\).

Proof

We must show that every point \(T\) with \(|ST| < r/4\) has a distance less than \(r\) from \(P\), since it is then in \(M\).

  1. Let \(|PS| < r/4\) and \(|ST| < r/4\). By the triangle inequality for numbers (lemma 1.6, item 1)
    \[|x_{\mathrm{T}} - x_{\mathrm{P}}| = |(x_{\mathrm{T}} - x_{\mathrm{S}}) + (x_{\mathrm{S}} - x_{\mathrm{P}})| \le |x_{\mathrm{T}} - x_{\mathrm{S}}| + |x_{\mathrm{S}} - x_{\mathrm{P}}| .\]
  2. No difference of coordinates is larger than the distance: \(|x_{\mathrm{T}} - x_{\mathrm{S}}| \le |ST|\) and \(|x_{\mathrm{S}} - x_{\mathrm{P}}| \le |PS|\), as in step 4 of the proof of theorem 1.88. By step 1, therefore, \(|x_{\mathrm{T}} - x_{\mathrm{P}}| < \tfrac{r}{4} + \tfrac{r}{4} = \tfrac{r}{2}\). Likewise \(|y_{\mathrm{T}} - y_{\mathrm{P}}| < r/2\) and \(|z_{\mathrm{T}} - z_{\mathrm{P}}| < r/2\).
  3. By lemma 1.28, that a larger nonnegative number has a larger square, each square of a difference of coordinates is less than \(r^2/4\), so
    \[|PT|^2 = (x_{\mathrm{T}} - x_{\mathrm{P}})^2 + (y_{\mathrm{T}} - y_{\mathrm{P}})^2 + (z_{\mathrm{T}} - z_{\mathrm{P}})^2 < 3 \cdot \frac{r^2}{4} < r^2 .\]
  4. By the same lemma \(|PT| < r\), so \(T\) is in \(M\). We chose the point \(T\) arbitrarily, so \(S\) is an interior point of the set \(M\).

Source: The lemma is not from a source.

Why exactly \(r/4\)? In figure 1.306 the point \(S\) is less than \(r/4\) from the point \(P\). So \(P\) lies in the disc with the radius \(r/4\) around \(S\), and \(S\) in the disc with the same radius around \(P\). The point \(T\) is less than \(r/4\) from \(S\), and \(S\) less than \(r/4\) from \(P\), so in each coordinate \(T\) differs from \(P\) by less than \(r/2\). There are three coordinates, and the sum of three squares, each less than \(r^2/4\), is less than \(3r^2/4\), hence less than \(r^2\). With \(r/2\) instead of \(r/4\) the sum of the squares would only be less than \(3r^2\), which is not enough. The bound \(r/4\) comes from the method of the proof, not from the property itself. Every point \(S\) with \(|PS| < r\) is in fact interior, because all points closer to \(S\) than \(r - |PS|\) are in \(M\) (LEBL1 Prop. 7.2.9, PDF p. 266). The proof of this needs the triangle inequality for distances between points in space, which we will prove in chapter 2, in section 2.3. For our purpose \(r/4\) is enough. The lemma says that every point of the set of interior points is again surrounded by interior points. In section 1.7.2 we called a set in which every point is an interior point an open set. So the set of interior points of any set is open.

Now we can name a field for which the assumptions of the theorem hold everywhere.

Definition 1.90 Continuously differentiable field (zvezno odvedljivo polje)

Let \(D\) be an open set of points, that is, a set in which every point is an interior point: for every point \(P\) of \(D\) there is a number \(r > 0\) such that all points whose distance from \(P\) is less than \(r\) are in \(D\). A function \(f\) of three variables defined on \(D\) is continuously differentiable (zvezno odvedljiva) on \(D\) if at every point of \(D\) all three of its partial derivatives exist (definition 1.25) and each of them is continuous at every point of \(D\) (definition 1.67). A field of vectors, for example a displacement field, is continuously differentiable on \(D\) if all three of its components are continuously differentiable on \(D\). A function of one variable defined on an interval is continuously differentiable on it if it has a derivative at every point of the interval, at an endpoint that belongs to the interval a one-sided derivative (definition 1.9), and this derivative is continuous at every point of the interval (definition 1.58).

Source: LEBL2 Def. 8.4.5 and Prop. 8.4.6 (PDF p. 48), for functions of \(n\) variables. The source defines continuous differentiability through the derivative of a function of several variables, and Proposition 8.4.6 shows that this is the same as the existence and continuity of the partial derivatives. Open set: LEBL1 Def. 7.2.4 (PDF p. 264).

For a continuously differentiable field, theorem 1.88 on the change of a field over a short step holds at every point \(P\) of the set \(D\). Since \(D\) is open, there is an \(r > 0\) such that all points closer to \(P\) than \(r\) are in \(D\). At all these points the partial derivatives exist, and at \(P\) they are continuous. The field of the hanging bar is continuously differentiable on the set of interior points of the bar, because its partial derivatives exist at every interior point and are polynomials, which are continuous by corollary 1.69, item 2.

The theorem on the change of a field over a short step also shows that a continuously differentiable function has no jumps. As \(Q\) goes to \(P\), all three products and the remainder in (1.34) go to zero, so the difference \(f(Q) - f(P)\) does too. Let us write this precisely, since we will refer to this property several more times. Figure 1.308 shows why.

Let us draw the figure. The axes show the length of the step \(|PQ|\) horizontally and the size of the change \(|f(Q) - f(P)|\) vertically. For every point \(Q\) near \(P\) there is a blackwhite dot. The blue line through the origin has the slope \(M + 1\), in the figure \(0{,}8\). Here \(M\) is the sum of the absolute values of the three partial derivatives at \(P\), and the number \(1\) is the error that the remainder allows. All dots are below the line, because by the proof below the change is at most \(M\,|PQ|\) for the three products and at most \(|PQ|\) for the remainder. The grey dashed horizontal line is the allowed error \(\eta\), in the figure \(1{,}6\). The line meets it at \(|PQ| = \eta/(M + 1)\), in the figure at \(2\). The numbers \(\delta_1\) and \(r\) are on the axis, and the values \(3{,}4\) and \(4{,}4\) are chosen for the figure. The number \(\delta_1\) says up to where the remainder is at most \(|PQ|\), by the theorem on the change of the field with the error \(1\). The number \(r\) is the radius of the neighbourhood of the point \(P\) in which all points are still in the domain. The blue line under the axis is the chosen band \(\delta\), the smallest of \(r\), \(\delta_1\) and \(\eta/(M + 1)\), that is \(2\). For a step shorter than \(\delta\) all dots are below the horizontal line \(\eta\). The two dots above it are at a step longer than \(\delta\), at \(2{,}9\) and \(3{,}6\).

Lemma 1.91 A continuously differentiable function is continuous (zvezno odvedljiva funkcija je zvezna)

Let \(D\) be an open set of points and \(f\) a function that is continuously differentiable on \(D\) (definition 1.90). Then \(f\) is continuous at every point of \(D\) (definition 1.67).

Proof

We must show: for every point \(P\) of \(D\) and every \(\eta > 0\) there is a \(\delta > 0\) such that \(|f(Q) - f(P)| < \eta\) for every point \(Q\) of \(D\) with \(|PQ| < \delta\).

  1. Let \(P\) be a point of \(D\). Since \(D\) is open, there is an \(r > 0\) such that all points at a distance less than \(r\) from \(P\) are in \(D\). At these points the partial derivatives of \(f\) exist, and at \(P\) they are continuous, so theorem 1.88 on the change of a field over a short step holds for \(f\) at \(P\).
  2. By this theorem, used with the error \(1\), there is a \(\delta_1 > 0\) such that the remainder of (1.34) is at most \(|PQ|\) in absolute value for every point \(Q\) with \(0 < |PQ| < \delta_1\).
  3. Let \(M = |\partial f/\partial x\,(P)| + |\partial f/\partial y\,(P)| + |\partial f/\partial z\,(P)|\) and let \(Q\) be a point with \(0 < |PQ|\), where \(|PQ|\) is smaller than \(r\) and than \(\delta_1\). By (1.34), by the triangle inequality for numbers, because the absolute value of a product is the product of the absolute values (lemma 1.6, items 1 and 2), and because no component of the step is longer than the step (step 4 of the proof of theorem 1.88), the dot of the point \(Q\) lies below the line \((M + 1)\,|PQ|\), since
    \[|f(Q) - f(P)| \le M\,|PQ| + |R(Q)| \le (M + 1)\,|PQ| .\]
  4. We choose \(\eta > 0\) and take for \(\delta\) the smallest of the positive numbers \(r\), \(\delta_1\) and \(\eta/(M + 1)\). For a point \(Q\) with \(0 < |PQ| < \delta\) step 3 gives \(|f(Q) - f(P)| \le (M + 1)\,|PQ| < \eta\), and for \(Q = P\) the difference is zero. The error \(\eta\) was arbitrary, so \(f\) is continuous at \(P\).

Source: LEBL2 Prop. 8.3.5 (PDF p. 37), for differentiable functions, and Prop. 8.4.6 (PDF p. 48), by which continuously differentiable functions are differentiable. The proof with the theorem on the change of a field is not from the source.

From now on we will assume for every body that its displacement field is continuously differentiable on the set of interior points of the body, which is open by lemma 1.89 on the neighbourhood of an interior point. Where the assumption does not hold, we will say so. What does it mean physically? The displacement is defined at every point of the body and, at the interior points, changes from point to point without jumps (lemma 1.91). So the material nowhere tears apart (figure 1.309, below) and nowhere slides along some surface inside the body. The partial derivatives, which measure how fast the displacement changes, have no jumps either. The strain that we will compute from them therefore changes continuously from point to point, and the displacement field has no kinks, as the graph of the displacement of a bar made of two materials has. CM makes this assumption at the start as part of the picture of a material that fills space continuously, without gaps (CM §1.1, PDF p. 30). Real material is made of atoms and grains, so this is an idealization that holds at lengths much larger than these particles. CM adds that these properties hold almost everywhere (skoraj povsod), that is, with exceptions like the ones below (CM §3.1, PDF p. 110).

Where does the assumption fail, and where does it hold only with difficulty? Figure 1.309 shows three cases.

  1. Two materials glued together. Take a bar made of two pieces of equal thickness, one of steel and one of rubber, glued at the joint, and pull it at both ends with equal forces. The rubber stretches much more than the steel. The displacement is continuous at the joint, because the pieces are glued, but its derivative along the bar, the strain, jumps at the joint. The displacement field has a kink there. The books therefore compute the field for each material separately and require at the joint that the displacements match (SADD §5.2, PDF pp. 107-108). We use the theorem in each piece separately. At the top the bar is shown before loading, in blackwhite: the left whiteblack piece is steel, the right grey piece rubber. The ticks are marks at equal spacing, each at a distance of \(0{,}5\) drawing units, where one unit is \(1\) cm. Below it is the same bar after loading, in orange: the steel piece and the rubber piece with the same marks. The two arrows show equal forces at both ends, which pull the bar. The left end is in the drawing at the same place as before, so that the displacement starts at zero. The spacing of the marks in the steel after loading is \(0{,}55\) drawing units instead of \(0{,}5\), so one tenth more, in the rubber \(0{,}8\) instead of \(0{,}5\), so \(0{,}6\) more, hence the rubber stretches six times more than the steel. The values are invented and strongly enlarged. Below it, the axes show the displacement \(u\) against the position \(x\) before loading. The blackwhite broken line is the graph of the displacement, and the grey dashed vertical line is the joint. The slope of the graph is the strain, \(0{,}1\) in the steel and \(0{,}6\) in the rubber.
  2. A crack (razpoka), a surface in the body along which the material is torn apart. Two points that were together on opposite sides of the crack before loading move differently, so the displacement jumps across the crack. This is so for a crack that forms during loading, as in the figure, and there the assumption fails. For a crack that exists before loading, however, its two faces are part of the surface of the body, where we made no assumption, and at the interior points the assumption can hold. At the tip of the crack the strains grow without bound (SADD §10.8, PDF p. 321, and §5.8, PDF pp. 120-121). In the middle of the figure the blackwhite rectangle is the body before loading and the blackwhite horizontal line from the left edge to the middle is the crack. The orange outline is the body after loading. The two arrows pull the upper and the lower part apart, so the crack opens into a wedge, while its tip stays in place. The upper and the lower part are in the drawing rotated about the tip. The blackwhite dot on the crack before loading becomes two orange dots, one in the upper and one in the lower part. Their distance is the jump of the displacement.
  3. A concentrated force (točkovna sila), that is, a force acting at a single point, and sharp corners and notches. When such a point lies on the surface of the body, as the force in the figure and every sharp corner and notch do, we made no assumption there, and at the interior points the assumption can hold. But as an interior point approaches such a point, the strains in the model, and with them the partial derivatives, grow without bound, so the step in theorem 1.88 on the change of a field over a short step must be ever shorter. Real forces are never gathered at a single point and real corners are never infinitely sharp, so such models are approximations (SADD §5.8, PDF pp. 120-121). On the right the body stands on a hatched base, which is grey in the figure. The arrow is a point force at the middle of the top face. The orange line is the top face after loading: next to the force it sinks sharply, in the figure by \(0{,}4\) below the original plane, and elsewhere it hardly moves. The sides after loading are not drawn.

For comparison, the figure shows two more examples that the assumption allows. The straight blue line below the bar is the graph of the displacement of a bar made of steel only: it has the same slope everywhere, so it has no kink. Below the middle body is a body without a crack: a blackwhite rectangle before the load, below it an orange one after it, when the force stretches it uniformly. Two neighbouring points stay neighbours, because the material nowhere tears apart.

These irregularities are confined to the neighbourhood of the joint, the crack, the corner or the point where the force acts (SADD §5.8, PDF p. 120). Elsewhere the assumption holds.

1.15.7 A fibre in any direction

Let us return to the fibre. Let \(P\) be an interior point of a body whose displacement field is continuously differentiable, and let \(r > 0\) be such that all points closer to \(P\) than \(r\) are interior points of the body. Such an \(r\) exists by lemma 1.89 on the neighbourhood of an interior point. Let the fibre reach from \(P\) to the point \(Q = (x_{\mathrm{P}} + \Delta x,\ y_{\mathrm{P}} + \Delta y,\ z_{\mathrm{P}} + \Delta z)\) with \(|PQ| < r\) (figure 1.293, the step \(\Delta x\), then \(\Delta y\) and \(\Delta z\)). Theorem 1.88 on the change of a field over a short step holds for each component of the displacement separately. Let us write all three equalities:

\[\begin{aligned} u_{\mathrm{x}}(Q) - u_{\mathrm{x}}(P) &= \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial x}(P)\,\Delta x + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial y}(P)\,\Delta y + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial z}(P)\,\Delta z + R_{\mathrm{x}} , \\ u_{\mathrm{y}}(Q) - u_{\mathrm{y}}(P) &= \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial x}(P)\,\Delta x + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial y}(P)\,\Delta y + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial z}(P)\,\Delta z + R_{\mathrm{y}} , \\ u_{\mathrm{z}}(Q) - u_{\mathrm{z}}(P) &= \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial x}(P)\,\Delta x + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial y}(P)\,\Delta y + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial z}(P)\,\Delta z + R_{\mathrm{z}} . \end{aligned} \tag{1.36}\]

Nine partial derivatives at \(P\) appear in these equalities. Let us collect them in a table, as in section 1.7.2: in a row is a component of the displacement, in a column the variable we differentiate with respect to, and all are taken at the point \(P\).

at the point \(P\) w.r.t. \(x\) w.r.t. \(y\) w.r.t. \(z\)
\(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial x\) \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial y\) \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial z\)
\(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial x\) \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial y\) \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial z\)
\(u_{\mathrm{z}}\) \(\partial u_{\mathrm{z}} / \partial x\) \(\partial u_{\mathrm{z}} / \partial y\) \(\partial u_{\mathrm{z}} / \partial z\)

Each line of equality (1.36) takes one row of the table, multiplies its three numbers by the components of the step \(\Delta x\), \(\Delta y\), \(\Delta z\) and adds the products. Figure 1.310 shows how the length of the vector of the remainders is related to the lengths of its components.

Let us draw the vector. The grey dashed edges are a box whose sides are the absolute values of the three components. The view is oblique: \(x\) points to the right, \(y\) up and \(z\) up and to the right, and it is drawn shortened to half so that the figure can be read. The blue path goes along three edges of the box: horizontally by \(|R_{\mathrm{x}}|\), vertically by \(|R_{\mathrm{y}}|\) and obliquely by \(|R_{\mathrm{z}}|\). The blackwhite arrow is the vector of the remainders from the start to the end of the path, that is the diagonal of the box, with the length \(\sqrt{R_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + R_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + R_{\mathrm{z}}^2}\). The path is longer than the diagonal, which the inequality below says.

For every error \(\eta > 0\) the theorem gives a band for each remainder. If we take the smallest of the three, then for every step shorter than it all three remainders are at most \(\eta\,|PQ|\) in absolute value. Then the vector of the remainders \((R_{\mathrm{x}}, R_{\mathrm{y}}, R_{\mathrm{z}})\) is also small compared with the step. Its length (definition 1.17) is not larger than the sum of the absolute values of its components,

\[\sqrt{R_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + R_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + R_{\mathrm{z}}^2} \le |R_{\mathrm{x}}| + |R_{\mathrm{y}}| + |R_{\mathrm{z}}| \le 3\eta\,|PQ| .\]

We get the first inequality by comparing the squares of the two sides. The square of the right side is \(R_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + R_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + R_{\mathrm{z}}^2 + 2\,|R_{\mathrm{x}}|\,|R_{\mathrm{y}}| + 2\,|R_{\mathrm{x}}|\,|R_{\mathrm{z}}| + 2\,|R_{\mathrm{y}}|\,|R_{\mathrm{z}}|\), which is not less than the square of the left side, because the last three terms are not negative. By lemma 1.28, that a larger nonnegative number has a larger square, the inequality therefore also holds between the two sides. Since \(\eta\) is arbitrary, \(3\eta\) is arbitrarily small too.

For a fibre in the direction of the unit vector \(\vek n = (n_{\mathrm{x}}, n_{\mathrm{y}}, n_{\mathrm{z}})\) and of length \(|h|\) we have \(Q = P + h\,\vek n\), with the components of the step \(\Delta x = h\,n_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\Delta y = h\,n_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(\Delta z = h\,n_{\mathrm{z}}\), as in section 1.7.3. There we showed for the hanging bar that the difference of displacements divided by \(h\) approaches the sum of the products of the partial derivatives with the components of the direction. Now this holds for every continuously differentiable field.

Corollary 1.92 Difference of displacements along a fibre in any direction (razlika pomikov vzdolž vlakna v poljubni smeri)

Let \(P\) be a point of a body and \(r > 0\) a number such that all points whose distance from \(P\) is less than \(r\) are in the body. Suppose that at all these points all partial derivatives of the components of the displacement \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(u_{\mathrm{z}}\) exist and that they are continuous at \(P\). Let \(\vek n = (n_{\mathrm{x}}, n_{\mathrm{y}}, n_{\mathrm{z}})\) be a unit vector. Then

\[\begin{aligned} \lim_{\mathrm{h} \to 0} \frac{u_{\mathrm{x}}(P + h\,\vek n) - u_{\mathrm{x}}(P)}{h} &= \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial x}(P)\,n_{\mathrm{x}} \\ &\quad + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial y}(P)\,n_{\mathrm{y}} + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial z}(P)\,n_{\mathrm{z}} , \end{aligned} \tag{1.37}\]

and likewise for \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) with the partial derivatives of \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) and for \(u_{\mathrm{z}}\) with the partial derivatives of \(u_{\mathrm{z}}\).

Proof

We must show that the function \(q(h) = (u_{\mathrm{x}}(P + h\,\vek n) - u_{\mathrm{x}}(P))/h\), defined for \(0 < |h| < r\), has at the point \(0\) the limit \(d = \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial x}(P)\,n_{\mathrm{x}} + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial y}(P)\,n_{\mathrm{y}} + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial z}(P)\,n_{\mathrm{z}}\). By the definition of the limit 1.5: for every \(\eta > 0\) there is a \(\delta > 0\) such that \(|q(h) - d| < \eta\) for every \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta\).

  1. We choose any error \(\eta > 0\).
  2. The point \(Q = P + h\,\vek n\) has the components of the step \(\Delta x = h\,n_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\Delta y = h\,n_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(\Delta z = h\,n_{\mathrm{z}}\), and its distance from \(P\) is \(|PQ| = |h|\), because \(\vek n\) is a unit vector (section 1.7.3). So for \(0 < |h| < r\) the point \(Q\) is in the body.
  3. We divide equality (1.34) for \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) with these components by \(h \neq 0\). Since \(\Delta x/h = n_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\Delta y/h = n_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\Delta z/h = n_{\mathrm{z}}\), we get
    \[q(h) = d + \frac{R_{\mathrm{x}}(Q)}{h} .\]
  4. By theorem 1.88 on the change of a field over a short step, used for \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) with the error \(\eta/2\), there is a \(\delta\) with \(0 < \delta \le r\) such that \(|R_{\mathrm{x}}(Q)| \le \tfrac{\eta}{2}\,|PQ|\) for every point \(Q\) with \(0 < |PQ| < \delta\). We take half the error so that the inequality in step 5 is strict.
  5. Let \(0 < |h| < \delta\). By step 2, \(0 < |PQ| = |h| < \delta\), and by steps 3 and 4
    \[|q(h) - d| = \frac{|R_{\mathrm{x}}(Q)|}{|h|} \le \frac{\tfrac{\eta}{2}\,|h|}{|h|} = \frac{\eta}{2} < \eta .\]
    The quotient \(|R_{\mathrm{x}}(Q)|/|h|\) is the height of the dot on the right half of figure 1.292, and for \(|h|\) smaller than \(\delta\) it lies below the horizontal line, which here is \(\eta/2\).
  6. We chose the error \(\eta\) arbitrarily and found a band for it, so the limit of the quotient \(q(h)\) is \(d\). For \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(u_{\mathrm{z}}\) we repeat the steps with their equalities from (1.36).

Source: LEBL2 §8.3 (PDF pp. 41-42), for differentiable functions of \(n\) variables, where the limit on the left is the directional derivative. The proof from the theorem on the change of a field over a short step is not from the source.

The books call the limit in the corollary the directional derivative (smerni odvod) of the component of the displacement in the direction \(\vek n\) (LEBL2 §8.3, PDF p. 41). It says how fast the component of the displacement changes as we move from \(P\) in the direction \(\vek n\), just as the partial derivative says the same for the direction of an axis. For \(\vek n = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = (1, 0, 0)\) the sum on the right side of (1.37) equals \(\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial x}(P)\), and the directional derivative is the partial derivative with respect to \(x\). The corollary says that for a continuously differentiable field we get the directional derivatives in all directions from the three partial derivatives. Without the assumptions this fails. For the field with kinks of section 1.15.5, the quotient in the direction \(\vek n = (\tfrac35, \tfrac45, 0)\) is \(\tfrac65\) for every \(h > 0\), while the sum of the products of the partial derivatives at \(O\) with the components of the direction is \(0\).

Let us return once more to the steel hanging bar, which stretches under its own weight and in the model is fixed only at the centre \(O\) of its top face, with the axis \(x\) vertically down. For it we already computed the limit for \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) at the point \(P\) with \(x_{\mathrm{P}} = 5\) m, \(y_{\mathrm{P}} = 4\) mm and \(z_{\mathrm{P}} = 0\), and in the direction \(\vek n = (\tfrac13, \tfrac23, \tfrac23)\) in section 1.7.3, \(6{,}5035 \cdot 10^{-7}\). We compute the other two components together with the first in section 1.16.2 with a single product of a table of numbers and a column of numbers, and there we also say what they mean.

We needed the difference of displacements because of the fibre. By (1.15) the vector of the fibre after loading is \(\overrightarrow{P_1 Q_1} = \overrightarrow{PQ} + \big(\vek u(Q) - \vek u(P)\big)\), the sum of the vector before loading and the difference of the displacements of the ends. For the fibre \(\overrightarrow{PQ} = h\,\vek n\), therefore, \(\overrightarrow{P_1 Q_1}/h\) is, for small \(h\), close to the sum of the vector \(\vek n\) and the vector whose components are the three sums of corollary 1.92. From this we will compute in section 1.17 by how much a short fibre in any direction stretches.

First, though, we need a shorter notation. The nine numbers of the table, and the three sums in which each row of the table is multiplied by the three components of a vector, will appear for every fibre and in every further computation. The next section introduces a notation that treats the table of nine numbers as a single mathematical object, and the three sums as a single product of this object with a vector.
