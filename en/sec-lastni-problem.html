Chapter 10 Vibration of systems with several degrees of freedom and modal analysis (nihanje sistemov z več prostostnimi stopnjami in modalna analiza)

10.2 The eigenvalue problem and natural modes (lastni problem in nihajni načini)

The two-storey frame A (okvir A) of section 10.1 is our model of a laboratory frame. Two rigid floors (stropa), each of mass \(m = 500\) kg, are joined to the ground and to each other by two steel columns (stebra) of length 1 m and cross section \(20 \times 40\) mm. The columns are clamped into the ground and into the floors, their mass is neglected and the floors move only horizontally. The column \(\vek q = (q_1, q_2)\) holds the horizontal displacements of the lower and the upper floor from equilibrium. A storey has stiffness \(k = 501\,760\) N/m, and by theorem 10.2 the equation of motion without damping and forces is

\[\mat M\ddot{\vek q} + \mat K\vek q = \vek 0 , \qquad \mat M = m\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} , \quad \mat K = k\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} . \tag{10.10}\]

Each floor feels both neighbouring parts of the frame: the lower one is pulled back by the spring of the storey below it and forward by the spring of the storey above it, the upper one only by the spring of the storey below it.

We ask: can the frame vibrate so that both floors move in step, that is with the same frequency, the same time dependence and so that both reach zero and the largest displacement at the same moments? For a system with one degree of freedom this is the only form of vibration (chapter 9). With two degrees of freedom we looked for it with a cosine in section 8.13.3 and called it a mode of vibration. Here we pose the question for any number \(n\) of coordinates and any stiffness matrix, because the next sections need systems with dozens of coordinates and systems with zero or negative stiffness.

First we say what "in step" means. A motion is synchronous (sinhrono) if \(\vek q(t) = \boldsymbol\varphi\,g(t)\), where \(\boldsymbol\varphi \ne \vek 0\) is a column of \(n\) numbers that fixes the ratios between the coordinates, and \(g\) is one function of time, the same for all coordinates. For frame A this means that the ratio \(q_2/q_1\) is the same at every time. We insert it into the equation of motion. Since \(\boldsymbol\varphi\) is constant, \(\ddot{\vek q} = \boldsymbol\varphi\,\ddot{g}\), and

\[(\mat M\boldsymbol\varphi)\,\ddot{g}(t) + (\mat K\boldsymbol\varphi)\,g(t) = \vek 0 . \tag{10.11}\]

The column \(\mat M\boldsymbol\varphi\) is not zero, because \(\boldsymbol\varphi^\T\mat M\boldsymbol\varphi > 0\) for a positive definite matrix \(\mat M\) (definition 6.27). Choose a row \(i\) with \((\mat M\boldsymbol\varphi)_{\mathrm{i}} \ne 0\). From it, \(\ddot{g} = -\lambda\,g\) with the number \(\lambda = (\mat K\boldsymbol\varphi)_{\mathrm{i}}/(\mat M\boldsymbol\varphi)_{\mathrm{i}}\), which does not depend on time. Inserting this back into equation (10.11) gives for every row \(j\) the equality \(\big((\mat K\boldsymbol\varphi)_{\mathrm{j}} - \lambda\,(\mat M\boldsymbol\varphi)_{\mathrm{j}}\big)\,g(t) = 0\). The function \(g\) is not zero everywhere, otherwise \(\vek q = \vek 0\), so all rows give the same condition, \(\mat K\boldsymbol\varphi = \lambda\,\mat M\boldsymbol\varphi\). The requirement of synchronous motion has therefore turned into an equation for the unknown \(\lambda\) and \(\boldsymbol\varphi\). We name it.

Definition 10.7 Eigenpair and eigenvalue problem (lastni par in lastni problem)

Let \(\mat K\) be a symmetric matrix and \(\mat M\) a symmetric positive definite matrix, both with \(n\) rows and \(n\) columns. A real number \(\lambda\) and a nonzero real column \(\boldsymbol\varphi\) with \(n\) elements form an eigenpair (lastni par) of the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\) if

\[\mat K\boldsymbol\varphi = \lambda\,\mat M\boldsymbol\varphi . \tag{10.12}\]

The search for such pairs is the generalized eigenvalue problem (posplošeni lastni problem), the number \(\lambda\) is the eigenvalue (lastna vrednost) and the column \(\boldsymbol\varphi\) the eigencolumn (lastni stolpec). If \(\lambda \ge 0\), then \(\omega = \sqrt\lambda\) is the natural circular frequency (lastna krožna frekvenca) and \(\boldsymbol\varphi\) the mode shape (način nihanja), as in definition 8.124.

Source: CK (9.4) (PDF p. 267) and (10.4) (PDF p. 301): inserting a harmonic motion into the equation of motion gives an algebraic eigenvalue problem. GR (2.22)-(2.25) (PDF p. 85-86): synchronous motion, eigenvalue problem, "eigenmode". The route to the equation through row \(i\) is not from the source.

The definition differs from definition 8.124 in two things. First, it allows every real \(\lambda\). Chapter 8 required \(\omega > 0\), but we shall also meet \(\lambda = 0\) for systems that the supports do not hold, and \(\lambda < 0\) for unstable equilibrium (theorem 8.119). Second, in this chapter we write the mode \(\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\), not \(\vek u_{\mathrm{r}}\) as in chapter 8, because \(\vek u\) means a displacement for bodies and for finite elements. For \(\mat M = \mat I\) equation (10.12) is the eigenvalue and eigenvector of the matrix \(\mat K\) by definition 2.59.

If \(\boldsymbol\varphi\) is an eigencolumn, so is \(c\,\boldsymbol\varphi\) for every number \(c \ne 0\), because equation (10.12) is linear in \(\boldsymbol\varphi\). A mode is therefore determined up to multiplication by a number (CK, PDF p. 301). Where we compare ratios between coordinates, we take one element equal to \(1\) and say each time which one (for frame A in this section the first, for bracket B the unit length of the column). Later we shall normalize the theorems with \(\boldsymbol\varphi^\T\mat M\boldsymbol\varphi = 1\) and say each time which normalization applies.

So far we only demanded synchronous motion. The next lemma says when the equation of motion really allows it, and that a frequency needs an eigenvalue, not an arbitrary number.

Lemma 10.8 Synchronous vibration and eigenpair (sinhrono nihanje in lastni par)

Let \(\mat K\) be a symmetric and \(\mat M\) a symmetric positive definite matrix of order \(n\), \(\omega > 0\) a number, \(\boldsymbol\varphi \ne \vek 0\) a real column with \(n\) elements and \((a, b) \ne (0, 0)\) a pair of real numbers. Then:

  1. The motion \(\vek q(t) = \boldsymbol\varphi\,(a\cos\omega t + b\sin\omega t)\) is a solution of \(\mat M\ddot{\vek q} + \mat K\vek q = \vek 0\) exactly when \(\mat K\boldsymbol\varphi = \omega^2\mat M\boldsymbol\varphi\).
  2. For every eigenpair \((\lambda, \boldsymbol\varphi)\) we have \(\lambda = \boldsymbol\varphi^\T\mat K\boldsymbol\varphi\,/\,\boldsymbol\varphi^\T\mat M\boldsymbol\varphi\). If \(\mat K\) is positive semidefinite (definition 7.15), then \(\lambda \ge 0\). If it is positive definite, then \(\lambda > 0\).
Proof

We show point 1 by insertion, and point 2 by multiplying the eigenvalue problem by \(\boldsymbol\varphi^\T\).

  1. Let \(g(t) = a\cos\omega t + b\sin\omega t\). By theorem 8.12 (equation (8.7) with \(c = 0\)), \(g'(t) = -a\omega\sin\omega t + b\omega\cos\omega t\), and differentiating once more, \(g''(t) = -\omega^2\,(a\cos\omega t + b\sin\omega t) = -\omega^2 g(t)\).
  2. Since \(\boldsymbol\varphi\) is constant, \(\ddot{\vek q} = \boldsymbol\varphi\,g'' = -\omega^2 g\,\boldsymbol\varphi\), hence \(\mat M\ddot{\vek q} + \mat K\vek q = (\mat K - \omega^2\mat M)\,\boldsymbol\varphi\;g(t)\).
  3. If \(\mat K\boldsymbol\varphi = \omega^2\mat M\boldsymbol\varphi\), this column is zero at every time.
  4. Conversely, let the column be zero at every time. At \(t_0 = 0\) we have \(g(t_0) = a\), and at \(t_0 = \pi/(2\omega)\) we have \(g(t_0) = b\). At least one of the numbers \(a\), \(b\) is not zero, so there is a \(t_0\) with \(g(t_0) \ne 0\). At it, step 2 gives \((\mat K - \omega^2\mat M)\boldsymbol\varphi = \vek 0\). This proves point 1.
  5. Let \(\mat K\boldsymbol\varphi = \lambda\mat M\boldsymbol\varphi\). Multiply from the left by \(\boldsymbol\varphi^\T\): \(\boldsymbol\varphi^\T\mat K\boldsymbol\varphi = \lambda\,\boldsymbol\varphi^\T\mat M\boldsymbol\varphi\). Since \(\mat M\) is positive definite and \(\boldsymbol\varphi \ne \vek 0\), we have \(\boldsymbol\varphi^\T\mat M\boldsymbol\varphi > 0\) (definition 6.27), and dividing gives the first sentence of point 2.
  6. For positive semidefinite \(\mat K\) the numerator is \(\ge 0\) (definition 7.15), for positive definite \(> 0\) (definition 6.27). The denominator is positive, so \(\lambda\) has the same sign as the numerator. This proves point 2.

Source: GR (2.22)-(2.27) (PDF p. 85-86): the same insertion and multiplication by \(\boldsymbol\varphi^\T\), there for positive definite \(\mat K\). The proof step by step is not from the source.

The lemma says two things. The frame can vibrate synchronously only at frequencies that are square roots of eigenvalues, and at these the synchronous vibration really exists, with any phase and amplitude, since \(a\) and \(b\) fix them. For a positive definite stiffness matrix all eigenvalues are positive, because the energy of the springs is positive for every nonzero displacement: \(\tfrac12\boldsymbol\varphi^\T\mat K\boldsymbol\varphi > 0\). If \(\lambda = 0\), then \(\mat K\boldsymbol\varphi = \vek 0\), and \(\vek q = \boldsymbol\varphi\,(a + b\,t)\) is a solution, since \(\mat M\ddot{\vek q} = \vek 0\) and \(\mat K\vek q = \vek 0\). This motion is not a vibration: the displacement grows linearly with time, the system moves like a rigid body, which we treat later in this chapter. If \(\lambda < 0\), then \(\boldsymbol\varphi^\T\mat K\boldsymbol\varphi < 0\) by point 2 of the lemma, and theorem 8.119 says that the displacement from such a starting position (in the direction \(\boldsymbol\varphi\), without initial velocity) grows at least linearly. Synchronous vibration with \(\omega > 0\) is therefore possible only at \(\lambda > 0\).

10.2.1 Frame A: the floors in step (okvir A: stropa v koraku)

We test the lemma on frame A. Equation (10.12) reads \((\mat K - \lambda\mat M)\boldsymbol\varphi = \vek 0\), that is

\[\begin{bmatrix} 2k - \lambda m & -k \\ -k & k - \lambda m \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} \varphi_1 \\ \varphi_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} . \tag{10.13}\]

Two homogeneous equations in two unknowns have a nonzero solution exactly when the determinant of the matrix is zero (theorem 8.125, point 1, with the determinant by definition 2.50). The frequency equation (frekvenčna enačba) is

\[\begin{aligned} \det(\mat K - \lambda\mat M) &= (2k - \lambda m)(k - \lambda m) - k^2 \\ &= m^2\lambda^2 - 3km\,\lambda + k^2 = 0 . \end{aligned} \tag{10.14}\]

Its solutions are \(\lambda = \tfrac{3 \mp \sqrt5}{2}\,\tfrac{k}{m}\). At \(k/m = 1003{,}52\) 1/s\(^2\) we get \(\lambda_1 = 383{,}31\) 1/s\(^2\) and \(\lambda_2 = 2627{,}25\) 1/s\(^2\) (table below). Both are positive and real, as theorem 8.125 guarantees. The book has no determinant of order \(n\), so we use this route only for \(n = 2\). For larger \(n\) we shall get the eigenvalues differently, in the next section.

We get the mode from the second row of system (10.13), \(-k\varphi_1 + (k - \lambda m)\varphi_2 = 0\), hence \(r = \varphi_2/\varphi_1 = k/(k - \lambda m)\). From the first row, \(r = 2 - \lambda m/k\). At every solution of equation (10.14) the two rows agree, because its determinant is zero.

mode \(\lambda\) (1/s\(^2\)) \(\omega\) (1/s) \(f = \omega/2\pi\) (Hz) \(T\) (s) \(\boldsymbol\varphi\)
1 383,31 19,578 3,1160 0,32093 \((1;\ 1{,}618)\)
2 2627,25 51,257 8,1578 0,12258 \((1;\ -0{,}618)\)

We check the eigenpair of the first mode. With \(\boldsymbol\varphi_1 = (1;\ 1{,}618)\) we have \(\mat K\boldsymbol\varphi_1 = (191\,655;\ 310\,105)\) N/m, and \(\lambda_1 m\,\boldsymbol\varphi_1 = 383{,}31 \cdot 500 \cdot (1;\ 1{,}618) = (191\,655;\ 310\,105)\) N/m. For the second mode both sides are \((1\,313\,625;\ -811\,865)\) N/m.

Let us also look at a column close to the first mode, \(\boldsymbol\varphi = (1;\ 1{,}5)\). For it to be an eigencolumn, there would have to be one number \(\lambda\) that solves both rows of equation (10.12) at the same time. That there is none means: for every \(\lambda\) at least one row is wrong. The computation gives \(\mat K\boldsymbol\varphi = k\,(0{,}5;\ 0{,}5) = (250\,880;\ 250\,880)\) N/m and \(\mat M\boldsymbol\varphi = m\,(1;\ 1{,}5) = (500;\ 750)\) kg. Row \(1\) demands \(\lambda = 250\,880/500 = 501{,}76\) 1/s\(^2\), and row \(2\) demands \(\lambda = 250\,880/750 = 334{,}51\) 1/s\(^2\). The numbers differ, so no value of \(\lambda\) solves both rows and \((1;\ 1{,}5)\) is not an eigencolumn. The condition that rejects it is the demand for a single \(\lambda\) in both rows. For \(\boldsymbol\varphi_1 = (1;\ 1{,}618)\) the demands agree, because both rows give \(\lambda_1 = 383{,}31\) 1/s\(^2\).

The ratios \(1{,}618\) and \(-0{,}618\) are not accidental. If we eliminate \(x\) from \(r = 2 - x\) and \(r = 1/(1 - x)\) for \(x = \lambda m/k\), we get \(r\,(r - 1) = 1\), that is \(r^2 - r - 1 = 0\), the golden ratio (zlati rez) \(r = (1 \pm \sqrt5)/2\). Why exactly this equation? The floors have equal masses, so the inertia force of a floor is proportional to its displacement, and the ratio of the inertia forces of the upper and the lower floor equals \(r\). The force of the storey on the upper floor is \(-k\,(r - 1)\,\varphi_1\) (only the spring of its storey acts, its elongation is \(q_2 - q_1\)). On the lower floor the spring below it acts with \(-k\,\varphi_1\), the spring above it with \(+k\,(r - 1)\,\varphi_1\), and the resultant is \(-k\,(2 - r)\,\varphi_1\). The ratio of these forces must also be \(r\), so \((r - 1)/(2 - r) = r\), which is the same equation. From it \(r - 1 = 1/r\): in the first mode the drift of the upper storey (storey drift), the difference \(q_2 - q_1\), is \(0{,}618\) times the drift of the lower one.

In the first mode both floors move in the same direction, the upper one \(1{,}618\) times more. In the second they move in opposite directions, the upper one \(0{,}618\) times as much as the lower. Figure 10.6 shows them.

By lemma 10.8 a vibration in one mode is \(\vek q(t) = \boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\,(a\cos\omega_{\mathrm{r}} t + b\sin\omega_{\mathrm{r}} t)\). Figure 10.7 (left) shows it for the first mode with amplitude \(5\) mm on the lower floor: the upper floor has amplitude \(5 \cdot 1{,}618 = 8{,}09\) mm and both floors pass through zero at the same moments, first at \(T_1/4 = 0{,}0802\) s.

And what about the sum of two modes? The equation is linear in \(\vek q\), so the sum of two solutions is a solution, as in step 3 of the proof of corollary 8.126. Take \(\vek q(t) = 5\,\boldsymbol\varphi_1\cos\omega_1 t + 2\,\boldsymbol\varphi_2\cos\omega_2 t\) mm, that is the first mode with amplitude \(5\) mm on the lower floor and the second with \(2\) mm. At \(t = 0\) we have \(\vek q(0) = (7;\ 6{,}854)\) mm, the ratio \(q_2/q_1 = 0{,}979\). At \(t = 0{,}05\) s the ratio is \(4{,}97\). Since the ratio is not constant, this motion is not synchronous. The lower floor first passes through zero at \(0{,}0593\) s, the upper at \(0{,}0835\) s, so not together (figure 10.7, right). Synchronous motions are therefore special solutions, the sum of two is not synchronous. Whether every motion is a sum of modes we prove in the next sections.

10.2.2 Bracket B: modes along the principal directions (konzola B: načini vzdolž glavnih smeri)

Bracket B (konzola B) is the truss of section 4.8.1. Bar \(AC\) is horizontal and \(4\) m long, bar \(BC\) is \(5\) m long and points from the wall to \(C\) in the direction \((4;\ -3)/5\), the pins \(A\) and \(B\) are fixed to the wall, \(EA = 19{,}6\) MN. At the pin \(C\) we put a mass \(m = 1000\) kg, the bars have no mass (our model, not from the source). The column \(\vek q = (u, v)\) holds the horizontal displacement to the right and the vertical displacement upwards of the pin \(C\). The stiffness matrix of the truss in these coordinates is (definition 7.4)

\[\mat K = \begin{bmatrix} 7{,}4088 & -1{,}8816 \\ -1{,}8816 & 1{,}4112 \end{bmatrix}\cdot 10^6\ \text{N/m} , \qquad \mat M = m\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} , \tag{10.15}\]

and its inverse is the flexibility matrix \(\mat D = (1\ \text{m}/EA)\begin{bmatrix} 4 & 16/3 \\ 16/3 & 21 \end{bmatrix}\) from equation (4.82) (lemma 10.3). The matrix \(\mat M\) is a multiple of the identity, so equation (10.12) is the eigenvalue problem of the matrix \(\mat K\) alone with the eigenvalue \(\lambda m\).

By step 3 of the proof of theorem 8.125 the frequency equation is \((\lambda m)^2 - (\operatorname{tr}\mat K)\,\lambda m + \det\mat K = 0\) with trace (sled) \(\operatorname{tr}\mat K = 8{,}82\cdot 10^6\) N/m and \(\det\mat K = 6{,}9149\cdot 10^{12}\) N\(^2\)/m\(^2\). The solutions are \(\lambda_1 m = 869\,771\) N/m and \(\lambda_2 m = 7\,950\,229\) N/m, so

\[\lambda_1 = 869{,}77\ \text{1/s}^2 , \quad \lambda_2 = 7950{,}23\ \text{1/s}^2 , \qquad \omega_1 = 29{,}492\ \text{1/s} , \quad \omega_2 = 89{,}164\ \text{1/s} ,\]

that is \(f_1 = 4{,}6938\) Hz and \(f_2 = 14{,}191\) Hz. The unit modes are \(\boldsymbol\varphi_1 = (0{,}27653;\ 0{,}96101)\), pointing \(73{,}95^\circ\) above the axis \(x\), and \(\boldsymbol\varphi_2 = (0{,}96101;\ -0{,}27653)\), pointing \(16{,}05^\circ\) below it. We check the first: \(\mat K\boldsymbol\varphi_1 = (240\,517;\ 835\,855)\) N/m, and \(\lambda_1 m\,\boldsymbol\varphi_1 = 869\,771\cdot(0{,}27653;\ 0{,}96101) = (240\,517;\ 835\,855)\) N/m.

The modes are perpendicular. By corollary 8.126 we have \(\boldsymbol\varphi_1^\T\mat M\boldsymbol\varphi_2 = 0\), which for \(\mat M = m\mat I\) is exactly \(\boldsymbol\varphi_1\cdot\boldsymbol\varphi_2 = 0\), and indeed \(0{,}27653 \cdot 0{,}96101 - 0{,}96101 \cdot 0{,}27653 = 0\). The motion of the pin in the first mode is therefore a straight line at the angle \(73{,}95^\circ\), in the second a line perpendicular to it. Why exactly so? The flexibility matrix gives the answer. If we multiply the equation \(\mat K\boldsymbol\varphi = \lambda m\,\boldsymbol\varphi\) from the left by \(\mat D = \mat K^{-1}\), we get

\[\boldsymbol\varphi = \lambda m\,\mat D\boldsymbol\varphi , \qquad \mat D\boldsymbol\varphi = \frac{1}{\lambda m}\,\boldsymbol\varphi . \tag{10.16}\]

A force in the direction of a mode therefore causes a displacement in the same direction. For a force of \(1\) kN in the direction \(\boldsymbol\varphi_1\) the displacement is \(1{,}1497\) mm (\(1/(\lambda_1 m) = 1{,}1497\cdot 10^{-6}\) m/N), in the direction \(\boldsymbol\varphi_2\) only \(0{,}12578\) mm. Since \(\boldsymbol\varphi_1\) and \(\boldsymbol\varphi_2\) are perpendicular unit columns, a force of magnitude \(1\) kN in an arbitrary direction is \(\vek F = 1\,\text{kN}\,(c_1\boldsymbol\varphi_1 + c_2\boldsymbol\varphi_2)\) with \(c_1^2 + c_2^2 = 1\). By equation (10.16) the displacement is \(\mat D\vek F = 1\,\text{kN}\,(c_1 d_1\boldsymbol\varphi_1 + c_2 d_2\boldsymbol\varphi_2)\) with \(d_{\mathrm{r}} = 1/(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} m)\). The components of the displacement \(x_{\mathrm{r}} = c_{\mathrm{r}} d_{\mathrm{r}}\cdot 1\,\text{kN}\) in the directions of the modes satisfy \((x_1/d_1)^2 + (x_2/d_2)^2 = (1\,\text{kN})^2\). This is an ellipse (elipsa) with semi-axes \(1{,}1497\) mm and \(0{,}12578\) mm, lying in the directions of the modes (figure 10.8). The modes are therefore eigenvectors of the flexibility matrix (definition 2.59), that is its principal directions. The first mode is the direction in which the pin is most flexible, so it has the lowest frequency.

Why is the flexibility in the direction \(\boldsymbol\varphi_1\) \(9{,}14\) times larger? When the pin is displaced by \(\vek q\), bar \(AC\) lengthens by \(u\) and bar \(BC\) by \(0{,}8\,u - 0{,}6\,v\) (section 4.8.1). For a displacement of \(1\) m in the direction \(\boldsymbol\varphi_1\) these are \(0{,}277\) m and \(-0{,}355\) m, for a displacement of \(1\) m in the direction \(\boldsymbol\varphi_2\) they are \(0{,}961\) m and \(0{,}935\) m. Twice the energy of the bars is \((EA/4)\,\Delta L_{\mathrm{AC}}^2 + (EA/5)\,\Delta L_{\mathrm{BC}}^2 = \boldsymbol\varphi^\T\mat K\boldsymbol\varphi\), and it gives \(\boldsymbol\varphi_1^\T\mat K\boldsymbol\varphi_1 = 869\,771\) N/m and \(\boldsymbol\varphi_2^\T\mat K\boldsymbol\varphi_2 = 7\,950\,229\) N/m, that is \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} m\) by lemma 10.8. In the first mode the bars deform little and in opposite senses (a lengthening of \(0{,}277\) m and a shortening of \(0{,}355\) m), in the second both deform a lot and in the same sense, each by almost the whole displacement.

10.2.3 Other coordinates: storey drifts (druge koordinate: etažni zamiki)

For frame A we chose the displacements of the floors as coordinates. Physically the storey drift (etažni zamik) would be just as natural, that is the displacement of a floor relative to the one below it. Let \(q_1' = q_1\) and \(q_2' = q_2 - q_1\) be the drifts of the first and the second storey. What happens to the eigenvalue problem? We write \(\vek q = \mat P\vek q'\) with the invertible matrix \(\mat P = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\) (\(\det\mat P = 1\)). As in section 8.13.3, the energies in the new coordinates are written with \(\mat K' = \mat P^\T\mat K\mat P\) and \(\mat M' = \mat P^\T\mat M\mat P\). We compute them:

\[\mat K' = k\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} , \qquad \mat M' = \begin{bmatrix} 2m & m \\ m & m \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1000 & 500 \\ 500 & 500 \end{bmatrix}\ \text{kg} . \tag{10.17}\]

The stiffness matrix is diagonal, because the potential energy \(\tfrac12 k\,q_1'^2 + \tfrac12 k\,q_2'^2\) is the sum of the energies of the storeys, each depending only on its own drift. The mass matrix has an off-diagonal element, because the kinetic energy is \(\tfrac12 m\,\dot{q}_1'^2 + \tfrac12 m\,(\dot{q}_1' + \dot{q}_2')^2\) and the upper floor moves with the drift of both storeys. The coupling has moved from the stiffness to the mass matrix. The eigenvalues stay the same:

  1. The frequency equation of the new coordinates is \(\det(\mat K' - \lambda\mat M') = (k - 1000\lambda)(k - 500\lambda) - (500\lambda)^2 = 250\,000\,\lambda^2 - 1500\,k\,\lambda + k^2\).
  2. At \(m = 500\) kg we have \(m^2 = 250\,000\) and \(3km = 1500\,k\), so this is the same equation as (10.14).
  3. Why? If \((\mat K' - \lambda\mat M')\,\boldsymbol\varphi' = \vek 0\), then \(\mat P^\T(\mat K - \lambda\mat M)\,\mat P\boldsymbol\varphi' = \vek 0\). The matrix \(\mat P^\T\) is invertible, so \(\mat P^\T\vek y = \vek 0\) implies \(\vek y = \vek 0\), and therefore \((\mat K - \lambda\mat M)\,\mat P\boldsymbol\varphi' = \vek 0\). The column \(\boldsymbol\varphi = \mat P\boldsymbol\varphi'\) is an eigencolumn in the old choice of coordinates with the same eigenvalue. Conversely with \(\mat P^{-1}\).
  4. The modes in drifts are \(\boldsymbol\varphi_1' = \mat P^{-1}\boldsymbol\varphi_1 = (1;\ 0{,}618)\) and \(\boldsymbol\varphi_2' = (1;\ -1{,}618)\). In the first mode the drift of the upper storey is \(0{,}618\) times the drift of the lower one, as we saw before.

The frequencies therefore depend only on the system, not on the coordinates in which we write it. The coordinates determine how we write the modes and where the coupling goes, not the physics.

10.2.4 Where next (kam naprej)

We found that frame A and bracket B have two modes each, every one with its own frequency, and that the modes are perpendicular in the sense fixed by the mass matrix (for B in the ordinary sense). We got this for \(n = 2\) with a \(2 \times 2\) determinant. A bar of \(16\) finite elements, which we meet as an example in this chapter (in chapter 7 we divided the bar into only a few elements), has \(16\) coordinates, and a \(2 \times 2\) determinant does not suffice for it. So we ask: does every such system have \(n\) modes? Are the eigenvalues always real? How are two modes related if their eigenvalues differ, and what if they are equal? And is every motion a sum of modes, as it was for \(n = 2\)? The next section answers all these questions.
