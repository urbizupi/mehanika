Chapter 13 Vibration of continuous systems: string, shaft and beam

13.7 Orthogonality of the modes of continuous systems

For a system with several degrees of freedom, chapter 10 showed that the modes are \(\mat M\)-orthogonal and \(\mat K\)-orthogonal (definition 10.17, theorem 10.21) and that the eigenvalues are real (lemma 10.26). Decoupling of the equations of motion and writing a motion as a sum of modes rested on this. We need the same for the modes of the string, bar, shaft and beam of sections 13.2, 13.3 and 13.6: writing any motion as a sum of modes (section 13.8) and the upper bound of the Ritz approximation (section 13.9). The question is whether the same orthogonality holds when the number of degrees of freedom is infinite and the matrices are replaced by derivatives. We show that it does, if the boundary conditions are such that the boundary terms vanish in the integration by parts. The proof is the one for matrices, with the symmetry of the matrix \(\mat K\) replaced by the vanishing of a boundary term.

What it means for two modes to be orthogonal we show first in figure 13.101. Take the steel cantilever of section 13.6. The beam is clamped at \(x = 0\), its straight position is the axis. Above the axis we draw its first mode \(\varphi_1\), which rises all the way from the clamp, and its second mode \(\varphi_2\), which rises, turns and crosses the axis at the node \(x_0\). We multiply the two modes point by point and draw the product \(\varphi_1\varphi_2\) above its own axis. Up to the node both modes are above the axis and the product is positive, beyond the node \(\varphi_2\) is below the axis and the product is negative. The blue area before the node and the orange one after it are equally large, so the integral of the product along the beam is zero. Why do we call this orthogonality? The name is taken from vectors: two vectors in the plane are perpendicular exactly when the sum of the products of their corresponding components is zero. A function has a value at every point as a vector has a component at every index, and the integral of the product is this sum for functions. How exactly the sum turns into the integral is shown below in figure 13.102. The bottom row of figure 13.101 is the same for the second derivatives, and we shall need it for the stiffness form.

13.7.1 Model and the boundary term

We treat the four systems of the chapter, each with the same properties along its length \(L\), on the interval \([0, L]\). The string, bar and shaft have an equation of second order, the beam an equation of fourth order. Let \(m > 0\) be the constant mass (for the shaft the moment of inertia) per unit length and \(S > 0\) the constant stiffness: for the string the tension \(T\) (section 13.1), for the bar \(EA\), for the shaft \(G I_{\mathrm{p}}\) with the polar second moment of the section \(I_{\mathrm{p}}\) (section 13.3), and for the beam the bending stiffness \(EI\) (definition 5.14). The mass per length is in turn \(\mu\), \(\rho A\), \(\rho I_{\mathrm{p}}\) and \(\rho A\). Constant \(m\) and \(S\) are an assumption of the sections in which we computed the modes, but they are not essential for the theorem of this section: for a varying cross section \(D\varphi = -(S\varphi')'\) or \((EI\,\varphi'')''\), the integration by parts below runs in the same way with \(S(x)\) under the integral, and the theorem stays unchanged (CK, PDF p. 420, says that the properties are not limited to uniform beams). What is essential is positivity, because with it we get \(\lambda \ge 0\) in section 13.7.4. In this section \(T\) is always the tension, \(\mu\) the mass per length of the string and \(\lambda\) the eigenvalue. The function \(u(x)\) is the displacement for the string and the bar, the twist for the shaft and the deflection for the beam. A mode (definition 13.6) is a nonzero real function \(\varphi\) and a number \(\omega\) such that \(w(x, t) = \varphi(x)\cos\omega t\) solves the equation of motion and satisfies the boundary conditions. Substituting this form into the equations of motion given by theorem 13.3 (the bar and the shaft have the same form, section 13.3) and theorem 13.37 gives the spatial equations for \(\varphi\), written with \(\lambda = \omega^2\):

\[\begin{aligned} -S\,\varphi'' &= \lambda\,m\,\varphi \qquad \text{(string, bar, shaft)}, \\ E I\,\varphi'''' &= \lambda\,m\,\varphi \qquad \text{(beam)} . \end{aligned} \tag{13.67}\]

Denote the left side by \(D\varphi\), that is \(D\varphi = -S\varphi''\) or \(D\varphi = EI\,\varphi''''\). For functions \(u\) and \(v\) on \([0, L]\) we write two numbers that we shall need, the mass inner product (masni skalarni produkt) and the stiffness form (togostna oblika),

\[\begin{aligned} (u, v)_{\mathrm{m}} &= \int_0^L m\,u\,v\,\mathrm{d}x , \\ B(u, v) &= \int_0^L S\,u'\,v'\,\mathrm{d}x \quad \text{or} \\ B(u, v) &= \int_0^L EI\,u''\,v''\,\mathrm{d}x , \end{aligned} \tag{13.68}\]

the first for second order, the second for the beam. Both are symmetric: \((u, v)_{\mathrm{m}} = (v, u)_{\mathrm{m}}\) and \(B(u, v) = B(v, u)\), since the product of real numbers does not change when \(u\) and \(v\) are swapped. For matrices these forms corresponded to \(\vek u^\T\mat M\,\vek v\) and \(\vek u^\T\mat K\,\vek v\). Why the integral really is the successor of the matrix product we see on the bar that theorem 10.52 divided into equal finite elements with lumped mass (definition 10.48).

Figure 13.102 shows a bar of \(n = 8\) elements. The bar of length \(L\) is fixed at \(x = 0\) and free at \(x = L\), on its axis are the nodes \(x_{\mathrm{j}} = jh\) at the spacing \(h = L/8\). Above the axis we draw the first and second mode of the continuous bar, \(\sin k_1 x\) and \(\sin k_2 x\) with \(k_{\mathrm{r}} = (2r - 1)\pi/(2L)\) (section 13.3). Theorem 10.52 says that the eigenvectors of the bar of elements are exactly the values of the first mode and the values of the second mode at the nodes, \(\vek\phi_{\mathrm{r}} = (\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}(x_1), \dots, \varphi_{\mathrm{r}}(x_8))\). The lumped mass matrix is diagonal, with the mass \(\rho A h\) at each node and \(\rho A h/2\) at the free end (lemma 10.50), so

\[\vek\phi_1^\T\mat M^{\mathrm z}\vek\phi_2 = \rho A\sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^{8} c_{\mathrm{j}}\,h\,\varphi_1(x_{\mathrm{j}})\,\varphi_2(x_{\mathrm{j}}) , \qquad c_1 = \dots = c_7 = 1 , \quad c_8 = \tfrac12 .\]

Each term is the area of a rectangle of width \(h\) (the last \(h/2\)) and height \(\varphi_1(x_{\mathrm{j}})\varphi_2(x_{\mathrm{j}})\) above the axis. For \(\rho A = L = 1\) the sum of the blue ones is \(0{,}2086\), the sum of the orange ones \(-0{,}2086\), together zero up to rounding, as the \(\mat M\)-orthogonality of the eigenvectors requires (theorem 10.21). As \(n\) grows, the rectangles fit the area under the curve \(\varphi_1\varphi_2\), which has the area \(0{,}2067\) before the node \(2L/3\) and \(-0{,}2067\) after it. The sum differs from a Riemann sum (definition 3.47) only by half of the last rectangle, which goes to zero with \(h\), so its limit is the integral \(\int_0^L \rho A\,\varphi_1\varphi_2\,\mathrm{d}x = (\varphi_1, \varphi_2)_{\mathrm{m}}\) (theorem 3.53, item 3). The column of values becomes the function, the diagonal of the mass matrix becomes \(m\) under the integral. In the same way \(\vek\phi_1^\T\mat K\,\vek\phi_2 = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} EA\,h\,(\Delta_{\mathrm{j}}\phi_1/h)(\Delta_{\mathrm{j}}\phi_2/h)\) with the difference of neighbouring values \(\Delta_{\mathrm{j}}\phi = \varphi(x_{\mathrm{j}}) - \varphi(x_{\mathrm{j}-1})\), and since \(\Delta_{\mathrm{j}}\phi/h\) tends to the derivative, this sum turns into \(B(\varphi_1, \varphi_2)\). Orthogonality of functions is therefore orthogonality of vectors with ever more components.

Where does a boundary term appear? If we multiply the equation \(D\varphi_{\mathrm{i}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{i}} m\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\) by \(\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\) and integrate, the left side is \(\int (D\varphi_{\mathrm{i}})\,\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{d}x\). Figure 13.103 shows integration by parts (lemma 6.13) for arbitrary functions \(N\) and \(w\). The axes are \(w\) and \(N\), the curve is the point \((w(x), N(x))\) from \(x = 0\) to \(x = L\). The area under it is \(\int N\,w'\,\mathrm{d}x\), the area to the left of it is \(\int N'\,w\,\mathrm{d}x\). The large rectangle \(N(L)\,w(L)\) without the small one \(N(0)\,w(0)\) is their sum. In the example of the figure \(0{,}37\) \(+\) \(0{,}71\) \(= 1{,}08\), and indeed \(1{,}2 \cdot 1\) \(-\) \(0{,}4 \cdot 0{,}3\) \(= 1{,}2 - 0{,}12 = 1{,}08\). So, besides the stiffness form \(B(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}, \varphi_{\mathrm{j}})\), there is a term at the ends, the difference of these two rectangles, which we write for each order separately:

\[b(u, v) = -S\,\big[u'\,v\big]_0^L \quad \text{or} \quad b(u, v) = EI\,\big[u'''\,v - u''\,v'\big]_0^L . \tag{13.69}\]

What is this term? For the string, bar and shaft, \(-S u'\) is up to sign the force (for the shaft the torque) in the cross section. Why? In the string the neighbouring part pulls along the tangent with the force \(T\), whose transverse part is \(T u'\) for a small slope (equation (13.1)). In the bar \(u'\) is the strain along the axis, the stress is \(E u'\) (equation (3.1)) and the axial force \(EA\,u'\). In the shaft \(G I_{\mathrm{p}}\,u'\) is the torque by equation (13.20). The term is the product of this force with the displacement (for the shaft the twist) at the end: the work of the force at the end. For the beam, \(EI\,u''\) is up to sign the bending moment and \(EI\,u'''\) up to sign the shear force (chapter 5), and the term is the work of the shear force on the deflection minus the work of the moment on the rotation. Figure 13.104 shows three ends of a string, bar or shaft, each in its own row. In each there is the axis of equilibrium and the shape of the string resting on it. If an end is clamped, the displacement is zero, although the force \(S u'\) acts. If an end is free, the force is zero, although the displacement \(v\) is nonzero. In both cases the product is zero (\(b = 0\)). This is exactly what we require.

Definition 13.68 Self-adjoint boundary conditions (samoadjungirani robni pogoji)

Let \([0, L]\) be an interval, \(S\), \(m\) or \(EI\), \(m\) constant positive numbers and \(b(u, v)\) the boundary term of (13.69). The boundary conditions of a system at the ends \(x = 0\) and \(x = L\) are self-adjoint, in the narrower sense of this book, if they are linear, homogeneous and with real coefficients, and if \(b(u, v) = 0\) for every pair of real functions \(u\) and \(v\) that satisfy them (the functions \(u\) and \(v\) are twice continuously differentiable on \([0, L]\), for the beam four times). It suffices that at each end every product in (13.69) is zero, and so it is with all conditions below. The following are self-adjoint:

  • for the string, bar and shaft: at an end \(u = 0\) (fixed, geometric condition) or \(u' = 0\) (free, natural condition: the force or torque \(S u'\) is zero), one of the two at each end,
  • for the beam: at an end clamped \(u = u' = 0\), simply supported \(u = 0\) and \(u'' = 0\), free \(u'' = u''' = 0\).

Source: CK (13.39) (PDF p. 420): homogeneous generalized boundary conditions, from which the fixed, simply supported and free ends follow, and "all of the boundary terms in Eq. 13.44 vanish due to either geometric boundary conditions or natural boundary conditions, regardless of the type of support at each end" (PDF p. 421). The notation with the boundary term \(b\) is ours. The name is narrower than the general one, which calls the operator \(D\) self-adjoint when \(\int (Du)\,v\,\mathrm{d}x = \int u\,(Dv)\,\mathrm{d}x\) (EP (6.2.33), p. 282), see the paragraph after lemma 13.69.

Does every listed condition really give \(b = 0\)? For second order, \(b(u, v) = -S\,(u'(L)\,v(L) - u'(0)\,v(0))\), and at each end \(u'\) and \(v\) are values of functions that satisfy the condition: at a fixed end \(v = 0\), at a free end \(u' = 0\). For the beam, \(b(u, v) = EI\,\big(u'''(L)v(L) - u''(L)v'(L) - u'''(0)v(0) + u''(0)v'(0)\big)\), with two products at each end: the shear force \(V\), up to sign \(EI\,u'''\), times the deflection \(v\) and the bending moment \(M\), up to sign \(EI\,u''\), times the rotation \(v'\). Figure 13.105 shows the end \(x = L\) for each condition in its own row. The clamped end is held by a wall. The force and the moment act, but the deflection and the rotation are zero, so both products are zero. The simply supported end lies on a pin that holds it on the axis and about which it rotates freely. The deflection is zero, the rotation is not, and the moment is zero because the pin cannot carry it. Again both products are zero. The free end moves and rotates, but neither a force nor a moment acts on it, so both products are zero. At each end therefore \(b = 0\), for every combination of the two ends. The cantilever of section 13.6 is clamped at \(x = 0\) and free at \(x = L\), so it is self-adjoint.

Which ends does the definition exclude? An end with a spring of stiffness \(k\) has the condition \(S u'(L) = -k\,u(L)\). Why? We take the end as a point without mass, so by Newton's law the forces on it add up to zero. The spring, compressed by the displacement \(u(L)\), pushes the end back with the force \(-k\,u(L)\). The string pulls it with the transverse force \(-S u'(L)\), just as the left neighbour pulls the piece of string in figure 13.4. From \(-S u'(L) - k\,u(L) = 0\) the condition follows, so the slope of the string at the end has the opposite sign to the displacement. The mass of the end is neglected here: with a mass \(M_{\mathrm{k}}\) the right side of the condition would be \(M_{\mathrm{k}}\,\omega^2 u(L) - k\,u(L)\) and the condition would depend on the frequency, as for the disk below. For a massless spring, therefore, \(b(u, v) = k\,u(L)\,v(L) \ne 0\). The boundary term is not zero for a shaft with a disk at \(x = L\) either (section 13.3): the torque \(G I_{\mathrm{p}}\,\varphi'(L)\) there equals \(\omega^2 J_{\mathrm{d}}\,\varphi(L)\), a condition that is homogeneous but depends on the frequency. The theorem below does not hold for such ends in the form stated, but it does not fail everywhere in the same way. For a spring the boundary term \(k\,u(L)\,v(L)\) is symmetric in \(u\) and \(v\), so mass orthogonality stays and the stiffness orthogonality \(B(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}, \varphi_{\mathrm{j}}) = 0\) fails (an example is below). For a disk the boundary term contains \(\lambda\) and is not symmetric, so it is the other way round: mass orthogonality fails, stiffness orthogonality stays. The same procedure gives orthogonality for a spring in the stiffness form \(B(u, v) + k\,u(L)\,v(L)\) and for a disk in the mass form \((u, v)_{\mathrm{m}} + J_{\mathrm{d}}\,u(L)\,v(L)\), because the boundary term is moved into the form. In this chapter we treat only conditions without springs and disks, so we do not develop this. CK says in footnote 4 (PDF p. 422) that the orthogonality equations take another form when springs and masses are attached at the ends, and leaves the derivation to the problems.

An example with a spring. A string with \(S = m = L = 1\) (all without units), clamped at \(x = 0\), has a spring with \(k = 1\) at \(x = L\), so \(S\,u'(L) = -k\,u(L)\). The modes are \(\varphi = \sin\beta x\), because \(\varphi(0) = 0\). The condition at \(x = L\) gives \(\beta\cos\beta = -\sin\beta\), with first roots \(\beta_1 = 2{,}02876\) and \(\beta_2 = 4{,}91318\) and eigenvalues \(\lambda_1 = 4{,}1159\) and \(\lambda_2 = 24{,}1393\). The values at the end, where the spring is, are \(\varphi_1(L) = 0{,}89696\) and \(\varphi_2(L) = -0{,}97991\): the first mode has the end above the axis, the second below it. Figure 13.106 shows at the top the clamp at \(x = 0\), the spring at \(x = L\) and the first and second mode above the axis of the string. Below them are the product \(\varphi_1\varphi_2\) above its axis, with a positive and a negative area, and the product of derivatives \(\varphi_1'\varphi_2'\) above its axis, with areas that do not cancel. At the bottom is the length \(L\). A calculation with 30 digits gives

\[\int_0^1 \varphi_1\varphi_2\,\mathrm{d}x = 0 \ \text{(up to rounding, } 5 \cdot 10^{-35}\text{)} , \qquad B(\varphi_1, \varphi_2) = \int_0^1 \varphi_1'\varphi_2'\,\mathrm{d}x = 0{,}8789 ,\]

so the mass product is zero, because the positive and negative area cancel, and the stiffness form is not, because the areas of the derivatives do not. The reason is visible in equation (13.70), which we prove in lemma 13.69: \(\lambda_1\,(\varphi_1, \varphi_2)_{\mathrm{m}} = B(\varphi_1, \varphi_2) + b(\varphi_1, \varphi_2)\). The left side is zero, hence \(B(\varphi_1, \varphi_2) = -b(\varphi_1, \varphi_2) = -k\,\varphi_1(L)\,\varphi_2(L) =\) \(0{,}8789\), the difference of the blue and the orange area of the derivatives. With the boundary term moved into the form, \(B(\varphi_1, \varphi_2) + k\,\varphi_1(L)\,\varphi_2(L) = 0{,}8789 - 0{,}8789 = 0\) (to \(10^{-30}\)). The same holds for the last claim of the theorem: \(B(\varphi_1, \varphi_1) = 1{,}6557\), \(\lambda_1\,(\varphi_1, \varphi_1)_{\mathrm{m}} = 4{,}1159 \cdot 0{,}59774 = 2{,}4602\), and the difference \(0{,}8045 = k\,\varphi_1(L)^2\) is exactly the boundary term.

13.7.2 Integration by parts for the eigenvalue problem

We must show that \(\int (Du)\,v\,\mathrm{d}x\) (for second order, up to sign, the area to the left of the curve) is the sum of the stiffness form and the boundary term. This is the computation that CK uses in (13.44), written for both orders at once.

Lemma 13.69 Integration by parts for the second and fourth order systems (integracija po delih za sistem drugega in četrtega reda)

Let \(S\), \(m\) or \(EI\) be constant positive numbers. Let \(u\) and \(v\) be real functions on \([0, L]\), twice continuously differentiable for second order, four times for the beam. Then, as the integration by parts in the figure shows,

\[\int_0^L (Du)\,v\,\mathrm{d}x = B(u, v) + b(u, v) , \tag{13.70}\]

and therefore

\[\int_0^L (Du)\,v\,\mathrm{d}x - \int_0^L u\,(Dv)\,\mathrm{d}x = b(u, v) - b(v, u) . \tag{13.71}\]
Proof

We must show that integration by parts from lemma 6.13, once for second order and twice for the beam, gives exactly the stiffness form and the boundary term. The functions we insert are continuously differentiable, hence piecewise continuously differentiable with a single piece (definition 6.11).

  1. Second order. Take \(N = S\,u'\) and \(w = v\) in lemma 6.13: \(\int_0^L S u'\,v'\,\mathrm{d}x\) \(=\) \(\big[S\,u'\,v\big]_0^L\) \(-\) \(\int_0^L S u''\,v\,\mathrm{d}x\). The last term is \(\int_0^L (Du)\,v\,\mathrm{d}x\) with a minus sign, so \(\int_0^L (Du)\,v\,\mathrm{d}x = B(u, v) - S\,\big[u'\,v\big]_0^L = B(u, v) + b(u, v)\).
  2. Beam, first part. Take \(N = EI\,u'''\) and \(w = v\): \(\int_0^L EI\,u'''\,v'\,\mathrm{d}x\) \(=\) \(\big[EI\,u'''\,v\big]_0^L\) \(-\) \(\int_0^L EI\,u''''\,v\,\mathrm{d}x\). Hence \(\int_0^L (Du)\,v\,\mathrm{d}x = \big[EI\,u'''\,v\big]_0^L - \int_0^L EI\,u'''\,v'\,\mathrm{d}x\).
  3. Beam, second part. Take \(N = EI\,u''\) and \(w = v'\) (the function \(v'\) is continuously differentiable): \(\int_0^L EI\,u''\,v''\,\mathrm{d}x\) \(=\) \(\big[EI\,u''\,v'\big]_0^L\) \(-\) \(\int_0^L EI\,u'''\,v'\,\mathrm{d}x\). The last term is therefore \(-\int_0^L EI\,u'''\,v' \mathrm{d}x = B(u, v) - \big[EI\,u''\,v'\big]_0^L\).
  4. Inserting the last expression into step 2 gives \(\int_0^L (Du)\,v\,\mathrm{d}x = B(u, v) + EI\,\big[u'''\,v - u''\,v'\big]_0^L = B(u, v) + b(u, v)\). This is equation (13.70) for the beam.
  5. We write equation (13.70) once more with the functions swapped, \(\int_0^L (Dv)\,u\,\mathrm{d}x = B(v, u) + b(v, u)\), and subtract. Since \(B(u, v) = B(v, u)\), the stiffness forms cancel, which gives (13.71).

Source: CK (13.43) and (13.44) (PDF p. 421): integration by parts "twice" for the beam. The notation with the boundary term \(b\), the second order case and the lemma on a single piece are ours.

The lemma says that the difference \(\int (Du)\,v - \int u\,(Dv)\), which for matrices is \(\vek v^\T\mat K\,\vek u - \vek u^\T\mat K\,\vek v = 0\), is here equal to the difference of the boundary terms. With self-adjoint conditions both are zero, so \(D\) is symmetric in the same sense as \(\mat K\): \(\int (Du)\,v = \int u\,(Dv)\). This is the origin of the name in definition 13.68. In general, the operator \(D\) on functions that satisfy the boundary conditions is called symmetric or self-adjoint (simetričen ali samoadjungiran) as soon as this equality holds for every pair of such functions (EP (6.2.33), p. 282), so by (13.71) as soon as \(b(u, v) = b(v, u)\). In this wider sense the operator at an end with a spring is self-adjoint too, since there \(b(u, v) = k\,u(L)\,v(L) = b(v, u)\). Our definition asks for more, \(b = 0\), because with it the theorem below also gives the stiffness orthogonality \(B(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}, \varphi_{\mathrm{j}}) = 0\), which a spring does not have (the spring example above).

13.7.3 Orthogonality in mass and stiffness

Theorem 13.70 Orthogonality of the modes of continuous systems (ortogonalnost načinov zveznih sistemov)

Let a string, bar, shaft or beam have the properties of section 13.7.1 and self-adjoint boundary conditions (definition 13.68). Let \((\lambda_{\mathrm{i}}, \varphi_{\mathrm{i}})\) and \((\lambda_{\mathrm{j}}, \varphi_{\mathrm{j}})\) be modes in this sense: \(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\) are nonzero real functions, twice (for the beam four times) continuously differentiable, that satisfy the boundary conditions and equation (13.67) with \(\lambda_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(\lambda_{\mathrm{j}}\). For \(\lambda > 0\) this is a mode of definition 13.6 with \(\omega = \sqrt\lambda\), but we also allow \(\lambda = 0\). Let \(\lambda_{\mathrm{i}} \ne \lambda_{\mathrm{j}}\). Then

\[(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}, \varphi_{\mathrm{j}})_{\mathrm{m}} = \int_0^L m\,\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\,\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{d}x = 0 \qquad \text{and} \qquad B(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}, \varphi_{\mathrm{j}}) = 0 . \tag{13.72}\]

For every mode, moreover, \(B(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}, \varphi_{\mathrm{i}}) = \lambda_{\mathrm{i}}\,(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}, \varphi_{\mathrm{i}})_{\mathrm{m}}\).

Proof

We must show that the difference of the equations for modes \(i\) and \(j\) gives \((\lambda_{\mathrm{i}} - \lambda_{\mathrm{j}})(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}, \varphi_{\mathrm{j}})_{\mathrm{m}} = 0\), and that the stiffness form is then exactly \(\lambda_{\mathrm{i}}\) times the mass form. We use lemma 13.69. We may use the lemma, because by the hypothesis of the theorem the modes are twice, for the beam four times, continuously differentiable.

  1. Multiply the equation \(D\varphi_{\mathrm{i}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{i}}\,m\,\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\) by \(\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\) and integrate over \([0, L]\). By linearity of the integral (lemma 6.10, item 2), \(\int_0^L (D\varphi_{\mathrm{i}})\,\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{d}x = \lambda_{\mathrm{i}}\,\)\((\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}, \varphi_{\mathrm{j}})_{\mathrm{m}}\). The same for mode \(j\) multiplied by \(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\): \(\int_0^L \varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\,(D\varphi_{\mathrm{j}})\,\mathrm{d}x = \lambda_{\mathrm{j}}\,(\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}, \varphi_{\mathrm{i}})_{\mathrm{m}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{j}}\,(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}, \varphi_{\mathrm{j}})_{\mathrm{m}}\), because the mass form is symmetric.
  2. Subtract the second equation from the first and use (13.71): \((\lambda_{\mathrm{i}} - \lambda_{\mathrm{j}})\,(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}, \varphi_{\mathrm{j}})_{\mathrm{m}} = b(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}, \varphi_{\mathrm{j}}) - b(\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}, \varphi_{\mathrm{i}})\).
  3. The functions \(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\) satisfy self-adjoint boundary conditions, so by definition 13.68 \(b(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}, \varphi_{\mathrm{j}}) = 0\) (for the beam at each end) and \(b(\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}, \varphi_{\mathrm{i}}) = 0\). The right side of step 2 is zero.
  4. Since \(\lambda_{\mathrm{i}} \ne \lambda_{\mathrm{j}}\), \((\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}, \varphi_{\mathrm{j}})_{\mathrm{m}} = 0\). This is the first equation in (13.72).
  5. Equation (13.70) for \(u = \varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(v = \varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\) together with step 1 gives \(\lambda_{\mathrm{i}}\,(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}, \varphi_{\mathrm{j}})_{\mathrm{m}} = B(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}, \varphi_{\mathrm{j}}) + b(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}, \varphi_{\mathrm{j}})\). By step 3, \(b = 0\), by step 4 the left side is zero, so \(B(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}, \varphi_{\mathrm{j}}) = 0\).
  6. For the last claim insert \(j = i\) in step 1 (the first equation, which does not need \(\lambda_{\mathrm{i}} \ne \lambda_{\mathrm{j}}\)) and \(u = v = \varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\) in (13.70): \(\lambda_{\mathrm{i}}\,(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}, \varphi_{\mathrm{i}})_{\mathrm{m}}\) \(=\) \(B(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}, \varphi_{\mathrm{i}})\) \(+ b(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}, \varphi_{\mathrm{i}}) = B(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}, \varphi_{\mathrm{i}})\), because by definition 13.68 \(b = 0\) for every pair of functions that satisfy the boundary conditions, also for \(u = v = \varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\).

Source: CK (13.46) to (13.51) (PDF pp. 421-422) for the beam and (13.43)-(13.45) for the relation between \(K_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(M_{\mathrm{r}}\). GR (4.68) to (4.72) (PDF p. 251) for a three-dimensional body with the same idea. The notation for second order and with the boundary term \(b\) is ours.

The last claim of the theorem is shown by figure 13.107 for the first mode of the cantilever. At the top, above the axis, is the mass form, the area under \(\varphi^2\). Below it, above the axis, is the stiffness form, the area under \((\varphi''/\beta^2)^2\). Both curves are squares, so they are not negative and neither area is negative. The blue area is \(L\) because we took the mode with the amplitude for which \(\int_0^L \varphi^2\,\mathrm{d}x = L\) (as in the test below), and the orange one is \(L\) as well. This is exactly the last claim of the theorem. For the cantilever \(\lambda = \omega^2 = EI\,\beta^4/m\) (section 13.6), so

\[B(\varphi, \varphi) = EI\,\beta^4\int_0^L \Big(\frac{\varphi''}{\beta^2}\Big)^2\,\mathrm{d}x , \qquad \lambda\,(\varphi, \varphi)_{\mathrm{m}} = EI\,\beta^4\int_0^L \varphi^2\,\mathrm{d}x ,\]

and the equality \(B(\varphi, \varphi) = \lambda\,(\varphi, \varphi)_{\mathrm{m}}\) is the equality of the orange and the blue area. At the bottom are two examples that we need in section 13.7.4: a constant, which has \(\varphi' = 0\), and a straight line through a support, which has \(\varphi'' = 0\). Below them is the length \(L\).

What did the theorem show? Two modes with different eigenvalues are orthogonal in the mass inner product and in the stiffness form. All we used is the symmetry of both forms and the vanishing of the boundary terms. Since, as we checked after the definition, clamped, simply supported and free ends are all self-adjoint, in every combination, the theorem holds for each such beam. For the string and the simply supported beam the modes are sines, and the theorem gives the orthogonality known from lemma 9.109 without computing the integrals. For the cantilever the modes are not sines, and the theorem still holds.

What happens when the condition fails is shown by the top of figure 13.108. The cantilever is clamped at \(x = 0\), its axis is straight. The mode \(\varphi_1\) and its triple have the same eigenvalue. Below them, above the axis, is the product \(3\varphi_1^2\), which is nonnegative everywhere, so its area is not zero. The condition \(\lambda_{\mathrm{i}} \ne \lambda_{\mathrm{j}}\) is essential for mass orthogonality (stiffness orthogonality follows from it), and in the computations of this chapter it is always met: in sections 13.2, 13.3 and 13.6 every root of the frequency equation gave a single mode, up to a multiple (for the beam by lemma 13.45). So two modes that are not multiples of each other have different eigenvalues, with one exception that we show below. For equal eigenvalues the modes would have to be chosen orthogonal, as for matrices (theorem 10.21).

Why does this mean "not multiples"? If \(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\) are modes with the same eigenvalue (except the rigid modes of a beam free at both ends), they are multiples of each other, say \(\varphi_{\mathrm{j}} = 3\,\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\), and the mass product is not zero: \((\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}, 3\varphi_{\mathrm{i}})_{\mathrm{m}} = 3\int_0^L m\,\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x > 0\), because the integrand is continuous, nonnegative and not zero everywhere (lemma 6.10, item 3). For the cantilever of this section with the amplitude \(\int_0^L\varphi_1^2\,\mathrm{d}x = L\) (\(m = 6{,}24\) kg/m, \(L = 1\) m) this is \(3\,m\,L = 18{,}72\) kg, while the product of the modes \(\varphi_1\) and \(\varphi_2\) with different eigenvalues is zero up to rounding (below).

The exception is a beam free at both ends. At \(\lambda = 0\) it has two rigid modes (theorem 13.59, item 2), shown at the top of figure 13.109. A beam without supports, lying on the axis, can have all its points raised by the same amount, which is mode \(1\), or be rotated about the left end, which is mode \(x\). The modes are not multiples of each other and are not orthogonal: their product \(1 \cdot x\) is nonnegative everywhere, its area is \(L^2/2\), so \((1, x)_{\mathrm{m}} = m L^2/2\). For this beam we therefore choose orthogonal rigid modes, for example \(1\) and the rotation about the middle \(x - L/2\). Their product has before the middle a negative and after it a positive area, both of size \(L^2/8\), so \(\int_0^L m\,(x - L/2)\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\).

We test the theorem on the cantilever of chapter 5: steel, section \(20 \times 40\) mm, \(L = 1\) m, \(EI = 20\,906{,}7\) N m² and \(m = \rho A = 6{,}24\) kg/m. By section 13.6, with \(\cos\beta_{\mathrm{k}} L\,\cosh\beta_{\mathrm{k}} L = -1\) and \(\beta_1 L = 1{,}8751\), \(\beta_2 L = 4{,}6941\), the modes are

\[\begin{aligned} \varphi_{\mathrm{k}}(x) &= \cosh\beta_{\mathrm{k}} x - \cos\beta_{\mathrm{k}} x - \sigma_{\mathrm{k}}\,\big(\sinh\beta_{\mathrm{k}} x - \sin\beta_{\mathrm{k}} x\big) , \\ \sigma_{\mathrm{k}} &= \frac{\cosh\beta_{\mathrm{k}} L + \cos\beta_{\mathrm{k}} L}{\sinh\beta_{\mathrm{k}} L + \sin\beta_{\mathrm{k}} L} . \end{aligned} \tag{13.73}\]

For these shapes \(\int_0^L \varphi_{\mathrm{k}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x = L\) without any extra factor (a computation with 30 digits gives \(1\) m for both shapes at \(L = 1\) m). At \(x = 0\), \(\varphi_{\mathrm{k}} = \varphi_{\mathrm{k}}' = 0\), at \(x = L\), \(\varphi_{\mathrm{k}}'' = 0\) by the definition of the number \(\sigma_{\mathrm{k}}\), and \(\varphi_{\mathrm{k}}'''(L) = \beta_{\mathrm{k}}^3\,[\sinh\beta_{\mathrm{k}} L - \sin\beta_{\mathrm{k}} L - \sigma_{\mathrm{k}}(\cosh\beta_{\mathrm{k}} L + \cos\beta_{\mathrm{k}} L)] = 0\) because \((\cosh\beta_{\mathrm{k}} L + \cos\beta_{\mathrm{k}} L)^2 - (\sinh\beta_{\mathrm{k}} L - \sin\beta_{\mathrm{k}} L)(\sinh\beta_{\mathrm{k}} L + \sin\beta_{\mathrm{k}} L) = 2 + 2\cosh\beta_{\mathrm{k}} L\,\cos\beta_{\mathrm{k}} L = 0\). For these modes \(\varphi_1(L) = 2\) and \(\varphi_2(L) = -2\). These are the shapes \(\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) of equation (13.56) without an extra factor, and figure 13.87 divides them by the end value so that it is \(1\) there. A computation with 30 digits gives

\[\Big|\int_0^L \varphi_1\,\varphi_2\,\mathrm{d}x\Big| < 10^{-29}\,L , \qquad \Big|EI\int_0^L \varphi_1''\,\varphi_2''\,\mathrm{d}x\Big| < 10^{-24}\ \text{N/m} ,\]

so zero up to rounding. For comparison, \(EI\int_0^L \varphi_1''^2\,\mathrm{d}x = 258\,456\) N/m and \(EI\int_0^L \varphi_2''^2\,\mathrm{d}x = 1{,}0151 \cdot 10^{7}\) N/m. Their ratio is \(39{,}27 = (202{,}99/32{,}39)^2\), as the last claim of the theorem requires: \(B(\varphi_{\mathrm{k}}, \varphi_{\mathrm{k}}) = \omega_{\mathrm{k}}^2\,m\,L\) with \(\omega_1^2 = (2\pi \cdot 32{,}39\ \text{Hz})^2\) and \(m L = 6{,}24\) kg, that is \(258\,456\) N/m. In figure 13.101 from the start of the section we now read off the numbers too. The node of mode 2 is at \(x_0 = 0{,}7834\,L\), the areas before and after it are \(0{,}3827\,L\) and \(-0{,}3827\,L\). At the bottom, above the axis, is the product of second derivatives \(\varphi_1''\varphi_2''\), which the stiffness form needs: the blue and the orange area cancel, and they are separated by the node \(x_1 = 0{,}2166\,L\) of the second derivative of mode 2. Below it is the length \(L\).

Orthogonality is a property of two modes, not of a mode and an arbitrary function. Take the same cantilever and the function \(\psi(x) = x^2\) (in metres, \(L = 1\) m). It satisfies the clamp, \(\psi(0) = \psi'(0) = 0\), but it is not a mode: at the free end \(\psi''(L) = 2 \ne 0\). The lower part of figure 13.108 shows the mode \(\varphi_1\) and the function \(\psi\) above the axis, and below them, above the axis, the product \(\varphi_1\psi\) with its area and the length \(L\). With the amplitude \(\int_0^L\varphi_1^2\,\mathrm{d}x = L\) we have

\[\int_0^L \varphi_1\,\psi\,\mathrm{d}x = 0{,}4454\ \text{m}^3 , \qquad EI\int_0^L \varphi_1''\,\psi''\,\mathrm{d}x = 2\,EI\,\varphi_1'(L) = 115\,113\ \text{N m} .\]

The first value is not zero: the cosine of the angle between \(\varphi_1\) and \(\psi\) is \(0{,}4454/\sqrt{1 \cdot 0{,}2} = 0{,}9959\), because \(\int_0^L\psi^2\,\mathrm{d}x = 1/5\) and \(\int_0^L\varphi_1^2\,\mathrm{d}x = 1\). The second is not zero either, and the two products are tied together as equation (13.70) says: \(\lambda_1\,(\varphi_1, \psi)_{\mathrm{m}} = (203{,}517\ \text{1/s})^2 \cdot 6{,}24\ \text{kg/m} \cdot 0{,}445386\ \text{m}^3 = 115\,113\ \text{N m}\). The boundary term \(b(\varphi_1, \psi) = 0\), because \(\varphi_1\) satisfies the conditions of the free end, but \(b(\psi, \varphi_1) = -2\,EI\,\varphi_1'(L) \ne 0\), because \(\psi\) does not satisfy them. The difference \(b(\varphi_1, \psi) - b(\psi, \varphi_1)\) is not zero and the theorem does not apply, because it requires both functions to be modes, and \(\psi\) satisfies neither the conditions of the free end nor equation (13.67), since \(EI\,\psi'''' = 0 \ne \lambda\,m\,\psi\).

Boundary conditions alone are not enough. In figure 13.110, at the clamp of the same cantilever, above the axis there is again the mode \(\varphi_1\), and beside it the function \(w = x^2(6L^2 - 4Lx + x^2)\). Since \(w'''' = 24\), \(q\,w/(24\,EI)\) is the solution of the equation \(EI\,w'''' = q\), so the deflection under a uniform load \(q\). The function \(w\) satisfies all four conditions of the cantilever, \(w(0) = w'(0) = 0\) and \(w''(L) = w'''(L) = 0\), so both boundary terms \(b(\varphi_1, w)\) and \(b(w, \varphi_1)\) are zero. It is not a mode, because \(w''''\) equals \(24\) everywhere and is not proportional to \(w\), as equation (13.67) requires. Both curves are above the axis, so their product above the axis is nonnegative everywhere and its area at \(L = 1\) m is not zero but \(\int_0^L \varphi_1\,w\,\mathrm{d}x = 1{,}5201\ \mathrm{m}^5\). Orthogonality therefore needs equation (13.67) for both functions, not only the boundary conditions.

13.7.4 Real frequencies

Figure 13.111 shows two motions along time \(t\). At the top, for real \(\omega\), the motion is \(\cos\omega t\) about the equilibrium. At the bottom, for complex \(\omega = a - \mathrm i\sigma\) with \(\sigma > 0\), the motion is \(\mathrm e^{\sigma t}\cos a t\), the real part of \(\mathrm e^{\mathrm i\omega t} = \mathrm e^{\sigma t}\,\mathrm e^{\mathrm i a t}\), about the equilibrium, and the envelope \(\mathrm e^{\sigma t}\) grows. In the sections on modes we took \(\omega\) to be a real number. For matrices, lemma 10.26 guaranteed it: a complex \(\omega\) would give a solution that grows (for \(\sigma > 0\)) or decays (for \(\sigma < 0\)) in time. A system without damping cannot do this, because its energy is conserved (for finitely many degrees of freedom theorem 8.121): a growing amplitude would need energy that nothing supplies, a decaying one would lose it without a damper. We must show the same for continuous systems.

Lemma 13.71 Eigenvalues of continuous systems are real and nonnegative (lastne vrednosti zveznih sistemov so realne in nenegativne)

Let the system be as in theorem 13.70 with self-adjoint boundary conditions. If for a complex number \(\lambda\) and a complex function \(\varphi \ne 0\) on \([0, L]\) (twice continuously differentiable, for the beam four times) \(D\varphi = \lambda\,m\,\varphi\) holds and \(\varphi\) satisfies the boundary conditions, then \(\lambda\) is real and \(\lambda \ge 0\). If \(\lambda = 0\), then \(\varphi\) is a constant (second order) or a polynomial of degree at most one (beam) that satisfies the boundary conditions, hence a rigid-body mode (as in definition 10.33). The mode can be chosen real.

Proof

We must show that \(\lambda\) is the quotient of a numerator and a denominator, two real nonnegative numbers, of which the denominator is positive. As in lemma 10.26 we multiply by \(\bar\varphi\), but instead of separating the imaginary part we use lemma 13.69. We extend the forms \(B\), \(b\) and \((\cdot, \cdot)_{\mathrm{m}}\) to complex functions with the same formulas. Equation (13.70) holds there too, because both sides are bilinear in \(u\) and \(v\), and they can be expanded into the real and imaginary parts of the functions.

  1. Let \(\varphi = u_1 + \mathrm i\,u_2\) with real functions \(u_1\), \(u_2\). The boundary conditions are linear and real, so \(u_1\) and \(u_2\) satisfy them too (separate the real and the imaginary part of the equations). By definition 13.68, \(b(u_1, u_1) = b(u_1, u_2) = b(u_2, u_1) = b(u_2, u_2) = 0\) (for the beam at each end).
  2. Since \(b\) is bilinear, \(b(\varphi, \bar\varphi) = b(u_1, u_1) - \mathrm i\,b(u_1, u_2) + \mathrm i\,b(u_2, u_1) + b(u_2, u_2) = 0\).
  3. Multiply the equation \(D\varphi = \lambda\,m\,\varphi\) by \(\bar\varphi\) and integrate, then use (13.70) for \(u = \varphi\) and \(v = \bar\varphi\) and step 2: \(\lambda\int_0^L m\,|\varphi|^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) \(=\) \(B(\varphi, \bar\varphi)\) \(+ b(\varphi, \bar\varphi) = B(\varphi, \bar\varphi)\).
  4. The stiffness form \(B(\varphi, \bar\varphi)\) is \(\int_0^L S\,|\varphi'|^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) or \(\int_0^L EI\,|\varphi''|^2\,\mathrm{d}x\), hence a real number \(\ge 0\) by lemma 6.10, item 3, since the integrand is nonnegative and continuous.
  5. Since \(\varphi \ne 0\) and \(|\varphi|^2\) is continuous and nonnegative, \(\int_0^L m\,|\varphi|^2\,\mathrm{d}x > 0\) by the same item (if the integral were zero, \(|\varphi|^2 = 0\) everywhere). A quotient of two real numbers whose denominator is positive and whose numerator is nonnegative is real and nonnegative: \(\lambda \ge 0\).
  6. If \(\lambda = 0\), then \(B(\varphi, \bar\varphi) = 0\), so by item 3 of lemma 6.10 \(\varphi' = 0\) everywhere (second order) or \(\varphi'' = 0\) everywhere (beam). A function with zero derivative on an interval is a constant, a function with zero second derivative is a polynomial of degree at most one (fundamental theorem, lemma 6.12, twice). Of these, only what satisfies the boundary conditions remains for rigid-body modes.
  7. If \(\lambda\) is real (step 5), \(u_1\) and \(u_2\) solve the same real equation \(D u = \lambda\,m\,u\). One of them is nonzero and satisfies the boundary conditions by step 1. It is a real mode.

Source: The proof is not from a source. The same proof for matrices is in lemma 10.26 (CM (2.5.34) to (2.5.36), PDF pp. 92-93).

What did the lemma show? With self-adjoint conditions \(\omega = \sqrt\lambda\) is real, and for \(\lambda > 0\) the function \(\varphi(x)\cos\omega t\) is a true oscillation: every point oscillates with frequency \(\omega\) and does not move away from equilibrium. For \(\lambda = 0\) only a rigid-body mode is possible, for example a bar that is free at both ends, or a beam that is simply supported at one end and free at the other (a rigid rotation about the support). The cantilever has \(\lambda > 0\) because it excludes all rigid-body modes. This is the analogue of theorem 10.34 from chapter 10, where zero eigenvalues belonged to a positive semidefinite stiffness matrix.

The comparison with chapter 10 is therefore this.

There is one difference. The spectral theorem of chapter 10 also gave the existence of \(n\) modes and the completeness of the modes (defined for continuous systems in section 13.9.3): every column is a sum of modes. Here we have proved only orthogonality and reality for the modes that exist. That there are enough of them to expand an arbitrary function is the subject of section 13.8.
