Chapter 10 Vibration of systems with several degrees of freedom and modal analysis (nihanje sistemov z več prostostnimi stopnjami in modalna analiza)

10.6 The transfer matrix method (metoda prenosnih matrik)

We obtained the frequencies of the wagons from the eigenvalue problem \(\mat K\,\vek\varphi = \lambda\mat M\,\vek\varphi\). For a chain of \(N\) masses this means a determinant of order \(N\). A chain has a special structure, however: each mass feels only its two neighbours. So we can walk along the chain from one end to the other, element by element, carrying only two numbers with us. This is the transfer matrix method (metoda prenosnih matrik). Which two data suffice, and how do we carry them across a spring and across a mass?

Take three wagons on a straight track without friction, joined by two buffers, which we replace by springs as in section 10.5. This is the chain of masses of theorem 10.2 with \(N = 3\) and no walls, so we number the springs as there: the spring \(k_{\mathrm{i}}\) is between the masses \(i - 1\) and \(i\), hence we have \(k_2\) and \(k_3\). In thought we cut the chain just before each mass and just after it. The cut just before mass \(i\) is marked with a minus, the cut just after it with a plus. At each cut we ask two things: by how much this place of the chain is displaced, and with what force the spring in the cut pulls.

We observe an oscillation with one circular frequency \(\omega\) in which all parts of the chain move in step, \(q_{\mathrm{i}}(t) = U_{\mathrm{i}}\,g(t)\) with \(g(t) = a\cos\omega t + b\sin\omega t\). Why this motion? By lemma 10.14 the natural vibrations are exactly such synchronous motions, so the frequencies we seek are those at which such a motion exists without external forces. The assumption does one thing: since \(\ddot g = -\omega^2 g\), the acceleration of every mass equals \(-\omega^2\) times its displacement, and time drops out of the equation of motion. It does not hold for the transient motion after a blow, which is a sum of several modes (theorem 10.31), nor for a chain with dampers, where the parts are no longer in step. We therefore drop the common factor \(g(t)\) and compute with amplitudes.

Definition 10.36 State vector and transfer matrix (vektor stanja in prenosna matrika)

Let a chain of masses (theorem 10.2) vibrate synchronously with the circular frequency \(\omega\), \(q_{\mathrm{i}}(t) = U_{\mathrm{i}}\,g(t)\). At a cut of the chain let \(U\) be the amplitude of the displacement of that place and \(N\) the amplitude of the force with which the spring in the cut pulls, positive in tension and negative in compression. The state vector (vektor stanja) at the cut is the column

\[\vek z = \begin{pmatrix} U \\ N \end{pmatrix} .\]

It is a column of two numbers with different units, the metre and the newton, and not a vector in space. The transfer matrix (prenosna matrika) of an element of the chain is the \(2\times 2\) matrix that maps the state at the cut on the left side of the element to the state at the cut on its right side. The transfer matrix of a spring is called the field matrix (matrika polja), the transfer matrix of a mass the point matrix (matrika točke).

Source: GR Problem 2.7, equation (P2.7.c) (PDF p. 159): the transfer matrix relates the force and the displacement at one end of an element to the force and the displacement at the other. GR writes the state in the order (force, displacement) and with its own sign convention for the force. The names field matrix and point matrix are not from the source.

The name says what the matrix does: it transfers the state across an element. A field is a piece of the chain between two masses that has no mass and only flexibility, a point is a mass that we take as gathered at one place. Both matrices follow from laws we already know, Hooke's for the spring and Newton's for the mass.

Lemma 10.37 Field matrix and point matrix (matrika polja in matrika točke)

Let a chain of masses vibrate synchronously with the circular frequency \(\omega > 0\), with the states of definition 10.36. Then: It describes the spring and the mass in the figure.

  1. For a massless spring of stiffness \(k > 0\) with the left end \(a\) and the right end \(b\), In the figure this is the spring between its ends.
    \[\vek z_{\mathrm{b}} = \mat F\,\vek z_{\mathrm{a}} , \qquad \mat F = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1/k \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} . \tag{10.54}\]
  2. For a mass \(m\) with the state \(\vek z^-\) just before it and \(\vek z^+\) just after it, The mass has forces on both sides.
    \[\vek z^+ = \mat P\,\vek z^- , \qquad \mat P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -\omega^2 m & 1 \end{pmatrix} . \tag{10.55}\]
  3. \(\det\mat F = 1\) and \(\det\mat P = 1\). This holds for the field and for the point.
Proof

We must show two matrices. Each follows from one equation for the displacement and one for the force, on the two lower drawings of figure 10.58.

  1. The spring. The spring before is grey and dashed, the spring after orange. At the left end its neighbour pulls it to the left with the force \(N_{\mathrm{a}}\), at the right end to the right with the force \(N_{\mathrm{b}}\). The spring has no mass, so by Newton's law the sum of the forces on it is zero: \(N_{\mathrm{b}} - N_{\mathrm{a}} = 0\), hence \(N_{\mathrm{b}} = N_{\mathrm{a}}\).
  2. The left end moves by \(u_{\mathrm{a}}\), the right end by \(u_{\mathrm{b}}\). The spring stretches by the difference \(u_{\mathrm{b}} - u_{\mathrm{a}}\), and by Hooke's law this stretch equals the force divided by the stiffness: \(u_{\mathrm{b}} - u_{\mathrm{a}} = N_{\mathrm{a}}/k\), hence \(u_{\mathrm{b}} = u_{\mathrm{a}} + N_{\mathrm{a}}/k\).
  3. Steps 2 and 1 are the rows of equation (10.54): the first row \(U_{\mathrm{b}} = 1\cdot U_{\mathrm{a}} + (1/k)\,N_{\mathrm{a}}\), the second \(N_{\mathrm{b}} = 0\cdot U_{\mathrm{a}} + 1\cdot N_{\mathrm{a}}\). This is the first point.
  4. The mass. The mass before is grey and dashed, the mass after orange, displaced by \(u\). The mass is a rigid body, so the cuts just before and just after it have the same displacement: \(U^+ = U^-\).
  5. The left spring pulls the mass to the left with the force \(N^-\), the right one to the right with the force \(N^+\). Newton's law for the mass is \(m\,\ddot q = N^+ g - N^- g\). In synchronous motion \(\ddot q = -\omega^2 U g\), so \(-\omega^2 m\,U g = (N^+ - N^-)\,g\) at every time, hence \(N^+ = N^- - \omega^2 m\,U\).
  6. Steps 4 and 5 are the rows of equation (10.55). This is the second point.
  7. The determinant of a \(2\times 2\) matrix is the product of the diagonal entries minus the product of the other two: \(\det\mat F = 1\cdot 1 - (1/k)\cdot 0 = 1\) and \(\det\mat P = 1\cdot 1 - 0\cdot(-\omega^2 m) = 1\). This is the third point.

Source: GR Problem 2.13, parts 1 and 2 (PDF p. 164): the transfer matrix of a spring and the transfer matrix of a vibrating mass are set there as exercises without a solution. The derivation is ours.

The matrices can be read off the figure. In the field matrix the only real datum is the stretch \(N/k\): the force passes through the spring unchanged, and the displacement grows by the stretch. In the point matrix the only real datum is \(-\omega^2 m\): the displacement passes across the mass unchanged, and the force changes by what the mass uses for its acceleration. The assumption of a massless spring entered in step 1. A real spring of mass \(m_{\mathrm{v}}\) would give there \(N_{\mathrm{b}} - N_{\mathrm{a}} = -\omega^2 m_{\mathrm{v}} U\) with a displacement \(U\) somewhere between \(u_{\mathrm{a}}\) and \(u_{\mathrm{b}}\), so at most \(\omega^2 m_{\mathrm{v}}\) times the largest displacement. For a buffer of mass \(200\) kg, \(\omega = 10\) s\(^{-1}\) and a displacement of \(1\) mm this is \(20\) N next to the force \(2000\) N in the buffer, one per cent. When the mass of the spring is not small compared with the masses beside it, the spring itself becomes a bar with mass, which section 10.8 treats.

Now we walk along the chain. We start just before the first mass and carry the state from left to right:

\[\vek z_1^+ = \mat P_1\vek z_1^- , \quad \vek z_2^- = \mat F_2\vek z_1^+ , \quad \vek z_2^+ = \mat P_2\vek z_2^- , \quad \vek z_3^- = \mat F_3\vek z_2^+ , \quad \vek z_3^+ = \mat P_3\vek z_3^- .\]

Together \(\vek z_3^+ = \mat P_3\mat F_3\mat P_2\mat F_2\mat P_1\,\vek z_1^-\): the matrices multiply in the order in which the state travels, the rightmost acts first, \(\mat P_1\) of the first mass, the leftmost last, \(\mat P_3\) of the third mass. At both free ends there is no spring, so the force there is zero. The initial state is therefore \((U_1, 0)\), and the demand that the force be zero at the far end too is a single equation for \(\omega\). The theorem states this in general.

Theorem 10.38 Frequency equation from transfer matrices (frekvenčna enačba iz prenosnih matrik)

Let a chain of masses of theorem 10.2 be given, with \(N \ge 1\) masses \(m_{\mathrm{i}} > 0\), stiffnesses \(k_{\mathrm{i}} > 0\) for \(i = 2, \dots, N\), \(k_1 \ge 0\) and \(k_{\mathrm{N}+1} \ge 0\), and with the matrices \(\mat M\) and \(\mat K\). The left end is fixed if \(k_1 > 0\) and free if \(k_1 = 0\), and likewise the right end with \(k_{\mathrm{N}+1}\). For a real number \(\lambda\) let The chain is in the figure with a free end.

\[\mat P_{\mathrm{i}}(\lambda) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -\lambda m_{\mathrm{i}} & 1 \end{pmatrix} , \qquad \mat F_{\mathrm{i}} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1/k_{\mathrm{i}} \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \ \ (k_{\mathrm{i}} > 0) ,\]
\[\mat T(\lambda) = \mat E\,\mat P_{\mathrm{N}}\mat F_{\mathrm{N}}\mat P_{\mathrm{N}-1}\cdots\mat F_2\mat P_1\,\mat S , \tag{10.56}\]

where \(\mat S = \mat F_1\) for a fixed and \(\mat S = \mat I\) for a free left end, \(\mat E = \mat F_{\mathrm{N}+1}\) for a fixed and \(\mat E = \mat I\) for a free right end. Let \(D(\lambda)\) be the entry of the matrix \(\mat T(\lambda)\) given by the table:

left end right end \(D(\lambda)\)
free free \(T_{21}\)
fixed free \(T_{22}\)
free fixed \(T_{11}\)
fixed fixed \(T_{12}\)

Further let \(\vek a = (1, 0)\) for a free and \(\vek a = (0, 1)\) for a fixed left end, \(\vek z_1^- = \mat S\,\vek a\), \(\vek z_{\mathrm{i}}^+ = \mat P_{\mathrm{i}}\vek z_{\mathrm{i}}^-\) for \(i = 1, \dots, N\) and \(\vek z_{\mathrm{i}+1}^- = \mat F_{\mathrm{i}+1}\vek z_{\mathrm{i}}^+\) for \(i = 1, \dots, N - 1\), and let \(U_{\mathrm{i}}\) be the first entry of the column \(\vek z_{\mathrm{i}}^-\). Then: The initial state is just before the first mass, then across the first mass.

  1. The number \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue of the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\) (definition 10.13) exactly when \(D(\lambda) = 0\). The condition is the last row of the column.
  2. If \(D(\lambda) = 0\), then \(\vek U = (U_1, \dots, U_{\mathrm{N}})\) is an eigencolumn for \(\lambda\), and every eigencolumn for \(\lambda\) is a multiple of it. The column collects the displacements of the masses.
  3. \(\det\mat T(\lambda) = 1\) for every \(\lambda\). The determinant is the product of the determinants of the points and fields.
Proof

We must show that the walk along the chain by itself satisfies all rows of the eigenvalue equation except the last, and that the last equals \(D(\lambda)\) up to a positive factor. The steps point at figure 10.58, where \(N = 3\) and both ends are free. We denote the second entry of the column \(\vek z_{\mathrm{i}}^-\) by \(N_{\mathrm{i}}^-\) and that of \(\vek z_{\mathrm{i}}^+\) by \(N_{\mathrm{i}}^+\).

  1. Across mass \(i\), by the form of the matrix \(\mat P_{\mathrm{i}}\), the displacement stays \(U_{\mathrm{i}}\) and the force becomes
    \[N_{\mathrm{i}}^+ = N_{\mathrm{i}}^- - \lambda m_{\mathrm{i}} U_{\mathrm{i}} .\]
  2. Across the spring \(k_{\mathrm{i}+1}\), by the form of the matrix \(\mat F_{\mathrm{i}+1}\), the force stays, \(N_{\mathrm{i}+1}^- = N_{\mathrm{i}}^+\), and the displacement becomes \(U_{\mathrm{i}+1} = U_{\mathrm{i}} + N_{\mathrm{i}}^+/k_{\mathrm{i}+1}\). Hence for \(i = 1, \dots, N - 1\)
    \[N_{\mathrm{i}}^+ = N_{\mathrm{i}+1}^- = k_{\mathrm{i}+1}\,(U_{\mathrm{i}+1} - U_{\mathrm{i}}) :\]
    the force at every cut is the stiffness of the spring multiplied by its stretch.
  3. The left end. At a free end \(\vek z_1^- = (1, 0)\), so \(U_1 = 1\) and \(N_1^- = 0 = k_1 U_1\), because \(k_1 = 0\). At a fixed end \(\vek z_1^- = \mat F_1\,(0, 1) = (1/k_1, 1)\): the wall does not move, the spring \(k_1\) pulls with the force \(1\) and stretches by \(1/k_1\). So \(U_1 = 1/k_1\) and \(N_1^- = 1 = k_1 U_1\). In both cases \(N_1^- = k_1 U_1\) and \(U_1 \ne 0\).
  4. We write row \(i\) of the column \((\mat K - \lambda\mat M)\,\vek U\) with the entries of the matrix \(\mat K\) of theorem 10.2 (with \(U_0 = 0\)) and use steps 2 and 3, then step 1. For \(i < N\)
    \[k_{\mathrm{i}}\,(U_{\mathrm{i}} - U_{\mathrm{i}-1}) - k_{\mathrm{i}+1}\,(U_{\mathrm{i}+1} - U_{\mathrm{i}}) - \lambda m_{\mathrm{i}} U_{\mathrm{i}} = N_{\mathrm{i}}^- - N_{\mathrm{i}}^+ - \lambda m_{\mathrm{i}} U_{\mathrm{i}} = 0 .\]
    The first \(N - 1\) rows are therefore zero for every \(\lambda\): they are Newton's laws of the masses \(1\) to \(N - 1\), which we built into the point matrices.
  5. The last row is, by the same computation,
    \[k_{\mathrm{N}}\,(U_{\mathrm{N}} - U_{\mathrm{N}-1}) + k_{\mathrm{N}+1} U_{\mathrm{N}} - \lambda m_{\mathrm{N}} U_{\mathrm{N}} = N_{\mathrm{N}}^+ + k_{\mathrm{N}+1} U_{\mathrm{N}} .\]
    At a free right end \(k_{\mathrm{N}+1} = 0\) and \(\mat E = \mat I\), the row is \(N_{\mathrm{N}}^+\), the second entry of the column \(\vek z_{\mathrm{N}}^+ = \mat T\,\vek a\). This is \(T_{21}\) for \(\vek a = (1, 0)\) and \(T_{22}\) for \(\vek a = (0, 1)\). At a fixed right end the first entry of the column \(\mat T\,\vek a = \mat F_{\mathrm{N}+1}\vek z_{\mathrm{N}}^+\) equals \(U_{\mathrm{N}} + N_{\mathrm{N}}^+/k_{\mathrm{N}+1}\), the displacement of the wall, so the row is \(k_{\mathrm{N}+1}\) times \(T_{11}\) or \(T_{12}\). In all four cases the last row equals \(c\,D(\lambda)\) with \(c = 1\) or \(c = k_{\mathrm{N}+1} > 0\).
  6. Let \(D(\lambda) = 0\). By steps 4 and 5, \((\mat K - \lambda\mat M)\,\vek U = \vek 0\), and by step 3, \(\vek U \ne \vek 0\). So \((\lambda, \vek U)\) is an eigenpair.
  7. Conversely, let \((\lambda, \vek\varphi)\) be an eigenpair. Row \(i < N\) of the equation \((\mat K - \lambda\mat M)\,\vek\varphi = \vek 0\) determines \(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}+1}\) from \(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}-1}\) (with \(\varphi_0 = 0\)), since we may divide it by \(k_{\mathrm{i}+1} > 0\):
    \[\varphi_{\mathrm{i}+1} = \frac{(k_{\mathrm{i}} + k_{\mathrm{i}+1} - \lambda m_{\mathrm{i}})\,\varphi_{\mathrm{i}} - k_{\mathrm{i}}\,\varphi_{\mathrm{i}-1}}{k_{\mathrm{i}+1}} .\]
    The first entry \(\varphi_1\), the displacement at the left end, thus determines all the others. If \(\varphi_1 = 0\), all would be zero, but an eigencolumn is not the zero column. Hence \(\varphi_1 \ne 0\).
  8. The column \(\vek U\) satisfies the same rows (step 4), hence the same rule, which is linear. The columns \(\vek\varphi\) and \((\varphi_1/U_1)\,\vek U\) have the same first entry and are therefore equal. This is the second point.
  9. The last row of the column \((\mat K - \lambda\mat M)\,\vek\varphi\) is zero, so the last row for \(\vek U\) is zero too, that is \(c\,D(\lambda) = 0\) and \(D(\lambda) = 0\). With step 6 this is the first point.
  10. For \(2\times 2\) matrices with the entries \(a, b, c, d\) and \(e, f, g, h\) by rows, the determinant of the product is \((ae + bg)(cf + dh) - (af + bh)(ce + dg) = (ad - bc)(eh - fg)\), the product of the determinants. Every matrix in the product (10.56) has determinant \(1\), so \(\det\mat T = 1\). This is the third point.

Source: GR Problem 2.7 (PDF pp. 159 to 161): the product of the transfer matrices along the chain (P2.7.n), \(\det\mat T = 1\) (P2.7.d), and Table 2.2 with the four combinations of end conditions, each of which sets one block of the matrix to zero. GR treats general elements with several coordinates and blocks instead of entries. The form for the chain of masses, the walk in steps and the proof are not from the source.

The theorem showed three things. The two end conditions pick a single entry of the matrix \(\mat T\): the left end says which column we start with, the right end which row we demand. All other equations of the system are already in the matrices. The second point says something the spectral theorem does not give: a chain has no two different modes with the same frequency, since the first displacement determines all the others. The third point is a check of the computation: if the determinant of the product is not \(1\), the multiplication contains an error.

Example 10.39 Three equal wagons (trije enaki vagoni)

Let the masses be \(m_1 = m_2 = m_3 = m = 20\) t and the stiffnesses \(k_2 = k_3 = k = 2\cdot 10^6\) N/m, so \(k/m = 100\) s\(^{-2}\). We introduce the dimensionless number \(p = \lambda m/k\) to keep the matrices readable:

\[\mat P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -pk & 1 \end{pmatrix} , \qquad \mat F = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1/k \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} .\]

Both ends are free, so by theorem 10.38 we need only \(T_{21}\), the second entry of the column \(\mat T\,(1, 0)\). The whole product need not be computed. We carry the column \((1, 0)\) from cut to cut:

cut displacement \(U\) force \(N\)
\(\vek z_1^-\) \(1\) \(0\)
\(\vek z_1^+\) \(1\) \(-pk\)
\(\vek z_2^-\) \(1 - p\) \(-pk\)
\(\vek z_2^+\) \(1 - p\) \(-pk - pk\,(1 - p) = -pk\,(2 - p)\)
\(\vek z_3^-\) \(1 - p - p\,(2 - p) = 1 - 3p + p^2\) \(-pk\,(2 - p)\)
\(\vek z_3^+\) \(1 - 3p + p^2\) \(-pk\,(2 - p) - pk\,(1 - 3p + p^2) = -pk\,(1 - p)(3 - p)\)

In every row only one number changes: across a mass the force by \(-pk\) times the displacement, across a spring the displacement by the force divided by \(k\). The frequency equation is

\[T_{21} = -pk\,(1 - p)(3 - p) = 0 , \qquad p \in \{0,\ 1,\ 3\} ,\]

so \(\lambda = 0\), \(k/m\) and \(3k/m\):

\[\omega_1 = 0 , \qquad \omega_2 = \sqrt{k/m} = 10\ \text{s}^{-1} , \qquad \omega_3 = \sqrt{3k/m} = 17{,}321\ \text{s}^{-1} ,\]

that is \(0\), \(1{,}5915\) and \(2{,}7566\) Hz. We read the modes from the column of displacements \((1,\ 1 - p,\ 1 - 3p + p^2)\): for \(p = 0\) it is \((1, 1, 1)\), the rigid-body mode of definition 10.33, for \(p = 1\) it is \((1, 0, -1)\), the middle wagon is at rest and the outer two move in opposite directions, for \(p = 3\) it is \((1, -2, 1)\). In both elastic modes the sum of the displacements is zero, so the centre of mass is at rest, as corollary 10.35 demands.

Let us check with the eigenvalue problem of the same chain. By theorem 10.2

\[\mat K - \lambda\mat M = k\begin{pmatrix} 1 - p & -1 & 0 \\ -1 & 2 - p & -1 \\ 0 & -1 & 1 - p \end{pmatrix} ,\]

and expanding the determinant along the first row gives \(k^3\,(1 - p)\big[(2 - p)(1 - p) - 1\big] - k^3\,(1 - p) = -k^3\,p\,(1 - p)(3 - p)\). This is \(k^2\,T_{21}\): the same equation, the same roots. NumPy returns for the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\) the values \(\lambda = (0,\ 100,\ 300)\) s\(^{-2}\) and the modes \((1, 1, 1)\), \((1, 0, -1)\), \((1, -2, 1)\). To test the third point of the theorem we also compute the whole product at \(p = 1\): \(\mat T = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 1/k \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}\) with determinant \(1\).

For the wagons both ends were free. Another entry of the matrix takes its turn when one end is fixed.

Example 10.40 Frame A as a chain with a fixed end (okvir A kot veriga z vpetim koncem)

Frame A of section 10.1.1 has \(\mat M = m\,\mat I\) and \(\mat K = k\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\) with \(m = 500\) kg and \(k = 501\,760\) N/m. It is a chain of masses with \(N = 2\), \(k_1 = k_2 = k\) and \(k_3 = 0\): the ground is the wall, the lower floor is mass 1, the upper one mass 2, the columns of each storey are a spring. The left end is fixed, the right end free, so by the table of theorem 10.38 we need \(T_{22}\), the second entry of the column \(\mat P\mat F\mat P\mat F\,(0, 1)\). With \(p = \lambda m/k\) the column \((0, 1)\) goes like this:

\[\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \to \begin{pmatrix} 1/k \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \to \begin{pmatrix} 1/k \\ 1 - p \end{pmatrix} \to \begin{pmatrix} (2 - p)/k \\ 1 - p \end{pmatrix} \to \begin{pmatrix} (2 - p)/k \\ 1 - p - p\,(2 - p) \end{pmatrix} .\]

The frequency equation is \(T_{22} = 1 - 3p + p^2 = 0\) with the solutions \(p = (3 \mp \sqrt 5)/2 = 0{,}38197\) and \(2{,}61803\). With \(k/m = 1003{,}52\) s\(^{-2}\) we get \(\lambda_1 = 383{,}31\) s\(^{-2}\) and \(\lambda_2 = 2627{,}25\) s\(^{-2}\), so \(\omega_1 = 19{,}578\) s\(^{-1}\) and \(\omega_2 = 51{,}257\) s\(^{-1}\), as in section 10.2.1. The ratio of the floor displacements is \(U_2/U_1 = 2 - p\), that is \(1{,}618\) and \(-0{,}618\), also the same as there.

10.6.1 The shooting view: the residual and Holzer's method (strelski pogled: ostanek in Holzerjeva metoda)

In both examples we multiplied with letters and obtained a polynomial. For a long chain with different masses such a polynomial is unreadable. But the same walk can be done with numbers: we choose a trial frequency \(\omega\), start at the left end with the displacement \(1\) mm and zero force, walk to the right end and look at what is left there. At a free end the force should be zero. The force that is left is called the residual (ostanek) \(R(\omega)\). By step 5 of the proof of theorem 10.38 the residual is the last row of the eigenvalue equation, so \(R = D(\omega^2)\) multiplied by the initial displacement.

Let us try it on the three wagons with the buffers on the track at \(\omega = 9\) s\(^{-1}\) (the top row of figure 10.59). We start with \(N = 0\) and the displacement \(1\) mm. For its acceleration the first wagon needs the force \(\omega^2 m U = 81\cdot 20\,000\cdot 0{,}001\) N \(= 1620\) N to the left, and since there is nothing on its left, the first buffer must push it: the force in it is \(-1620\) N, compression. The buffer therefore shortens by \(1620/(2\cdot 10^6)\) m \(= 0{,}81\) mm and the second wagon is displaced by only \(0{,}19\) mm. It uses \(81\cdot 20\,000\cdot 0{,}00019\) N \(= 307{,}8\) N, so the second buffer carries \(-1927{,}8\) N, it shortens by \(0{,}9639\) mm and the third wagon lands at \(-0{,}774\) mm. The third wagon is displaced to the left and for its acceleration needs the force \(81\cdot 20\,000\cdot 0{,}0007739\) N \(= 1253{,}7\) N to the right. The second buffer pushes it to the right with the compression \(1927{,}8\) N, which is \(674{,}1\) N too much. The excess is the residual \(R = 1253{,}7\ \text{N} - 1927{,}8\ \text{N} = -674{,}1\) N: with a force of this size somebody would have to push the third wagon to the left from outside for the chain really to vibrate at \(\omega = 9\) s\(^{-1}\). There is no external force, so \(9\) s\(^{-1}\) is not a natural frequency.

At \(\omega = 10\) s\(^{-1}\) (the bottom row) the same computation with the same initial displacement \(1\) mm runs like this: the first buffer carries \(-2000\) N and shortens by a whole millimetre, so the second wagon is at rest and uses nothing, the second buffer carries the same \(-2000\) N, the third wagon lands at \(-1\) mm and for its acceleration needs exactly \(100\cdot 20\,000\cdot 0{,}001\) N \(= 2000\) N to the right, as much as the buffer gives it by its compression. The residual is zero: the chain vibrates like this by itself, \(10\) s\(^{-1}\) is a natural frequency and the displacements \((1, 0, -1)\) mm are its mode. Hence the name shooting method (strelska metoda): we set the frequency like the elevation of a barrel, the walk along the chain is the flight, the residual says by how much we missed the target at the other end, and we correct the setting by it. Holzer introduced it for torsional chains in 1921, and Myklestad extended it to bending in 1945 (GR, PDF p. 161), so it is also called Holzer's method (Holzerjeva metoda).

How do we correct the frequency? Let us draw the residual as a function of the trial frequency (figure 10.60). By example 10.39, with the start \(1\) mm,

\[R(\omega) = -20\,\omega^2\Big(1 - \frac{\omega^2}{100}\Big)\Big(3 - \frac{\omega^2}{100}\Big)\ \text{N} \qquad (\omega\ \text{in s}^{-1}) ,\]

which is the curve over the \(\omega\) axis. It crosses the axis at \(0\), \(10\) and \(17{,}32\) s\(^{-1}\), the natural frequencies. Between them it keeps its sign, negative below \(10\) s\(^{-1}\) and above \(17{,}32\) s\(^{-1}\), positive in between.

When we compute with numbers we do not know the curve, only single shots. Two suffice if their residuals have different signs: at \(9\) s\(^{-1}\), \(R = -674\) N, at \(11\) s\(^{-1}\), \(R = +910\) N. The residual is a polynomial in \(\omega\), hence a continuous function, and by the intermediate value theorem (theorem 2.79) it has a zero between \(9\) and \(11\) s\(^{-1}\). Where? We join the two points by a straight segment and take its intersection with the axis:

\[\omega = 9 + (11 - 9)\,\frac{674{,}1}{674{,}1 + 909{,}7}\ \text{s}^{-1} = 9{,}851\ \text{s}^{-1} .\]

A new shot at \(9{,}851\) s\(^{-1}\) gives \(R = -116{,}3\) N, the same step with the points \(11\) and \(9{,}851\) gives \(9{,}981\) s\(^{-1}\) with \(R = -14{,}8\) N, the next one, with the chord through the last two shots \(9{,}851\) and \(9{,}981\), gives \(10{,}0004\) s\(^{-1}\) with \(R = 0{,}34\) N. Three corrections give the frequency to four digits.

A change of sign is a reliable mark of a zero, but an equal sign is no mark that there is none. The shots at \(9\) and \(18\) s\(^{-1}\) give \(-674\) N and \(-3484\) N, both negative, yet there are two natural frequencies between them. So we scan the frequencies with a step smaller than the smallest distance between them, which we do not know beforehand, and check the result by counting: by the spectral theorem (theorem 10.21) a chain of \(N\) masses has \(N\) eigenvalues, here three.

The shape helps with the counting. The right part of the figure shows the displacements at the three natural frequencies: at \(0\) all are on the same side, at \(10\) s\(^{-1}\) the shape crosses zero once, at \(17{,}32\) s\(^{-1}\) twice. A place where a shape crosses zero is a node (vozel), the chain is at rest there. Each higher shape of our wagons has one node more. So if we find a shape with two nodes, we know that two more frequencies lie below it. Here we only observe this rule on the example and do not prove it.

In the lectures the two matrices are called the field matrix and the point matrix, the amplitudes are denoted \(X_{\mathrm{i}}\), and a shot starts with \(X_1 = 1\). According to GR (PDF p. 161) the transfer matrix method has lost much of its interest with the development of numerical matrix methods. It remains the shortest way for a hand computation of a chain, and it shows that the frequency equation is merely a condition at the far end of the chain. For systems that are not a chain, for example a truss, where a joint has more than two neighbours, there is no walk from one end to the other and the eigenvalue problem remains.
