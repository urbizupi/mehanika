The lecture of this chapter (figures built as it is explained)

We start this chapter with bodies that spin: a shaft with a disk in a turbine, a pump or a fan. We can already describe their motion in two separate parts, which figure 24.1 shows. The first part is a shaft that vibrates but does not spin. From the straight position it bends and returns to it. The shaft is a beam from chapter 5, which we compute with finite elements by chapter 12. The vibration of masses and springs was treated in chapters 9 and 10, and the bending vibration of a beam and the torsional vibration of a shaft with a disk in chapter 13. The second part is a rigid body that spins but does not vibrate, in the figure a disk on a rigid shaft that spins with angular velocity \(\Omega\). The equations of motion of a rigid body and its kinetic energy were written in chapter 8. Here we join the two kinds of knowledge and ask what changes when the shaft spins while it vibrates.

The chapter name has two words. A rotor (rotor) is, in mechanics, any body that turns about its own axis, here the shaft together with the disks mounted on it. Dynamics (dinamika) is the part of mechanics that computes motion under forces. Rotordynamics is therefore the computation of the motion of spinning shafts with disks, and above all of their vibrations across the axis, which the spin excites and changes. The rotor does not only vibrate, it also spins about its own axis, and its axis also circles about the bearing axis, the line through the two bearings. In the figure we see this at the disk centre \(S\), which goes round the bearing axis on a closed path. We will call this circling motion whirl (vrtinčenje).

Every fast machine shows why this matters. A turbine wheel, a pump rotor or a fan turns at a speed where even a small unbalance bends the shaft and loads the two bearings. The unbalance of chapter 23 here means that the centre of mass \(G\) of the disk is not exactly at the disk centre \(S\) on the shaft axis, but slightly to one side. When the disk spins, \(G\) circles about the axis and therefore has an acceleration toward the axis. Only the shaft can give it the force for this. By Newton's third law the disk therefore pulls the shaft outward, the shaft bends and the two bearings hold it. The displacement of the disk centre from the axis grows strongly at certain speeds. This is the resonance of chapter 9: the unbalance pulls the shaft in a direction that turns once per revolution, and when the spin speed matches the natural frequency of the shaft with the disk, the displacement builds up until damping limits it. So the designer must know where these speeds lie and either take the rotor through these ranges during run-up or avoid them, so that the machine runs far from them.

For a steel shaft with a disk of mass \(49\) kg, which we will compute in the chapter as example R1, \(G\) lies only \(0{,}05\) mm from \(S\), which is \(1/4000\) of the disk radius. Yet at the peak of the response at \(1484\) rev/min the disk centre moves \(1{,}25\) mm away from the bearing axis and each bearing carries \(739\) N, \(3{,}07\) times as much as under the weight of the disk, \(240\) N.

A spinning disk can also carry only a limited speed itself, because the spin stretches it. Each piece of the disk circles about the axis and has an acceleration toward the axis of size \(\Omega^2 r\), where \(\Omega\) is the angular velocity of the disk and \(r\) the distance of the piece from the axis. Only the neighbouring material, which pulls it, can give it the force for this, so the material of the disk is stretched. At twice the speed the acceleration is four times as large, and since the stress in a linear elastic disk grows in proportion to the load, the stress is four times as large too: at the centre of disk R1 it is \(12{,}70\) MPa at \(3000\) rev/min and \(50{,}8\) MPa at \(6000\) rev/min. At some speed the stress reaches what the material can still carry.

The chapter promises the following. First we take the simplest model, one disk at the middle of a shaft, and solve it completely, then compute the response to unbalance and the speed at which the response peaks. Then we look at how a disk that tilts resists the tilting, and how the whirl frequencies therefore change with the spin speed. Next we divide the shaft into finite elements and so treat rotors with several masses. We will show how damping in the spinning material can make a rotor unstable, how a rotor is balanced, and how stresses set the speed limit of a spinning disk. At the end we connect everything with the other chapters.
