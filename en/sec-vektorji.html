Chapter 1 Strain

1.5 Mathematics: points and vectors

Once again we put mechanics aside for now. To describe displacements in space we need two things. First we must say with numbers where a point is. Then we must say with numbers where and how far it has moved. We will build both from an experience everyone has, measuring a room with a tape measure.

1.5.1 Coordinates

How do we tell someone over the phone where a lamp hangs in a room? We can say: start in the corner of the room, walk 2 m along one wall, then 3 m along the other wall, and then 2 m up. Three numbers, \(2\), \(3\) and \(2\), fix the position of the lamp exactly, provided we have agreed on three things beforehand: at which point we start, in which three directions we walk, and in what order we list the numbers. We choose the directions perpendicular to each other, like the edges where the floor and the two walls meet in the corner of the room, and we measure distances along all three with the same tape.

Three light grey faces are two walls and the floor, which meet in the corner of the room. From the corner \(O\) come three blackwhite arrows, which show the directions of walking: along the edges in which the floor and the walls meet. They are labelled \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\). The path from the corner is drawn blue dashed. The first stretch is \(2\) m along the first wall, in the direction \(x\). The second is \(3\) m along the second wall, in the direction \(y\). The third is \(2\) m up, in the direction \(z\). The path ends at the lamp, which is a blue dot labelled \((2, 3, 2)\). The same numbers in another order are the other point. The orange dashed path first goes \(3\) m along the first wall (its first stretch lies over the blue first stretch and extends it), then \(2\) m along the second and \(2\) m up, and ends in an orange dot labelled \((3, 2, 2)\). The orange numbers \(3\), \(2\) and \(2\) stand beside the stretches of this path, the blue ones beside the blue path. The thick blackwhite arrow to the point \(Q = (3, 5, 0)\) is the flight of a fly, which we will discuss later.

From history

Apollonius of Perga had already related the points of a conic, that is, a curve obtained when a cone is cut by a plane, to the points of a straight line that he called the diameter. From the diameter he measured distances at a constant angle (figure 1.50), and the length of each was determined by the position of the point on the diameter (CAJORI, PDF p. 191). In 1637 René Descartes published, in his Discourse on Method, an essay of 106 pages on geometry (CAJORI, PDF p. 190). He noticed: if a problem has a finite number of solutions, it leads to an equation with one unknown. An equation with two unknowns, however, is satisfied by infinitely many points, so one must find the line on which all of them lie (CAJORI, PDF p. 190). He chose a straight line and associated each of its points with a point of the line sought, in such a way that it can be constructed once the first point is known (CAJORI, PDF p. 191). The new feature was an equation with two unknowns, from which for each value of one length the other can be computed (CAJORI, PDF p. 191). Such an equation does not give one number but a line, because each value of the first length gives the other length, and there are infinitely many such pairs, all lying together on a line.

Let us draw what Apollonius did. The blue closed curve is a conic, here an ellipse, drawn as an example. The grey line is its diameter. From two points of the diameter, shown by the blackwhite dots, each blackwhite segment goes to the ellipse. The ticks at the ends of the segments show that we are interested in their length. The points on the diameter are equally far on both sides from the centre of the ellipse, shown by the grey cross, so the segments are equally long. A point on the diameter and the length of a segment fix a point of the ellipse. In the left figure the angle between the diameter and the segment is right, as the small grey square shows. In the right figure is the same ellipse with an oblique diameter, its centre is again shown by the grey cross. The angle there is oblique, as the grey arc between the diameter and the right segment shows. The angle is the same for both segments, so the segments that start at the blackwhite dots on the diameter are parallel. They got the direction in such a way that the diameter would halve each segment that we extended across the diameter to the ellipse on the other side. At a right angle this holds because the ellipse is symmetric about the diameter. At an oblique angle it is a special direction that the ellipse determines.

The angle between the two lengths was usually a right angle with Descartes, but he wrote explicitly that it need not be (oblique angle) (CAJORI, PDF p. 191). He never formally introduced another line perpendicular to the first. Descartes and Fermat, and their successors down to the middle of the eighteenth century, used measuring at an oblique angle, that is, not at a right angle, more often than later mathematicians did (CAJORI, PDF p. 191). Fermat set down similar ideas in a treatise Ad locos planos et solidos isagoge (Introduction to Plane and Solid Loci), which was not published until 1679 (CAJORI, PDF p. 190). The agreement on three things that we described for the lamp has a name of its own. It is the simplest of several kinds of such agreements, and we will talk about the others at the end of this subsection.

Definition 1.12 Rectangular Cartesian coordinate system and coordinates (pravokotni kartezični koordinatni sistem in koordinate)

A rectangular Cartesian coordinate system (pravokotni kartezični koordinatni sistem) consists of a chosen point \(O\), called the origin (izhodišče), and three mutually perpendicular straight lines through \(O\), the coordinate axes (koordinatne osi) \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\). On each axis a positive direction is chosen, and lengths are measured in the same unit on all three axes. The position of a point \(P\) is described by three numbers, its coordinates (koordinate) \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\): we reach \(P\) if, starting at the origin, we walk the distance \(x\) in the direction of the axis \(x\), then the distance \(y\) in the direction of the axis \(y\), and then the distance \(z\) in the direction of the axis \(z\). A negative number means walking in the opposite direction. We write \(P = (x, y, z)\).

Source: KREYSZIG §9.1, Fig. 167 (PDF p. 382).

The words coordinate and axes of coordinates were first used by Leibniz, who introduced the word coordinate in 1692 (CAJORI, PDF p. 191, 227).

Let us return to the lamp of the room. Coordinates are numbers, and the coordinate system is the agreement that gives these numbers their meaning. The lamp of our example is the point \((2, 3, 2)\) if the axes \(x\) and \(y\) lie along the walls, the axis \(z\) points vertically up and the units are metres (figure 1.51). The order matters: the point \((3, 2, 2)\) is the second point, 3 m along the first wall and 2 m along the second. The direction of the axis \(y\) is fixed by the axes \(x\) and \(z\): seen from above, we go from the axis \(x\) to the axis \(y\) counterclockwise, as in the figure. This is called a right-handed system (desnosučni sistem), and in the book we use only this one (EP, p. 16). The right hand shows it: the thumb points along the axis \(x\), the index finger along the axis \(y\) and the middle finger along the axis \(z\). In a mirror system the same triple of numbers would mean the mirror position of the lamp, so we must agree on one system. We say that coordinates are an ordered triple (urejena trojica) of numbers. It matters which number stands in which place, because each place belongs to its own axis.

Let us draw this with unknown distances. Three blackwhite arrows are the axes, which start at the origin \(O\). The blue dashed path has three stretches. The first, labelled \(x\), goes from \(O\) along the axis \(x\) for the distance \(x\). The second, \(y\), goes parallel to the axis \(y\), the third, \(z\), parallel to the axis \(z\). The third stretch ends at the point \(P = (x, y, z)\), which is a blackwhite dot. A thin grey auxiliary line goes from the place \(y\) on the axis \(y\) parallel to the axis \(x\) to the end of the second stretch. It carries the distance \(y\), which we measure along the axis \(y\), to the end of the second stretch.

Let us draw the same lamp in two coordinate systems (figure 1.52).

Let the room be 5 m, 6 m and 3 m. We measure it only so that we can compare numbers. The grey box is the room. The origin \(O\) of the blue system is in the corner on the floor, as before. The blue path goes \(2\) m, \(3\) m and \(2\) m along the axes to the lamp. The origin \(O'\) of the orange system is in the opposite corner, under the ceiling. Its axes point into the room, so opposite to the previous ones, and downwards. The orange path goes \(3\) m, \(3\) m and \(1\) m to the same lamp, because the lamp is \(5 - 2 = 3\) m, \(6 - 3 = 3\) m and \(3 - 2 = 1\) m from the opposite walls.

In another coordinate system the same lamp has other coordinates. If we start in the opposite corner of the room, all three numbers change, while the lamp stays where it is. So coordinates always belong to a point together with a coordinate system. In each problem we will choose the system once and keep it. Other books often write coordinates as \(x_1\), \(x_2\), \(x_3\) or, for the position before loading, \(X_1\), \(X_2\), \(X_3\) (CM §3.2.1, PDF p. 111), because with numbered axes the calculations that repeat for all three directions are easier to write. As long as we can, we will stay with \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\).

Let us look at two more systems that are not rectangular Cartesian (figure 1.53).

On the left are oblique axes: the axis \(x\) is horizontal, the axis \(y\) makes an angle of \(60^\circ\) with it. We reach the point \(P\) along the path: \(2\) steps along the axis \(x\) and then \(1{,}5\) steps parallel to the axis \(y\). On the right are the axes \(x\), \(y\), \(z\) from the origin. The blue arrow \(r\) is the distance of the point from the origin. The orange arc \(\alpha\) is the angle between the axis \(z\) and this arrow. The grey dashed line on the floor is the shadow of the arrow on the floor, the orange arc \(\beta\) is the angle from the axis \(x\) to this shadow, and the grey vertical line says that the point is above the shadow. The point \(P\) is given by the triple \((r, \alpha, \beta)\).

The system we have described is the simplest, but not the only one. A system whose axes are straight and each carries a fixed unit is called Cartesian (kartezičen), and one with perpendicular axes and the same unit on all three rectangular Cartesian (pravokoten kartezičen) (EP §1.2, p. 16). The axes can also be oblique, and then too every point gets its own triple of numbers (EP §1.2). But coordinates need not be distances along straight axes at all. For a pipe it is more natural to say how far a point is from the axis of the pipe, at what angle around the axis it lies and how high it is. These are cylindrical coordinates (cilindrične koordinate) \((r, \theta, z)\). For a ball we give the distance from the centre and two angles, much like latitude and longitude on the Earth. These are spherical coordinates (sferične koordinate) (SADD (1.9.1), (1.9.2), PDF p. 29).

The figure has two halves. The left one shows what cylindrical coordinates are, the right one where they fail, and we describe the right half later, where we show the failure. Let us draw the left figure. The axes \(x\) and \(y\) with the origin \(O\) lie in the plane of the floor, the axis \(z\) points up. The point \(P\) hangs above the floor. The blue dashed line from the floor to \(P\) is the height \(z\) above the floor. The blue dashed line on the floor from the origin to the place below \(P\) is the distance \(r\), measured from the axis \(z\). The blue arc is the angle \(\theta\), measured from the axis \(x\) to this line, toward the axis \(y\). The point is the triple \((r, \theta, z)\).

All these systems share a single requirement (CM (2.4.7), (2.4.8), PDF pp. 70-71, SADD §1.9). Let us draw the requirement as a map from space to triples of numbers (figure 1.55).

In each of the four parts of the figure, on the left is a circle, a part of space with points, and on the right a rectangle, the set of triples of numbers. In the first part one arrow leads from the point \(P\) to one triple, and a second arrow is forbidden, because a point must not have two triples. In the second part two different points, \(P\) and \(Q\), get different triples, and the same triple for both is forbidden. In the third part nearby points get nearby triples, as the dashed circle around each pair shows, and a triple far away is forbidden. In the fourth part the arrows are reversed: nearby triples come from nearby points, and a triple from a distant point is forbidden.

A coordinate system in some part of our space is a rule that fulfils four conditions:

  1. it assigns to every point exactly one triple of numbers,
  2. it assigns different triples to different points,
  3. it assigns nearby triples to nearby points,
  4. nearby triples also belong to nearby points.

Nearby points are points with a small distance, and nearby triples are triples with small differences of the numbers, as in the limit of section 1.3.3. Cylindrical coordinates (figure 1.54) break this requirement in two places. The right figure is a view from above, the axes \(x\) and \(y\) lie in the plane of the figure. The axis \(z\) points toward us, so it is seen only as a blue dot, beside which \(r = 0\) is written. The horizontal axis \(x\) is where the angle \(\theta\) starts at zero, and the angle grows toward the vertical axis \(y\). The point \(A\) lies a little above it, its angle is \(10^\circ\), and the blue arc shows it. A thin grey line from the axis \(z\) to \(A\) shows from where the angle is measured. The point \(B\) lies a little below it, at the same distance from the axis, and its angle is \(350^\circ\), because we count the angle counterclockwise. A thin grey line leads from the axis \(z\) to \(B\) as well. The orange arc around the blue dot therefore goes almost around the full circle. On the axis \(z\) the distance from the axis is zero, and every angle \(\theta\) describes the same point, so the point does not have a single triple and the first condition is broken. Where the angle completes a full turn and starts again at zero, two neighbouring points on either side get very different angles, and the third condition is broken. That is why a coordinate system often covers only a part of space.

How many numbers we need depends on what we describe (figure 1.56).

Upper left is a line with the origin \(O\) and on it a point, given by one distance \(x\). Upper right is a sphere and on it a point \(P\). It is given by the latitude \(\varphi\), which the orange arc measures upwards from the equator, and by the longitude \(\lambda\), which the orange arc measures along the equator from a chosen meridian. Lower left is a pendulum, a rod that can turn about a point below the ceiling. Its position is fixed by the angle \(\psi\) from the vertical. Lower right, a double pendulum has two rods, so its position is fixed by the angle \(\psi_1\) of the first and the angle \(\psi_2\) of the second rod, both measured from the vertical.

We need three numbers because our space has three dimensions, not because coordinates have to come in threes. The same requirement works with any number of coordinates. On a line one number is enough, the distance from a chosen point. On a curved surface, where there are no straight axes at all, two are enough (VK §11.1, PDF p. 308). On the Earth these are latitude and longitude. The number of numbers a space needs is called its dimension (razsežnost). The precise definition for flat spaces is given by KREYSZIG §7.4 (PDF p. 312), and for curved surfaces by SPONG §12.1.1 (PDF p. 432). The set of all ordered \(n\)-tuples of real numbers is written \(\mathbb{R}^n\) (KREYSZIG §7.9, PDF p. 335). So a coordinate system gives every point of a space with \(n\) dimensions its own element of \(\mathbb{R}^n\): our space corresponds to \(\mathbb{R}^3\), a plane to \(\mathbb{R}^2\). Nothing stops at three. \(\mathbb{R}^4\), the set of all ordered quadruples of numbers, is as good a set as \(\mathbb{R}^3\), only we cannot draw it. In mechanics such spaces turn up by themselves. The position of a mechanism with several moving parts is described by as many numbers as it has independent ways to move, its degrees of freedom (prostostne stopnje). These numbers are called generalized coordinates (posplošene koordinate) (GR §1.2.3, PDF p. 38). A pendulum swinging in a plane needs only one, its angle (GR, Figure 1.5). The positions of a mechanism with \(n\) degrees of freedom form a space with \(n\) dimensions (SPONG §1.1.2, PDF p. 28), and it needs \(n\) coordinates, also when \(n\) is larger than three. We will meet them in chapter 8. The most general form of a coordinate system, for spaces with any number of dimensions and also for curved spaces, is called a chart (karta) in mathematics, and it is the starting point of differential geometry (SPONG §12.1.1, PDF p. 432).

Let us see why the distance cannot be read from the differences of coordinates. The view is from above, as in the right half of figure 1.54. The blackwhite dot \(O\) is the axis \(z\), pointing toward us. Two grey rays start from it, the first horizontal, the second turned from it by \(30^\circ\), and the arc between them shows this difference of angles \(\Delta\theta\). Each pair of points lies on both rays, one point on each, so both pairs have the same difference of angles. The point \(P_1\) is on the first ray, the point \(Q_1\) on the second, both at distance \(1{,}2\) from the axis (in units of the figure), so near it. The blue distance \(d_1\) between them is short. The point \(P_2\) and the point \(Q_2\) are on the same rays, both at distance \(3{,}0\) from the axis, and the blue distance \(d_2\) between them is \(1{,}55\) against \(0{,}62\) for \(d_1\), in units of the figure. The ratio of the distances \(1{,}55/0{,}62 = 2{,}5\) equals the ratio of the distances from the axis \(3{,}0/1{,}2\). The difference of angles is the same for both pairs, but the distances differ: at the same difference of angles the distance is proportional to the distance from the axis.

What we derive next, the move from one point to another as the differences of their coordinates and the formula that tells from the coordinates how far apart two points are, holds only in a rectangular Cartesian system. In cylindrical coordinates two points with the same difference of angles are further apart the further from the axis they lie, as figure 1.57 shows, so the distance does not come from the differences of the coordinates in the same way (SADD (1.9.4)). Until we say otherwise, a coordinate system in this book means a rectangular Cartesian system.

1.5.2 Vector

Now let a point move. A fly sits on the lamp \(P = (2, 3, 2)\). It flies off and lands on the floor at the point \(Q = (3, 5, 0)\) (figure 1.49). In the figure the flight is a thick blackwhite arrow from the lamp to the blackwhite dot labelled \(Q\). The arrow points toward \(Q\), because the flight ends there. How do we say with numbers where it has moved? Let us compare the coordinates. The coordinate \(x\) has increased by \(1\), the coordinate \(y\) by \(2\), and the coordinate \(z\) has decreased by \(2\). The triple of changes \((1, 2, -2)\) tells where the fly ended its flight relative to where it began: 1 m further in the direction of the axis \(x\), 2 m further in the direction of the axis \(y\) and 2 m lower. It does not tell where the fly started. A fly that flew from the point \((0, 0, 2)\) to the point \((1, 2, 0)\) would have the same triple of changes, although it flew somewhere else. Nor does the triple tell along which path the fly flew, only where it ended. Such a triple is a new kind of object. It does not describe a position but a move.

From history

Two forces that act at the same point act together as one force along the diagonal of the parallelogram that they span. This principle of statics was stated already by Galileo, but he did not fully recognize its scope (CAJORI, PDF p. 188). Two forces are therefore not added like numbers, and we must take their directions into account too. Calculating with directions grew out of a search. For several years William Rowan Hamilton considered what the product of each pair of perpendicular directed line segments should be (CAJORI, PDF p. 349). A directed line segment is a segment that has a direction as well as a length, like the move of a fly from point \(P\) to point \(Q\). The idea of quaternions (kvaternioni), a calculus with three units \(i\), \(j\) and \(k\), came to him on 16 October 1843 on a walk along the Royal Canal in Dublin. He carved the basic formula \(i^2 = j^2 = k^2 = ijk = -1\) in a stone of Brougham Bridge (CAJORI, PDF p. 349). From this formula it follows that the order of multiplication matters, which we show ourselves. If we multiply \(ijk = -1\) by \(k\) on the right and use \(k^2 = -1\), we get \(-ij = -k\), so \(ij = k\). The reversed product gives \((ji)(ij) = j\,i^2\,j = -j^2 = 1\), so \((ji)\,k = 1\), and after multiplying by \(k\) on the right \(-ji = k\), so \(ji = -k\). Swapping the order changes the sign. He wrote a point as \(p = ix + jy + kz\), where \(i\), \(j\), \(k\) are units in three mutually perpendicular directions (CAJNOT2, PDF p. 156-157). The three numbers \(x\), \(y\), \(z\), multiplied by the units, therefore determine a directed line segment from the origin to the point, just as the triple \((1, 2, -2)\) determines the move of the fly. At about the same time Hermann Günther Grassmann and George Boole also arrived at algebras in which the order of multiplication matters. Grassmann's Ausdehnungslehre appeared in 1844 (BALL, PDF p. 501).

We write the move of a fly, a directed line segment, simply as a triple of numbers, and calculating with them is calculating with numbers (section 1.5.3). The flight of the fly and its directed segment are shown in figure 1.58.

The blackwhite arrows are the axes \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\) from the previous figures. The blue arrow is the flight of the fly from the lamp \(P = (2, 3, 2)\) to the floor at the point \(Q = (3, 5, 0)\). Its changes of coordinates are \((1, 2, -2)\). Under it is the shadow, grey dashed: first a vertical line from \(P\) to the floor, of length \(2\), as far as the fly falls, then a line along the floor to the point \(Q\), which shows the shift \(1\) and \(2\) along the floor. A fly that flew from the point \((0, 0, 2)\) to the point \((1, 2, 0)\) has the same changes \((1, 2, -2)\). Its flight is the second blue arrow. It is as long as the first and points the same way, because the changes of all three coordinates are equal. Its shadow on the floor goes from the origin to the point \((1, 2, 0)\), because the start of the arrow lies exactly above the origin. Both shadows have the same shift along the floor, \(1\) and \(2\).

Definition 1.13 Vector (vektor)

In a chosen rectangular Cartesian coordinate system we write a vector (vektor) \(\vek a\) as an ordered triple of real numbers, \(\vek a = (a_{\mathrm{x}}, a_{\mathrm{y}}, a_{\mathrm{z}})\). The numbers \(a_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(a_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(a_{\mathrm{z}}\) are the components (komponente) of the vector. Two vectors are equal if they agree in all three components. The vector \(\vek 0 = (0, 0, 0)\) is the zero vector (ničelni vektor).

The vector from the point \(P = (x_{\mathrm{P}}, y_{\mathrm{P}}, z_{\mathrm{P}})\) to the point \(Q = (x_{\mathrm{Q}}, y_{\mathrm{Q}}, z_{\mathrm{Q}})\) has the differences of the coordinates as its components:

\[\overrightarrow{PQ} = (x_{\mathrm{Q}} - x_{\mathrm{P}},\ y_{\mathrm{Q}} - y_{\mathrm{P}},\ z_{\mathrm{Q}} - z_{\mathrm{P}}) . \tag{1.8}\]

Source: KREYSZIG §9.1 (1) (PDF p. 382), CM §2.2.1 (PDF p. 39).

For the fly, \(\overrightarrow{PQ}\) \(= (3 - 2,\ 5 - 3,\ 0 - 2) = (1, 2, -2)\). So a vector, like a point, is an ordered triple of numbers, but it means something else. A point tells where something is. A vector tells how to get from one point to another. Bold type, \(\vek a\), distinguishes vectors from numbers. On paper we write a vector with an arrow over the letter, \(\vec a\).

We draw a vector as an arrow from the point \(P\) to the point \(Q\). The same vector can be drawn from any starting point, just as both flies in the example above had the same vector (figure 1.58): the arrows start at different places, but both point 1 m in the direction \(x\), 2 m in the direction \(y\) and 2 m down. The vector from the origin \(O = (0, 0, 0)\) to the point \(P\) has the coordinates of \(P\) as its components, because the coordinates of the origin are zero. Books call it the position vector (krajevni vektor) of the point \(P\).

The components of a vector depend on the coordinate system, just like the coordinates of points. The arrow itself, however, does not depend on the coordinate system. The fly made the same flight however we placed the axes. The triple of numbers is only how the arrow is written in the chosen system, just as the coordinates are how the position of a point is written, and if we turn the axes, the same vector has other components. So when we say that a vector is a triple of numbers, we always mean how it is written in the chosen system. Other books also write the components as \(a_1\), \(a_2\), \(a_3\) (CM) or \([a_1, a_2, a_3]\) (KREYSZIG (1)).

1.5.3 Calculating with vectors

On the floor the fly crawls a little further, 2 m in the direction of the axis \(x\), that is, by the vector \((2, 0, 0)\). Where is it now? Each coordinate changes by the corresponding component, and from \(Q = (3, 5, 0)\) it gets to the point \((5, 5, 0)\). From the lamp \(P = (2, 3, 2)\) to there the vector is \((5 - 2,\ 5 - 3,\ 0 - 2) = (3, 2, -2)\). We get the same vector if we add the components of the two parts of the path: \(1 + 2 = 3\), \(2 + 0 = 2\) and \(-2 + 0 = -2\). So two moves one after the other have the same effect as a single move whose components are the sums of the components. On this model we define the addition of vectors. The difference and the multiple are defined in a similar way.

Figure 1.59: the axes are the same as before, the blue arrow is the first move from \(P\) to \(Q\), the orange one the second to \((5, 5, 0)\), the blackwhite one a single move from \(P\) to \((5, 5, 0)\). On the right the lengths in each row are laid one after another: in the row \(x\) the blue \(1\) and the orange \(2\) give the blackwhite \(3\), in the row \(y\) the orange move is zero, which a dot shows, in the row \(z\) the blue arrow points left, because \(-2\) is a negative number.

Definition 1.14 Sum, difference and multiple of vectors (vsota, razlika in večkratnik vektorjev)

For vectors \(\vek a = (a_{\mathrm{x}}, a_{\mathrm{y}}, a_{\mathrm{z}})\) and \(\vek b = (b_{\mathrm{x}}, b_{\mathrm{y}}, b_{\mathrm{z}})\) and a number \(k\), the sum (vsota), the difference (razlika) and the multiple (večkratnik) are the vectors

\[\begin{aligned} \vek a + \vek b &= (a_{\mathrm{x}} + b_{\mathrm{x}},\ a_{\mathrm{y}} + b_{\mathrm{y}},\ a_{\mathrm{z}} + b_{\mathrm{z}}) , \\ \vek a - \vek b &= (a_{\mathrm{x}} - b_{\mathrm{x}},\ a_{\mathrm{y}} - b_{\mathrm{y}},\ a_{\mathrm{z}} - b_{\mathrm{z}}) , \\ k\,\vek a &= (k\,a_{\mathrm{x}},\ k\,a_{\mathrm{y}},\ k\,a_{\mathrm{z}}) . \end{aligned} \tag{1.9}\]

A vector is added to a point in the same way: for a point \(P = (x, y, z)\), \(P + \vek a = (x + a_{\mathrm{x}},\ y + a_{\mathrm{y}},\ z + a_{\mathrm{z}})\) is the point we reach from \(P\) by the move \(\vek a\). The difference of points \(Q - P\) is the vector \(\overrightarrow{PQ}\) of (1.8).

Source: KREYSZIG §9.1 (3), (5) (PDF pp. 383-384), CM §2.2.1.1 and §2.2.1.2 (PDF p. 40).

We compute all these operations by components: the component \(x\) of the result comes only from the components \(x\), and the same for \(y\) and \(z\).

We draw the sum (figure 1.60) by attaching the arrow of the vector \(\vek b\) to the tip of the arrow of the vector \(\vek a\). The sum is the arrow from the start of the first to the tip of the second. If we draw both arrows from the same point, the sum is the diagonal of the parallelogram that the two arrows span, a four-sided figure whose opposite sides are parallel (KREYSZIG §9.1, Fig. 172, PDF p. 383).

Let us draw this (figure 1.61). The upper arrow is the vector \(\vek a\), below it is one twice as long, \(2\,\vek a\), in the same direction. Below them are \(\vek a\) and after it the orange \((-1)\,\vek a\), which points back, because the minus reverses the direction, and together they arrive back at the start, the zero vector. On the right are \(\vek a\) and \(\vek b\) from the same point. The orange arrow from the tip of \(\vek b\) to the tip of \(\vek a\) is the difference \(\vek a - \vek b\), because it is the vector we must add to the vector \(\vek b\) to get \(\vek a\). Below is \(1\,\vek a = \vek a\), under it \(m\,\vek a\) for \(m = 2\), then \(k\,(m\,\vek a)\) for \(k = 3\), three times \(m\,\vek a\), and last a single multiple \((k\,m)\,\vek a\) of the same length.

The multiple \(2\,\vek a\) is the move we get if we make the move \(\vek a\) twice in a row, since component by component \(\vek a + \vek a = 2\,\vek a\). The multiple \((-1)\,\vek a\) undoes the move \(\vek a\), since \(\vek a + (-1)\,\vek a = \vek 0\). It is the move back. The difference \(\vek a - \vek b\) is the vector we must add to the vector \(\vek b\) to get \(\vek a\), and the difference of points \(Q - P\) is the vector we must add to the point \(P\) to reach the point \(Q\).

We will calculate with vectors as we do with numbers: move the terms of a sum around, shift brackets and factor out numbers. Are we allowed to? The operations are defined component by component, and the components are numbers, for which we know these rules. So we expect the rules to hold for vectors too. Let us look at them first on arrows (figure 1.62). Let us check.

In rule (3) the first row shows the arrow \(\vek a\) and at its end the zero vector, a dot, which moves nowhere, so the sum stays \(\vek a\). The second row shows \(\vek a\) (blue) and the opposite vector \(-\vek a\) (orange), which leads back to the start, so the sum is the zero vector. In rule (5) the first row shows that the arrow \(1\,\vek a\) coincides with \(\vek a\). The second row shows \(k\,(m\,\vek a)\) for \(m = 2\) and \(k = 2\), that is, two copies of \(m\,\vek a\) one after the other, and the third shows a single vector \((k\,m)\,\vek a\), which is equally long and points the same way.

Lemma 1.15 Rules of vector arithmetic (računska pravila za vektorje)

For all vectors \(\vek a\), \(\vek b\), \(\vek c\) and all numbers \(k\), \(m\):

  1. \(\vek a + \vek b = \vek b + \vek a\),
  2. \((\vek a + \vek b) + \vek c = \vek a + (\vek b + \vek c)\),
  3. \(\vek a + \vek 0 = \vek a\) and \(\vek a - \vek a = \vek 0\),
  4. \(k\,(\vek a + \vek b) = k\,\vek a + k\,\vek b\) and \((k + m)\,\vek a = k\,\vek a + m\,\vek a\),
  5. \(k\,(m\,\vek a) = (k\,m)\,\vek a\) and \(1\,\vek a = \vek a\).
Proof

We must show that for each rule the vectors on the left and on the right are equal, as the two paths in the figure to the same point show.

  1. Two vectors are equal if they agree in all three components (definition 1.13). So we check each rule from the figure separately for the components \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\).
  2. All operations in the rules are defined component by component ((1.9)), so the component \(x\) of each side is calculated only from the components \(x\) of the vectors \(\vek a\), \(\vek b\), \(\vek c\) and from the numbers \(k\), \(m\). It is the same for the rules on arrows, where each arrow is nothing but a triple of such numbers.
  3. Rule 1. The component \(x\) of the left side is \(a_{\mathrm{x}} + b_{\mathrm{x}}\), of the right side \(b_{\mathrm{x}} + a_{\mathrm{x}}\). The two numbers are equal, because the order of adding numbers does not change the sum.
  4. Rule 2. The component \(x\) of the left side is \((a_{\mathrm{x}} + b_{\mathrm{x}}) + c_{\mathrm{x}}\), of the right side \(a_{\mathrm{x}} + (b_{\mathrm{x}} + c_{\mathrm{x}})\). The two numbers are equal, because when adding three numbers it does not matter which two we add first.
  5. Rule 3. The component \(x\) of the vector \(\vek a + \vek 0\) is \(a_{\mathrm{x}} + 0 = a_{\mathrm{x}}\), which is the component \(x\) of the vector \(\vek a\). The component \(x\) of the vector \(\vek a - \vek a\) is \(a_{\mathrm{x}} - a_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\), which is the component \(x\) of the zero vector.
  6. Rule 4. The component \(x\) of the vector \(k\,(\vek a + \vek b)\) is \(k\,(a_{\mathrm{x}} + b_{\mathrm{x}})\), and the component \(x\) of the vector \(k\,\vek a + k\,\vek b\) is \(k\,a_{\mathrm{x}} + k\,b_{\mathrm{x}}\). The two numbers are equal by the rule for multiplying a sum by a number. The component \(x\) of the vector \((k + m)\,\vek a\) is \((k + m)\,a_{\mathrm{x}}\), and the component \(x\) of the vector \(k\,\vek a + m\,\vek a\) is \(k\,a_{\mathrm{x}} + m\,a_{\mathrm{x}}\). These two numbers are equal by the same rule.
  7. Rule 5. The component \(x\) of the vector \(k\,(m\,\vek a)\) is \(k\,(m\,a_{\mathrm{x}})\), and the component \(x\) of the vector \((k\,m)\,\vek a\) is \((k\,m)\,a_{\mathrm{x}}\). The two numbers are equal, because when multiplying three numbers it does not matter which two we multiply first. The component \(x\) of the vector \(1\,\vek a\) is \(1 \cdot a_{\mathrm{x}} = a_{\mathrm{x}}\), which is the component \(x\) of the vector \(\vek a\).
  8. For the components \(y\) and \(z\) the calculation is the same, with \(x\) replaced everywhere by \(y\) or \(z\), for each rule on arrows.
  9. The left and the right side of each rule agree in all three components, so by step 1 they are equal, as the arrows on the left and on the right show for each rule.

Source: KREYSZIG §9.1 (4), (6) (PDF p. 384), CM §2.2.1.1 (PDF p. 40).

The proof has shown a principle we will use all the time: we check an equality of two vectors by checking three equalities of numbers, one for each component. Every rule for adding, subtracting and multiplying by a number that we will need will be checked in this way.

These rules matter more than they seem to. Mathematics turns them around. It takes any set in which we can add two elements and multiply an element by a real number so that the result is again an element of the set. Then it requires that all the rules of vector arithmetic of lemma 1.15 hold. Only in rule 3 no difference is given in advance, so instead of \(\vek a - \vek a = \vek 0\) it requires that every element has an opposite element which, added to it, gives the zero element.

Definition 1.16 Vector space (vektorski prostor)

Let \(X\) be a set in which to every two elements \(\vek a\) and \(\vek b\) there belongs an element \(\vek a + \vek b\) of \(X\), their sum, and to every element \(\vek a\) and every real number \(k\) an element \(k\,\vek a\) of \(X\), a multiple. The set \(X\) together with these two operations is a vector space (vektorski prostor) if for all elements \(\vek a\), \(\vek b\), \(\vek c\) of \(X\) and all real numbers \(k\), \(m\):

  1. \(\vek a + \vek b = \vek b + \vek a\),
  2. \((\vek a + \vek b) + \vek c = \vek a + (\vek b + \vek c)\),
  3. there is an element \(\vek 0\) of \(X\), the zero element, with \(\vek a + \vek 0 = \vek a\) for every \(\vek a\), and for every \(\vek a\) there is an element \(-\vek a\) of \(X\), the opposite element, with \(\vek a + (-\vek a) = \vek 0\),
  4. \(k\,(\vek a + \vek b) = k\,\vek a + k\,\vek b\) and \((k + m)\,\vek a = k\,\vek a + m\,\vek a\),
  5. \(k\,(m\,\vek a) = (k\,m)\,\vek a\) and \(1\,\vek a = \vek a\).

The elements of a vector space are called vectors (vektorji).

Source: LEBL2 Def. 8.1.1 (PDF p. 8), KREYSZIG §7.9, definition of a real vector space (PDF pp. 335-336).

Triples of numbers with addition and multiplication by a number component by component ((1.9)) are a vector space. Rules 1, 2, 4 and 5 are the rules of lemma 1.15. The zero element is the zero vector \(\vek 0 = (0, 0, 0)\), since \(\vek a + \vek 0 = \vek a\) by rule 3 of the lemma. The opposite element of the vector \(\vek a\) is \((-1)\,\vek a\), since component by component \(a_{\mathrm{x}} + (-1)\,a_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\), and the same for \(y\) and \(z\). So whether something is a vector does not depend on that object alone. It depends on the set in which we look at it, and on how we add and multiply by numbers in that set. If only one rule fails, for example \(1\,\vek a = \vek a\), the set is not a vector space.

Let us look at the triples that also satisfy the condition \(a_{\mathrm{x}} \ge 0\), with the same two operations component by component (figure 1.63). The triples have three components, but in the figure we draw only the first two, because the third does not affect the condition. The horizontal axis \(a_{\mathrm{x}}\) and the vertical axis \(a_{\mathrm{y}}\) show these two components. The grey half-plane to the right of the vertical axis is all the points with \(a_{\mathrm{x}} \ge 0\), as written in its corner. The blue arrow is the vector \(\vek a = (1, 2)\). It lies in the half-plane, because \(1 \ge 0\). The orange arrow is \((-1)\,\vek a = (-1, -2)\). It points the opposite way, because we multiplied \(\vek a\) by \(-1\), and ends to the left of the vertical axis, outside the half-plane. The sum of two such triples again has \(a_{\mathrm{x}} \ge 0\), so it is in the set. A multiple is not always in it: \((-1)\,(1, 2, 3) = (-1, -2, -3)\) has \(a_{\mathrm{x}} = -1 < 0\) and leaves the set. The definition requires that the multiple of every element by every real number is in the set, which does not hold here. More precisely, for \(\vek a = (1, 2, 3)\) the set has no opposite element from rule 3, since the only triple \(\vek b\) with \(\vek a + \vek b = \vek 0\) is the triple \((-1, -2, -3)\), which is not in the set. The set differs from the triples of the previous paragraph only by the condition \(a_{\mathrm{x}} \ge 0\), and this very condition rejects the opposite elements.

If in the definition we take complex numbers instead of real ones, we get a complex vector space (KREYSZIG §7.9, PDF p. 336), which we will not need in this book. The numbers \(k\) and \(m\) would then be points in a plane (figure 1.64).

In the figure, on the left is the line of real numbers with the example numbers \(k = -1\) and \(m = 2\), each number is one point. On the right is the plane of complex numbers, on the horizontal axis is the first and on the vertical the second of the two real numbers that fix a point of this plane. The examples \(k = 1 + 2\,\mathrm{i}\) and \(m = -1 - \mathrm{i}\) are points of this plane, each number is now a pair of real numbers.

Arrows in space are only one example. Let us draw functions (figure 1.65). On the horizontal axis \(x\) the numbers \(a\) and \(b\) are marked, between which the functions are defined. The blackwhite line is the graph of the function \(f\), the grey curve the graph of the function \(g\). Let us choose one \(x\), shown by the vertical dashed line. The blackwhite dot on the line, the grey dot on the curve and the blue dot on the sum show the points of the graphs at this \(x\). The grey measuring line to the left of it shows the height \(f(x)\), from the axis to the blackwhite line. To the right of it the second grey measuring line shows the height \(g(x)\). This is the height of the grey curve above the axis, which the grey dot shows, moved to the top of the height \(f(x)\). Its end is on the blue curve, the graph of the sum \(f + g\). So the sum is the function whose height at every \(x\) is \(f(x) + g(x)\). Vectors in this sense are also functions (figure 1.65), defined for all numbers between two given numbers, which we add by adding their values, and multiply by a number by multiplying all the values by this number (KREYSZIG §7.9, PDF p. 335, LEBL2 Example 8.1.4, PDF p. 9, for functions without jumps, defined for all numbers between \(0\) and \(1\)), and tables of numbers of size \(2 \times 2\) (KREYSZIG §7.9, Example 1). What we derive only from these rules will therefore hold for all such objects at once. In mechanics, however, the word vector says more than this. What, we will see in section 1.5.5, when we look at how the components change if we turn the axes.

1.5.4 Length of a vector

How far did the fly travel when it flew from the lamp to the floor by the vector \((1, 2, -2)\)? Not \(1 + 2 + 2 = 5\) m, because that would be the path along the axes: first 1 m in the direction \(x\), then 2 m in the direction \(y\) and then 2 m down. But the fly flies straight. We work out the distance in two steps (figure 1.66).

The grey arrows are the axes. The lamp \(P\) is a blackwhite dot, the landing \(Q\) is the end of the thick blue arrow. First we look only at the floor. The point on the floor right below the fly moves by \(1\) m in the direction \(x\) and by \(2\) m in the direction \(y\). These two paths are the legs of a right triangle on the floor, since the axes are perpendicular, and the straight path of the point on the floor is its hypotenuse. The grey triangle on the floor has the legs \(1\) and \(2\) in blackwhite and the hypotenuse \(\sqrt 5\) in grey, and a small grey mark at the corner between the legs shows that the angle there is a right angle. By the Pythagorean theorem from school, the square of the hypotenuse is the sum of the squares of the legs, \(1^2 + 2^2 = 5\), so the point on the floor moves by \(\sqrt 5\) m.

Then we add the height. The fly descended by \(2\) m vertically, and the vertical is perpendicular to every direction on the floor. The straight path of the point on the floor and the vertical descent are therefore the legs of a second, vertical right triangle, whose hypotenuse is the path of the fly. The light blue triangle has the vertical leg \(2\), the other leg \(\sqrt 5\) on the floor and the hypotenuse \(3\), the thick blue arrow. The right-angle mark shows that the vertical stands perpendicular to the grey hypotenuse. The square of the path of the fly is \(5 + 2^2 = 9\), and the path itself is \(\sqrt 9 = 3\) m.

The two steps together say that the square of the distance is the sum of the squares of all three components, \(1^2 + 2^2 + (-2)^2 = 9\). The sign of a component does not matter here, because the square of a negative number is positive.

Definition 1.17 Length of a vector and distance (dolžina vektorja in razdalja)

The length (dolžina) or magnitude (velikost) of the vector \(\vek a = (a_{\mathrm{x}}, a_{\mathrm{y}}, a_{\mathrm{z}})\) is the number

\[|\vek a| = \sqrt{a_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + a_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + a_{\mathrm{z}}^2} . \tag{1.10}\]

The distance (razdalja) between the points \(P\) and \(Q\) is the length of the vector between them, \(|\overrightarrow{PQ}|\).

Source: KREYSZIG §9.1 (2) (PDF p. 382), CM §2.2.1 (PDF p. 39).

Let us see what length is and what it cannot be, on four examples (figure 1.67).

Upper left is the number line of lengths. The length \(|\vek a|\) lies to the right of zero, the length of the zero vector is exactly zero, and to the left of zero there is no length, which the crossed-out orange part shows. Upper right are \(\vek a\), \(2\,\vek a\) and \((-2)\,\vek a\): the second and third vectors are twice as long as the first, the third points the opposite way, and both have the same length \(2\,|\vek a|\), because length does not know direction. Lower left is a triangle of the vectors \(\vek a = (4, 1)\) and \(\vek b = (1, 3)\), drawn reduced in scale. Beside the sides are the lengths \(\sqrt{17}\), \(\sqrt{10}\) and \(\sqrt{41}\), and below it is the comparison: the third side is shorter than the sum of the first two. Lower right is the vector \((-2, 0, 0)\) on the axis \(x\), its length is \(2\).

So the length is a number that is never negative, and it has the same unit as the components. We do not prove the Pythagorean theorem in this book. We measure coordinates with the same tape along three mutually perpendicular axes, and for such coordinates school geometry gives us the formula (1.10), as we saw with the fly. From now on this formula is what the word length means, and everything we claim about lengths we will derive from it. It holds only for components in a rectangular Cartesian system, where the axes are perpendicular and the units equal.

Length too has a more general form. Mathematics calls a norm (norma) any rule that assigns a number to a vector so that three requirements hold: the number is never negative and is zero only for the zero vector, for a multiple of a vector it is multiplied by the absolute value of the number, and the norm of a sum is not larger than the sum of the norms (LEBL2 Def. 8.2.1, PDF p. 20). Our length is an example of a norm, called the Euclidean norm (LEBL2, PDF p. 20). More general still is a distance between the elements of any set, which must meet similar requirements (LEBL1 Def. 7.1.1, PDF p. 255).

For a vector with a single nonzero component, for example \((a_{\mathrm{x}}, 0, 0)\), the length is \(\sqrt{a_{\mathrm{x}}^2}\), the nonnegative number whose square is \(a_{\mathrm{x}}^2\). This is exactly the absolute value \(|a_{\mathrm{x}}|\), the distance of the number \(a_{\mathrm{x}}\) from zero from section 1.3. That is why we use the same sign for the length of a vector as for the absolute value. The length of a vector measures distances in space as the absolute value measures them on the number line.

The distance between the lamp \(P = (2, 3, 2)\) and the point \(Q = (3, 5, 0)\) on the floor is the length of the vector \(\overrightarrow{PQ} = (1, 2, -2)\), that is, \(3\) m. This is exactly what we will need in section 1.6: the distance between two points of a body before and after loading.

1.5.5 Unit vectors and steps along the axes

With the length we can separate the two things an arrow carries: how long it is and where it points. The flight of the fly \(\vek a = (1, 2, -2)\) is \(3\) m long. If we multiply it by \(1/3\), we get the vector \(\tfrac13 (1, 2, -2) = (\tfrac13, \tfrac23, -\tfrac23)\). It points the same way, and its length is \(\sqrt{\tfrac19 + \tfrac49 + \tfrac49} = 1\). Such a vector carries only the direction.

Definition 1.18 Unit vector (enotski vektor)

A unit vector (enotski vektor) is a vector of length \(1\).

Source: CM §2.2.1 (PDF p. 39).

Every vector except the zero vector can be shortened or lengthened into a unit vector that points the same way. Let us look at the proof in the plane (figure 1.68).

Let us draw the triangle of the vector. On the left is the vector \(\vek a\), a blue arrow from a blackwhite dot, its start. Its components are the grey dashed legs: \(a_{\mathrm{x}}\) horizontally and \(a_{\mathrm{y}}\) vertically. The triangle is right-angled, because the axes are perpendicular, so the length \(|\vek a|\) is the hypotenuse. For drawing we took \(a_{\mathrm{x}} = 3\) and \(a_{\mathrm{y}} = 4\), so \(|\vek a| = 5\), and shrank the figure to \(0{,}8\) of its size. This is a shrinking of the whole figure and does not affect the ratios. On the right is the same triangle divided by \(|\vek a|\). Its legs, again grey dashed, are \(a_{\mathrm{x}}/|\vek a|\) and \(a_{\mathrm{y}}/|\vek a|\), because we divide all three sides by the same number. The right triangle is in reality \(|\vek a| = 5\) times smaller than the left one, but in the figure it is enlarged to the size of the left one so that we can compare them, and the ratios of the sides are the same. As in a shrunken photograph, the triangle stays similar, only smaller. The hypotenuse, an orange arrow, has length \(|\vek a|/|\vek a| = 1\).

Lemma 1.19 Unit vector in the direction of a vector (enotski vektor v smeri vektorja)

For every vector \(\vek a \neq \vek 0\) the vector

\[\hat{\vek a} = \frac{1}{|\vek a|}\,\vek a \tag{1.11}\]

is a unit vector, and \(\vek a = |\vek a|\,\hat{\vek a}\).

Proof

We compute the length of \(\hat{\vek a}\) from its components.

  1. Since \(\vek a \neq \vek 0\), at least one component of \(\vek a\) is not zero, since two vectors are equal only if they agree in all components (definition of a vector 1.13). Its square is positive and the squares of the other two are not negative, so \(a_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + a_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + a_{\mathrm{z}}^2 > 0\) and \(|\vek a| > 0\). So we may divide by \(|\vek a|\).
  2. By the definition of the multiple ((1.9)), the vector \(\hat{\vek a} = (a_{\mathrm{x}}/|\vek a|,\ a_{\mathrm{y}}/|\vek a|,\ a_{\mathrm{z}}/|\vek a|)\) has these components.
  3. The square of a quotient is the quotient of the squares, so the squares of the components are \(a_{\mathrm{x}}^2/|\vek a|^2\), \(a_{\mathrm{y}}^2/|\vek a|^2\) and \(a_{\mathrm{z}}^2/|\vek a|^2\).
  4. Quotients with the same denominator are added by adding the numerators: the sum of the squares is \((a_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + a_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + a_{\mathrm{z}}^2)/|\vek a|^2\).
  5. By (1.10) the numerator is \(|\vek a|^2\), so the sum of the squares is \(1\), and by the same formula \(|\hat{\vek a}| = \sqrt 1 = 1\).
  6. By rule 5 of the rules of vector arithmetic (lemma 1.15), \(|\vek a|\,\hat{\vek a}\) \(= (|\vek a| \cdot \tfrac{1}{|\vek a|})\,\vek a = 1\,\vek a = \vek a\).

Source: CM (2.2.1), (2.2.2) (PDF p. 39).

So every vector except the zero vector is its length times a unit vector that gives its direction. The hat over the letter is the usual mark of a unit vector (CM (2.2.1)). For the fly, \(\hat{\vek a} = (\tfrac13, \tfrac23, -\tfrac23)\) and \(\vek a = 3\,\hat{\vek a}\). Let us draw this example (figure 1.69).

Both vectors have the same start, the blackwhite dot. The thinner blackwhite arrow is the vector \(\vek a\), drawn with length \(3\). The thick blue arrow is \(\hat{\vek a}\). It points the same way as \(\vek a\), because \(\hat{\vek a} = \tfrac{1}{|\vek a|}\,\vek a\) is a multiple of the vector \(\vek a\) by a positive number and the direction stays. Under them are two measuring lines with end ticks. The shorter one shows the length \(1\) of the vector \(\hat{\vek a}\), the longer one the length \(|\vek a| = 3\) of the vector \(\vek a\). The longer one is three times longer, so \(\vek a\) is the vector \(\hat{\vek a}\) stretched \(|\vek a|\) times.

We described the flight of the fly \((1, 2, -2)\) in words as three moves: 1 m in the direction of the axis \(x\), 2 m in the direction of the axis \(y\) and 2 m down. With the sum and the multiple we can now write this with vectors. The unit vector in the positive direction of the axis \(x\) is \((1, 0, 0)\): it points along the axis \(x\), and its length is \(\sqrt{1^2 + 0^2 + 0^2} = 1\). In the same way \((0, 1, 0)\) is the unit vector along the axis \(y\) and \((0, 0, 1)\) along the axis \(z\). The flight of the fly is one such step in the direction of the axis \(x\), two in the direction of the axis \(y\) and two against the direction of the axis \(z\):

\[1\,(1, 0, 0) + 2\,(0, 1, 0) + (-2)\,(0, 0, 1) = (1, 0, 0) + (0, 2, 0) + (0, 0, -2) = (1, 2, -2) .\]

So the components of a vector are the numbers of steps along the individual axes from which the vector is made. We will need these three vectors often, so they get a name.

Definition 1.20 Unit vectors of the axes (enotski vektorji osi)

In a chosen rectangular Cartesian coordinate system the unit vectors of the axes (enotski vektorji osi) are the vectors

\[\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = (1, 0, 0) , \quad \vek e_{\mathrm{y}} = (0, 1, 0) , \quad \vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = (0, 0, 1) . \tag{1.12}\]

Each of them is the unit vector in the positive direction of its axis.

Source: KREYSZIG §9.1 (8), (9) (PDF p. 385), SADD §1.2 (PDF p. 12).

Let us draw this (figure 1.70). The blackwhite arrows are the axes. Beside them, at the origin, are three short thick blue arrows: \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), each of length \(1\) and pointing along its axis. The arrow \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) looks shorter, because the axis \(x\) points toward us at an angle. The long thick blue arrow is the flight of the fly from the lamp \(P\) to the landing \(Q\). Beside it are the steps, thin blackwhite arrows, that make it up and follow each other from \(P\) to \(Q\). First two steps \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) along the axis \(y\), then one step \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) along the axis \(x\) and finally two steps \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\). The last ones point down, because the minus turns \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), which points up. The order of the steps does not matter, because the sum is the same.

Every vector can be made from such steps, and in only one way. Let us draw the steps for an arbitrary vector (figure 1.71).

The axes are the same as before. The vector \(\vek a\) is the blue arrow from the origin. We reach its tip in three steps. The first step \(a_{\mathrm{x}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) goes along the axis \(x\) and ends at the point \((a_{\mathrm{x}}, 0, 0)\). The second step \(a_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) goes parallel to the axis \(y\) and ends at the point \((a_{\mathrm{x}}, a_{\mathrm{y}}, 0)\) on the floor. The third step \(a_{\mathrm{z}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) goes up and ends at the tip of the vector \(\vek a\).

Lemma 1.21 A vector as a sum of steps along the axes (vektor kot vsota korakov vzdolž osi)

For every vector \(\vek a = (a_{\mathrm{x}}, a_{\mathrm{y}}, a_{\mathrm{z}})\)

\[\vek a = a_{\mathrm{x}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} + a_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} + a_{\mathrm{z}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} . \tag{1.13}\]

If \(\vek a = k\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} + m\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} + n\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) for some numbers \(k\), \(m\) and \(n\), then \(k = a_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(m = a_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(n = a_{\mathrm{z}}\).

Proof

First we calculate the right side, then we compare components.

  1. By the definition of the multiple ((1.9)), \(a_{\mathrm{x}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) \(= (a_{\mathrm{x}} \cdot 1,\ a_{\mathrm{x}} \cdot 0,\ a_{\mathrm{x}} \cdot 0) = (a_{\mathrm{x}}, 0, 0)\).
  2. In the same way, \(a_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} = (0, a_{\mathrm{y}}, 0)\) and \(a_{\mathrm{z}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = (0, 0, a_{\mathrm{z}})\).
  3. By the definition of the sum, \((a_{\mathrm{x}}, 0, 0) + (0, a_{\mathrm{y}}, 0) = (a_{\mathrm{x}} + 0,\ 0 + a_{\mathrm{y}},\ 0 + 0) = (a_{\mathrm{x}}, a_{\mathrm{y}}, 0)\).
  4. We add \((0, 0, a_{\mathrm{z}})\) and get \((a_{\mathrm{x}}, a_{\mathrm{y}}, a_{\mathrm{z}})\), that is, \(\vek a\). We write no brackets in the sum of three vectors, because by rule 2 of the rules of vector arithmetic (lemma 1.15) it does not matter which two we add first. This proves the first claim.
  5. For the second claim we repeat steps 1 to 4 with the numbers \(k\), \(m\), \(n\) in place of \(a_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(a_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(a_{\mathrm{z}}\) and get \(k\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} + m\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} + n\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = (k, m, n)\).
  6. By assumption this vector equals the vector \(\vek a = (a_{\mathrm{x}}, a_{\mathrm{y}}, a_{\mathrm{z}})\). Two vectors are equal if they agree in all three components (definition 1.13), so \(k = a_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(m = a_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(n = a_{\mathrm{z}}\).

Source: KREYSZIG §9.1 (8) (PDF p. 385).

The lemma says two things. The components are exactly the numbers of steps along the axes that make up the vector. And there are no other numbers that give the same vector, so the components in the chosen system tell everything about the vector and nothing more. Many books write the unit vectors of the axes as \(\vek i\), \(\vek j\), \(\vek k\), and the flight of the fly there would be \(\vek i + 2\,\vek j - 2\,\vek k\) (KREYSZIG (8), PDF p. 385). Others write them \(\vek e_1\), \(\vek e_2\), \(\vek e_3\) (SADD §1.2, PDF p. 12), and so will we, once we need digits instead of the letters \(x\), \(y\), \(z\).

Now let us show what we only claimed after the definition of a vector 1.13: the arrow is one, but its components depend on the coordinate system. Let us first draw the left part of the figure, a view from above. The blackwhite axes are \(x\) and \(y\), the origin is \(O\). Only the part of the flight along the floor is drawn, because the axis \(z\) is not visible from above. The dashed steps are \(1\) along \(x\) and \(2\) along \(y\). The blue arrow is the flight that these two steps make up. Let us turn the axes about the vertical axis \(z\) by a quarter turn (figure 1.72). In the right part the old axes are drawn grey, the new axes blue, the origin is the same. The old axis \(y\) lies under the new axis \(\bar x\), so only its grey letter is visible. It is drawn so that the new axis \(x\) points where the old axis \(y\) pointed, the new axis \(y\) opposite to the old axis \(x\), and the axis \(z\) stays as it was. We mark the unit vectors of the new axes with a bar on top. Written in the old system they are

\[\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{x}} = (0, 1, 0) , \quad \bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{y}} = (-1, 0, 0) , \quad \bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{z}} = (0, 0, 1) .\]

The flight of the fly is one step along the old axis \(x\), two along the old axis \(y\) and two down. The old axis \(y\) is now the new axis \(x\), so two steps along the old axis \(y\) are two steps along the new axis \(x\). The old axis \(x\) points opposite to the new axis \(y\), so one step along the old axis \(x\) is one step back along the new axis \(y\). The vertical part does not change. In the right figure the vertical dashed step labelled \(2\) is the step along \(\bar x\). The horizontal step labelled \(-1\) is drawn to the right, because this is the opposite direction of the axis \(\bar y\), which points to the left. The blue arrow is the same arrow as in the left part, only the steps are different. Let us check:

\[2\,\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{x}} + (-1)\,\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{y}} + (-2)\,\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{z}} = (0, 2, 0) + (1, 0, 0) + (0, 0, -2) = (1, 2, -2) .\]

So by lemma 1.21 on a vector as a sum of steps along the axes, applied in the turned system, where \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{z}}\) are the unit vectors of the axes, the same flight has the components \((2, -1, -2)\) in the turned system. The arrow is the same, its length \(\sqrt{2^2 + (-1)^2 + (-2)^2} = 3\) m is the same, but the numbers are different. How the components change under any turn of the axes we will derive when we need it.

Let us draw that this is not the only choice (figure 1.73).

On the left are three vectors \(\vek f_1\), \(\vek f_2\), \(\vek f_3\) from the origin that do not lie in one plane and are not perpendicular. We get the vector \(\vek a\) as a sum of their multiples: \(\vek f_1\), then \(1{,}5\,\vek f_2\) and then \(\vek f_3\). On the right three vectors \(\vek g_1\), \(\vek g_2\), \(\vek g_3\) lie in the same plane, the grey surface. All their multiples and sums stay in it, so we do not reach the vector \(\vek a'\) that sticks out of it.

Nor are the unit vectors of the axes the only triple from which every vector can be made. Any three vectors that, drawn from one point, do not lie in one plane serve equally well, for example vectors along axes that are not perpendicular. Every vector is in exactly one way a sum of their multiples, and the numbers in this sum are the components of the vector with respect to these three vectors (CM (2.2.30), PDF p. 48). The unit vectors of rectangular axes are the simplest choice, because the components with respect to them are simply the differences of coordinates of (1.8). Until we say otherwise, a component of a vector always means a component with respect to the unit vectors of the axes of a rectangular Cartesian coordinate system.

Now we can also answer the question that the vector space left open: is every triple of numbers a vector? In the mathematical sense yes, since we add triples and multiply them by numbers component by component, and all the rules hold. In mechanics, however, the word vector says more. Take the masses of three parts of a machine, for example \((2, 5, 1)\) kg. Two such machines together have \((4, 10, 2)\) kg, so it makes sense to add such triples and multiply them by numbers. But arrows they are not. When we turn the axes, the components of the fly's flight change from \((1, 2, -2)\) to \((2, -1, -2)\), while the masses of the machine parts stay as they are. A vector in mechanics is a quantity with a size and a direction, an arrow whose components change in a definite way when the axes are turned, while the arrow itself stays the same. We saw the definite way for the quarter turn about the axis \(z\): from \((a_{\mathrm{x}}, a_{\mathrm{y}}, a_{\mathrm{z}})\) we get \((a_{\mathrm{y}}, -a_{\mathrm{x}}, a_{\mathrm{z}})\). For the masses \((2, 5, 1)\) kg nothing changes when the axes are turned (SADD (1.4.6), PDF p. 17, BARBER §1.1.4, PDF p. 26). So three numbers that come out of some calculation may or may not be the components of a vector. We decide by how they change when we turn the axes (BOWER Appendix B.1.3, PDF p. 758).
