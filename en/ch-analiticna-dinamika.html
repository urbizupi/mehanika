The whole chapter on one page (loads more slowly)

Chapter 4 wrote equilibrium in a new way: through the work of forces on imagined displacements that the supports allow. We used it for one rigid body, then for a bar, a truss and a body of any shape. Chapter 5 obtained the equations of a beam from the same idea. Everywhere it was one body or one structure held by the ground. Machines are put together differently: from several rigid bodies joined by pins, hinges, guides and springs. Such is the two-bar toggle mechanism made of two bars, a pin, a hinge and a slider on a guide. Such is a pendulum, a bar on two springs, and a compressed bar that buckles under a large enough force.

For such systems we ask three questions. First: at which position is the system in equilibrium, if we do not wish to compute every force in the pins and guides? Second: does an equilibrium we have found persist if the system is moved slightly, or does it collapse at the smallest disturbance? Third: how does the system move when it is not in equilibrium, and how does it move after a small disturbance of equilibrium? The goal of the chapter is twofold. We want the equations of motion of such systems and their form for small departures from equilibrium, which chapters 9 and 10 need, and the load at which a compressed bar buckles.

This chapter differs from chapter 4 in five things. First, the system has several bodies, and each has its own displacement. Second, the bodies are joined, so they cannot move independently and we must take the relations between them into account. Third, we may describe the position of the system with any numbers we choose ourselves, not only with displacements of points in a Cartesian system. Fourth, for equilibrium we also ask whether it holds under a small disturbance. Fifth, a body that accelerates demands an additional force because of its mass, and this will let us treat motion with exactly the same tool as rest.

The route is as follows. First we treat rest, because time does not enter it. In the first section virtual work is extended to a system of rigid bodies, and with it we compute a mechanism without knowing the forces in the pins. Two mathematical sections follow. In the first we define the angle and two functions of it exactly, which we have not differentiated so far. In the second we prove the theorem that tells when a condition between numbers determines one by the other, and the rule for an extremum under such conditions. Then we describe the position of a system with numbers we choose ourselves and convert the forces to them. In the next section we say when equilibrium holds under a small disturbance. The second part begins with motion. We add only one new thing, the force that the acceleration of a mass demands, and all the tools of rest carry over to motion. From it we derive the equations of motion for any system, the quantities that do not change during motion, and the theorem on when the motion after a disturbance stays near equilibrium. The equations for small departures from equilibrium are written in their own section and handed over to the chapters on oscillation. The last section asks the same question, whether equilibrium holds, for a compressed bar and from it computes the load at which a straight bar buckles.
