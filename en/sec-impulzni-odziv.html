Chapter 9 Vibration of a system with one degree of freedom (nihanje sistema z eno prostostno stopnjo)

9.9 Impact and the impulse response (udarec in impulzni odziv)

So far the cantilever \(A\), the steel cantilever with a mass at its end from section 9.1, was excited by a harmonic force. Often the load is not harmonic. A weight that we suddenly let go, a hammer that hits the end of the cantilever, a machine that stops: in all these cases the force starts to act at some time and either ends or settles to a constant. For the bar with a load we saw in section 4.1 that a suddenly applied load stretches the bar twice as much as a slowly applied one. When does the shape of the force decide under a sudden load, and when only its total amount?

The path is this. First we put a constant force on the cantilever that starts to act at one instant (a step). Then we switch the force off after a time \(t_{\mathrm{d}}\) (a rectangular pulse) and ask how the response depends on \(t_{\mathrm{d}}\). For a short pulse it turns out that only the total amount of the force acts, its integral over time, which we shall call the impulse, and that the response to the unit impulse is the key function of this section. At the end we show that the same holds for an arbitrary system from chapter 8.

The model is the cantilever \(A\) from section 9.1: a mass \(m = 50\) kg at the end of a steel cantilever, stiffness \(k = 62\,720\) N/m, natural circular frequency \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = 35{,}4175\) 1/s and natural period \(T_{\mathrm{n}} = 2\pi/\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = 0{,}17740\) s. The weight of the mass is \(490{,}5\) N, the static deflection \(7{,}8205\) mm and the stress at the clamp under the weight \(91{,}97\) MPa. The damping will be \(\zeta = 0{,}01\) unless said otherwise. The coordinate \(q\) in this section is the deflection of the end downward, in the direction of the weight, so that the numbers have a positive sign. The equation \(m\,\ddot q + c\,\dot q + k\,q = Q(t)\) is linear, so reversing the direction of \(q\) and \(Q\) only reverses the signs of both. The force \(Q(t)\) is piecewise continuous, it is zero before the time \(t = 0\), and the cantilever is at rest at \(t = 0\), \(q(0) = 0\) and \(\dot q(0) = 0\).

9.9.1 The step (stopnica)

The simplest sudden load is a force that is zero until the time \(t = 0\) and then stays constant: \(Q(t) = Q_0\) for \(t \ge 0\). Such a load is called a step load (stopnična obremenitev). It is an idealization, because a real force cannot rise in zero time. The step is a model of a force that rises in a time much shorter than \(T_{\mathrm{n}}\). How much shorter we compute at the end of this section (lemma 9.82), where we also get a number for the cantilever \(A\). CK says that a load acts slowly when its rise time is longer than about \(3\,T_{\mathrm{n}}\) (CK, PDF p. 141), and that dynamic effects are then not needed.

What do we expect? For the bar we saw that without damping the largest displacement is twice the static one. The reason was energy. The force \(Q_0\) does the work \(Q_0\,q\) along the path \(q\), the spring stores \(\tfrac12\,k\,q^2\). At the turning point the velocity is zero, so all the energy is in the spring, \(Q_0\,q = \tfrac12\,k\,q^2\) and \(q = 2\,Q_0/k\). Damping uses up some of the work, so we expect somewhat less than twice the value. How exactly, the next theorem says.

Theorem 9.81 Response to a step load (odziv na stopničasto obremenitev)

Let \(m > 0\), \(c \ge 0\), \(k > 0\) and \(0 \le \zeta < 1\) (the damping ratio from definition 9.5), \(\omega_{\mathrm{d}} = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}\) and \(T_{\mathrm{d}} = 2\pi/\omega_{\mathrm{d}}\). Let \(Q(t) = Q_0\) for \(t \ge 0\) and let \(q(0) = 0\), \(\dot q(0) = 0\). Then the motion for \(t \ge 0\) is unique and

\[q(t) = \frac{Q_0}{k}\Big(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\Big(\cos\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t + \frac{\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}}}{\omega_{\mathrm{d}}}\,\sin\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t\Big)\Big) . \tag{9.80}\]

The largest value of \(q\) is

\[q_{\max} = \frac{Q_0}{k}\Big(1 + \mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\pi/\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}}\Big) , \tag{9.81}\]

reached at \(t = T_{\mathrm{d}}/2\). For \(\zeta = 0\) this is \(2\,Q_0/k\).

Proof

We must show that the formula solves the equation with the given initial values and that its first extremum is the largest.

  1. For \(t \ge 0\) we have \(Q = Q_0\). The constant function \(q_{\mathrm{p}} = Q_0/k\) solves the equation, because \(m \cdot 0 + c \cdot 0 + k\,Q_0/k = Q_0\).
  2. By lemma 9.6 the difference \(y = q - Q_0/k\) solves the free equation \(m\,\ddot y + c\,\dot y + k\,y = 0\). Its initial values are \(y(0) = -Q_0/k\) and \(\dot y(0) = 0\).
  3. By theorem 9.48 (case \(\zeta < 1\)) we have \(y = \mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\big(y(0)\cos\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t + \frac{\dot y(0) + \zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} y(0)}{\omega_{\mathrm{d}}}\sin\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t\big)\), which for the initial values \(y(0) = -Q_0/k\) and \(\dot y(0) = 0\) equals \(-\frac{Q_0}{k}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\big(\cos\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t + \frac{\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}}}{\omega_{\mathrm{d}}}\sin\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t\big)\). We add \(Q_0/k\) and obtain equation (9.80). By theorem 9.41 the solution with the given initial values is only one.
  4. Let \(a = \zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) and \(b = \omega_{\mathrm{d}}\). The derivative of the expression \(\mathrm{e}^{-a t}(\cos b t + \frac{a}{b}\sin b t)\) is, by the product rule and theorem 8.13, \(\mathrm{e}^{-a t}\big({-a}\cos b t - \frac{a^2}{b}\sin b t - b\sin b t + a\cos b t\big) = -\mathrm{e}^{-a t}\,\frac{a^2 + b^2}{b}\,\sin b t\). Since \(a^2 + b^2 = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2(\zeta^2 + 1 - \zeta^2) = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\), we get
    \[\dot q(t) = \frac{Q_0}{k}\,\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2}{\omega_{\mathrm{d}}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\,\sin\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t .\]
  5. The exponential function is positive, so \(\dot q = 0\) exactly at \(t_{\mathrm{j}} = j\pi/\omega_{\mathrm{d}}\) for \(j = 0, 1, 2, \dots\), and \(\dot q > 0\) on \((0, t_1)\), after which the sign alternates. At \(t_{\mathrm{j}}\) we have \(\cos\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t_{\mathrm{j}} = (-1)^j\), \(\sin\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t_{\mathrm{j}} = 0\) and \(\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t_{\mathrm{j}} = j\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}\), so
    \[q(t_{\mathrm{j}}) = \frac{Q_0}{k}\Big(1 - (-1)^j\,\mathrm{e}^{-j\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}}\Big) .\]
  6. Between neighbouring zeros of the derivative \(q\) is monotonic, so all values of \(q\) lie between the smallest and the largest of the values from step 5. For odd \(j\) we have \(q(t_{\mathrm{j}}) = \frac{Q_0}{k}(1 + \mathrm{e}^{-j\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}})\), this number does not increase with \(j\) (for \(\zeta > 0\) it decreases), so it is largest at \(j = 1\). For even \(j\) we have \(q(t_{\mathrm{j}}) \in [0, Q_0/k]\). The largest value is therefore \(q(t_1)\) at \(t_1 = \pi/\omega_{\mathrm{d}} = T_{\mathrm{d}}/2\).

Source: CK (5.5), (5.7), (5.8) (PDF p. 136-137): the formula for \(q\), the response ratio and the double displacement without damping. CK (PDF p. 137) says that the overshoot is largest after half a cycle. The proof by steps, step 6 in particular, is not from the source.

The proof showed that after a step the cantilever does not come to rest at the static position \(Q_0/k\) but oscillates about it, and that the first swing is the largest. The same formula explains how damping reduces the overshoot. The offset from the static position at \(t = 0\) is \(-Q_0/k\). After half a cycle (step 3 at \(t = T_{\mathrm{d}}/2\), where \(\cos\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t = -1\) and \(\sin\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t = 0\)) it is on the opposite side, \(+\frac{Q_0}{k}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\pi/\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}}\), reduced in size by the factor \(\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\pi/\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}}\). Point 3 of corollary 9.51 says the same, the first smallest value of a free motion from rest is \(-q_0\,\mathrm{e}^{-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}}\), and point 1 says it for a whole cycle. Figure 9.93 shows the response for three dampings, horizontally the time in periods \(t/T_{\mathrm{n}}\), vertically the deflection in units of the static deflection \(k\,q/Q_0\). All three oscillate about the static value \(1\). For \(\zeta = 0{,}01\) this factor is \(0{,}9691\), the largest value is \(1{,}969\,Q_0/k\). Without damping the largest value is \(2\). The factor \(2\) is therefore an upper bound that damping lowers a little: for \(\zeta = 0{,}05\) the largest value is \(1{,}854\,Q_0/k\). The dots are these three largest values, all at \(t = T_{\mathrm{d}}/2\).

A sudden load is not a new worry. Before we return to the cantilever, let us see when engineers first met it.

From history

Young published in 1807 a course of lectures on natural philosophy and the mechanical arts, which holds his principal contributions to the strength of materials, among them the chapter on passive strength and friction (TIMO, PDF p. 100). For impact, according to him, not the weight of the striking body decides but the amount of its kinetic energy (TIMO, PDF p. 101). Young takes a horizontal stroke, so that gravity does no additional work during the stretching (TIMO, PDF p. 101). His example is this: if a weight of 100 pounds breaks a substance after statically stretching it through an inch, it will also break it when it strikes horizontally with the velocity that a body gains by falling from a height of half an inch (TIMO, PDF p. 101). The reason, which TIMO does not give and which is our own computation, is the same as for our bar (section 4.1). A static force of 100 pounds along a path of one inch stores only half of the work, \(\tfrac12 \cdot 100 \cdot 1 = 50\) pound-inches, and the fall of the weight from half an inch gives the kinetic energy \(100 \cdot \tfrac12 = 50\) pound-inches, which equals the stored energy. The balance holds only for a horizontal stroke. In a vertical one the weight would do another \(100 \cdot 1 = 100\) pound-inches of work along the path of one inch. According to TIMO, Young was the first to show how important the dynamic effect of a load can be (TIMO, PDF p. 96).

Poncelet, influenced by the practice of building suspension bridges of his time, studied the dynamic action in more detail (TIMO, PDF p. 96). From tensile-test diagrams he showed that up to the elastic limit an iron bar can absorb only a small amount of kinetic energy and that impact can easily cause a permanent set (TIMO, PDF p. 96). Analytically he showed that a suddenly applied load produces twice the stress of a load applied gradually (TIMO, PDF p. 96). He also showed that when a pulsating force acts on a loaded bar the amplitude of forced vibrations can build up at resonance, and that a detachment of soldiers marching over a suspension bridge can therefore be dangerous (TIMO, PDF p. 96). He recommended a working stress seldom greater than half of the elastic limit, because of his study of the dynamic resistance of materials (TIMO, PDF p. 96). This is our conclusion, not Poncelet's, and it agrees with theorem 9.81: under a step the stress is at most twice the static one, so a static stress up to half of the limit gives a stress up to the limit.

Let us return to the cantilever \(A\) under a step. Such a step arises if we let go of the mass that was supported until the time \(t = 0\): the weight \(Q_0 = m\,g = 490{,}5\) N starts to act on the cantilever at once, like the load on the bar. The static deflection is \(Q_0/k = 7{,}8205\) mm. Without damping the end reaches \(2 \cdot 7{,}8205 = 15{,}641\) mm and swings by \(7{,}8205\) mm about the static position. For \(\zeta = 0{,}01\) the largest deflection is \(1{,}9691 \cdot 7{,}8205 = 15{,}399\) mm at \(T_{\mathrm{d}}/2 = 88{,}71\) ms. Does the cantilever bear this elastically? The stress at the clamp is proportional to the deflection, \(91{,}97\) MPa under the weight, \(2 \cdot 91{,}97 = 183{,}9\) MPa at double deflection, and \(181{,}1\) MPa for \(\zeta = 0{,}01\). This is below the yield stress \(220\) MPa. We see the same from the swing: \(7{,}82\) mm about the static position is less than \(10{,}887\) mm, at which the stress reaches the yield stress (section 9.1). The bar from section 4.1 was different: there the weight alone gives \(127{,}3\) MPa and the double value \(254{,}6\) MPa exceeds the limit \(220\) MPa. For the cantilever \(A\) the static stress is smaller, so it survives the sudden load. The largest mass that the cantilever still bears elastically under a sudden load corresponds to \(2\,\sigma_{\mathrm{st}} = 220\) MPa, which is a force \(490{,}5 \cdot 220/183{,}94 = 586{,}7\) N, a mass of \(59{,}8\) kg. For slow loading it would bear \(490{,}5 \cdot 220/91{,}97 = 1173{,}3\) N.

How short must the rise time be for the step to be a good idealization? The goal is a number that says by how much the swing differs from the swing of the step. We take a system without damping, because the computation is then transparent, and a force that rises linearly from zero to \(Q_0\) in a time \(t_{\mathrm{r}}\) (the rise time, čas naraščanja) and then stays constant. Such a force is called a ramp (rampa). For \(t_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\) it is the step. CK does this computation in equations (5.14)-(5.20) and shows it in figure 5.6 (PDF p. 139-141), we write it with the swing about the static value.

Lemma 9.82 Response to a load with a finite rise time (odziv na silo s končnim časom naraščanja)

Let \(m > 0\), \(k > 0\), \(c = 0\), \(\omega = \sqrt{k/m}\), \(t_{\mathrm{r}} > 0\), \(\theta = \omega t_{\mathrm{r}}/2 = \pi t_{\mathrm{r}}/T_{\mathrm{n}}\), \(Q(t) = Q_0\,t/t_{\mathrm{r}}\) for \(0 \le t \le t_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(Q(t) = Q_0\) for \(t > t_{\mathrm{r}}\), \(q(0) = 0\), \(\dot q(0) = 0\). Then for \(0 \le t \le t_{\mathrm{r}}\) the function \(q\) is increasing and for \(t \ge t_{\mathrm{r}}\)

\[q(t) = \frac{Q_0}{k}\Big(1 - \frac{\sin\theta}{\theta}\,\cos\omega\Big(t - \frac{t_{\mathrm{r}}}{2}\Big)\Big) . \tag{9.82}\]

The largest value of \(q\) is \(\dfrac{Q_0}{k}\Big(1 + \dfrac{\sin\theta}{\theta}\Big)\) if \(\theta \le \pi\), and in general \(\dfrac{Q_0}{k}\Big(1 + \Big|\dfrac{\sin\theta}{\theta}\Big|\Big)\). For the swing \(a = |\sin\theta|/\theta\) about the static value \(Q_0/k\) we have \(a \le 1/\theta\) and, when \(0 < \theta \le \pi\), \(1 - \theta^2/6 \le a \le 1\).

Proof

We must show the form of the motion during and after the rise, then the largest value and the two estimates of the swing. From the solution for a ramp that keeps rising we assemble a ramp that stops.

  1. Let \(R(t) = \frac{Q_0}{k}\big(\frac{t}{t_{\mathrm{r}}} - \frac{\sin\omega t}{\omega t_{\mathrm{r}}}\big)\). The derivatives are \(R' = \frac{Q_0}{k\,t_{\mathrm{r}}}(1 - \cos\omega t)\) and \(R'' = \frac{Q_0}{k\,t_{\mathrm{r}}}\,\omega\sin\omega t\) (theorem 8.13). Hence \(m\,R'' = \frac{Q_0}{t_{\mathrm{r}}}\,\frac{\sin\omega t}{\omega}\) (because \(m\,\omega^2 = k\)) and \(k\,R = \frac{Q_0\,t}{t_{\mathrm{r}}} - \frac{Q_0}{t_{\mathrm{r}}}\,\frac{\sin\omega t}{\omega}\), the sum is \(Q_0\,t/t_{\mathrm{r}}\). The function \(R\) therefore solves the equation with the ramp \(Q_0\,t/t_{\mathrm{r}}\) for every \(t \ge 0\), with \(R(0) = 0\) and \(R'(0) = 0\). By theorem 9.41 this is the only solution, so \(q = R\) for \(0 \le t \le t_{\mathrm{r}}\), and \(q' = R' \ge 0\), so \(q\) is increasing there.
  2. For \(t \ge t_{\mathrm{r}}\) we have \(Q(t) = Q_0\,t/t_{\mathrm{r}} - Q_0\,(t - t_{\mathrm{r}})/t_{\mathrm{r}}\). The function \(t \mapsto R(t - t_{\mathrm{r}})\) solves the equation with the force \(Q_0\,(t - t_{\mathrm{r}})/t_{\mathrm{r}}\) (chain rule, the coefficients are constant), so by lemma 9.6 (point 1 with \(a = 1\), \(b = -1\)) the function \(\tilde q(t) = R(t) - R(t - t_{\mathrm{r}})\) solves the equation with the force \(Q\) for \(t \ge t_{\mathrm{r}}\).
  3. At \(t_{\mathrm{r}}\) we have \(\tilde q(t_{\mathrm{r}}) = R(t_{\mathrm{r}}) - R(0) = R(t_{\mathrm{r}}) = q(t_{\mathrm{r}})\) and \(\tilde q'(t_{\mathrm{r}}) = R'(t_{\mathrm{r}}) - R'(0) = q'(t_{\mathrm{r}})\). By lemma 9.6 (point 2) the difference \(q - \tilde q\) solves the free equation with zero initial values at \(t_{\mathrm{r}}\), and by theorem 8.138 (point 1, shifted time) it is therefore zero. So \(q = \tilde q\) for \(t \ge t_{\mathrm{r}}\).
  4. We write the difference of sines with the addition formulas (corollary 8.16), \(\alpha = \omega(t - t_{\mathrm{r}}/2)\) and \(\beta = \theta\): \(\sin(\alpha + \beta) - \sin(\alpha - \beta) = 2\cos\alpha\,\sin\beta\). Since \(\omega t = \alpha + \beta\), \(\omega(t - t_{\mathrm{r}}) = \alpha - \beta\) and \(\omega t_{\mathrm{r}} = 2\theta\), we get \(\tilde q = \frac{Q_0}{k}\big(1 - \frac{2\cos\alpha\,\sin\theta}{2\theta}\big)\), which is (9.82).
  5. For \(t \ge t_{\mathrm{r}}\) the angle \(\alpha\) runs through all values from \(\theta\) onward, so also those where \(\cos\alpha = \pm 1\). Hence the largest value of the expression (9.82) is \(\frac{Q_0}{k}(1 + |\sin\theta|/\theta)\). During the rise we have \(q \le q(t_{\mathrm{r}})\), because \(q\) is increasing, and \(q(t_{\mathrm{r}})\) is a value of the expression (9.82), so the largest value of the whole motion is not greater. For \(\theta \le \pi\) we have \(\sin\theta \ge 0\) and the absolute value sign drops.
  6. Since \(|\sin\theta| \le 1\), we have \(a \le 1/\theta\). For \(0 < \theta \le \pi\) lemma 8.20 gives \(a \ge 1 - \theta^2/6\), and the mean value theorem (theorem 1.85, derivative \(\sin' = \cos\) by theorem 8.13) gives \(\sin\theta = \cos\xi\cdot\theta \le \theta\), so \(a \le 1\).

Source: CK (5.14)-(5.20) (PDF p. 139-141): the ramp and its solution for \(t \ge t_{\mathrm{r}}\), there in the form \(1 + \frac{1}{\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t_{\mathrm{r}}}[\sin\omega_{\mathrm{n}}(t - t_{\mathrm{r}}) - \sin\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t]\) (CK (5.19)), which is the same as (9.82). The swing \(|\sin\theta|/\theta\), its estimates and step 5 are not from the source.

9.9.2 The rectangular pulse (pravokotni sunek)

The step says what a force that lasts forever does. A hammer, however, acts on the end of the cantilever only for a moment. A force that is zero outside one finite time interval is called a pulse (sunek). Whether a pulse is short or long is not measured in seconds but in comparison with \(T_{\mathrm{n}}\). Besides the shape of the pulse we shall be interested in its total amount. That it has a name and what it means we see for a free mass: from \(m\,\ddot q = Q\) it follows by the fundamental theorem of calculus for a function with kinks (lemma 6.12, applied to \(\dot q\)) that \(m\,\big(\dot q(t_2) - \dot q(t_1)\big) = \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} Q\,\mathrm{d}t\). The number on the right is for this mass the change of momentum and we shall use it for every mass.

Definition 9.83 Impulse of a force (impulz sile)

Let \(Q(t)\) be a piecewise continuous generalized force on the coordinate \(q\) and \(t_1 < t_2\). The impulse of the force (impulz) in the time from \(t_1\) to \(t_2\) is the area under the graph of the force, that is the number

\[I = \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} Q(t)\,\mathrm{d}t .\]

Its unit is the unit of force multiplied by a second: N s if \(q\) is a length, and N m s if \(q\) is an angle. The letter \(I\) in this section means the impulse and not the second moment of area of a cross section.

Source: CK (5.21) (PDF p. 141), GR (1.67) (PDF p. 59): the impulse as the time integral of the force.

Let us take a force \(Q_0\) that acts from \(t = 0\) to \(t = t_{\mathrm{d}}\) and then ends (figure 9.96, grey rectangles, end of the pulse \(t_{\mathrm{d}}\)). Such a force is called a rectangular pulse (pravokotni sunek), its impulse is \(Q_0\,t_{\mathrm{d}}\). We neglect damping so that the computation is transparent. For \(\zeta = 0{,}01\) the damping changes the first swing by only a few percent, as figure 9.93 shows.

Theorem 9.84 Response to a rectangular pulse (odziv na pravokotni sunek)

Let \(m > 0\), \(k > 0\), \(c = 0\), \(Q(t) = Q_0\) for \(0 \le t \le t_{\mathrm{d}}\) and \(Q(t) = 0\) for \(t > t_{\mathrm{d}}\), \(q(0) = 0\), \(\dot q(0) = 0\). Then

\[\begin{aligned} q(t) &= \frac{Q_0}{k}\big(1 - \cos\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\big) \quad (0 \le t \le t_{\mathrm{d}}) , \\ q(t) &= \frac{2\,Q_0}{k}\,\sin\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t_{\mathrm{d}}}{2} \\ &\quad\,\sin\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\Big(t - \frac{t_{\mathrm{d}}}{2}\Big) \quad (t \ge t_{\mathrm{d}}) . \end{aligned} \tag{9.83}\]

The largest value \(\max_{\mathrm{t}} |q|\) is \(\dfrac{Q_0}{k}\cdot 2\sin\dfrac{\pi t_{\mathrm{d}}}{T_{\mathrm{n}}}\) for \(t_{\mathrm{d}} \le T_{\mathrm{n}}/2\) and \(\dfrac{Q_0}{k}\cdot 2\) for \(t_{\mathrm{d}} \ge T_{\mathrm{n}}/2\).

Proof

We must show the form of the motion after the pulse and then the largest value in both cases. We abbreviate \(\omega = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\).

  1. For \(0 \le t \le t_{\mathrm{d}}\) we have \(Q = Q_0\) and by theorem 9.81 with \(\zeta = 0\) (\(\omega_{\mathrm{d}} = \omega\)) we get \(q = \frac{Q_0}{k}(1 - \cos\omega t)\). Hence \(q(t_{\mathrm{d}}) = \frac{Q_0}{k}(1 - \cos\omega t_{\mathrm{d}})\) and \(\dot q(t_{\mathrm{d}}) = \frac{Q_0}{k}\,\omega\sin\omega t_{\mathrm{d}}\).
  2. For \(t \ge t_{\mathrm{d}}\) we have \(Q = 0\). The motion has continuous \(q\) and \(\dot q\) also at \(t_{\mathrm{d}}\) (a solution of order 2 by theorem 9.41), so the function \(s \mapsto q(t_{\mathrm{d}} + s)\) for \(s \ge 0\) has the initial values from step 1 and by the chain rule solves the same free equation (the coefficients are constant). By theorem 8.138 (point 1) we get \(q(t_{\mathrm{d}} + s) = q(t_{\mathrm{d}})\cos\omega s + \frac{\dot q(t_{\mathrm{d}})}{\omega}\sin\omega s\).
  3. We insert step 1: \(q(t_{\mathrm{d}} + s) = \frac{Q_0}{k}\big[(1 - \cos\omega t_{\mathrm{d}})\cos\omega s + \sin\omega t_{\mathrm{d}}\,\sin\omega s\big]\). By the addition formula (corollary 8.16) \(\cos(\omega t_{\mathrm{d}} + \omega s) = \cos\omega t_{\mathrm{d}}\cos\omega s - \sin\omega t_{\mathrm{d}}\sin\omega s\), so the square bracket equals \(\cos\omega s - \cos(\omega t_{\mathrm{d}} + \omega s)\). With \(t = t_{\mathrm{d}} + s\) we get \(q(t) = \frac{Q_0}{k}\big[\cos\omega(t - t_{\mathrm{d}}) - \cos\omega t\big]\).
  4. Let \(\alpha = \omega(t - t_{\mathrm{d}}/2)\) and \(\beta = \omega t_{\mathrm{d}}/2\). Then \(\omega(t - t_{\mathrm{d}}) = \alpha - \beta\) and \(\omega t = \alpha + \beta\). By the addition formula (with the evenness of cosine and the oddness of sine) \(\cos(\alpha - \beta) - \cos(\alpha + \beta) = 2\sin\alpha\,\sin\beta\). This is the second equation in (9.83), because \(\beta = \pi t_{\mathrm{d}}/T_{\mathrm{n}}\).
  5. Let \(t_{\mathrm{d}} \le T_{\mathrm{n}}/2\), so \(\beta \le \pi/2\). By step 4 the amplitude for \(t \ge t_{\mathrm{d}}\) is \(\frac{2Q_0}{k}\sin\beta\), reached at \(\alpha = \pi/2\), that is at \(t = t_{\mathrm{d}}/2 + T_{\mathrm{n}}/4\), and this time is not smaller than \(t_{\mathrm{d}}\), because \(t_{\mathrm{d}} \le T_{\mathrm{n}}/2\). During the pulse \(q = \frac{Q_0}{k}(1 - \cos\omega t)\) is increasing on \([0, t_{\mathrm{d}}]\) (because \(\omega t_{\mathrm{d}} \le \pi\)), so it is below the value \(1 - \cos 2\beta = 2\sin^2\beta \le 2\sin\beta\). The largest value is therefore \(\frac{Q_0}{k}\,2\sin\beta\).
  6. Let \(t_{\mathrm{d}} \ge T_{\mathrm{n}}/2\). At \(t = T_{\mathrm{n}}/2 \le t_{\mathrm{d}}\) we have \(q = \frac{Q_0}{k}(1 - \cos\pi) = 2\,\frac{Q_0}{k}\). Everywhere \(|q| \le 2\,Q_0/k\): during the pulse because \(1 - \cos \le 2\), after the pulse because \(|2\sin\alpha\,\sin\beta| \le 2\). The largest value is \(2\,Q_0/k\).

Source: CK (5.9)-(5.13) (PDF p. 138): the response to a rectangular pulse without damping, its largest value during the pulse and after it. CK (PDF p. 139): a pulse longer than \(T_{\mathrm{n}}/2\) gives twice the static displacement. The proof by steps is not from the source.

Why exactly \(T_{\mathrm{n}}/2\)? After a step the first swing is largest at \(T_{\mathrm{n}}/2\) (theorem 9.81). If the force acts until that moment, the cantilever gets all of its acceleration and reaches \(2\,Q_0/k\). If the pulse is shorter, the force ends before the cantilever reaches the first turning point, and the cantilever does not get all the work possible. For \(t_{\mathrm{d}} < T_{\mathrm{n}}/2\) the value \(2\sin(\pi t_{\mathrm{d}}/T_{\mathrm{n}})\) is less than \(2\). It exceeds the value \(1\), the static one, if \(\sin(\pi t_{\mathrm{d}}/T_{\mathrm{n}}) > \tfrac12\), that is for \(t_{\mathrm{d}} > T_{\mathrm{n}}/6\) (CK, PDF p. 138). A shorter pulse gives less than the static displacement, although the force is the same. Figure 9.96 shows three such pulses. In each panel the force and the response are drawn over the time \(t/T_{\mathrm{n}}\), the response is blue, and the dashed lines are its largest values. The longest pulse, \(t_{\mathrm{d}} = T_{\mathrm{n}}\), acts beyond \(T_{\mathrm{n}}/2\) and the response reaches \(2\). The curve \(t_{\mathrm{d}}/T_{\mathrm{n}} \mapsto \max|q|\,k/Q_0\) has a special meaning. It says how large the largest response to a pulse of a given shape is at each natural frequency. Such curves are called response spectra.

Definition 9.85 Response spectrum of a pulse (odzivni spekter sunka)

Let \(Q(t)\) be a piecewise continuous force that is zero outside the interval \([0, t_{\mathrm{d}}]\), \(Q_{\max} = \max|Q|\), and let \(\zeta \ge 0\) be a given damping ratio. For every natural circular frequency \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} > 0\) let \(q\) be the motion \(m\,\ddot q + c\,\dot q + k\,q = Q(t)\) with \(k = m\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\) and \(c = 2\,\zeta\,m\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\), from rest, \(q(0) = \dot q(0) = 0\). The response spectrum (odzivni spekter) of the pulse at damping \(\zeta\) is a function of one real variable that assigns to the natural period \(T_{\mathrm{n}} = 2\pi/\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) the real number \(\max_{\mathrm{t}} |q(t)|\,k/Q_{\max}\), the largest response in units of the static displacement \(Q_{\max}/k\). For \(\zeta = 0\) we call it the undamped response spectrum. The response spectrum of a pulse is also called the shock spectrum (udarni spekter).

Source: CK §5.5 (PDF p. 146-153) and CK figures 5.4 and 5.6 (PDF p. 139-140). CK (PDF p. 147): the spectrum is the largest response versus a system parameter, generally the undamped natural frequency, with several curves for different levels of damping. The definition in this form is ours.

Why do we plot it over \(t_{\mathrm{d}}/T_{\mathrm{n}}\) and not over \(T_{\mathrm{n}}\)? Take pulses of the same shape that differ only in duration, \(Q(t) = Q_{\max}\,f(t/t_{\mathrm{d}})\) with a function \(f\) on \([0, 1]\). We measure time in units of \(1/\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\), \(\tau = \omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\), and write \(w = k\,q/Q_{\max}\). We divide the equation by \(k = m\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\) and get \(w'' + 2\zeta\,w' + w = f\big(\tfrac{T_{\mathrm{n}}}{t_{\mathrm{d}}}\,\tfrac{\tau}{2\pi}\big)\), where the prime is the derivative with respect to \(\tau\), with \(w(0) = w'(0) = 0\). Only \(\zeta\) and the ratio \(t_{\mathrm{d}}/T_{\mathrm{n}}\) appear in the equation, so the largest value of \(w\) depends only on them. The undamped response spectrum of the rectangular pulse from theorem 9.84 is the function \(t_{\mathrm{d}}/T_{\mathrm{n}} \mapsto 2\sin(\pi t_{\mathrm{d}}/T_{\mathrm{n}})\) for \(t_{\mathrm{d}}/T_{\mathrm{n}} \le \tfrac12\) and \(2\) for larger values, drawn in figure 9.97, horizontally \(t_{\mathrm{d}}/T_{\mathrm{n}}\), vertically \(k\,q_{\max}/Q_0\). 9.9.3 The impulse and the impulse response (impulz in impulzni odziv)

For a pulse much shorter than \(T_{\mathrm{n}}\) the cantilever hardly moves during the pulse. Look at the mass of the cantilever \(A\) at the end of a rectangular pulse with the force \(Q_0 = 490{,}5\) N (figure 9.98). We draw the cantilever as a mass \(m\) on a spring \(k\) hung from a fixed support, and cut the mass out as a free body. On it act the force of the pulse \(Q_0\) downward and the force of the spring upward, which is proportional to the displacement from the position before the pulse. The spring pulls back, so the acceleration is at most \(Q_0/m\) and the displacement during the time \(t_{\mathrm{d}}\) at most \(\tfrac12\,(Q_0/m)\,t_{\mathrm{d}}^2\). For \(t_{\mathrm{d}} = T_{\mathrm{n}}/10\) this is \(1{,}5\) mm, so the spring pulls with at most \(62\,720 \cdot 1{,}54 \cdot 10^{-3} = 97\) N, a fifth of the force of the pulse. For \(t_{\mathrm{d}} = T_{\mathrm{n}}/50\) the displacement is only \(0{,}062\) mm and the spring pulls with at most \(62\,720 \cdot 0{,}062 \cdot 10^{-3} = 3{,}9\) N, which is \(0{,}8\ \%\) of the force of \(490{,}5\) N. During this time therefore almost only inertia acts: \(m\,\ddot q \approx Q\).

This is the assumption of a short pulse, so let us say when it holds and what it costs. It holds when the force of the spring at the end of the pulse is small against \(Q_0\). Its share is at most \(k \cdot \tfrac12\,(Q_0/m)\,t_{\mathrm{d}}^2/Q_0 = \tfrac12\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\,t_{\mathrm{d}}^2 = 2\pi^2\,(t_{\mathrm{d}}/T_{\mathrm{n}})^2\): \(0{,}8\ \%\) at \(T_{\mathrm{n}}/50\) and \(20\ \%\) at \(T_{\mathrm{n}}/10\). At \(T_{\mathrm{n}}/4\) the estimate exceeds \(100\ \%\) and the assumption fails: there the spring already pulls during the pulse with a force comparable to \(Q_0\), and the shape of the pulse also determines the response. What the assumption does to the result is measured by lemma 9.87 below: at \(T_{\mathrm{n}}/10\) the amplitude is wrong by \(1{,}6\ \%\), at \(T_{\mathrm{n}}/50\) by \(0{,}07\ \%\). If we integrate the equation \(m\,\ddot q = Q\) over the duration of the pulse, we get \(m\,\Delta\dot q = \int Q\,\mathrm{d}t\). The number on the right is the impulse of the pulse (definition 9.83), which we introduced for the rectangular pulse, the area of the grey rectangle.

For the cantilever \(A\) the following holds: in the limit \(\varepsilon \to 0\) at a fixed impulse \(I\), a pulse of length \(\varepsilon\) gives the cantilever the velocity \(\Delta\dot q = I/m\) before it moves. Why? We integrate the equation \(m\,\ddot q + c\,\dot q + k\,q = Q\) over the pulse \([0, \varepsilon]\): \(m\,(\dot q(\varepsilon) - \dot q(0)) = I - \int_0^{\varepsilon} (c\,\dot q + k\,q)\,\mathrm{d}t\). If \(q\) and \(\dot q\) stay bounded during the pulse by a number independent of \(\varepsilon\), the last term tends to zero as \(\varepsilon \to 0\). In the limit the pulse thus starts a motion with the velocity \(I/m\) from the position \(q = 0\), and the response of the system after the pulse is the free motion with these initial values. For a pulse of finite length this holds only approximately, and lemma 9.87 says how closely. The special case \(I = 1\) N s has a name and will recur throughout the chapter.

Definition 9.86 Impulse response (impulzni odziv)

Let \(m > 0\), \(c \ge 0\), \(k > 0\), \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = \sqrt{k/m}\), \(\zeta = c/(2\sqrt{km})\), \(\omega_{\mathrm{d}} = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}\) for \(\zeta < 1\) and \(\omega_* = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\sqrt{\zeta^2 - 1}\) for \(\zeta > 1\) as in theorem 9.48. The impulse response (impulzni odziv) is the function \(h : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}\) with the value

\[h(t) = \frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\,\sin\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t}{m\,\omega_{\mathrm{d}}} \quad (0 \le \zeta < 1) , \tag{9.84}\]
\[h(t) = \frac{t\,\mathrm{e}^{-\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}}{m} \quad (\zeta = 1) , \qquad h(t) = \frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\,\operatorname{sinh}\omega_* t}{m\,\omega_*} \quad (\zeta > 1) \tag{9.85}\]

for every real \(t\). It solves the free equation \(m\,\ddot h + c\,\dot h + k\,h = 0\) on the whole axis with the initial values \(h(0) = 0\) and \(\dot h(0) = 1/m\), and for \(t \ge 0\) it is the free motion of theorem 9.48 with \(q_0 = 0\) and \(\dot q_0 = 1/m\). The function \(g = m\,h\), the impulse response of the equation divided by the mass, is called in the lectures the impulse transfer function (impulzna prenosna funkcija, section 9.9.5).

Source: CK (5.28)-(5.30) (PDF p. 142), GR (2.114) (PDF p. 111), where \(h_{\mathrm{r}}\) is called the "impulse response function". Here \(h\) is given by the formulas of the free motion and not by the Dirac function, because the book has no Dirac function.

What \(h\) looks like is shown at the top of figure 9.99, drawn for \(\zeta = 0{,}05\) so that the decay is visible. Horizontally is the time in periods, vertically \(m\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\,h\), a number without a unit. At \(t = 0\) a blow with the impulse \(1\) N s hits the cantilever. For \(t \ge 0\) \(h\) is a free damped vibration from the position zero, with the initial slope \(\dot h(0) = 1/m\), that is with the velocity the blow gives. The formula has values also for \(t < 0\): there the vibration grows backward in time, because \(\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\) is larger than \(1\) for negative \(t\). This branch is not a response to the blow, since the cantilever is at rest before the blow. It is only the continuation of the same solution of the free equation, and we keep it so that \(h\) is one smooth function that we may differentiate and shift without a special case at \(t = 0\). What holds for the cantilever before the blow, the orange line at zero, and what the bottom panel says, we explain in section 9.9.5.

The formulas are those of theorem 9.48 with \(q_0 = 0\) and \(\dot q_0 = 1/m\), written for every real \(t\). That they really solve the equation on the whole axis we see as follows. For \(\zeta \ne 1\) the characteristic polynomial \(P(s) = m s^2 + c s + k\) has two different roots \(s_{1,2} = -\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} \pm \mathrm{i}\,\omega_{\mathrm{d}}\) (\(\zeta < 1\)) or \(-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} \pm \omega_*\) (\(\zeta > 1\)) (theorem 9.48, proof, steps 2 and 4). By the definition of the complex exponential function (definition 9.33) and of the hyperbolic functions (definition 9.46) we have \(\sin\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t = (\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t} - \mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t})/(2\mathrm{i})\) and \(\operatorname{sinh}\omega_* t = (\mathrm{e}^{\omega_* t} - \mathrm{e}^{-\omega_* t})/2\), so in both cases

\[h(t) = \frac{\mathrm{e}^{s_1 t} - \mathrm{e}^{s_2 t}}{m\,(s_1 - s_2)} , \tag{9.86}\]

because \(s_1 - s_2 = 2\mathrm{i}\,\omega_{\mathrm{d}}\) or \(2\omega_*\). The functions \(\mathrm{e}^{s_1 t}\) and \(\mathrm{e}^{s_2 t}\) solve the equation for every real \(t\), because by theorem 9.34 (point 3, the derivative at every point) \(m\,s_{\mathrm{j}}^2\,\mathrm{e}^{s_{\mathrm{j}} t} + c\,s_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{e}^{s_{\mathrm{j}} t} + k\,\mathrm{e}^{s_{\mathrm{j}} t} = P(s_{\mathrm{j}})\,\mathrm{e}^{s_{\mathrm{j}} t} = 0\). So their difference solves it too. Its derivative at \(0\) is \((s_1 - s_2)/(m(s_1 - s_2)) = 1/m\) and \(h(0) = 0\). For \(\zeta = 1\) we have \(h = t\,\mathrm{e}^{s t}/m\) with the double root \(s = -\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\), which is a solution for every real \(t\) by lemma 9.42: \(P(s) = m\,(s + \omega_{\mathrm{n}})^2\), so \(m\,\ddot h + c\,\dot h + k\,h = m\,\big(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} + \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\big)^2\big(\mathrm{e}^{-\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\,t/m\big) = \mathrm{e}^{-\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\,(t)'' = 0\), with \(h(0) = 0\) and \(\dot h(0) = 1/m\). The function \(h\) is therefore differentiable as many times as we like with continuous derivatives. For \(\zeta = 0\) we have \(h(t) = \sin(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t)/(m\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}})\). The unit of \(h\) is m/(N s): the displacement due to an impulse of \(1\) N s. For the cantilever \(A\) we have \(1/(m\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}) = 0{,}5647\) mm per N s, and the first peak of \(h\) for \(\zeta = 0{,}01\) is \(0{,}5559\) mm per N s at \(t = 44{,}07\) ms. The response to the impulse \(I\) is \(I\,h(t)\), because the equation is linear and \(I\,h\) has the initial velocity \(I/m\).

Is a pulse really equivalent to an impulse? For a rectangular pulse without damping we can check this exactly. A pulse of length \(\varepsilon\) and impulse \(I\) has the force \(Q_0 = I/\varepsilon\).

Lemma 9.87 A short rectangular pulse and the impulse response (kratek pravokotni sunek in impulzni odziv)

Let \(c = 0\), \(Q(t) = I/\varepsilon\) for \(0 \le t \le \varepsilon\) and \(Q = 0\) for \(t > \varepsilon\), \(q(0) = \dot q(0) = 0\), \(\omega = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) and \(\theta = \omega\varepsilon/2 = \pi\varepsilon/T_{\mathrm{n}}\). Then for \(t \ge \varepsilon\)

\[q(t) = \frac{I}{m\,\omega}\,\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta}\,\sin\omega\Big(t - \frac{\varepsilon}{2}\Big) , \tag{9.87}\]

which is the response \(I\,h(t - \varepsilon/2)\) to the impulse \(I\), shifted by \(\varepsilon/2\) and multiplied by \(\sin\theta/\theta \in [1 - \theta^2/6,\, 1]\). The difference between \(q\) and \(I\,h\) (\(h\) for \(\zeta = 0\)) is bounded for \(t \ge \varepsilon\) by

\[|q(t) - I\,h(t)| \le \frac{I}{m\,\omega}\Big(\frac{\theta^2}{6} + \theta\Big) , \tag{9.88}\]

which tends to zero as \(\varepsilon \to 0\) at constant \(I\).

Proof

We must show formula (9.87) and the estimate. We get the formula from theorem 9.84, the estimate from small angles.

  1. By theorem 9.84 with \(Q_0 = I/\varepsilon\) and \(t_{\mathrm{d}} = \varepsilon\) we have for \(t \ge \varepsilon\) \(q(t) = \frac{2I}{k\varepsilon}\sin\theta\,\sin\omega(t - \varepsilon/2)\).
  2. Since \(k = m\,\omega^2\) and \(\varepsilon = 2\theta/\omega\), we have \(\frac{2}{k\varepsilon} = \frac{2}{m\,\omega^2\cdot 2\theta/\omega} = \frac{1}{m\,\omega\,\theta}\). We obtain (9.87).
  3. By lemma 8.20 we have \(|\sin\theta/\theta - 1| \le \theta^2/6\), so \(\sin\theta/\theta \ge 1 - \theta^2/6\). By the mean value theorem (theorem 1.85, derivative \(\sin' = \cos\) by theorem 8.13) we have \(\sin\theta = \cos\xi\cdot\theta \le \theta\) for \(\theta > 0\), so \(\sin\theta/\theta \in [1 - \theta^2/6, 1]\).
  4. By the mean value theorem (theorem 1.85, derivative \(\sin' = \cos\) by theorem 8.13) we have \(|\sin a - \sin b| = |\cos\xi|\,|a - b| \le |a - b|\). With \(a = \omega(t - \varepsilon/2)\) and \(b = \omega t\) we get \(|\sin\omega(t - \varepsilon/2) - \sin\omega t| \le \omega\varepsilon/2 = \theta\).
  5. We write \(q - I\,h = \frac{I}{m\omega}\big[\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta}\sin\omega(t - \varepsilon/2) - \sin\omega t\big]\), which is \(\frac{I}{m\omega}\big[(\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta} - 1)\sin\omega(t - \varepsilon/2) + (\sin\omega(t - \varepsilon/2) - \sin\omega t)\big]\). By the triangle inequality, step 3 and step 4 we have \(|q - I\,h| \le \frac{I}{m\omega}(\theta^2/6 + \theta)\). The estimate tends to zero as \(\varepsilon \to 0\), because \(\theta \to 0\).

The exact form (9.87), the shift \(\varepsilon/2\) and the estimate (9.88) are ours. For a damped system and a pulse of arbitrary shape the corollary in section 9.10.8 holds.

9.9.4 The impulse in Lagrange's equations (impulz v Lagrangeevih enačbah)

So far the system was one mass. Does the same hold for a system with several coordinates, like the bars and pendulums from chapter 8? What changes then is what "the velocity given by the impulse" means: the coordinates have different units and every mass moves differently. Instead of \(m\,\Delta\dot q = I\) we get an equation with the matrix of the kinetic energy, which chapter 8 introduced (theorem 8.97).

Let the system be described by Lagrange's equations (theorem 8.99) with \(n\) coordinates \(q_{\mathrm{s}}\) and scleronomic constraints, so \(T = \tfrac12\,\dot{\vek q}^{\mathrm T}\mat M(\vek q)\,\dot{\vek q}\). We divide the forces into two parts: the bounded part \(Q_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm b}\) (springs, dampers, weight, everything that stays finite) and the pulse \(Q_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm p}\), which is piecewise continuous and zero outside the interval \([t_0, t_0 + \varepsilon]\). Its impulse is \(I_{\mathrm{s}} = \int_{\mathrm{t}_0}^{t_0+\varepsilon} Q_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm p}\,\mathrm{d}t\). The question: what happens to the coordinates and velocities as \(\varepsilon \to 0\) at constant \(I_{\mathrm{s}}\)? The answer has one condition that GR uses, and we therefore name it explicitly: during the pulse the velocities stay bounded by a number \(K\) and the terms under the integral by a number \(C\), both independent of \(\varepsilon\). The inequality for the term \(\partial T/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}\) follows from the inequality for \(K\) when \(\partial\mat M/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}\) is continuous and \(\vek q\) stays in a bounded region, because \(\partial T/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}} = \tfrac12\,\dot{\vek q}^{\mathrm T}(\partial\mat M/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}})\,\dot{\vek q}\). For \(Q_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm b}\) it suffices that it is a continuous function of position, velocity and time. We write both so that the theorem needs no additional assumptions about \(\mat M\).

Point 3 of the theorem, about constraints, needs one more hypothesis, which is not from GR. The constraint force is not bounded during the pulse. If we give a ball on a rod of length \(l\) a pulse along the rod, the rod must take the whole impulse in the time \(\varepsilon\), so its force is of the order \(I/\varepsilon\): for \(I = 3\) N s and \(\varepsilon = 1\) ms this is \(3000\) N and it grows without bound as \(\varepsilon\) decreases. Only its impulse stays bounded. From the equations alone it follows only that the integral of the term \(\sum_{\mathrm{r}}\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} A_{\mathrm{rs}}\) is bounded (this is the jump of \(p_{\mathrm{s}}\) in step 2 below), but not the integral of the absolute values of the individual \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\), because values of opposite signs can cancel. We need the absolute values in step 5, so that the error due to the change of the matrix \(\mat A\) during the pulse tends to zero. We therefore require the hypothesis.

Theorem 9.88 Impulsive loading in Lagrange's equations (impulzna obremenitev v Lagrangeevih enačbah)

Let \(T = \tfrac12\,\dot{\vek q}^{\mathrm T}\mat M(\vek q)\,\dot{\vek q}\) be the kinetic energy of a system from theorem 8.99 with scleronomic constraints, \(\mat M\) continuous, and \(T\) and \(\partial T/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}\) continuous functions of \((\vek q, \dot{\vek q})\). Let \(Q_{\mathrm{s}} = Q_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm b} + Q_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm p}\) as above, \(Q_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm b}\) piecewise continuous. For every \(\varepsilon > 0\) let a motion be given with the same initial state \(\vek q_0 = \vek q(t_0)\), \(\dot{\vek q}_- = \dot{\vek q}(t_0)\) and with the same impulses \(I_{\mathrm{s}}\). Let the motion \(\vek q(t)\) be continuously differentiable with a piecewise continuously differentiable velocity and let it satisfy Lagrange's equations everywhere except at the times of the jumps of the force. Let there exist numbers \(K\) and \(C\), independent of \(\varepsilon\), such that for all \(s\) and all \(t \in [t_0, t_0 + \varepsilon]\) \(|\dot q_{\mathrm{s}}| \le K\) and \(|\partial T/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}| + |Q_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm b}| \le C\). Let \(\dot{\vek q}_+ = \dot{\vek q}(t_0 + \varepsilon)\). Then:

  1. \(|\vek q(t_0 + \varepsilon) - \vek q_0| \le \sqrt{n}\,K\,\varepsilon\), so the coordinates have no jump during the pulse.
  2. As \(\varepsilon \to 0\) we have \(\mat M(\vek q_0)\,(\dot{\vek q}_+ - \dot{\vek q}_-) - \vek I \to \vek 0\), where \(\vek I = (I_1, \dots, I_{\mathrm{n}})\). Since \(\mat M(\vek q_0)\) is positive definite (theorem 8.97), \(\dot{\vek q}_+\) tends to \(\dot{\vek q}_- + \mat M(\vek q_0)^{-1}\vek I\). In the limit the jump of the generalized momenta (definition 8.126) equals the impulses: \(\mat M\,\Delta\dot{\vek q} = \vek I\).
  3. Let the coordinates also have constraints \(f_{\mathrm{r}}(\vek q) = 0\) with a continuous matrix \(\mat A = \partial\vek f/\partial\vek q\) with \(m\) pivots (as in theorem 8.109), and let \(\int|\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}|\,\mathrm{d}t\) for the multipliers \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) of that theorem be bounded over the pulse by a number independent of \(\varepsilon\). Then the integrals \(\int\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\,\mathrm{d}t\) over the pulse converge as \(\varepsilon \to 0\), their limits \(\Lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) are the constraint impulses, and \(\mat M\,\Delta\dot{\vek q} = \vek I + \mat A^{\mathrm T}\vek\Lambda\) and \(\mat A\,\Delta\dot{\vek q} = \vek 0\).
Proof

We must show that the integral of Lagrange's equations over the pulse gives the jump of momentum and that all other terms vanish as \(\varepsilon \to 0\).

  1. Point 1: \(q_{\mathrm{s}}\) is continuously differentiable, so by the fundamental theorem of calculus for a function with kinks (lemma 6.12) \(q_{\mathrm{s}}(t_0 + \varepsilon) - q_{\mathrm{s}}(t_0) = \int \dot q_{\mathrm{s}}\,\mathrm{d}t\) and by lemma 3.54 (point 3 with the bounds \(\pm K\), on every piece where \(\dot q_{\mathrm{s}}\) is continuous, then summing over the pieces by equation (6.5)) \(|\dots| \le K\varepsilon\). Each of the \(n\) components is bounded by \(K\varepsilon\), so the length of the vector is bounded by \(\sqrt{n}\,K\varepsilon\).
  2. By definition 8.126 \(p_{\mathrm{s}} = \partial T/\partial\dot q_{\mathrm{s}} = \sum_{\mathrm{b}} M_{\mathrm{sb}}\,\dot q_{\mathrm{b}}\). Lagrange's equations read \(\dot p_{\mathrm{s}} = \partial T/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}} + Q_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm b} + Q_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm p}\) everywhere except at finitely many times of the jumps of the force. The function \(p_{\mathrm{s}}\) is continuous and by this equation its derivative equals a piecewise continuous right-hand side, so by lemma 6.12 \(p_{\mathrm{s}}(t_0 + \varepsilon) - p_{\mathrm{s}}(t_0) = \int_{\mathrm{t}_0}^{t_0+\varepsilon}\big(\partial T/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}} + Q_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm b}\big)\,\mathrm{d}t + I_{\mathrm{s}}\).
  3. By the hypothesis the integrand has absolute value at most \(C\), so the integral has by lemma 3.54 (point 3 with the bounds \(\pm C\), piece by piece) absolute value at most \(C\,\varepsilon\).
  4. We write \(\vek p = \mat M(\vek q)\,\dot{\vek q}\), so \(\mat M(\vek q(t_0 + \varepsilon))\,\dot{\vek q}_+ - \mat M(\vek q_0)\,\dot{\vek q}_- = \vek I + \vek r\) with \(|r_{\mathrm{s}}| \le C\varepsilon\). We subtract \(\mat M(\vek q_0)\,\dot{\vek q}_+\): \(\mat M(\vek q_0)(\dot{\vek q}_+ - \dot{\vek q}_-) - \vek I = \vek r - \big(\mat M(\vek q(t_0 + \varepsilon)) - \mat M(\vek q_0)\big)\dot{\vek q}_+\). The first term is bounded by \(C\varepsilon\), the second tends to zero because \(\mat M\) is continuous, \(\vek q(t_0 + \varepsilon) \to \vek q_0\) (point 1) and \(|\dot{\vek q}_+| \le \sqrt n K\). The matrix \(\mat M(\vek q_0)\) is positive definite (theorem 8.97), so \(\mat M(\vek q_0)\,\vek x = \vek 0\) gives \(\vek x^{\mathrm T}\mat M(\vek q_0)\,\vek x = 0\) and \(\vek x = \vek 0\). It is therefore invertible and \(\dot{\vek q}_+ - \dot{\vek q}_- = \mat M(\vek q_0)^{-1}\big(\vek I + \text{remainder}\big) \to \mat M(\vek q_0)^{-1}\vek I\). This is point 2.
  5. Point 3: by theorem 8.109 we add to the right-hand side the term \(\sum_{\mathrm{r}} \lambda_{\mathrm{r}} A_{\mathrm{rs}}\) (our \(Q_{\mathrm{s}}\) also contains \(-\partial V/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}\), so the equation is the same as in the theorem, since \(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\dot q_{\mathrm{s}} = \partial T/\partial\dot q_{\mathrm{s}}\)). The same computation as in step 2 gives in \(\dot p_{\mathrm{s}}\) the additional \(\sum_{\mathrm{r}}\int\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\,A_{\mathrm{rs}}(\vek q(t))\,\mathrm{d}t\). Since \(\mat A\) is continuous and \(\vek q(t) \to \vek q_0\), we have \(|A_{\mathrm{rs}}(\vek q(t)) - A_{\mathrm{rs}}(\vek q_0)| \to 0\) uniformly, so this term is \(\sum_{\mathrm{r}} A_{\mathrm{rs}}(\vek q_0)\int\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\,\mathrm{d}t\) up to an error that tends to zero, because \(\int|\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}|\,\mathrm{d}t\) is bounded. Thus \(\sum_{\mathrm{r}} A_{\mathrm{rs}}\Lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) is the term \((\mat A^{\mathrm T}\vek\Lambda)_{\mathrm{s}}\).
  6. The constraints \(\mat A(\vek q)\,\dot{\vek q} = \vek 0\) hold at \(t_0\) and \(t_0 + \varepsilon\). The difference is \(\mat A(\vek q_0)(\dot{\vek q}_+ - \dot{\vek q}_-) = \big(\mat A(\vek q_0) - \mat A(\vek q(t_0 + \varepsilon))\big)\dot{\vek q}_+ \to \vek 0\) for the same reason as in step 4.
  7. The limits exist. Let \(\vek L\) be the vector of the integrals \(\int\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\,\mathrm{d}t\) over the pulse, and take the matrices \(\mat M\) and \(\mat A\) at \(\vek q_0\). Steps 5 and 6 say that the pair \((\Delta\dot{\vek q}, \vek L)\) solves the system \(\mat M\,\Delta\dot{\vek q} - \mat A^{\mathrm T}\vek L = \vek I + \vek r_1\), \(\mat A\,\Delta\dot{\vek q} = \vek r_2\) with remainders \(\vek r_1, \vek r_2 \to \vek 0\). The system with zero right-hand side has only the zero solution: from \(\mat A\,\vek x = \vek 0\) and \(\mat M\,\vek x = \mat A^{\mathrm T}\vek y\) we get \(\vek x^{\mathrm T}\mat M\,\vek x = (\mat A\,\vek x)^{\mathrm T}\vek y = 0\), so \(\vek x = \vek 0\) (positive definiteness, step 4), then \(\mat A^{\mathrm T}\vek y = \vek 0\) and \(\vek y = \vek 0\), because the rows of \(\mat A\) with \(m\) pivots are independent. The square matrix of the system is therefore invertible, and \((\Delta\dot{\vek q}, \vek L)\) converges to the solution of the system with \(\vek r_1 = \vek r_2 = \vek 0\). These are the limits of point 3.

Source: GR §1.5 (1.66)-(1.76) (PDF p. 59-61): GR derives (1.74) from Hamilton's principle and introduces (1.76) from \(\int Q_{\mathrm{s}}\,\mathrm{d}t\) with the hypothesis that the terms under the integral stay finite between \(t_-\) and \(t_+\). GR (PDF p. 61) mentions that the same is obtained by integrating Lagrange's equations, which we do here. Point 3 and the estimates (\(\varepsilon \to 0\) with constants) are not from the source.

The proof showed that the integral of Lagrange's equations over a short time gives the jump of momentum and that all forces with bounded effect achieve nothing in this time. For the cantilever \(A\) (\(n = 1\), \(M = m\)) this is \(m\,\Delta\dot q = I\), as we guessed from Newton's law.

Let us also mention the Dirac function. Other textbooks write an ideally short pulse as the Dirac function \(\delta(t)\), which is zero everywhere except at one point and has the integral \(1\). KREYSZIG says that this is not a function in the ordinary sense of analysis but a generalized function, and that for problems with impulses it is convenient to compute with it as if it were an ordinary function (KREYSZIG, PDF p. 252). The book has no distributions, so we do not use the Dirac function. Its task is done by the impulse response \(h\) (definition 9.86), which is an ordinary function, and by the estimate from lemma 9.87. How to read the notation with \(\delta\) when we meet it in the lectures or in other books is told in section 9.9.5.

Let us look at two examples. The first is the hammer (figure 9.101). A hammer blow on the mass of the cantilever \(A\) that gives the impulse \(I = 5\) N s has, by theorem 9.88 with \(n = 1\) and \(M = m = 50\) kg, a velocity jump \(\Delta\dot q = I/m = 5/50 = 0{,}1\) m/s. The velocity on the left is zero before the blow and \(0{,}1\) m/s just after it. The deflection on the right stays \(0\) at the blow and swings out from zero. Both curves are drawn over the time \(t\). After the blow \(q(t) = I\,h(t)\). For \(\zeta = 0{,}01\) the envelope of the amplitude has the value \(I/(m\,\omega_{\mathrm{d}}) = 2{,}8236\) mm, and without damping \(I/(m\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}) = 2{,}8235\) mm (the difference is in the fifth digit, because \(\omega_{\mathrm{d}} = 0{,}99995\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\)). We do the energy computation without damping, because then the energy is conserved: the hammer gave the energy \(\tfrac12\,m\,\dot q^2 = I^2/(2m) = 0{,}25\) J, and the spring stores it at the largest deflection \(2{,}8235\) mm, \(\tfrac12\,k\,(2{,}8235\ \text{mm})^2 = 0{,}25\) J. For \(\zeta = 0{,}01\) the first peak is \(0{,}5559 \cdot 5 = 2{,}780\) mm, and the envelope \(2{,}8236\) mm. The largest stress at the clamp is proportional to the deflection, \(2{,}8236/7{,}8205 \cdot 91{,}97 = 33{,}2\) MPa (the same with \(2{,}8235\) mm), far below the limit \(220\) MPa. If the hammer acts for \(T_{\mathrm{n}}/10\) with a constant force (a rectangular pulse), that force is \(281{,}8\) N and by the table the amplitude is smaller by \(1{,}6\) % than the undamped value \(2{,}8235\) mm. For other shapes of the force the loss is different, for a half-sine pulse of the same length it is \(0{,}93\) %.

The second example has a constraint. A ball of mass \(m = 2\) kg hangs on a rigid weightless rod of length \(l = 1{,}5\) m (the pendulum from chapter 8) and rests in the lower position \(\theta = 0\) (figure 9.102). We draw it twice: left with a horizontal blow, right with a vertical one. The rod is held by a pivot on a fixed support. A horizontal blow with the impulse \(I = 3\) N s acts in the direction of the axis \(x\). The generalized force on \(\theta\) is \(Q_\theta = F\cdot\partial x/\partial\theta = F\,l\cos\theta\), at \(\theta = 0\) the impulse is \(I_\theta = I\,l = 4{,}5\) N m s, the mass \(M = m\,l^2 = 4{,}5\) kg m\(^2\), and by point 2 of theorem 9.88 \(\Delta\dot\theta = I_\theta/M = 1\) rad/s. For a vertical blow downward with the same impulse (\(I_{\mathrm{y}} = -3\) N s), however, \(Q_\theta = F_{\mathrm{y}}\,\partial y/\partial\theta = F_{\mathrm{y}}\,l\sin\theta = 0\) at \(\theta = 0\), so \(\Delta\dot\theta = 0\): the rod takes the blow. We show this with the Cartesian coordinates \((x, y)\) and the constraint \(f = x^2 + y^2 - l^2 = 0\), which has at \((0, -l)\) the matrix \(\mat A = (2x, 2y) = (0, -2l)\). By point 3 we have \(\mat A\,\Delta\dot{\vek q} = 0\), so \(\Delta\dot y = 0\), and \(m\,\Delta\dot y = I_{\mathrm{y}} + A_{\mathrm{y}}\Lambda\) gives \(A_{\mathrm{y}}\Lambda = -I_{\mathrm{y}} = 3\) N s: the constraint impulse (of the rod on the ball) \(\mat A^{\mathrm T}\vek\Lambda = (0, 3)\) N s points upward and cancels the blow. For the horizontal blow \(\Delta\dot x = I/m = 1{,}5\) m/s and \(\Delta\dot\theta = \Delta\dot x/l = 1\) rad/s, as by the coordinate \(\theta\).

9.9.5 The impulse response in other notations (impulzni odziv v drugih zapisih)

We meet the impulse response in lectures, problem collections and other books in notations that differ from ours in four things: the equation is divided by the mass, \(h\) is set to zero before the blow, the area under \(h\) has its own name and the blow is written with the Dirac function. The content is the same. In this section we translate each notation into ours, so that the reader reads both and does not divide by the mass twice. The model is throughout the cantilever \(A\) with \(m = 50\) kg, \(k = 62\,720\) N/m, \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = 35{,}4175\) 1/s, \(T_{\mathrm{n}} = 0{,}17740\) s and \(\zeta = 0{,}01\), and the blow is a hammer with the impulse \(I = 5\) N s, which gives a jump of velocity of \(0{,}1\) m/s.

The equation divided by the mass. We divide our equation \(m\,\ddot q + c\,\dot q + k\,q = Q(t)\) by \(m\). Since \(c/m = 2\,\zeta\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) and \(k/m = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\) (definition 9.5), we get

\[\ddot q + 2\,\zeta\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\,\dot q + \omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\,q = f(t) , \qquad f(t) = \frac{Q(t)}{m} . \tag{9.89}\]

The right side \(f\) is the force per unit mass, with the unit N/kg, that is m/s\(^2\): the acceleration the force would give to the mass without the spring and the damper. In this equation the coefficient of \(\ddot q\) is \(1\), so its impulse response is the formula (9.84) with \(1\) in place of \(m\):

\[g(t) = m\,h(t) = \frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\,\sin\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t}{\omega_{\mathrm{d}}} \quad (0 \le \zeta < 1) . \tag{9.90}\]

The function \(g\) solves the free equation with \(g(0) = 0\) and \(\dot g(0) = 1\): it is the motion after a jump of velocity of \(1\) m/s. The time integral of \(f\) over the pulse is \(I/m\), with the unit m/s, and it is exactly this jump of velocity (theorem 9.88). The unit of \(g\) is therefore metre per m/s, that is the second, and the unit of \(h\) is m/(N s).

The response to a blow with the impulse \(I\) is thus written in two ways, and in each we divide by the mass exactly once:

\[q(t) = I\,h(t) = \frac{I}{m}\,g(t) . \tag{9.91}\]

For the hammer \(I/m = 5/50 = 0{,}1\) m/s. The first peak of \(g\) is \(50 \cdot 0{,}5559 = 27{,}80\) mm per m/s, at the same time \(44{,}07\) ms as the first peak of \(h\). We put all three computations one under another, the last column is the first peak of the deflection:

notation impulse first peak of the response to a unit impulse first peak of the deflection
force and \(h\) \(I = 5\) N s \(0{,}5559\) mm/(N s) \(5 \cdot 0{,}5559 = 2{,}780\) mm
force per unit mass and \(g\) \(I/m = 0{,}1\) m/s \(27{,}80\) mm/(m/s) \(0{,}1 \cdot 27{,}80 = 2{,}780\) mm
wrong: \(I/m\) and \(h\) \(0{,}1\) m/s \(0{,}5559\) mm/(N s) \(0{,}0556\) mm, \(50\) times too little

The unit gives the mistake in the third row away: m/s times m/(N s) is m/kg and not a metre. The rule is: the mass is carried either by the force (\(f = Q/m\)) or by the response (\(h = g/m\)), never by both. The factor \(50\) is exactly the mass of the cantilever \(A\) in kilograms. The correct peak of the deflection lies under the envelope with the amplitude \(2{,}82\) mm.

In the lectures and problem collections on vibration the displacement is \(x\), the force \(F\), the force per unit mass \(f = F/m\), the impulse \(\Delta I\), the natural circular frequency \(\omega_0\) (our \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\)), the damped one \(\omega_{0\mathrm \mathrm{d}}\) (our \(\omega_{\mathrm{d}}\)) and the damping ratio \(\delta\) (our \(\zeta\)). The function \(g\) is called there the impulse transfer function (impulzna prenosna funkcija). Some texts denote by \(g\) our \(h\), that is \(\sin\omega_0 t/(m\,\omega_0)\). Which of the two we have in front of us is told by the unit of the right side of the equation: N for \(h\), m/s\(^2\) for \(g\).

The equation in rotation. When the coordinate is an angle, the coefficient of the second derivative is a moment of inertia and the right side a moment. The disc on a shaft from section 9.1.6 has the equation \(J\,\ddot\varphi + c_{\mathrm{t}}\,\dot\varphi + k_{\mathrm{t}}\,\varphi = M_{\mathrm{t}}(t)\), so its impulse response is \(h(t) = \mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\sin\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t/(J\,\omega_{\mathrm{d}})\) with the unit rad/(N m s), and the impulse is \(\int M_{\mathrm{t}}\,\mathrm{d}t\) in N m s (definition 9.83). The general rule: in \(h\) stands the coefficient of the second derivative of that equation whose right side we multiply. In problem collections the moment of inertia about the pivot is denoted by \(J_0\). An example is the pendulum of figure @fig-imp-nihalo-udarec with \(m = 2\) kg and \(l = 1{,}5\) m. Its linear equation without damping is by example 9.11 \(m\,l^2\,\ddot\theta + m\,g\,l\,\theta = M(t)\), so \(J_0 = m\,l^2 = 4{,}5\) kg m\(^2\) and \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = \sqrt{9{,}81/1{,}5} = 2{,}5573\) 1/s. The horizontal blow with the impulse \(3\) N s has the lever arm \(l\), so the impulse of the moment is \(3 \cdot 1{,}5 = 4{,}5\) N m s, and it gives a jump of angular velocity of \(4{,}5/4{,}5 = 1\) rad/s. The amplitude of the vibration is \(1/2{,}5573 = 0{,}391\) rad, that is \(22{,}4^\circ\). At such an angle the linear equation is no longer accurate: the neglected term is by example 9.11 smaller than \(\theta^2/6 = 2{,}5\ \%\) of the kept one, so the amplitude may be wrong by that much as well: the true amplitude from the conservation of energy is \(0{,}3936\) rad, the linear one is \(0{,}65\) % too small. If we divide the same equation by \(l\), the right side is a force in N, the coefficient of \(\ddot\theta\) is \(J_0/l = m\,l = 3\) kg m, and in \(h\) stands \(m\,l\): the impulse of the force \(3\) N s gives the same jump \(3/3 = 1\) rad/s.

Causality. The cantilever is at rest before the blow. The response to a blow with the impulse \(I\) at the time \(0\) is therefore zero for \(t < 0\) and \(I\,h(t)\) for \(t \ge 0\) (figure 9.99, top). Our formula for \(h\), however, has values also before the blow. The lectures therefore define the function differently: before the blow they set it to zero.

Definition 9.89 Causal impulse response (vzročni impulzni odziv)

Let \(h\) be the impulse response of definition 9.86. The causal impulse response (vzročni impulzni odziv) is the function \(h_+ : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}\) with the values

\[h_+(t) = 0 \quad (t \le 0) , \qquad h_+(t) = h(t) \quad (t > 0) . \tag{9.92}\]

It is continuous, because \(h(0) = 0\). Its derivative has at \(t = 0\) a jump from \(0\) to \(1/m\).

Source: GR (PDF p. 111, with footnote 6): the response composed of the responses to impulses at times up to \(t\) satisfies the principle of causality, by which a cause always precedes its effect. The symbol \(h_+\) and the definition in this form are ours.

Why the word causality (vzročnost)? The cause is the blow, the effect the motion, and the effect does not come before the cause. The break of the slope at \(t = 0\) is the jump of velocity \(1/m\) that the impulse \(1\) N s gives (theorem 9.88). Which of the two functions we use is a matter of notation and not of physics. The book computes with the smooth \(h\) and excludes the past by the limits of the integral: in section 9.10 we shall write the response to a force \(Q\) that starts to act at \(t = 0\) as \(\int_0^t h(t - s)\,Q(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\). The lectures write \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} h_+(t - s)\,Q(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\). The two integrals are equal. For \(s < 0\) we have \(Q(s) = 0\), there is no force yet. For \(s \ge t\) we have \(h_+(t - s) = 0\), because a blow at the time \(s\) does not affect the motion at an earlier time \(t\). Of the infinite limits there remains the piece from \(0\) to \(t\), on which \(h_+ = h\). In the lectures the age of the blow \(t - s\) is denoted by \(\tau\) and the same rule is written as \(h(\tau) = 0\) for \(\tau \le 0\).

With \(h_+\) we also write several blows briefly. Let us strike twice (figure 9.99, bottom): with the impulse \(I_1\) at the time \(0\) and with the impulse \(I_2\) at the time \(t_1\). Each blow by theorem 9.88 only changes the velocity by \(I_{\mathrm{j}}/m\), and in between the cantilever moves freely. What is the motion?

Lemma 9.90 Response to a sequence of blows (odziv na zaporedje udarcev)

Let \(m > 0\), \(c \ge 0\), \(k > 0\), let \(t_1 < t_2 < \dots < t_{\mathrm{N}}\) be times and \(I_1, \dots, I_{\mathrm{N}}\) real numbers. Let \(q\) be a continuous function on \(\mathbb{R}\) that is zero for \(t < t_1\), solves the free equation \(m\,\ddot q + c\,\dot q + k\,q = 0\) on every interval between neighbouring times and for \(t > t_{\mathrm{N}}\), and has at every \(t_{\mathrm{j}}\) a left and a right limit of the velocity with the difference \(I_{\mathrm{j}}/m\). Then for every \(t\)

\[q(t) = \sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^{N} I_{\mathrm{j}}\,h_+(t - t_{\mathrm{j}}) . \tag{9.93}\]
Proof

We must show that the sum meets all the requirements and that only one function meets them.

  1. The function \(t \mapsto h(t - t_{\mathrm{j}})\) solves the free equation for every \(t\), because \(h\) solves it and the coefficients are constant: by the chain rule a shift of time does not change the derivatives. The response to a later blow is therefore the same curve, shifted by \(t_{\mathrm{j}}\).
  2. The term \(I_{\mathrm{j}}\,h_+(t - t_{\mathrm{j}})\) is zero for \(t \le t_{\mathrm{j}}\) and \(I_{\mathrm{j}}\,h(t - t_{\mathrm{j}})\) for \(t > t_{\mathrm{j}}\). It is continuous, because \(h(0) = 0\). Its velocity is zero to the left of \(t_{\mathrm{j}}\), and to the right it has the limit \(I_{\mathrm{j}}\,\dot h(0) = I_{\mathrm{j}}/m\) (definition 9.86), so the difference is \(I_{\mathrm{j}}/m\). Elsewhere the velocity is continuous.
  3. The sum \(S\) of all the terms is therefore continuous, is zero for \(t < t_1\), has at \(t_{\mathrm{j}}\) the difference of velocity \(I_{\mathrm{j}}/m\) and between the times solves the free equation by lemma 9.6: we add the curves of the single blows.
  4. Uniqueness. The difference \(d = q - S\) is continuous and zero for \(t < t_1\). At every \(t_{\mathrm{j}}\) the functions \(q\) and \(S\) have the same difference of velocity, so \(d\) has a continuous velocity there. On the interval from \(t_1\) to \(t_2\) the function \(d\) solves the free equation with \(d(t_1) = 0\) and \(\dot d(t_1) = 0\), so by theorem 9.41 it is zero there. Since \(\dot d\) has the right limit \(0\) at \(t_1\) and \(d\) is continuous, \(d\) is differentiable from the right at \(t_1\) with derivative \(0\) (mean value theorem), and by the equation \(\ddot d\) has a limit too, so \(d\) solves the equation on the closed interval. We argue the same way from interval to interval. Hence \(q = S\).

Source: GR (2.113)-(2.114) (PDF p. 111): a jump of velocity is equivalent to an impulse of the force, and the response to a force decomposed into a sequence of impulses is the sum of the responses to them. The lemma in this form and the proof are ours.

The lemma says two things, and these two are the meaning of the abbreviation LTI. The system is time-invariant (časovno nespremenljiv): \(m\), \(c\) and \(k\) do not change with time, so the response to a blow at \(t_1\) is the same curve as the response to a blow at \(0\), only shifted. And it is linear: the response to two blows is the sum of the responses, the response to a \(0{,}6\) times weaker blow is \(0{,}6\) times smaller. The lectures call such a system LTI (linear time-invariant). In the figure the first response is alone up to \(t_1\), then the second is added to it: the largest value of the sum after \(t_1\) is \(0{,}88\) in units of \(I_1/(m\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}})\), while the first response alone would reach only \(0{,}68\) there.

The sentence from the lectures that \(h\) describes an LTI system completely can now be read exactly. Whoever knows the one curve \(h\) knows the response from rest to every sequence of blows, without the equation and without the numbers \(m\), \(c\), \(k\) (equation (9.93)). Every force can be cut into short pulses, each of which acts almost as a blow (lemma @lem-kratek-pravokotni), so the same holds for an arbitrary force. This step is made exact by section 9.10. When does the sentence not hold? A system whose mass or stiffness changes during the motion is not time-invariant, and a system with a nonlinear spring is not linear. For the first the response to a blow depends on when we strike, for the second on how hard, and one curve \(h\) does not suffice.

The area under \(h\). The step \(Q_0\) is a constant force that does not switch off. From the proof of theorem 9.81 (step 4) we read the velocity of the response to it: \(\dot q(t) = \frac{Q_0}{k}\,\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2}{\omega_{\mathrm{d}}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\sin\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t = Q_0\,h(t)\), because \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2/k = 1/m\). The response to the step is therefore the area under \(h\) from \(0\) to \(t\), multiplied by \(Q_0\). Where does this area go as \(t\) grows?

Lemma 9.91 Area under the impulse response (ploščina pod impulznim odzivom)

Let \(m > 0\), \(k > 0\), \(c \ge 0\) and \(h\) the impulse response of definition 9.86. Then for every \(t \ge 0\)

\[\int_0^t h(s)\,\mathrm{d}s = \frac{1 - m\,\dot h(t) - c\,h(t)}{k} . \tag{9.94}\]

This function of \(t\) is the motion from rest under the step \(Q_0 = 1\). For \(0 < \zeta < 1\) it differs from \(1/k\) by at most \(\dfrac{1}{k}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\Big(1 + \dfrac{3\,\zeta}{\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}}\Big)\), so it tends to \(1/k\) as \(t\) grows. For \(c = 0\) it equals \((1 - \cos\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t)/k\) and has no limit.

Proof

We must show the formula for the integral, that it is the response to the step, and the estimate.

  1. The function \(h\) solves the free equation, so for every \(s\) we have \(k\,h(s) = -m\,\ddot h(s) - c\,\dot h(s)\).
  2. We integrate from \(0\) to \(t\). By the fundamental theorem of calculus (lemma 6.12, \(h\) is arbitrarily many times continuously differentiable) \(\int_0^t \ddot h\,\mathrm{d}s = \dot h(t) - \dot h(0) = \dot h(t) - 1/m\) and \(\int_0^t \dot h\,\mathrm{d}s = h(t) - h(0) = h(t)\). Hence \(k\int_0^t h\,\mathrm{d}s = -m\,\dot h(t) + 1 - c\,h(t)\), which is equation (9.94).
  3. Denote \(w(t) = \int_0^t h\,\mathrm{d}s\). Then \(w(0) = 0\), \(\dot w = h\), \(\dot w(0) = 0\) and \(\ddot w = \dot h\). By step 2 we have \(m\,\ddot w + c\,\dot w + k\,w = m\,\dot h + c\,h + (1 - m\,\dot h - c\,h) = 1\). The function \(w\) therefore solves the equation with the force \(1\) from rest, and by theorem 9.41 there is only one such solution: the response to the step \(Q_0 = 1\).
  4. Let \(0 < \zeta < 1\). From equation (9.84) we have \(|h(t)| \le \mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}/(m\,\omega_{\mathrm{d}})\), because \(|\sin| \le 1\). The derivative is \(\dot h(t) = \mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\,(\omega_{\mathrm{d}}\cos\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t - \zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\sin\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t)/(m\,\omega_{\mathrm{d}})\), so \(m\,|\dot h(t)| \le \mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\,(1 + \zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}}/\omega_{\mathrm{d}})\). With \(c = 2\,\zeta\,m\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) we have \(c\,|h(t)| \le 2\,\zeta\,(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}/\omega_{\mathrm{d}})\,\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\). The sum of the two is at most \(\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\,(1 + 3\,\zeta\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}/\omega_{\mathrm{d}})\) and \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}/\omega_{\mathrm{d}} = 1/\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}\). This is the estimate of the statement: the vibration about the static value decays.
  5. For \(c = 0\) we have \(h = \sin\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t/(m\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}})\) and \(\dot h = \cos\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t/m\), so the right side of equation (9.94) equals \((1 - \cos\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t)/k\). This value oscillates between \(0\) and \(2/k\) and approaches no number.

Source: CK (5.5) (PDF p. 136): the response to a step. The lemma and the proof are ours.

In the lectures the statement is written as \(\int_0^\infty h(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau = 1/k\), where the upper limit \(\infty\) means the limit as the upper limit \(t\) grows beyond all bounds. The meaning is the static value: a constant force gives after a long enough time the deflection \(Q_0/k\), and \(1/k\) is the deflection per unit force. For the cantilever \(A\) we have \(1/k = 15{,}94\) µm/N, so the force \(490{,}5\) N gives \(7{,}8205\) mm. The unit agrees: m/(N s) times second is m/N. How long is "long enough"? For \(\zeta = 0{,}01\) after \(50\) periods, that is after \(8{,}87\) s, the value \(k\int_0^t h\,\mathrm{d}s\) equals \(0{,}957\) (the estimate of the lemma allows a deviation of \(0{,}045\)), while for \(\zeta = 0{,}05\) it is \(0{,}998\) already after \(20\) periods. Without damping there is no limit, so the notation \(\int_0^\infty h = 1/k\) holds only for a damped system (for \(\zeta \ge 1\) the same follows from the formulas (9.85), which also decay exponentially). For \(g = m\,h\) the limit is \(m/k = 1/\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\).

The Dirac function. There remains the notation of the blow. In the lectures and in exams a blow with the impulse \(\Delta I\) at the time \(t_1\) is written as the force \(F(t) = \Delta I\,\delta(t - t_1)\), and the rule of computation for it is \(\int g(t)\,\delta(t - t_1)\,\mathrm{d}t = g(t_1)\) for a continuous function \(g\) (KREYSZIG, PDF p. 252, where the rule is called the "sifting property"). The letter \(\delta\) has two meanings in the lectures: without an argument it is the damping ratio (our \(\zeta\)), with an argument, \(\delta(t)\), the Dirac function. How do we read the rule without generalized functions? As a shorthand for a limit of narrower pulses.

Take a pulse \(p_\varepsilon\) of area \(1\) that lasts from \(t_1\) to \(t_1 + \varepsilon\) (figure 9.103). At the top we draw three such pulses: a wide one, a middle one and a narrow one. All have the same area, so a narrower one is taller. At the bottom is the curve \(g\), for example \(g = m\,h\) of the cantilever \(A\), and on it the point \(g(t_1)\). For a rectangular pulse the integral \(\int g\,p_\varepsilon\,\mathrm{d}t\) is the average of \(g\) on the window from \(t_1\) to \(t_1 + \varepsilon\), since \(p_\varepsilon = 1/\varepsilon\). As we narrow the window, only values close to \(g(t_1)\) remain in it, and the average tends to it.

Lemma 9.92 A short pulse of unit area picks out the value of a function (kratek sunek s ploščino 1 izbere vrednost funkcije)

Let \(\varepsilon_0 > 0\), \(g\) a continuous function on the interval \([t_1, t_1 + \varepsilon_0]\) and \(0 < \varepsilon \le \varepsilon_0\). Let \(p_\varepsilon\) be a piecewise continuous function on \([t_1, t_1 + \varepsilon]\) that is nonnegative everywhere and has \(\int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_1+\varepsilon} p_\varepsilon(t)\,\mathrm{d}t = 1\). Then

\[\Big|\int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_1+\varepsilon} g(t)\,p_\varepsilon(t)\,\mathrm{d}t - g(t_1)\Big| \le \max_{\mathrm{t}_1 \le \mathrm{t} \le \mathrm{t}_1+\varepsilon} |g(t) - g(t_1)| , \tag{9.95}\]

and the right side tends to zero as \(\varepsilon \to 0\).

Proof

We must show the estimate and that its right side tends to zero.

  1. Since \(\int p_\varepsilon\,\mathrm{d}t = 1\), we have \(g(t_1) = \int g(t_1)\,p_\varepsilon(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\) (lemma 6.10, point 2, constant factor). The difference on the left side of the estimate is therefore \(\int \big(g(t) - g(t_1)\big)\,p_\varepsilon(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\).
  2. Let \(M_\varepsilon\) be the largest value of \(|g(t) - g(t_1)|\) on the window. It exists by theorem 1.74, because the function is continuous on a closed interval. This is the most by which the curve on the window departs from the value \(g(t_1)\).
  3. Since \(p_\varepsilon \ge 0\), the functions \(M_\varepsilon\,p_\varepsilon - (g - g(t_1))\,p_\varepsilon\) and \(M_\varepsilon\,p_\varepsilon + (g - g(t_1))\,p_\varepsilon\) are nonnegative. By lemma 6.10 (points 3 and 2) their integrals are nonnegative as well, so \(\big|\int (g - g(t_1))\,p_\varepsilon\,\mathrm{d}t\big| \le M_\varepsilon\int p_\varepsilon\,\mathrm{d}t = M_\varepsilon\). An average with a nonnegative weight cannot be farther from \(g(t_1)\) than the farthest value on the window.
  4. The function \(g\) is continuous at \(t_1\) (definition 1.59): for every \(\eta > 0\) there is an \(\varepsilon\) such that \(|g(t) - g(t_1)| < \eta\) for all \(t\) on the window, and then \(M_\varepsilon \le \eta\). A narrower window has a smaller range of values.

Source: KREYSZIG §6.4 (PDF p. 252): the rule \(\int g(t)\,\delta(t - a)\,\mathrm{d}t = g(a)\) for a continuous \(g\), there justified as plausible from the limit of short rectangular pulses. The lemma with the estimate and the proof are ours.

The condition \(p_\varepsilon \ge 0\) is used in step 3. Without it a pulse that is partly positive and partly negative could, for the same area \(1\), have arbitrarily large pieces of both signs, and the estimate with \(M_\varepsilon\) would not hold. For the cantilever \(A\) with \(t_1 = 0{,}62\,T_{\mathrm{n}} = 110{,}0\) ms we have \(g(t_1) = -18{,}59\) ms. The short ticks to the left of \(t_1\) are the averages on the three windows:

window \(\varepsilon\) average of \(g\) on the window (ms) difference from \(g(t_1)\) (ms) estimate \(M_\varepsilon\) (ms)
\(T_{\mathrm{n}}/4\) \(-24{,}25\) \(5{,}66\) \(8{,}35\)
\(T_{\mathrm{n}}/10\) \(-23{,}33\) \(4{,}74\) \(7{,}92\)
\(T_{\mathrm{n}}/50\) \(-19{,}77\) \(1{,}18\) \(2{,}31\)

The first two windows reach across the bottom of the wave, so their averages are almost equal. Only a window that is short against the period gives a value close to \(g(t_1)\). The notation with \(\delta\) is therefore read like this:

  • \(\int g(t)\,\delta(t - t_1)\,\mathrm{d}t = g(t_1)\) means: \(\int g\,p_\varepsilon\,\mathrm{d}t\) tends to \(g(t_1)\) as \(\varepsilon \to 0\) (lemma 9.92).
  • \(F(t) = \Delta I\,\delta(t - t_1)\) means: a pulse \(\Delta I\,p_\varepsilon\) with the impulse \(\Delta I\) (definition 9.83), so short that its shape does not count. How short is told by lemma 9.87: for \(\varepsilon = T_{\mathrm{n}}/50\) the amplitude is wrong by \(0{,}07\ \%\), for \(T_{\mathrm{n}}/10\) by \(1{,}6\ \%\).
  • "\(h\) is the response to \(\delta(t)\)" means: the responses to the pulses \(p_\varepsilon\) at \(t_1 = 0\) tend to \(h\) as \(\varepsilon \to 0\). For the rectangular pulse without damping this is lemma 9.87 with \(I = 1\), for a damped system and a pulse of arbitrary shape the corollary in section 9.10.8. The response to \(F = \Delta I\,\delta(t - t_1)\) is therefore \(\Delta I\,h_+(t - t_1)\), one term of the sum (9.93).

The proofs in the book stay with ordinary functions and estimates. The notation with \(\delta\) is their shorthand, and whoever can expand it into a limit may compute with it.

9.9.6 The half-sine pulse and the shock spectrum (polsinusni sunek in udarni spekter)

The rectangular pulse switches on in an instant. The force of a real blow, for example of a hammer with a rubber tip or of a fall onto a soft base, rises and falls gradually. The simplest model of such a force is half of a sine wave: \(Q(t) = Q_0\,\sin(\pi t/t_{\mathrm{d}})\) for \(0 \le t \le t_{\mathrm{d}}\) and \(Q = 0\) afterwards. Such a force is called a half-sine pulse (polsinusni sunek). The question of the section is whether the response spectrum (definition 9.85) depends on the shape of the pulse, and if it does, where. We expect two things. A very short pulse acts only with its impulse, so the shape should not count. A very long half-sine pulse, however, rises slowly, like the slow ramp of lemma 9.82, so we expect an almost static response \(Q_0/k\) and not twice that as for the rectangular pulse.

The model is the cantilever \(A\) from rest, without damping, \(c = 0\). We neglect damping for the same reason as for the rectangular pulse: the largest value occurs in the first cycle, where \(\zeta = 0{,}01\) lowers it by at most a few percent (for the step \(1{,}969\) instead of \(2\)). The assumption is no longer good for large damping or when we are interested in the motion long after the pulse. During the pulse the force is a piece of a harmonic force with the circular frequency \(\Omega = \pi/t_{\mathrm{d}}\). We denote its ratio to the natural circular frequency by \(r = \Omega/\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = T_{\mathrm{n}}/(2\,t_{\mathrm{d}})\). For \(t_{\mathrm{d}} = T_{\mathrm{n}}/2\) we have \(r = 1\): the pulse lasts exactly half of a natural cycle.

Figure 9.104 shows three such pulses, each in its own panel. Horizontally is the time \(t/T_{\mathrm{n}}\), vertically the force and the response in units of \(Q_0\) and \(Q_0/k\). The grey curve is the force, which lasts until \(t_{\mathrm{d}}\), the blue one is the response, the dot its largest value, the dashed line the static value \(1\). The short pulse, \(t_{\mathrm{d}} = 0{,}25\,T_{\mathrm{n}}\), is over before the cantilever has moved much, and the largest value occurs after it. For \(t_{\mathrm{d}} = 0{,}81\,T_{\mathrm{n}}\) the force acts during almost the whole first swing and drives it to \(1{,}768\). The long pulse, \(t_{\mathrm{d}} = 3\,T_{\mathrm{n}}\), the cantilever follows: the blue curve winds around the grey one. The next theorem says all three with formulas.

Theorem 9.93 Response to a half-sine pulse (odziv na polsinusni sunek)

Let \(m > 0\), \(k > 0\), \(c = 0\), \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = \sqrt{k/m}\), \(t_{\mathrm{d}} > 0\), \(\Omega = \pi/t_{\mathrm{d}}\) and \(r = \Omega/\omega_{\mathrm{n}} \ne 1\). Let \(Q(t) = Q_0\sin\Omega t\) for \(0 \le t \le t_{\mathrm{d}}\) and \(Q(t) = 0\) for \(t > t_{\mathrm{d}}\), \(q(0) = 0\) and \(\dot q(0) = 0\). Then:

  1. During the pulse, for \(0 \le t \le t_{\mathrm{d}}\),
    \[q(t) = \frac{Q_0}{k}\,\frac{\sin\Omega t - r\,\sin\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}{1 - r^2} . \tag{9.96}\]
  2. After the pulse, for \(t \ge t_{\mathrm{d}}\),
    \[q(t) = \frac{Q_0}{k}\,\frac{2\,r\,\cos\dfrac{\pi}{2 r}}{r^2 - 1}\,\sin\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\Big(t - \frac{t_{\mathrm{d}}}{2}\Big) . \tag{9.97}\]
  3. For \(r > 1\), that is \(t_{\mathrm{d}} < T_{\mathrm{n}}/2\), \(q\) is increasing during the pulse and \(\max_{\mathrm{t}} |q| = \dfrac{Q_0}{k}\,\dfrac{2\,r\cos\frac{\pi}{2r}}{r^2 - 1}\), reached after the pulse.
  4. For \(r < 1\), that is \(t_{\mathrm{d}} > T_{\mathrm{n}}/2\), \(q\) has stationary points on the open interval \((0, t_{\mathrm{d}})\) exactly at those times \(t = \dfrac{2\pi j}{\omega_{\mathrm{n}} + \Omega}\) and \(t = \dfrac{2\pi j}{\omega_{\mathrm{n}} - \Omega}\), \(j = 1, 2, \dots\), that are smaller than \(t_{\mathrm{d}}\). The values of \(q\) at them are \(\dfrac{Q_0}{k}\,\dfrac{1}{1 - r}\,\sin\dfrac{2\pi j\,r}{1 + r}\) and \(\dfrac{Q_0}{k}\,\dfrac{1}{1 + r}\,\sin\dfrac{2\pi j\,r}{1 - r}\) respectively.
  5. For \(r < 1\) and \(0 \le t \le t_{\mathrm{d}}\) we have \(\Big|q(t) - \dfrac{Q_0}{k}\sin\Omega t\Big| \le \dfrac{Q_0}{k}\,\dfrac{r}{1 - r}\), and \(\max_{\mathrm{t}} |q|\) lies between \(\dfrac{Q_0}{k}\Big(1 - \dfrac{r}{1 - r}\Big)\) and \(\dfrac{Q_0}{k}\Big(1 + \dfrac{r}{1 - r}\Big)\).
Proof

We must show the form of the motion during the pulse and after it, then where the largest value is for a short and where for a long pulse. We abbreviate \(\omega = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\).

  1. Point 1. The function \(q_{\mathrm{p}} = \frac{Q_0}{k}\,\frac{\sin\Omega t}{1 - r^2}\) solves the equation \(m\,\ddot q + k\,q = Q_0\sin\Omega t\): since \(\ddot q_{\mathrm{p}} = -\Omega^2 q_{\mathrm{p}}\) and \(m\,\Omega^2 = k\,r^2\), we have \(m\,\ddot q_{\mathrm{p}} + k\,q_{\mathrm{p}} = k\,(1 - r^2)\,q_{\mathrm{p}} = Q_0\sin\Omega t\). This is the motion in step with the force.
  2. The function \(-\frac{Q_0}{k}\,\frac{r}{1 - r^2}\sin\omega t\) solves the free equation (theorem 8.138), so the sum of the two, which is (9.96), solves the equation with the force by lemma 9.6. Its value at \(0\) is zero, its derivative \(\frac{Q_0}{k}\,\frac{\Omega\cos\Omega t - r\,\omega\cos\omega t}{1 - r^2}\) equals at \(0\) \(\frac{Q_0}{k}\,\frac{\Omega - r\,\omega}{1 - r^2} = 0\), because \(r\,\omega = \Omega\). By theorem 9.41 the solution with the given initial values is unique. The natural vibration is added in exactly the amount that makes the cantilever start from rest.
  3. Point 2. At the end of the pulse \(\Omega t_{\mathrm{d}} = \pi\), so \(\sin\Omega t_{\mathrm{d}} = 0\) and \(\cos\Omega t_{\mathrm{d}} = -1\). From step 2 we have \(q(t_{\mathrm{d}}) = -\frac{Q_0}{k}\,\frac{r}{1 - r^2}\sin\omega t_{\mathrm{d}}\) and \(\dot q(t_{\mathrm{d}}) = \frac{Q_0}{k}\,\frac{-\Omega - r\,\omega\cos\omega t_{\mathrm{d}}}{1 - r^2} = -\frac{Q_0}{k}\,\frac{r\,\omega}{1 - r^2}\,(1 + \cos\omega t_{\mathrm{d}})\).
  4. For \(t \ge t_{\mathrm{d}}\) the force is zero and the motion free. As in the proof of theorem 9.84 (step 2) we have \(q(t_{\mathrm{d}} + s) = q(t_{\mathrm{d}})\cos\omega s + \frac{\dot q(t_{\mathrm{d}})}{\omega}\sin\omega s\). We insert step 3: \(q(t_{\mathrm{d}} + s) = -\frac{Q_0}{k}\,\frac{r}{1 - r^2}\big[\sin\omega t_{\mathrm{d}}\cos\omega s + \cos\omega t_{\mathrm{d}}\sin\omega s + \sin\omega s\big]\). By the addition theorem (corollary 8.16) the first two terms are \(\sin\omega(t_{\mathrm{d}} + s)\), so with \(t = t_{\mathrm{d}} + s\) the square bracket is \(\sin\omega t + \sin\omega(t - t_{\mathrm{d}})\).
  5. With \(\alpha = \omega(t - t_{\mathrm{d}}/2)\) and \(\beta = \omega t_{\mathrm{d}}/2\) we have \(\sin(\alpha + \beta) + \sin(\alpha - \beta) = 2\sin\alpha\cos\beta\) (corollary 8.16). Since \(\beta = \omega\pi/(2\Omega) = \pi/(2r)\) and \(-\frac{r}{1 - r^2} = \frac{r}{r^2 - 1}\), we get (9.97): a free vibration with a constant amplitude.
  6. Point 3. Let \(r > 1\). The derivative from step 2 is \(\dot q = \frac{Q_0}{k}\,\frac{\Omega\,(\cos\Omega t - \cos\omega t)}{1 - r^2}\). For \(0 < t < t_{\mathrm{d}}\) we have \(0 < \omega t < \Omega t < \pi\), and the cosine decreases on \([0, \pi]\), so \(\cos\Omega t < \cos\omega t\). The numerator and the denominator are negative, hence \(\dot q > 0\): during the pulse the response only rises.
  7. During the pulse therefore \(q \le q(t_{\mathrm{d}})\), and \(q(t_{\mathrm{d}})\) is one of the values of the free vibration of step 5, so in size it is not larger than its amplitude. The amplitude is reached after the pulse, when \(\sin\omega(t - t_{\mathrm{d}}/2) = \pm 1\). For \(r > 1\) we have \(\pi/(2r) < \pi/2\) and \(\cos\frac{\pi}{2r} > 0\), so the amplitude is the expression of point 3: the dot in the top panel.
  8. Point 4. Let \(r < 1\). As in step 5, with \(\alpha = (\omega + \Omega)t/2\) and \(\beta = (\omega - \Omega)t/2\), we have \(\cos\Omega t - \cos\omega t = \cos(\alpha - \beta) - \cos(\alpha + \beta) = 2\sin\alpha\sin\beta\). The derivative \(\dot q\) is therefore zero exactly when \(\sin\frac{(\omega + \Omega)t}{2} = 0\) or \(\sin\frac{(\omega - \Omega)t}{2} = 0\), that is at \((\omega \pm \Omega)\,t = 2\pi j\) with an integer \(j \ge 1\) (the zeros of the sine are the multiples of \(\pi\), lemma 8.11). These are the peaks and the valleys of the response during the pulse.
  9. At \((\omega + \Omega)\,t = 2\pi j\) we have \(\omega t = 2\pi j - \Omega t\), so \(\sin\omega t = -\sin\Omega t\) and from (9.96) \(q = \frac{Q_0}{k}\,\frac{(1 + r)\sin\Omega t}{1 - r^2} = \frac{Q_0}{k}\,\frac{\sin\Omega t}{1 - r}\) with \(\Omega t = 2\pi j\,\Omega/(\omega + \Omega) = 2\pi j\,r/(1 + r)\). At \((\omega - \Omega)\,t = 2\pi j\) we have \(\sin\omega t = \sin\Omega t\) and \(q = \frac{Q_0}{k}\,\frac{\sin\Omega t}{1 + r}\) with \(\Omega t = 2\pi j\,r/(1 - r)\). For \(j = 1\) and \(r = 0{,}617\) the first formula gives \(\sin(2{,}398)/0{,}383 = 1{,}768\).
  10. Point 5. From (9.96) we have \(q - \frac{Q_0}{k}\sin\Omega t = \frac{Q_0}{k}\,\frac{r^2\sin\Omega t - r\sin\omega t}{1 - r^2}\), in size at most \(\frac{Q_0}{k}\,\frac{r^2 + r}{1 - r^2} = \frac{Q_0}{k}\,\frac{r}{1 - r}\). The response departs from the grey curve of the force by at most this much.
  11. At \(t = t_{\mathrm{d}}/2\) we have \(\sin\Omega t = 1\), so there \(q \ge \frac{Q_0}{k}(1 - \frac{r}{1 - r})\). During the pulse \(|q| \le \frac{Q_0}{k}(1 + \frac{r}{1 - r})\), because \(|\sin\Omega t| \le 1\). After the pulse the amplitude of step 5 is at most \(\frac{Q_0}{k}\,\frac{2r}{1 - r^2}\), and this is not more than \(\frac{Q_0}{k}\,\frac{1}{1 - r} = \frac{Q_0}{k}(1 + \frac{r}{1 - r})\), because \(2r \le 1 + r\). The largest value is therefore close to the static one.

Source: The theorem, the proof and the numbers (NumPy) are ours, the project books do not treat the half-sine pulse. CK §5.5, example 5.3 (PDF pp. 147-149) does the same for a triangular pulse: the largest value during the pulse and after it, the spectrum is the larger of the two.

The proof showed that the response is a sum of two vibrations, one in step with the force and one with the natural frequency, and that for a short pulse the largest value occurs after the pulse, for a long one during it. We left out the case \(r = 1\), because both formulas have a zero in the denominator there. The orange one is the spectrum of the half-sine pulse, the dots are the three pulses of figure 9.104. Let us read it from left to right.

For short pulses the orange curve keeps to the line \(4\,t_{\mathrm{d}}/T_{\mathrm{n}}\). Where does the \(4\) come from? The impulse of the half-sine pulse is the area under half of the wave, \(I = \int_0^{t_{\mathrm{d}}} Q_0\sin(\pi t/t_{\mathrm{d}})\,\mathrm{d}t = 2\,Q_0\,t_{\mathrm{d}}/\pi\). A blow with this impulse gives the amplitude \(I/(m\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}})\), so in units of \(Q_0/k\) it is \(2\,t_{\mathrm{d}}\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}/\pi = 4\,t_{\mathrm{d}}/T_{\mathrm{n}}\). For the rectangular pulse the impulse is \(Q_0\,t_{\mathrm{d}}\) and the line \(2\pi\,t_{\mathrm{d}}/T_{\mathrm{n}}\). The ratio of the two is \(2/\pi = 0{,}637\), the ratio of the areas: at \(t_{\mathrm{d}} = 0{,}1\,T_{\mathrm{n}}\) the half-sine pulse gives \(0{,}3963\), the rectangular one \(0{,}6180\), and the ratio is \(0{,}641\). A short pulse therefore acts only with its area, the shape does not count.

For middle durations the shape counts. The half-sine spectrum exceeds the static value \(1\) at \(t_{\mathrm{d}}/T_{\mathrm{n}} = 0{,}267\) (the rectangular one already at \(1/6\)) and reaches the largest value \(1{,}768\) at \(t_{\mathrm{d}}/T_{\mathrm{n}} = 0{,}81\). Why exactly there? The force pushes in the direction of the motion during almost the whole first swing of the cantilever, it neither dies too early nor persists when the cantilever is already returning. It does not reach the value \(2\), because the force does not switch on at once.

For long pulses the half-sine spectrum falls toward \(1\). Point 5 of the theorem with \(r = 1/(2a)\) gives the bound \(1 + \frac{r}{1 - r} = 1 + \frac{1}{2a - 1}\), the grey dashed curve: at \(t_{\mathrm{d}} = 3\,T_{\mathrm{n}}\) the spectrum is \(1{,}170\) below the bound \(1{,}2\), and at \(10\,T_{\mathrm{n}}\) it is \(1{,}050\) below the bound \(1{,}053\). The blackwhite spectrum stays at \(2\), whatever the duration. The factor \(2\) of the rectangular pulse therefore does not come from the duration of the force but from the sudden switch-on: at the switch-on the cantilever rests at the position zero, the static position is \(Q_0/k\) away, and it swings about it with the swing \(Q_0/k\). A half-sine pulse of length \(t_{\mathrm{d}}\) rises for the time \(t_{\mathrm{d}}/2\), and by lemma 9.82 the swing after a rise in the time \(t_{\mathrm{r}}\) is at most \(1/\theta = T_{\mathrm{n}}/(\pi\,t_{\mathrm{r}})\) of the static value: a slow switch-on hardly wakes the vibration.

9.9.7 What decides, the shape or the impulse (kaj odloča, oblika ali impulz)

Let us return to the question from the introduction. The cantilever \(A\) shows three regions, depending on how the duration of the force \(t_{\mathrm{d}}\) compares with the period \(T_{\mathrm{n}}\). For \(t_{\mathrm{d}} \ge T_{\mathrm{n}}/2\) the size of the force decides, the response is \(2\,Q_0/k\) no matter how long the force still acts (theorem 9.84). This holds for a pulse that switches on in an instant. For the half-sine pulse, which rises gradually, the largest response is \(1{,}77\,Q_0/k\) and for a long duration only \(Q_0/k\) (theorem 9.93): in this region the size of the force and the speed of its switch-on decide. For \(t_{\mathrm{d}} \le T_{\mathrm{n}}/10\) almost only the impulse \(Q_0\,t_{\mathrm{d}}\) decides, the response differs from \(I\,h\) by at most \(1{,}6\) % in the largest value (rectangular pulse without damping) and by a shift \(t_{\mathrm{d}}/2\) in time (lemma 9.87). Between the two regions (in our table \(t_{\mathrm{d}} = T_{\mathrm{n}}/4\), with \(1{,}414\)) both quantities decide. The condition "short" is therefore not a matter of seconds but of the ratio \(t_{\mathrm{d}}/T_{\mathrm{n}}\): the same hammer is a long force for a very stiff cantilever (small \(T_{\mathrm{n}}\)) and a short one for a soft one. In section 9.10 we build from the impulse response the response to a force of arbitrary shape.
