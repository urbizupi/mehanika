Chapter 22 Shells

Chapter 22Lecture: Shells

1 A curved wall carries pressure by pulling

Take a steel pipe, closed at both ends, and fill it with oil under pressure. The pressure pushes on the wall perpendicular to it. But the wall is as thin as a sheet of metal and can only pull along itself, like an inflated rubber inner tube. How can a force that lies along the wall hold a force that is perpendicular to it? That is the question of this hour.

We write the numbers of the pipe on the board, we will need them all hour:

  • mid radius \(R = 50\) mm, wall thickness \(h = 2\) mm,
  • oil pressure \(p = 3\) MPa,
  • steel with \(E = 196\) GPa and \(\nu = 0.3\).

The wall is only \(h/R = 2/50 = 1/25\) of the radius thick.

First we draw the pipe in cross section, a circle. We keep only the upper half of the wall and cut it along the horizontal diameter. From the inside the pressure \(p\) pushes on it, at every point perpendicular to the wall, so each time in a different direction. What holds this arc so that it does not fly upward? Only the wall below the cut, which pulls it down in both cuts with the force \(N_\theta\) per millimetre of pipe length.

The claim of this hour is this: because the wall is curved, the pulls at the two ends of every small piece of wall have different directions, their sum points inward and holds the pressure. So the wall carries the pressure by tension alone, without bending, and the force in both cuts is \(N_\theta = pR = 3\ \text{MPa}\cdot 50\ \text{mm} = 150\) N/mm. Why exactly so, we now go down step by step.

1.1 Why is the pressure force on half the pipe \(p\cdot 2R\cdot L\)?

The pressure points a different way at every point of the arc. How can we add such forces at all? Take two points on the arc that lie symmetric about the vertical. The pressure at the left point points up and to the left, at the right point up and to the right. The horizontal parts are equal and opposite, so they cancel. The vertical parts add up. But how large is the vertical part on one small piece of wall?

We think of the arc as made of short straight pieces. Take one of them, of length \(s\), tilted by the angle \(\alpha\) from the horizontal. The pressure pushes on it at a right angle with the force \(p\,s\) per millimetre of pipe length. This force is tilted from the vertical by the same angle \(\alpha\), so its vertical part is \(p\,s\cos\alpha\). Now look at the shadow of the piece on the horizontal: its length is \(s\cos\alpha\). So the vertical part of the force is the pressure times the shadow of the piece.

The shadows of all pieces lie side by side and together make the shadow of the arc, for every division of the arc exactly \(2R\) wide. The shorter the pieces, the better they fit the arc. The shadow of the arc is a rectangle of width \(2R\) and length \(L\), so the pressure force upward is \(p\cdot 2R\cdot L\).

The arc is at rest. By Newton the sum of forces on it is therefore zero. Upward acts the pressure \(p\cdot 2RL\), downward the wall in both cuts, together \(2N_\theta L\):

\[2N_\theta L = 2pRL \quad\Longrightarrow\quad N_\theta = pR = 150\ \text{N/mm} .\]

We call this force the hoop force (obročna sila), because it pulls around the circumference just like the iron hoops that hold a barrel. The stress in the wall is the force per millimetre divided by the thickness: \(N_\theta/h = 150/2 = 75\) MPa.

1.2 Why may we put the pressure on the middle surface?

The wall has a thickness, and the oil pushes only on its inner side. How far off are we when we put the pressure at the radius \(R\)? Let us draw the wall with its thickness, strongly enlarged.

The wall has the thickness \(h\). In the calculation we replaced it with a single line in the middle, the middle surface of radius \(R\). But the oil pushes on the inner surface, at the radius \(a = R - h/2 = 49\) mm. The shadow the pressure really pushes on is therefore \(2a = 98\) mm wide, not \(2R = 100\) mm. The true force in both cuts is \(pa = 3\cdot 49 = 147\) N/mm, we got \(150\) N/mm.

So we state the assumption with three things.

  • Why it is right here: the difference is \(150 - 147 = 3\) N/mm, that is \(2\ \%\), and that is exactly \(h/(2R) = 2/100\). For a thin wall \(a\) is almost equal to \(R\).
  • When it does not hold: for a thick pipe with radii 40 and 60 mm, \(h/R = 0.4\) and the error is \(20\ \%\). Then we compute the wall as a thick body (In the book: 22.5.2).
  • What it does to the result: our force is too large by these \(2\ \%\). The largest stress, at the inner surface, is by the exact solution \(75.03\) MPa, almost exactly our \(75\) MPa (Perspective 3 below).

1.3 Why is the axial force half as large?

The oil also pushes on the end of the pipe. Who holds the end, and does the wall pull as hard there as around the circumference? We cut the pipe differently, across the axis. We keep the left part. On the left it is closed by the end, a circle of radius \(R\). The oil pushes outward on the end, in total the pressure times the area of the end, \(p\pi R^2\). What holds the left part so that it does not fly to the left? The wall at the cut, which pulls it to the right with the force \(N_\varphi\) per millimetre of circumference. The circumference is \(2\pi R\):

\[N_\varphi\cdot 2\pi R = p\pi R^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad N_\varphi = \frac{pR}{2} = 75\ \text{N/mm} .\]

Why exactly half? In the first cut the pressure pushed on a shadow of width \(2R\) and area \(2R\cdot L\), carried by cuts of total length \(2L\). Each millimetre of cut gets \(2RL/(2L) = R\) of area. In the second cut the pressure pushes on the end of area \(\pi R^2\), carried by the circumference \(2\pi R\). Each millimetre of cut gets \(\pi R^2/(2\pi R) = R/2\) of area. The same pressure, but each millimetre of cut carries half the area. The stresses are therefore \(75\) MPa around the circumference and \(37.5\) MPa along the axis.

1.4 Why do we need curvature at all?

The cuts gave the numbers, but not the reason. How can a pull along the wall hold a pressure perpendicular to it at all? For the answer we take a single small piece out of the wall and look at it as a piece of rope.

We draw the centre \(O\) and around it the arc of rope. The arc has the radius \(\rho\) and we see it from the centre under the angle \(\psi\). At both ends the rope pulls on the next piece of rope with the force \(S\), each time along the tangent, away from our piece. The rope has no bending stiffness, so it can only pull along itself.

Now comes what you have already felt with your hand. A taut rope that goes over the edge of a table pushes on the edge. Two forces that do not lie on one line have a sum that points between them. Let us add our two pulls by drawing them one after the other. The horizontal parts cancel, the vertical ones add up to the blue arrow toward the centre. Its size is \(2S\sin(\psi/2)\), because each pull contributes \(S\sin(\psi/2)\): the tangent at the end of the arc is tilted from the horizontal by half the angle \(\psi\).

From the inside the pressure \(p\) pushes on the arc. We get its sum as for the half pipe, with the shadow: the pressure times the chord, so \(p\cdot 2\rho\sin(\psi/2)\), outward. The piece is at rest, so the two sums are equal:

\[2S\sin(\psi/2) = p\cdot 2\rho\sin(\psi/2) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad S = p\rho .\]

The angle \(\psi\) has cancelled. For every piece, large or small, the same holds: the pull is the pressure times the radius.

And a flat wall? Let us make the radius larger and larger until the rope becomes straight. The pulls now lie on one line and point in opposite directions. Their sum is zero. The pressure still pushes, and nothing holds it. A flat wall cannot carry pressure by tension. It must bend, and bending is, as we will see in the next part, much softer. Curvature is what lets a wall carry load by pulling.

For the rope we assumed that the wall has no bending moments and no transverse forces, only the pull along itself. We call this the membrane state (membransko stanje), after the skin of a drum, which carries only by pulling in its own plane.

  • Why it is right here: far from both ends the wall is smooth, the pressure is continuous and nothing prevents the wall from expanding. The book lists, after its source, four conditions for the membrane state, without proof: an edge without transverse force and moment, free displacement and rotation of the edge, a smooth surface, a continuous load (In the book: 22.30).
  • When it does not hold: at an end or a joint that does not let the wall expand, and under a point force. There bending comes in (the next two parts).
  • What it does to the result: the forces follow from equilibrium alone, as above. They contain no \(E\), no \(\nu\), no \(h\). If we replace the steel by aluminium, \(N_\theta = 150\) N/mm stays.

1.5 What does all this stand on?

When we ask why to the end, we arrive at three things that need no explanation.

  • Two forces that do not lie on one line have a sum that points between them. A rope over an edge pushes on the edge. That is the triangle of pulls.
  • A body at rest has a zero sum of forces. That is Newton, and we used it for the arc of the pipe, for the end and for the piece of rope.
  • Pressure is force per area and is perpendicular to the wall. Those are the pressure arrows.

1.6 Back up: from the rope to the pipe and the sphere

Now we go from the ground back up. A curved rope holds the pressure with the pull \(S = p\rho\). The wall of the pipe is such a rope around the circumference, with radius \(\rho = R\). So \(N_\theta = pR = 150\) N/mm, the same number the cut of the half pipe gave.

Along the axis, however, the pipe is straight. There the wall is like the straight rope and the axial pull does not help against the pressure on the side wall. The axial force \(N_\varphi\) only holds the end.

A sphere is a rope in both directions at once, with the same radius \(R\). Each direction contributes \(S/R\), together

\[\frac{S}{R} + \frac{S}{R} = p \quad\Longrightarrow\quad S = \frac{pR}{2} = 75\ \text{N/mm} ,\]

and this is exactly the force that the cut across the axis gave for the pipe. A sphere of the same steel and with the same wall carries the pressure with half the force of the pipe.

1.7 Why does a general shell have two curvatures?

The pipe and the sphere are special cases. What if the curvature changes from point to point, as in a dome or a vessel with a rounded end? We look at a shell obtained by turning a curve about an axis.

We draw the axis of revolution \(z\) and the curve we turn, the meridian (meridian). On a globe this is a line from pole to pole: all places on it have noon at the same time. From the shell we cut a belt between two horizontal circles, parallels, the lower one at the angle \(\varphi_{\mathrm{b}}\). From the inside the load \(p_{\mathrm{n}}\) pushes on the belt. On both parallels the wall pulls it along the meridian with the meridional force \(N_\varphi\), away from the belt.

The view from above. The meridian in the middle of the belt lies on the \(x\) axis, the belt is a piece of an annulus between the meridians at the angles \(\pm\alpha\). From above we also see the meridional force, as a horizontal arrow along the radius. On both edges along the meridians the hoop force \(N_\theta\) pulls, along the tangent of the parallel. This is the rope again: both hoop forces pull the belt toward the axis and add up, like the pulls on the arc of rope.

So the wall is a rope in two directions at once. Each direction contributes its own pull, \(N_\varphi\) and \(N_\theta\), divided by its own radius of curvature, \(R_1\) along the meridian and \(R_2\) around the axis:

\[\frac{N_\varphi}{R_1} + \frac{N_\theta}{R_2} = p_{\mathrm{n}} .\]

This is the equation (22.32). The idea of the proof is the equilibrium of the belt in the \(x\) direction, combined with the equilibrium along the axis. In the book: 22.32. Navier wrote the same equation as \(S(1/\rho + 1/\rho_1) = p\).

Let us test it on the two numbers we already know. For the pipe the meridian is a straight line, \(1/R_1 = 0\), and \(N_\theta = pR\) remains. For the sphere \(R_1 = R_2 = R\) and \(N_\varphi = N_\theta\), so \(N = pR/2\).

Here too we assumed something: axial symmetry.

  • Why it is right here: the shape and the load are the same all around the axis, the oil pressure does not pick a side.
  • When it does not hold: under a load that is not the same around the axis, such as wind on a dome. Then the equilibrium of the cap is not enough (In the book: 22.32 and the paragraph after the corollary 22.34).
  • What it does to the result: the shear force in the wall \(N_{\varphi\theta}\) drops out, and one cap and one belt suffice to get both forces.

1.8 Perspective 1: the cut of a whole piece

The first perspective was the cut of the half pipe. There we needed no geometry of curvature, only the shadow of width \(2R\) and equilibrium. What does it add? The number in one line, which an engineer checks on a napkin.

1.9 Perspective 2: the rope

The second perspective was the piece of rope. This is the few-minute explanation: the pulls at the ends of a curved piece are not on one line, so their sum points toward the centre and holds the pressure. A flat wall does not have this. What does it add? The reason, and the flat wall as the limiting case where the reason disappears.

1.10 Perspective 3: numbers against the exact solution

How good is a single number \(pR/h\) for the whole thickness of the wall? We compare it with the three-dimensional solution of the thick pipe, the Lamé solution.

First we draw the horizontal line at \(1\): this is our membrane stress, a single value across the whole wall. Below it we draw the radius axis from the inner to the outer surface of our thin pipe, \(h/R = 0.04\). The true hoop stress falls from \(1.0004\) at the inner to \(0.960\) at the outer surface. The radial stress, which the membrane model does not have, goes from \(-0.04\) to zero. The difference from the membrane stress is everywhere smaller than the pressure \(p\), for our pipe under \(4\ \%\).

On the right the same picture for the thick pipe with the axis from 40 to 60 mm. Here the hoop stress falls from \(1.04\) to \(0.64\), the radial one is \(-0.40\) at the inner surface. A single number is not enough here. What does this perspective add? The bound, with a number, up to which everything we computed today holds. In the book: 22.37.

1.11 Perspective 4: history

Navier, in his book on the strength of materials, first printed in 1826, began its last chapter, on thin shells, exactly with the curved rope and got \(S = p\rho\) from it. Then he loaded thin iron spheres with the ratio \(R/h = 60\) by internal pressure until they burst. He found that the bursting strength was about the same as for a bar in tension. The comparison is made possible by the equation \(\sigma = p\rho/(2h)\), which gives the stress in the wall from the bursting pressure. This is our sphere from the part Back up, the pull \(pR/2\) divided by the thickness. What does it add? The formula was tested by breaking, not only derived.

Next time we look at what happens when the load is not a pressure that a pull can balance.

2 When the wall bends: the ring under two forces

A can holds the pressure of the drink inside it, yet between two fingers we squeeze it with no effort. The same wall, the same sheet metal. Where does such a difference come from? The answer tells when a shell carries load by tension and when it has to bend.

Our numbers stay on the board:

  • mid radius \(R = 50\) mm, wall thickness \(h = 2\) mm, so \(R/h = 25\),
  • pressure \(p = 3\) MPa,
  • \(E = 196\) GPa, \(\nu = 0.3\).

Let us draw a ring, a cross-section of a can or a pipe, one millimetre wide along the axis. We draw the original ring of radius \(R\) dashed. Into it we let the pressure \(p\). Every arrow pushes on the wall at a right angle, outward. From the first part we know what the wall does: it pulls along itself with the hoop force \(pR\) and widens uniformly by \(w = pR^2/(Eh) = 19.1\ \mu\)m.

Now take the same ring once more, again dashed, of radius \(R\). This time we squeeze it with two forces \(F\) along the vertical diameter, as fingers squeeze a can. Let us take \(F = pR = 150\) N/mm, exactly the hoop force under pressure. The ring is squeezed into an oval: top and bottom are pressed in, the sides bulge out.

What do we claim? The point where the force acts moves \(558\) times farther than the wall under pressure. A load that a pull along the wall cannot balance is carried by the wall in bending (upogib). And a thin wall is about \((R/h)^2\) times softer in bending than in tension (nateg).

Three words in this claim each ask for their why: why the pull cannot, why that means bending, and why bending is so soft.

2.1 Why can tension not hold the force \(F\)?

With what can a thin wall hold a force that presses perpendicular to it at all? Let us start from the rope, which we have all seen. We stretch a rope between two hooks and hang a weight \(G\) in the middle. Under the weight the rope breaks into a sharp corner. Why? Each piece of rope can only pull along itself, with the force \(S\). Since the two pieces at the corner do not lie on one line, their sum points upward and holds the weight. A straight rope with no corner would have both forces \(S\) on the same horizontal line and would give nothing upward.

Now the top of the ring, where the force \(F\) presses. The ring is smooth, it has no corner. So the tangent at the top is horizontal, the same to the left and to the right of the point. In the membrane state (membransko stanje) the wall can only act along itself, with the forces \(N\) along the tangent, whether it pulls or pushes. What is a component of a force? How much of the force points in a given direction. A force along a horizontal line has no vertical component. So no membrane force can balance the vertical force \(F\).

Why was it different under pressure? Pressure is spread over the whole arc. Each small piece of the arc holds it with its own small change of direction, like the rope of the first part. A point force would need a finite change of direction at one point, that is a corner, which a smooth ring does not have.

So what holds \(F\)? Let us cut the ring to the left and to the right of the top. In each cut a shear force (strižna sila) \(F/2\) acts, perpendicular to the mid line of the ring. The two halves together hold \(F\). In the book: 22.5.6.

2.2 Why does a shear force mean bending?

The shear at the top holds the force \(F\). But what does this force do to the wall farther from the top? Let us also cut the ring along the horizontal diameter and take a quarter. We draw the arc from the top to the cut on the right, with centre \(O\) and radius \(R\). A place on the arc is given by the angle \(\theta\) from the top, that is from the point of the force.

In the right cut the lower half of the ring pushes the upper one with the force \(F/2\) upward. At the top this half of the force was a shear force, here it is axial, because the wall is vertical here. The shear force here is zero by symmetry, but the moment \(M_0\) is unknown.

Look at the piece of arc from the cut at the angle \(\theta\) to the right cut. The force \(F/2\) does not act through the cut at \(\theta\). The horizontal distance between them is \(R(1 - \sin\theta)\), the lever arm. A force on a lever arm turns, so the cut at \(\theta\) must carry a moment

\[M(\theta) = M_0 - \frac{F}{2}\,R\,(1 - \sin\theta) .\]

The lever arm is largest at the top, where \(\theta = 0\). There the moment is largest in absolute value, \(FR/\pi\), with our force \(2387\) N mm/mm.

How large is \(M_0\)? Equilibrium does not give it, the ring is statically indeterminate. Among all \(M_0\) that satisfy equilibrium the ring picks the one with the least energy. This gives \(M_0 = \tfrac{FR}{2}\big(1 - \tfrac{2}{\pi}\big) = 0.18\,FR\). In the book: 22.41, points 1 to 3 of the proof.

A moment in the wall is bending: where there is a moment, the mid line curves. The top and the bottom are pressed in, the sides widen, and the ring becomes an oval.

2.3 Why is bending so much softer than tension?

We can pull the same piece of sheet metal or bend it. What happens differently in the wall in the one case and in the other? Take a short piece of wall and hold its left end. Before loading it is a rectangle, \(h\) thick. First we pull it along the wall. The piece gets longer. All fibres across the thickness, at the top, in the middle and at the bottom, lengthen equally. They all resist equally, so a wall twice as thick means twice as many fibres: the stiffness in tension grows like \(h\). For our wall \(C = 430\,769\) N/mm.

Now the same piece, again before loading, is bent. The right end face turns about the middle. The upper fibres lengthen, the lower ones shorten, the more the farther they are from the middle. The middle fibre does not lengthen at all. Only part of the fibres resist bending, and the force of each acts on a lever arm of at most \(h/2\).

What happens if we make the wall twice as thick? Let us count on the board, for the same turn of the end face:

  • the fibres at the edge are twice as far from the middle, they stretch twice as much: \(\times 2\),
  • their force acts on a lever arm twice as long: \(\times 2\),
  • there are twice as many fibres: \(\times 2\),
  • together \(\times 8 = 2^3\).

The bending stiffness grows like \(h^3\). For our wall \(D = 143\,590\) N mm, for a ring of width one \(EI = Eh^3/12\).

How do \(h\) and \(h^3\) turn into \((R/h)^2\)? Under pressure the wall stretches, \(w = pR^2/(Eh)\). Under the force it bends, the displacement of the point of the force is proportional to \(FR^3/(Eh^3)\). With \(F = pR\) the quotient is proportional to \(R^2/h^2\). The lemma of the book gives exactly

\[\frac{\delta/2}{w} = 0.8927\,\Big(\frac{R}{h}\Big)^2 = 558 \quad\text{at } R/h = 25 .\]

In the book: 22.41, point 3, and 22.3.3 and 22.3.6 for \(h\) and \(h^3\).

2.4 Why then does a pressure vessel not turn oval?

If bending is so cheap, why does not every shell bend? Because only some shapes allow bending without stretching the mid surface. Let us try by hand. A sheet of paper rolls into a cylinder with no effort, but we cannot stretch it. A table tennis ball, which bulges everywhere like a dome, cannot be pressed in without stretching its surface.

What tells them apart? The dome has at every point two curvatures of the same sign: through the point with the normal \(\vek n\) go two sections, and the first section and the second section curve to the same side of the tangent. The pipe has one curvature zero, it is straight along the axis. So we can unroll it into a flat sheet and roll it back without stretching, and this is exactly what the ring under the two forces does. The saddle has curvatures of opposite signs, we do not need it here.

The book states after VK, without proof, that closed convex surfaces cannot bend without stretching, nor can cylinders, cones and domes with rigid rings at their edges. There the membrane state stands. The ring is a section of a cylinder without such rings, so it bends without stretching (neraztezno upogibanje), and the oval is that bending: the mid line hardly lengthens. In the book: 22.5.6, last paragraph, and 22.2.4.

2.5 The ground: what we know without explanation

All three levels rest on three statements:

  • a force along a line has no component perpendicular to it, that is the meaning of the word component (the horizontal forces \(N\)),
  • a sheet of paper bends easily but we cannot stretch it, that is experience (tension and bending),
  • among the states a body may take, it takes the one with the least energy (Rayleigh for displacements, in the book 22.5.6, for \(M_0\) the same principle among the equilibrium states, the moment \(M_0\)).

2.6 Back up

Let us now build it back up, this is the one minute explanation. A smooth ring has no corner at the top, so the wall forces do not reach the force \(F\) and the shear carries it. Shear on a lever arm gives a moment, the moment curves the wall. Only the fibres far from the middle resist bending, on a lever arm up to \(h/2\), so the wall is soft in bending.

On the first figure this reads as follows. Under pressure the wall widens by \(w = 19.1\ \mu\)m. Under the force \(F = pR\) the point of the force moves by

\[\frac{\delta}{2} = 558\,w = 10.7\ \text{mm} .\]

That is why the left figure is drawn about \(500\) times more magnified than the right one.

2.7 The view through energy

We can say the same without forces. The stretching energy of the wall is proportional to \(h\), the bending energy to \(h^3\), like the stiffnesses. For the ring under the two forces the lemma gives

\[\frac{U_{\mathrm{b}}}{U_{\mathrm{m}}} = 2.273\,\Big(\frac{R}{h}\Big)^2 = 1421 .\]

At the same force the energy is the larger, the more compliant the mode. So bending is \(1421\) times more compliant. Turned around: stretching would cost a factor of order \((R/h)^2\) more energy, so the ring avoids it. What does this view add? We need no tangent and no corner, only a comparison of two prices.

Rayleigh saw the same difference in the vibration of thin shells. He told apart extensional vibration with energy proportional to the thickness and bending vibration with energy proportional to the cube of the thickness. He concluded that in a very thin shell the displacement will be purely bending, if the given conditions allow it. In the book: the history box in 22.5.6.

2.8 The view through numbers

Let us write both states side by side on the board, at \(F = pR\):

pressure \(p\) forces \(F = pR\)
force in the wall tension \(pR = 150\) N/mm moment up to \(FR/\pi = 2387\) N mm/mm
displacement \(w = 19.1\ \mu\)m \(\delta/2 = 10.7\) mm
ratio of displacements \(1\) \(558\)

The first row is on the figure under pressure and under the force, the second is the widening and the oval. What does this view add? The same sheet metal is barely moved by one load and moved by a fifth of the radius by the other.

2.9 Assumptions

Four assumptions entered the calculation. For each let us say why it holds here, when it does not, and what it costs.

Bending energy only. It entered at the moment \(M_0\), which we got from the least bending energy. Why does it hold? Since \(U_{\mathrm{b}}/U_{\mathrm{m}} = 1421\), the share of stretching is \(1/1421 = 0.07\ \%\). The shear force gives an energy of the same order as stretching, so also negligible. When does it not hold? For a thick ring, since the ratio falls like \((R/h)^2\): at \(R/h = 2.5\) it is only \(14.2\) and the share \(7\ \%\). Effect: \(\delta\) is too small by that share.

The energy of a straight beam for an arc. The expression for the bending energy holds for a straight beam, for an arc we assumed it. Let us draw a piece of the pipe wall between two radii that enclose the angle \(\Delta\theta\), and the fibres along the normal. The mid arc has the length we compute with. The inner arc is shorter, the outer one longer, at the distance \(z\) by the factor \(1 + z/R\). Why does it hold? The fibres differ in length by at most \(h/(2R) = 2\ \%\). Effect: an error of order \(2\ \%\) at \(R/h = 25\). When does it not hold? At \(h/R = 0.4\), as drawn, the arcs are in the ratio \(60 : 40\).

The normal stays straight (Kirchhoff and Love). This is how we drew the end face of the piece in bending: straight, so the elongation of a fibre grows linearly with the distance from the middle. Why does it hold? For a thin wall the error of this assumption is \(2\ \%\), measured against the exact Lamé solution of the pipe. When does it not hold? For a thick pipe with \(h/R = 0.4\), where the displacements of the inner and outer surfaces differ by \(27\ \%\). In the book: 22.24.

Small displacements. We measured the lever arm on the undeformed ring. Why does it hold? When the displacement is small compared with \(R\), the lever arm hardly changes. When not? At \(F = pR\) we have \(\delta/2 = 10.7\) mm, a fifth of the radius, so this number is only an estimate. At a force ten times smaller the displacement is \(1.07\) mm, \(2\ \%\) of the radius. Effect on the claim: both displacements are proportional to the force, so the ratio \(558\) does not depend on the force and the claim holds.

3 The edge effect: bending stays near the edge

We stay with our steel pipe. The same numbers are still on the board:

  • mid radius \(R = 50\) mm and wall thickness \(h = 2\) mm,
  • oil pressure \(p = 3\) MPa,
  • steel with \(E = 196\) GPa and \(\nu = 0.3\).

From the first part we know that the wall carries the pressure by pulling. By Hooke's law it widens by \(w_{\mathrm{p}} = 16.263\ \mu\)m while doing so. Now we weld the end of the pipe to a thick flat plate. The plate is rigid, so it holds the edge of the wall still. The pipe wants to widen, the edge stays at zero. How far into the pipe does this conflict reach? To the other end of the pipe, or only a little way?

Let us draw. First the axis of the pipe, along it we measure the distance \(x\) from the edge. Above the axis is the wall, at the radius \(R = 50\) mm from the axis, \(h = 2\) mm thick. On the left is the plate the wall is welded to. The hatching means that the plate does not move. From inside, the oil pushes on the wall with the pressure \(p = 3\) MPa. Before the pressure the mid line of the wall was here, straight. Where does it go under pressure? Like this: at the plate it stays in place and horizontal, then it rises. Far from the plate the wall widens by exactly the membrane displacement \(w_{\mathrm{p}} = 16.263\ \mu\)m, as if there were no plate.

Where does the whole transition happen? In a strip at the edge, only \(\ell = \pi/\beta = 2.444\sqrt{Rh} = 24.44\) mm long. That is half the radius, or twelve thicknesses. So the claim of this part is: where an edge or a joint does not allow the wall its membrane displacement, bending appears, which oscillates and dies out over this distance. Beyond that the simple membrane calculation of the first part holds.

The claim holds four whys, we take them in turn.

3.1 Why does the edge disturb the wall at all?

What does the membrane want, and what does the plate want? Let us look at the two demands that clash. The membrane solution wants the wall to be out by \(w_{\mathrm{p}}\) everywhere. The plate wants two things: displacement \(w = 0\) and slope \(w' = 0\), since a welded edge neither moves nor turns. Both demands cannot hold at the same point. So the wall has to pass somewhere from zero to \(w_{\mathrm{p}}\), and on that transition it curves.

Why is this new? The membrane of the first part only has pull along the wall, it has no moment and no shear force. A curved transition means a moment in the wall. How does the moment get in? The ring right at the plate does not widen, a ring a little farther widens more. The hoop force grows with the widening, so two neighbouring rings push on the wall with different forces. A shear force in the wall has to take the difference. But in a beam a shear force never comes alone: where it acts, the moment changes along the beam (\(M' = -Q\), 5). This is how bending gets into the wall. In the book: 22.6.

Do we need the plate? No. Take a vessel closed by a hemisphere. We draw the axis and the wall before the pressure: the straight line of the cylinder and a quarter circle of the hemisphere. Under pressure the cylinder widens by \(16.263\ \mu\)m, the hemisphere only by \(6.696\ \mu\)m, because the pull in the wall of a sphere is half the hoop pull in the pipe (first part). Between the ends a gap \(\Delta = 9.566\ \mu\)m would remain. The wall must not tear. In thought we cut the joint and move the shells apart: the end of the cylinder hangs higher than the end of the hemisphere. The axial forces are equal on both sides, \(pR/2\), and balance each other. Radially the joint pushes the ends together with the forces \(Q_0\), and the joined wall settles at the joint halfway. Here too two membrane answers disagree. That is all an edge is: a place where the membrane alone cannot meet the demands. In the book: 22.7, (22.50).

Assumption at the joint: the connection is smooth, with no notch and no stiff ring, and the displacements are small. Why does this hold? The largest displacement, \(16.263\ \mu\)m, is only \(0.008\) of the thickness. When not? A ring at the joint would take part of the force \(Q_0\) itself and the calculation would change.

3.2 Why does the disturbance die out instead of reaching along the whole pipe?

A straight beam clamped at one end feels the clamp along its whole length. Why should the wall be different?

We cut a strip out of the wall. We draw the outline of the pipe, seen along the axis, and cut it with two planes through the axis that make the angle \(\alpha\). From inside the oil pushes on the strip. The rest of the wall pulls it along the tangent at both cuts with the hoop force (obročna sila) \(N_2\). Because the wall is curved, the two pulls are not on one line. Their sum points toward the axis, just like the rope over a corner in the first part.

What happens when the wall widens by \(w\)? The circumference \(2\pi R\) grows by \(2\pi w\), that is by the fraction \(w/R\). By Hooke's law the hoop force therefore grows by \(Eh\,w/R\), and this larger force, curved around the axis, pulls the wall back toward the axis with \(Eh\,w/R^2\) per unit area. We know this from our hands: a rubber band stretched around the fingers squeezes them the harder, the more we stretch it.

Now we look at the same strip from the side, along the axis \(x\), with the deflection \(w\) outward. It is a beam. From below the pressure \(p\) loads it, from above the hoop forces pull it back, and at its cuts the beam has its own shear force \(Q\) and moment \(M\). The balance of this strip is the equation of the cylinder:

\[D\,w'''' + \frac{Eh}{R^2}\,w = p - \frac{\nu N_1}{R} .\]

The first term is the beam that resists curving, with its moment and shear force and the bending stiffness \(D = Eh^3/(12(1-\nu^2))\). The second term is the hoop forces, which act as springs: the larger the deflection, the harder they pull back. On the right is the pressure with a small correction from the axial force \(N_1\). The derivation is in the book: 22.42.

This model has a name. Winkler proposed a foundation that answers at every point with a force proportional to the deflection, so we speak of a beam on a Winkler foundation (Winklerjeva podlaga). Here the foundation is no new material. The foundation is the hoop forces of the wall itself, for our pipe with the stiffness \(Eh/R^2 = 156.80\) N/mm\(^3\).

Let us go back to the first figure and draw these springs. The orange arrows pull the wall toward the axis, their length proportional to the deflection. At the plate they are short, where the wall is higher they are longer. Where \(w = w_{\mathrm{p}}\) they exactly balance the pressure. So every ring is tied to its own spring that pulls it toward \(w_{\mathrm{p}}\). The disturbance from the edge cannot get far, because every spring on the way swallows part of it.

And without the springs? If the radius were infinite, the wall would be a straight beam. A strip \(100\) mm long, clamped at one end, would sag by \(261.2\) mm under the same pressure. Thanks to the springs the pipe widens by \(16.263\ \mu\)m. The curvature is what confines the disturbance.

3.3 Why exactly \(\sqrt{Rh}\)?

How long will the transition from zero to \(w_{\mathrm{p}}\) be that the edge forces on the wall, that is the strip at the edge? Why not shorter, and why not longer? Let us compare three bulges of the wall, all of the same height.

First we draw the wall before the pressure, three times. At the top is a short bulge, only a few millimetres long. What resists it? The beam, with its moment: every derivative in \(D\,w''''\) divides by \(\ell\), so the resistance of the beam is of order \(D\,w/\ell^4\). For such a short bulge the beam's resistance is huge, and there are only one or two springs. At the bottom is a long bulge, much longer than \(\sqrt{Rh}\). Here the beam is almost a straight line and hardly resists, but many springs are stretched, each with \(Eh\,w/R^2\), so the springs win.

What follows? A short transition is expensive for the beam, a long one is expensive for the springs. The wall picks the length in between, \(\ell = 24.44\) mm, where the resistance of the beam and the resistance of the springs are of the same order:

\[\frac{D}{\ell^4} \sim \frac{Eh}{R^2} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \ell^4 \sim \frac{R^2h^2}{12(1 - \nu^2)} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \ell \sim \sqrt{Rh} .\]

This estimate gives only the order of magnitude, without the number in front. The exact length is carried by the decay constant (konstanta upadanja) \(\beta = \sqrt[4]{3(1 - \nu^2)}/\sqrt{Rh}\), named so because the disturbance decays as \(\mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}\) ((22.43)). For us \(\sqrt{Rh} = 10\) mm and \(1/\beta = 7.780\) mm. Up to the point where the disturbance has fallen to \(\mathrm{e}^{-\pi} = 0.043\) of its value at the edge, it is \(\ell = \pi/\beta = 24.44\) mm. In the book: 22.44.

What does \(\sqrt{Rh}\) tell us? The length is the geometric mean of the radius and the thickness, between \(2\) mm and \(50\) mm. A thinner wall has a shorter strip, but not in proportion: a wall four times thinner has a strip only half as long.

3.4 Why does the wall rise above \(w_{\mathrm{p}}\)?

Would you expect the wall to approach \(w_{\mathrm{p}}\) from below and stay there? Let us draw the deflection as a graph. The distance \(x\) from the edge runs to the right. At I is the clamped edge, the dashed line is \(w_{\mathrm{p}}\). The deflection starts at zero, horizontally, rises, overshoots \(w_{\mathrm{p}}\) and then approaches it.

Why does it overshoot? The equation of the cylinder without pressure has the solutions \(\mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}\cos\beta x\) and \(\mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}\sin\beta x\). The factor \(\mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}\) decays, the cosine and sine oscillate, both at the same rate \(\beta\). Beam and springs take turns: the spring pulls the wall toward \(w_{\mathrm{p}}\), the beam does not turn at once because of its stiffness, so the wall misses a little. At the clamped edge

\[w = w_{\mathrm{p}}\big(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}(\cos\beta x + \sin\beta x)\big) ,\]

largest at \(\beta x = \pi\), that is at \(x = 24.44\) mm, where \(w = (1 + \mathrm{e}^{-\pi})\,w_{\mathrm{p}} = 1.043\,w_{\mathrm{p}}\). This is why we chose \(\ell\) exactly so: at the end of the strip the disturbance is at its first peak and has already fallen to 4 %. In the book: 22.43, 22.45.

Where did the solutions with \(\mathrm{e}^{+\beta x}\) go? They grow. Far from the edge the wall does not know about the edge and its deflection must stay finite, so their coefficients are zero.

And the moment? Below it is largest at the edge, \(M_0 = 2D\beta^2 w_{\mathrm{p}} = 77.17\) N mm/mm, and it oscillates and dies out over the same length. At the edge this moment gives an axial stress of \(153.25\) MPa on the inner face, \(2.04\) times the membrane hoop stress of \(75\) MPa. So a thin pipe at a clamp is not safe just because the membrane stress is small.

3.5 Back up

Let us build it from the ground up. A stretched ring pulls back (Hooke, the rubber band around the fingers). A curved pull pushes toward the axis (the rope over a corner of the first part). Together they put springs of stiffness \(Eh/R^2\) under every strip of the wall. The strip itself is a beam that resists curving with \(D\). A beam on springs that we hold at zero at one end gives way only near that end, over the length where beam and springs are equally stiff. That is \(\sqrt{Rh}\) times \(2.444\), the strip of \(24.44\) mm. To the right of it the wall lies at the membrane value.

What does this mean for the whole vessel? The simple numbers of the first part hold everywhere except in strips \(24.44\) mm wide at edges and joints. There we calculate bending.

3.6 Perspective: the hinged edge

What if we only support the edge so that it cannot move outward but may turn? That is the right part, II, with a simply supported edge. The moment is zero at the edge, as we demand, but the bending has not gone, it has only moved into the wall: it reaches a third of the clamped moment already \(6.11\) mm from the edge. The deflection overshoots \(w_{\mathrm{p}}\) even more, by 7 %. And the bar is as long as at the clamped edge. What this view adds: the length \(\ell\) belongs to the wall, not to the edge. The edge only sets the constants \(C_1\) and \(C_2\). In the book: 22.46.

3.7 Perspective: the joint of cylinder and hemisphere

At the joint there is no plate. Is there a strip there too? We draw the axis \(x\) along the meridian, hemisphere on the left, cylinder on the right. Far from the joint the displacement lies at its membrane values, \(6.696\) and \(16.263\ \mu\)m. The displacement bridges the gap near the joint, at the joint it is exactly halfway, \(11.480\ \mu\)m. The moment is zero at the joint, largest at \(7.317\) N mm/mm at \(6.11\) mm, and zero again at \(24.44\) mm on both sides. The same strip, on both sides of the joint. What this view adds: an edge is any place where two membrane answers disagree. In the book: 22.48.

3.8 Perspective: finite elements

How long may a finite element of the pipe be? The horizontal axis is the element length \(h_{\mathrm{e}}\), measured in \(1/\beta\), the coarse mesh is on the right. On the left is the moment at the clamp divided by the true one, the dashed line is the true one. An element longer than the strip does not see the strip: at \(\beta h_{\mathrm{e}} > 4.3\) the moment is less than a quarter of the true one. When the element is much shorter than \(1/\beta\), the error falls below 5 % and 1 %, along the blue line, in proportion to \(h_{\mathrm{e}}^2\): an element half as long, a quarter of the error. What this view adds: \(\ell\) decides the mesh. Fine elements are needed only in the first \(24\) mm. Twelve elements of \(2\) mm there and three long ones beyond give an error of \(1.03\) %. In the book: 22.8.

3.9 Assumptions

Every assumption costs something. Let us say why it holds, when it does not and how much it changes.

  • The pressure acts on the mid surface, although the oil pushes on the inner one. As in the first part, for a thin wall this costs \(h/(2R) = 0.02\), that is 2 %, for a thick wall with \(h/R = 0.4\) it costs 20 %.
  • We write the balance of the strip on the undeformed shape. The axial force \(N_1\) on the tilted strip would add a term \(N_1 w''\). It holds because its ratio to the beam term is \(N_1/(2D\beta^2) = 0.016\), that is 1.6 %, at \(N_1 = 75\) N/mm. An axial tension stiffens the pipe slightly. When it does not hold: under a large axial compression the pipe can buckle, and the equation without this term does not see it.
  • The pipe is long, so we dropped the growing solutions. It holds when the other edge is farther than a few lengths \(\ell\). When not: in a pipe clamped at both ends and \(10\) mm long the two disturbances overlap and the moment at the edge is only 27 % of the value for a long pipe. The error of the moment is below 5 % for \(\beta L \ge 4.33\), that is \(L \ge 33.7\) mm or \(0.67\,R\). In the book: 22.6.4.
  • Kirchhoff and Love: the normal to the wall stays straight, there is no transverse shear strain. It holds because the thickness is small against the length over which the bending changes, \(h\beta = 0.26\). The book gives no number for this error, that would need a shell theory with shear strain.
  • Axial symmetry and constant \(p\) and \(N_1\). It holds because shape and pressure are the same all around the axis. When not: under a load that is not the same all around the axis. Effect: we calculate a single strip instead of the whole wall.
