Chapter 18 Plates

18.2 Equilibrium of a plate

In the previous section we described the plate by the deflection \(w(x, y)\) of its middle plane and computed from it the displacements, strains, stresses and the moments \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(M_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(M_{\mathrm{xy}}\) per unit length. The deflection itself is not yet known. For a beam we get it from the deflection curve equation \(EI\,v'''' = q\) (theorem 5.42), which follows from the equilibrium of a piece of the beam (lemma 5.10). This section takes the same road in two dimensions. We ask three questions. Which forces besides the moments must a piece of plate carry to be in equilibrium? Which equation connects the deflection \(w\) with the load \(q\)? And which stress belongs to these forces, when the Kirchhoff model cannot compute them from the strain, which in it is zero (section 5.3.4)?

Take the plate as fixed in section 18.1: homogeneous, isotropic, linearly elastic, of constant thickness \(h\), with modulus of elasticity \(E\) and Poisson's ratio \(\nu\), and with rigidity \(D = E h^3/(12(1 - \nu^2))\) (definition 18.6). The middle plane is the plane \(xy\) and the axis \(z\) is perpendicular to it and points the same way as the deflection \(w\) and the load \(q\), as in equation (13.42) for the beam. Our example is a steel plate with \(E = 196\) GPa, \(\nu = 0{,}3\), \(h = 10\) mm. Then \(D = 17\,948{,}7\) N m and the shear modulus is \(G = 75{,}385\) GPa. The load is \(q = 10\) kPa, that is \(10\,000\) N in the direction \(z\) on every square metre of the middle plane.

18.2.1 Transverse shear forces

Cut a piece out of the plate: a rectangle \(R = [x_1, x_2] \times [y_1, y_2]\) of the middle plane together with the material above and below it up to both surfaces of the plate (piece, figure 18.12). We drew the thickness much larger than the true one, and the dashed plane is the middle plane. We put the coordinates beside the piece as axes \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\), with \(z\) pointing up, as in section 18.1, and the load \(q\) positive in the direction \(z\).

Three influences act on the piece. The first is the load \(q\), a force \(q\) per unit area, acting on the upper surface. It is drawn for \(q > 0\), so upward. A downward load, such as weight, has \(q < 0\) (section 18.1). Because of it the total force on the piece is \(\int_{\mathrm{R}} q\,\mathrm{d}A\) and it points in the direction \(z\). The second and third come through the side faces, where the rest of the plate is cut away: there the stress vector acts on the piece (definition 2.20). By Newton the equilibrium of the piece demands that the sum of all forces on it is zero (definition 2.3). At least one of these forces must therefore oppose the load in the direction \(z\).

Which one? Look at the moment \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\) on the right face. It is drawn as a pair of forces: at the top the normal stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) pushes the face inward, at the bottom it pulls it outward, because \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) is linear through the thickness and changes sign at \(z = 0\) (theorem 18.3). Both forces are horizontal, their sum in the direction \(z\) is zero, and so the pair cannot carry the load. The same holds for the pair \(M_{\mathrm{y}}\) on the front face and for the pairs \(M_{\mathrm{xy}}\), which the shear stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) in the plane of the plate give. By Cauchy's formula the component \(z\) of the stress vector on a face with normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\), and on a face with normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) it is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}\) (equation (2.27)). The vertical force through the side faces is therefore carried by the shear stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}\) alone. This is the same conclusion as for the beam: there the transverse force \(V\) came from the shear stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) (lemma 5.9). For the plate we write it per unit length of the edge, like the moments.

Definition 18.9 Transverse shear forces of a plate (prečni sili plošče)

Let \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}\) be continuous functions on a plate of thickness \(h\) whose middle plane lies in the plane \(xy\). The transverse shear forces of the plate are the functions of the point \((x, y)\) of the middle plane

\[Q_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y) = \int_{-\mathrm{h}/2}^{h/2} \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}(x, y, z)\,\mathrm{d}z , \qquad Q_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y) = \int_{-\mathrm{h}/2}^{h/2} \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}(x, y, z)\,\mathrm{d}z . \tag{18.11}\]

Their unit is N/m, force per unit length of edge. \(Q_{\mathrm{x}}\) is the \(z\) component of the force per unit length with which the part of the plate at larger \(x\) acts on the part at smaller \(x\), and similarly \(Q_{\mathrm{y}}\). The load \(q\) is the force per unit area of the middle plane in the direction \(z\) and includes the weight of the plate.

Source: EP (7.2.100) (p. 433) and ONATE2 (PDF p. 272-273) write \(Q_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(Q_{\mathrm{y}}\) for the same shear forces, VK (PDF p. 29) too. The sign with respect to the normal of the face is ours, as in lemma 5.9.

In the figure we see them as \(Q_{\mathrm{x}}\) on the right face and \(Q_{\mathrm{y}}\) on the front face. On the name: the force is transverse because it acts across the middle plane, perpendicular to it, in contrast to the forces in the plane of the plate. The transverse force of a beam \(V\) was a force in N, while \(Q\) is a force per unit length of the edge in N/m, and also \(q\) is now a force per unit area, not per unit length. The forces in the plane of the middle plane, for instance \(N_{\mathrm{x}} = \int \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}z\), are zero in pure bending: the push at the top and the pull at the bottom in the pair \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\) are equally large, because \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) is an odd function of \(z\) and the integral of an odd function over \([-h/2, h/2]\) is zero. So the load passes through the piece by \(Q_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(Q_{\mathrm{y}}\) alone.

For the moments we keep the sign of the previous section, which follows chapter 5 (\(M = -\int y\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}A\) for a beam): \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\) \(= -\int z\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}z\), \(M_{\mathrm{y}}\) \(= -\int z\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\,\mathrm{d}z\), \(M_{\mathrm{xy}}\) \(= -\int z\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\mathrm{d}z\), all integrals over \([-h/2, h/2]\) (definition 18.5). By theorem 18.7 \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = D(w_{\mathrm{xx}} + \nu\,w_{\mathrm{yy}})\), \(M_{\mathrm{y}} = D(w_{\mathrm{yy}} + \nu\,w_{\mathrm{xx}})\) and \(M_{\mathrm{xy}} = D(1 - \nu)\,w_{\mathrm{xy}}\).

18.2.2 Equilibrium of a piece of plate

Now we can write the equilibrium of the piece. The piece transmits forces and moments only through its side faces. We write them with the stress vector, which Cauchy's formula gives from the stresses (theorem 2.26). For this we look at the piece from above (figure 18.13).

We draw the axes \(x\) and \(y\) of the middle plane. The axis \(z\) points out of the figure towards us, so we draw it as a small circle with a dot at the origin. On the axes we mark the rectangle \(R\) from \(x_1\) to \(x_2\) and from \(y_1\) to \(y_2\). This is the piece seen from above, and its four side faces are the four sides. From each side we draw the outward normal: on the right \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), on the left \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), at the back \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\), at the front \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\). It points out of the piece, because on this face the rest of the plate outside it acts on the piece. Let \(\vek n = (n_{\mathrm{x}}, n_{\mathrm{y}})\) be the outward normal of the boundary \(\partial R\) and \(Q_{\mathrm{n}} = Q_{\mathrm{x}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}} + Q_{\mathrm{y}}\,n_{\mathrm{y}}\). On the right side \(Q_{\mathrm{n}} = Q_{\mathrm{x}}\), on the left \(-Q_{\mathrm{x}}\), at the back \(Q_{\mathrm{y}}\) and at the front \(-Q_{\mathrm{y}}\).

On the back side we pick a piece of length \(\Delta s\). Since \(Q_{\mathrm{n}}\) is a force per unit length of the edge, a vertical force of about \(Q_{\mathrm{n}}\,\Delta s\) acts on the piece through it. We write \(\int_{\partial \mathrm{R}} f\,\mathrm{d}s\) for the sum of the integrals over the four sides of the rectangle, that is the limit of the sum over such pieces as they all get shorter. The vertical force turns the piece about the axis \(x\) with a lever arm that is the perpendicular distance from the axis to the line of the force, here the distance \(y\) of the piece from the axis \(x\). About the axis \(y\) it turns it with the lever arm \(x\). In the same way a piece \(\Delta A\) inside \(R\), on which the load acts with the force \(q\,\Delta A\), has its coordinates \(y\) and \(x\) as its two lever arms.

Lemma 18.10 Equilibrium of a piece of a plate (ravnovesje kosa plošče)

Let the plate be as above and in equilibrium, and let all stress components be continuous. On one surface let a force \(q(x, y)\) per unit area act in the direction \(z\) with a continuous function \(q\), let the other surface be free, and let body forces be included in \(q\). Let \(R = [x_1, x_2] \times [y_1, y_2]\) be a rectangle in the middle plane that does not reach beyond the edge of the plate. Then

\[\int_{\partial \mathrm{R}} Q_{\mathrm{n}}\,\mathrm{d}s + \int_{\mathrm{R}} q\,\mathrm{d}A = 0 , \tag{18.12}\]
\[\int_{\partial \mathrm{R}} \big( y\,Q_{\mathrm{n}} + M_{\mathrm{xy}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}} + M_{\mathrm{y}}\,n_{\mathrm{y}} \big)\,\mathrm{d}s + \int_{\mathrm{R}} y\,q\,\mathrm{d}A = 0 , \tag{18.13}\]
\[\int_{\partial \mathrm{R}} \big( x\,Q_{\mathrm{n}} + M_{\mathrm{x}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}} + M_{\mathrm{xy}}\,n_{\mathrm{y}} \big)\,\mathrm{d}s + \int_{\mathrm{R}} x\,q\,\mathrm{d}A = 0 . \tag{18.14}\]
Proof

We must show three equalities: the equilibrium of forces in the direction \(z\) and the equilibrium of moments about the axis \(x\) and the axis \(y\) through the origin. The moment of every force is computed as \(\vek r\times\vek t\) and integrated through the thickness.

  1. The piece \(P = R \times [-h/2, h/2]\) is a body made of boxes and it is in equilibrium, so the sum of the forces on it is zero and the sum of the moments about the origin is zero (definition 2.3, equations (2.2) and (2.45)). The forces on it are: the forces through the four side faces (\(Q_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(Q_{\mathrm{y}}\) and the pairs \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(M_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(M_{\mathrm{xy}}\)), the force of the load on one surface and nothing through the free surface. The force through a face is the integral of the stress vector \(\vek t = \ten\sigma\,\vek n\) (theorem 2.26, equation (2.27)), with \(t_{\mathrm{z}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}\,n_{\mathrm{y}}\), since \(n_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\).
  2. The side face \(x = x_2\) is the rectangle \([y_1, y_2] \times [-h/2, h/2]\) with the normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), so \(n_{\mathrm{x}} = 1\), \(n_{\mathrm{y}} = 0\). The integral over it is by the definition of the integral over a rectangle an iterated integral, first over \(z\) and then over \(y\). The inner integral of the component \(t_{\mathrm{z}}\) is \(\int \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\,\mathrm{d}z = Q_{\mathrm{x}}(x_2, y)\) (definition 18.9). Over \(y\) we integrate \(Q_{\mathrm{n}} = Q_{\mathrm{x}}\). The same holds on the face \(x = x_1\) with \(n_{\mathrm{x}} = -1\), and on the faces \(y = y_2\), \(y = y_1\) with \(Q_{\mathrm{y}}\,n_{\mathrm{y}}\). The sum of the \(z\) components of the forces through the side faces is therefore the limit of the sum of the forces on the pieces of the boundary, \(\int_{\partial \mathrm{R}} Q_{\mathrm{n}}\,\mathrm{d}s\).
  3. The force of the load in the direction \(z\) is the limit of the sum of the forces \(q\,\Delta A\) on the pieces, \(\int_{\mathrm{R}} q\,\mathrm{d}A\). The equilibrium of forces in the direction \(z\) is therefore equation (18.12).
  4. The moment about the origin of the forces through a face is the integral \(\int \vek r\times\vek t\,\mathrm{d}A\) with \(\vek r = (x, y, z)\) (definition of the moment of a force, as in lemma 11.2). The component \(x\) of \(\vek r\times\vek t\) is \(y\,t_{\mathrm{z}} - z\,t_{\mathrm{y}}\) and the component \(y\) is \(z\,t_{\mathrm{x}} - x\,t_{\mathrm{z}}\) (equation (2.39)). In the component \(x\), \(y\,t_{\mathrm{z}}\) is a vertical force with the lever arm \(y\), and \(z\,t_{\mathrm{y}}\) is a horizontal force with the lever arm \(z\) through the thickness, which gives the pairs \(M_{\mathrm{xy}}\) and \(M_{\mathrm{y}}\). On a side face with normal \((n_{\mathrm{x}}, n_{\mathrm{y}}, 0)\) we have \(t_{\mathrm{y}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\,n_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(t_{\mathrm{x}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\,n_{\mathrm{y}}\). On this face \(x\) and \(y\) are constant when we integrate over \(z\). So the integral of the component \(x\) through the thickness is \(y\,Q_{\mathrm{n}} - n_{\mathrm{x}}\int z\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\mathrm{d}z - n_{\mathrm{y}}\int z\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\,\mathrm{d}z = y\,Q_{\mathrm{n}} + M_{\mathrm{xy}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}} + M_{\mathrm{y}}\,n_{\mathrm{y}}\), where we used the definitions of the moments and the symmetry of stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) (theorem 2.41). In the same way the integral of the component \(y\) through the thickness is \(-\big(x\,Q_{\mathrm{n}} + M_{\mathrm{x}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}} + M_{\mathrm{xy}}\,n_{\mathrm{y}}\big)\).
  5. The load is a distributed force with density \(q\) in the direction \(z\). Its moment is \(\int_{\mathrm{R}} \vek r\times(0, 0, q)\,\mathrm{d}A = \int_{\mathrm{R}} (y\,q, -x\,q, 0)\,\mathrm{d}A\), with the lever arms \(y\) and \(x\) of the piece \(\Delta A\), whichever surface it acts on, since the height \(z\) of the point of application does not enter the components \(x\) and \(y\). So we may also add to \(q\) the weight of the plate, which acts through the whole thickness: in these three equations only the force above each point of \(R\) counts, not the height at which it acts. For forces in the plane of the plate, for instance the weight of a plate standing upright, this would not hold, but the lemma does not treat those. The equilibrium of the component \(x\) of the moments is therefore equation (18.13), and that of the component \(y\) is (18.14) after multiplication by \(-1\).
  6. The other three balances (forces in the directions \(x\) and \(y\), moment about the axis \(z\)) are not needed here. The forces in the plane are zero because \(N_{\mathrm{x}} = N_{\mathrm{y}} = 0\) and the normal stresses are odd in \(z\) (the push and the pull of the pair \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\) are equally large).

Source: The proof is not from the sources. The sources derive the same three equations with a small element of the plate: VK (2.19)-(2.21) (PDF p. 29), ONATE2 (5.21)-(5.23) (PDF p. 272-273), EP with Hamilton's principle (7.2.98) (p. 433). Our way does not use a small element but a finite piece, as for the beam (lemma 5.10).

Let us test the lemma on a field that is a simple polynomial, \(w = q\,(x^4 + y^4)/(48\,D)\). It is not the solution of any supported plate, but it is a field with known moments. By theorem 18.7 \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = (q/4)(x^2 + \nu\,y^2)\), \(M_{\mathrm{y}} = (q/4)(y^2 + \nu\,x^2)\), \(M_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\). We take the transverse forces from the moment balances, as the theorem below will get them in a point: \(Q_{\mathrm{x}} = -q\,x/2\) and \(Q_{\mathrm{y}} = -q\,y/2\) (SymPy). So the test does not check the moment balances, which hold by construction, but the balance of forces, which the true \(w\) satisfies only because of the plate equation. We draw the test in figure 18.14.

Take the piece \([0{,}1,\ 0{,}3] \times [0{,}2,\ 0{,}4]\) m, drawn on axes in metres, at \(q = 10\) kPa. Its area is \(0{,}04\) m², the force of the load is \(400\) N. Over the sides \(\int Q_{\mathrm{n}}\,\mathrm{d}s\) is \(-300\) N on the right (\(x = 0{,}3\)), \(+100\) N on the left (\(x = 0{,}1\)), \(-400\) N on the back (\(y = 0{,}4\)) and \(+200\) N on the front (\(y = 0{,}2\)), together \(-400\) N, and equation (18.12) holds: \(-400 + 400 = 0\).

We draw the moment equations on the right as bars above the zero line, equation (18.13) on the left, (18.14) on the right. First only the moment pairs on the boundary: \(+60\) N m and \(+40\) N m. Then the moment of the load, \(+120\) N m and \(+80\) N m, in the same direction. What if we left out the transverse forces, as if the model did not need them? The sums would be \(60 + 120 = 180\) N m and \(40 + 80 = 120\) N m instead of zero, and the piece would turn. So the moment pairs alone do not balance the piece. The balance holds only with the transverse forces, which contribute \(-180\) N m and \(-120\) N m on the boundary, and the sums are zero: \(60 + 120 - 180 = 0\) and \(40 + 80 - 120 = 0\) (SymPy).

18.2.3 The plate equation

Before we turn the equilibrium of the piece into an equation at a point, let us see what this equation will say (figure 18.15). In a beam the load travels along the beam to its two ends. A plate, however, can rest on all four edges.

We draw the square plate in plan view. The hatching along all four sides means that the plate rests there on a support that allows no vertical displacement. Beside the plate we put the axes \(x\) and \(y\). We load the plate only on a small patch in the middle. Where does this force go? The first path is the strip along \(x\), a narrow band of the plate through the patch, which bends like a beam and carries the force to the left and right edges. The second path is the strip along \(y\), which carries it to the bottom and top edges. The third path is new. The neighbouring strip along \(x\), drawn dashed, sags less than the strip through the middle, so the plate between them twists, and the twisting moment \(M_{\mathrm{xy}}\) carries part of the force to the neighbouring strips. The plate equation will write these three paths term by term, each in the colour of its strip. How much each one carries we compute after the theorem.

Equations (18.12), (18.13) and (18.14) hold for every rectangle. From them we want equations at a point. For the beam we shrank the piece and got a derivative. For the plate we turn the boundary integrals into integrals over the piece: this is the divergence theorem in the plane, which we prove for a rectangle right here, with the same steps as point 1 of theorem 4.14 (figure 18.16, left). Then we use that a function whose integral over every rectangle is zero is zero (as in lemma 4.83, figure 18.16, right).

Lemma 18.11 Divergence theorem in the plane for a rectangle (izrek o divergenci v ravnini za pravokotnik)

Let \(U\) be an open set in the plane \(xy\).

  1. Let \(\vek v = (v_{\mathrm{x}}, v_{\mathrm{y}})\) be a continuously differentiable field on \(U\) (definition 1.91). For every rectangle \(R = [x_1, x_2] \times [y_1, y_2] \subseteq U\) with outward normal \(\vek n = (n_{\mathrm{x}}, n_{\mathrm{y}})\) on \(\partial R\)
    \[\int_{\mathrm{R}} \Big( \frac{\partial v_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial v_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial y} \Big)\,\mathrm{d}A = \int_{\partial \mathrm{R}} \big( v_{\mathrm{x}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}} + v_{\mathrm{y}}\,n_{\mathrm{y}} \big)\,\mathrm{d}s . \tag{18.15}\]
  2. If \(f\) is continuous on \(U\) and \(\int_{\mathrm{R}} f\,\mathrm{d}A = 0\) for every rectangle \(R \subseteq U\) with sides parallel to the axes, then \(f = 0\) at every point of \(U\).
Proof

We must show both points. The first is point 1 of theorem 4.14 in the plane, the second is a conclusion by shrinking a rectangle around a point.

  1. The function \(\partial v_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial x\) is continuous on the rectangle \(R\). By the theorem on the order of integration (theorem 4.9) \(\int_{\mathrm{R}} \partial v_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial x\,\mathrm{d}A = \int_{\mathrm{y}_1}^{y_2}\int_{\mathrm{x}_1}^{x_2} \partial v_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial x\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\). The inner integral runs along the horizontal line at height \(y\) and by the fundamental theorem of calculus (theorem 3.55, point 1) it is \(v_{\mathrm{x}}(x_2, y) - v_{\mathrm{x}}(x_1, y)\): what the field carries out through the right side, minus what it brings in through the left. On the side \(x = x_2\) we have \(n_{\mathrm{x}} = 1\), on the side \(x = x_1\) we have \(n_{\mathrm{x}} = -1\) and on the horizontal sides \(n_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\), so this is \(\int_{\partial \mathrm{R}} v_{\mathrm{x}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}}\,\mathrm{d}s\).
  2. For \(v_{\mathrm{y}}\) we exchange the roles of \(x\) and \(y\) and get \(\int_{\partial \mathrm{R}} v_{\mathrm{y}}\,n_{\mathrm{y}}\,\mathrm{d}s\). The sum of both equalities is (18.15).
  3. Let \(f(P) \ne 0\) at some point \(P\) of the set \(U\), without loss of generality \(f(P) > 0\). Around the value \(f(P)\) we draw a band that reaches down to \(f(P)/2\). By continuity there is a \(\delta > 0\) such that the square \(R\) of half-side \(\delta\) around \(P\) lies in \(U\) and \(f \ge f(P)/2\) on \(R\): on every line through \(P\) the graph within the distance \(\delta\) of \(P\) does not leave the band. By monotonicity and the constant function (lemma 4.10, points 4 and 2, for a rectangle) \(\int_{\mathrm{R}} f\,\mathrm{d}A \ge f(P)/2 \cdot 4\delta^2 > 0\): the volume under the surface \(f\) over the square contains the box of height \(f(P)/2\). This contradicts the assumption. Hence \(f(P) = 0\).

Source: Point 1 follows the proof of point 1 of theorem 4.14 for a box with the variable \(z\) removed. Point 2 is the plane form of lemma 4.83, without weights, because the function is continuous. The proof is not from the sources.

Continuity in point 2 is necessary. The function that equals \(1\) only at the point \(P\) and is zero everywhere else has integral zero over every rectangle, and yet it is not zero. Step 3 fails for it, because around \(P\) there is no square on which \(f \ge f(P)/2\).

The story of this equation begins far from mechanics.

From history

Chladni covered a plate with fine sand and showed the nodal lines of its various modes of vibration, and with them he also determined the corresponding frequencies (TIMO PDF p. 127). What such lines look like for a simply supported square is shown by the figure in section 18.7. In 1809 the French Academy invited him to demonstrate his experiments, Napoleon attended, and at the emperor's suggestion the Academy proposed as the subject of a prize essay a mathematical theory of the vibration of plates, compared with experiment (TIMO PDF p. 127). In October 1811, at the closing date of the competition, only one candidate appeared, Sophie Germain (TIMO PDF p. 128). She knew Euler's work on elastic curves, where the equation of the deflection is derived from the energy of bending with the calculus of variations, and she decided to proceed in the same way: she assumed that the strain energy of a plate is the integral of an expression with the principal radii of curvature \(\rho_1\) and \(\rho_2\) of the surface (TIMO PDF p. 128). She made a mistake in calculating the variation, did not find the correct equation and did not win the prize. Lagrange, one of the judges, noticed the error and after corrections obtained the required equation (TIMO PDF p. 128, VK PDF p. 30). The Academy proposed the subject again with a new closing date in October 1813. Germain now had the correct equation, but the judges required a physical justification of her assumption about the energy. She again did not succeed, and she won the prize only the third time, in 1816, although the judges were not quite satisfied with her work (TIMO PDF p. 128). Poisson showed that the equation follows not only from Germain's integral but also from another integral, which with a proper choice of constants is the correct expression for the strain energy of bending. This explained why Germain found the correct equation, although her quantity is not the strain energy of a bent plate (TIMO PDF p. 129).

So the equation was found from an energy that was not the right one, and yet the equation was right. How this is possible we shall test on an example, but first we derive the equation from the equilibrium of a piece, which needs no energy.

Theorem 18.12 Plate equation (enačba plošče)

Let the plate be as in lemma 18.10, with constant \(E\) and \(h\). Let \(w\) be four times continuously differentiable on the middle plane, let \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(M_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(M_{\mathrm{xy}}\) be as in theorem 18.7, and let \(Q_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(Q_{\mathrm{y}}\) be continuously differentiable. Then

\[Q_{\mathrm{x}} = -D\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\nabla^2 w , \qquad Q_{\mathrm{y}} = -D\,\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\nabla^2 w , \tag{18.16}\]

and

\[D\,\nabla^4 w = q , \qquad \nabla^4 w = \frac{\partial^4 w}{\partial x^4} + 2\,\frac{\partial^4 w}{\partial x^2\,\partial y^2} + \frac{\partial^4 w}{\partial y^4} . \tag{18.17}\]
Proof

We must show that the equilibrium of every rectangle gives three equations at a point, and that these give equation (18.17). Every rectangle in the middle plane is a piece as in lemma 18.10. We use the divergence theorem in the plane (lemma 18.11) and the vanishing integrand.

  1. The field \(\vek v = (Q_{\mathrm{x}}, Q_{\mathrm{y}})\) is continuously differentiable, so by lemma 18.11, point 1, the sum of the forces on the pieces of the boundary equals an integral over the piece, \(\int_{\partial \mathrm{R}} Q_{\mathrm{n}}\,\mathrm{d}s = \int_{\mathrm{R}} (Q_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{x}} + Q_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{y}})\,\mathrm{d}A\). Equation (18.12) gives \(\int_{\mathrm{R}} (Q_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{x}} + Q_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{y}} + q)\,\mathrm{d}A = 0\) for every rectangle. By point 2 of the lemma
    \[Q_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{x}} + Q_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{y}} + q = 0 . \tag{18.18}\]
  2. The field \(\vek v = (y\,Q_{\mathrm{x}} + M_{\mathrm{xy}},\ y\,Q_{\mathrm{y}} + M_{\mathrm{y}})\), a force with the lever arm \(y\) and an edge pair, is continuously differentiable (the differentiability of \(M\) follows from \(w\) being four times continuously differentiable). Its divergence is \(Q_{\mathrm{y}} + y\,(Q_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{x}} + Q_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{y}}) + M_{\mathrm{xy},\mathrm{x}} + M_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{y}}\), since \(\partial y/\partial y = 1\). Equation (18.13) by the lemma, point 1, and by step 1 gives \(\int_{\mathrm{R}} \big( Q_{\mathrm{y}} + M_{\mathrm{xy},\mathrm{x}} + M_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{y}} \big)\,\mathrm{d}A + \int_{\mathrm{R}} y\,(Q_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{x}} + Q_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{y}} + q)\,\mathrm{d}A = 0\). The second integral is zero by equation (18.18). By point 2 of the lemma \(Q_{\mathrm{y}} = -(M_{\mathrm{xy},\mathrm{x}} + M_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{y}})\). We see each term on a small piece \(\Delta x \times \Delta y\) seen along the \(x\) axis. The force \(Q_{\mathrm{y}}\,\Delta x\) points up on the face \(y + \Delta y\) and down on the face \(y\). Their lever arms differ by \(\Delta y\), so together they turn the piece with the moment \(Q_{\mathrm{y}}\,\Delta x\,\Delta y\), which is where the term \(Q_{\mathrm{y}}\) comes from. The pairs \(M_{\mathrm{y}}\) on the faces \(y\) and \(y + \Delta y\) turn in opposite senses, the difference \(M_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{y}}\,\Delta y\,\Delta x\) remains. Likewise the twisting pairs on the faces \(x\) and \(x + \Delta x\) leave the difference \(M_{\mathrm{xy},\mathrm{x}}\,\Delta x\,\Delta y\). The piece does not turn, so the couple balances both differences. For the sine hill \(w = w_0\sin(\pi x/a)\sin(\pi y/a)\) at \(\nu = 0{,}3\) the difference of \(M_{\mathrm{y}}\) carries the share \((1 + \nu)/2 = 65\,\%\) of the couple, the difference of the twist \((1 - \nu)/2 = 35\,\%\) (SymPy).
  3. The field \((x\,Q_{\mathrm{x}} + M_{\mathrm{x}},\ x\,Q_{\mathrm{y}} + M_{\mathrm{xy}})\) with the lever arm \(x\) has the divergence \(Q_{\mathrm{x}} + x\,(Q_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{x}} + Q_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{y}}) + M_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{x}} + M_{\mathrm{xy},\mathrm{y}}\). The same conclusion with equation (18.14) gives \(Q_{\mathrm{x}} = -(M_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{x}} + M_{\mathrm{xy},\mathrm{y}})\): the couple of the forces \(Q_{\mathrm{x}}\) on the faces \(x\) and \(x + \Delta x\) balances the differences of the pairs \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(M_{\mathrm{xy}}\), only the roles of \(x\) and \(y\) are exchanged.
  4. We insert the moments of theorem 18.7, \(D\) is constant: \(M_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{x}} + M_{\mathrm{xy},\mathrm{y}} = D\,(w_{\mathrm{xxx}} + \nu\,w_{\mathrm{yyx}} + (1 - \nu)\,w_{\mathrm{xyy}}) = D\,(w_{\mathrm{xxx}} + w_{\mathrm{xyy}})\), since mixed derivatives are equal (theorem 8.31). This is \(D\,\partial\nabla^2 w/\partial x\). Similarly \(M_{\mathrm{xy},\mathrm{x}} + M_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{y}} = D\,\partial\nabla^2 w/\partial y\). Equations (18.16) are proved.
  5. We insert into (18.18): \(-D\,(\nabla^2 w)_{\mathrm{xx}} - D\,(\nabla^2 w)_{\mathrm{yy}} + q = 0\), and \((\nabla^2 w)_{\mathrm{xx}} + (\nabla^2 w)_{\mathrm{yy}} = w_{\mathrm{xxxx}} + 2\,w_{\mathrm{xxyy}} + w_{\mathrm{yyyy}}\), the three terms. This is (18.17).

Source: EP (7.2.98) (p. 433) with \(M_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and \(M_{\mathrm{xy}}\) of the opposite sign, because EP writes \(M_{\mathrm{xx}} = \int \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,z\,\mathrm{d}z\) (7.2.83), VK (2.19)-(2.24) (PDF p. 29-30), ONATE2 (5.21)-(5.23) (PDF p. 272-273). The sources use a small element of the plate, we a finite piece. Named after Germain and Lagrange: TIMO PDF p. 128, VK PDF p. 30 ascribes the equation to Lagrange.

The operator \(\nabla^4 = \nabla^2\nabla^2\) is the biharmonic operator: \(\nabla^2\) is the Laplace operator (definition 11.5), after which the harmonic functions with \(\nabla^2 f = 0\) are named, and \(\nabla^4\) applies it twice, hence the prefix bi-. Equation (18.17) is the plate equation, abroad often called the Germain-Lagrange equation after the people in the story above, and in the lectures, after the operator, the biharmonic plate equation (biharmonska enačba plošče). Per unit width, with \(w = w(x)\), it reduces to \(D\,w'''' = q\), which is the deflection curve equation (theorem 5.42) with \(EI\) replaced by \(D\).

Now we see how the equation shares the load among the three paths. We insert \(Q_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(Q_{\mathrm{y}}\) from steps 3 and 2 into equation (18.18):

\[q = M_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{xx}} + 2\,M_{\mathrm{xy},\mathrm{xy}} + M_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{yy}} .\]

The factor two is there because the twist \(M_{\mathrm{xy}}\) enters both transverse forces, once in \(Q_{\mathrm{x}}\) and once in \(Q_{\mathrm{y}}\). Each moment is the rigidity times curvatures (theorem 18.7), so we write each term separately:

\[\begin{aligned} M_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{xx}} &= D\,\big(w_{\mathrm{xxxx}} + \nu\,w_{\mathrm{xxyy}}\big) , \\ 2\,M_{\mathrm{xy},\mathrm{xy}} &= D\,\big(2\,w_{\mathrm{xxyy}} - 2\nu\,w_{\mathrm{xxyy}}\big) , \\ M_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{yy}} &= D\,\big(w_{\mathrm{yyyy}} + \nu\,w_{\mathrm{xxyy}}\big) . \end{aligned}\]

The first line is the strip along \(x\), the third the strip along \(y\), the middle one the twist between neighbouring strips. The parts with \(\nu\) are \(+\nu\), \(-2\nu\) and \(+\nu\), their sum is zero, and the three terms \(w_{\mathrm{xxxx}} + 2\,w_{\mathrm{xxyy}} + w_{\mathrm{yyyy}}\) remain. What the two strips gain with their parts \(+\nu\), the twist loses with its part \(-2\nu\), and so \(\nu\) is not in the equation.

How much each path carries depends on the shape of the deflection. Take one sine hill \(w = w_0 \sin(\pi x/a)\,\sin(\pi y/a)\) on a square \(a \times a\). Every derivative with respect to \(x\) or to \(y\) brings a factor \(\pi/a\), so all three fourth derivatives equal \((\pi/a)^4\,w\). The lines above then give the shares \((1 + \nu)/4\), \(2(1 - \nu)/4\) and \((1 + \nu)/4\), at \(\nu = 0{,}3\) \(32{,}5\,\%\), \(35\,\%\) and \(32{,}5\,\%\) (SymPy). Here the twist carries more than each strip alone, so it is no small correction.

The theorem has two assumptions that we must check when we use it.

  • The deflection \(w\) is four times continuously differentiable, \(Q_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(Q_{\mathrm{y}}\) are continuously differentiable. Why it holds here: under a load spread over an area, such as weight or snow, the moments are smooth functions. When not: under a point force the moment near the point grows without bound. What it costs: at the point of the force equation (18.17) does not hold, while the equilibrium of a finite piece of lemma 18.10 still does.
  • The modulus \(E\) and the thickness \(h\) are constant, so the rigidity \(D\) is the same everywhere. Why it holds here: the plate is cut from one piece of sheet. When not: a plate of varying thickness. What it costs: only with a constant \(D\) can step 4 take it out in front of the derivatives, otherwise terms with derivatives of \(D\) remain in the equation.

Let us test the theorem on the field of the previous section. For \(w = q\,(x^4 + y^4)/(48\,D)\) we have \(w_{\mathrm{xxxx}} = w_{\mathrm{yyyy}} = 24\,q/(48\,D) = q/(2D)\) and \(w_{\mathrm{xxyy}} = 0\), so \(D\,\nabla^4 w = q\). Its \(Q_{\mathrm{x}} = -D\,\partial_{\mathrm{x}}\nabla^2 w = -D\,\partial_{\mathrm{x}}\,\big(q\,(x^2 + y^2)/(4D)\big) = -q\,x/2\), which agrees with the previous example, where the transverse forces balanced the load.

18.2.4 Germain's energy: the same equation, a different energy

The story said that Germain got the correct equation from a wrong energy. Let us see what this means. Her energy, written with the same constant \(D/2\) as the correct one, is \(\tfrac{D}{2}\int (\nabla^2 w)^2\,\mathrm{d}A\): the sum of the curvatures in two perpendicular directions, \(w_{\mathrm{xx}} + w_{\mathrm{yy}}\), which does not depend on the choice of axes, squared. The correct energy, which we shall derive in section 18.3, has in the square bracket one more term, \(-2(1 - \nu)(w_{\mathrm{xx}}\,w_{\mathrm{yy}} - w_{\mathrm{xy}}^2)\). That the equation cannot tell the two apart is due to this term. Its integral can be written with edge terms alone, as section 18.3 will show (lemma 18.18), so it does not change the equation in the interior. In derivatives this shows as \(\partial^2(w_{\mathrm{yy}})/\partial x^2 - 2\,\partial^2(w_{\mathrm{xy}})/\partial x\,\partial y + \partial^2(w_{\mathrm{xx}})/\partial y^2 = w_{\mathrm{xxyy}} - 2\,w_{\mathrm{xxyy}} + w_{\mathrm{xxyy}} = 0\) for a four times continuously differentiable \(w\) (theorem 8.31). The two energies are told apart by the edge and by the value of the energy itself.

We can do the experiment ourselves. Take a flat plate of cardboard, dashed in the figure, and hold it by the four corners: two diagonal ones up and two down. The plate twists into the surface \(w = c\,x\,y\), in which every mesh line is straight. Everywhere \(w_{\mathrm{xx}} = w_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0\), so \(\nabla^2 w = 0\), and Germain's energy is zero. But we cannot twist the plate without the forces \(F\) at the corners. The correct energy has the density \(\tfrac{D}{2}\cdot 2(1 - \nu)\,w_{\mathrm{xy}}^2 = D(1 - \nu)\,w_{\mathrm{xy}}^2\), which is not zero. At \(w_{\mathrm{xy}} = c = 0{,}01\) 1/m we have \(M_{\mathrm{xy}} = D(1 - \nu)\,w_{\mathrm{xy}} = 125{,}64\) N m/m and the correct energy per unit area is \(1{,}256\) J/m², Germain's is zero (SymPy). Equation (18.17) for this \(w\) says \(0 = q\) and it holds, because there is no load in the interior. The equation therefore does not see the twist, because its work is at the edge, where the moment \(M_{\mathrm{xy}}\) is carried by the forces \(F\) at the corners. How, section 18.3 shows.

The equation is the same for both energies, so it cannot tell them apart. The edge conditions we need for the problem to be solvable we shall get from the correct energy in section 18.3. That the equation does not determine the correct energy is therefore a warning: agreement of the equation does not prove the energy.

18.2.5 Shear stress through the thickness

The third question is still waiting. In the Kirchhoff model the shear strains \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \gamma_{\mathrm{yz}}\) are zero by definition, because the normal stays perpendicular to the middle plane, so the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\) cannot be obtained from Hooke's law, yet the transverse force \(Q_{\mathrm{x}} \ne 0\) is, as we saw, necessary. This is the same contradiction as for the beam (section 5.3.4). The remedy is the same too: we get the stress from equilibrium, not from the strain. Why may we leave out the shear strain but not the shear stress? The stress is demanded by the equilibrium of the piece, however small the strain that belongs to it. And this strain is small against the normal one, \(9\,\%\) in the example at the end of the section, and its energy density only \(0{,}3\,\%\). When this does not hold: in thick plates, where the normal really turns with respect to the middle plane. What it costs us: the deflection due to shear, \(0{,}21\,\%\) for our plate, about \(21\,\%\) for one ten times thicker. Where these numbers come from, the example at the end of the section shows.

Which stress equilibrium gives is shown in figure 18.20, and the lemma below proves it.

We cut the plate with a face of normal \(+\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and look at the cut from the side, the upper surface at the top and the thickness \(h\) vertical. On the cut we draw the normal stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) as arrows as long as the stress: they grow linearly with the distance from the middle plane, push at the top, pull at the bottom, and together give a pair, the moment \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\). To the right, on the same cut, we draw the shear stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\). It acts vertically, but we draw its size horizontally so that we see how it varies: at both surfaces it is zero, because the surface is free, and in the middle it is largest. Its sum through the thickness is \(Q_{\mathrm{x}}\).

Lemma 18.13 Shear stresses through the thickness of a plate (strižne napetosti po debelini plošče)

Let the plate be as in theorem 18.12, without body forces, with continuous derivatives of the stresses, and let both surfaces carry no tangential load (\(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\) at \(z = \pm h/2\)). Then

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \frac{3\,Q_{\mathrm{x}}}{2h}\Big( 1 - \frac{4z^2}{h^2} \Big) , \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = \frac{3\,Q_{\mathrm{y}}}{2h}\Big( 1 - \frac{4z^2}{h^2} \Big) . \tag{18.19}\]

The largest value is \(\tfrac{3}{2}\,Q_{\mathrm{x}}/h\) at \(z = 0\), that is one and a half times the mean.

Proof

We must show the form of \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\) in \(z\) at every \((x, y)\). We use the equilibrium equation in the direction \(x\) and the known normal stresses.

  1. The stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) are linear in \(z\) (Hooke's law in plane stress for the strains \(-z\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(-z\,w_{\mathrm{xy}}\), theorem 18.7): \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = z\,s_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = z\,s_{\mathrm{xy}}\) with coefficients independent of \(z\). From the definition \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = -\int z\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}z = -s_{\mathrm{x}}\,h^3/12\) we get \(s_{\mathrm{x}} = -12\,M_{\mathrm{x}}/h^3\), and likewise \(s_{\mathrm{xy}} = -12\,M_{\mathrm{xy}}/h^3\).
  2. The first equilibrium equation ((2.38)) without body forces is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx},\mathrm{x}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yx},\mathrm{y}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zx},\mathrm{z}} = 0\), with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\) (theorem 2.41). So \(\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}/\partial z = -z\,(s_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{x}} + s_{\mathrm{xy},\mathrm{y}}) = 12\,z\,(M_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{x}} + M_{\mathrm{xy},\mathrm{y}})/h^3\). We see what this means on the slice between \(x\) and \(x + \Delta x\) (width \(\Delta x\)) above the height \(z\), the axes \(x\) and \(z\) point right and up, and the horizontal line at this height reaches the graphs on the right. At the left end \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(x)\) pulls on it, at the right end the larger \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(x + \Delta x)\), and the upper surface is free. The difference of the two pulls, together with the difference of \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\) on the front and back faces, can only be held by the shear stress on the lower cut: as much as the normal stress changes along \(x\), so much must the shear stress change over the height.
  3. By equation (18.16) \(M_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{x}} + M_{\mathrm{xy},\mathrm{y}} = -Q_{\mathrm{x}}\) (step 3 of the proof of theorem 18.12). Hence \(\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}/\partial z = -12\,z\,Q_{\mathrm{x}}/h^3\).
  4. By the fundamental theorem of calculus (theorem 3.55) \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = -6\,Q_{\mathrm{x}}\,z^2/h^3 + c(x, y)\), a parabola in \(z\).
  5. On the surface \(z = h/2\) the normal is \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) and by Cauchy's formula \(t_{\mathrm{x}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\), which is zero by assumption. So \(c = 6\,Q_{\mathrm{x}}\,(h/2)^2/h^3 = 3\,Q_{\mathrm{x}}/(2h)\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}(z) = 6\,Q_{\mathrm{x}}\,(h^2/4 - z^2)/h^3\), the area under the line from \(z\) to \(h/2\), which grows with \(h^2/4 - z^2\) down to the middle plane. The surface \(z = -h/2\) gives the same value of \(c\), since \(z^2\) is the same there: below the middle plane the line lies on the other side of the axis, so the area shrinks and is zero again on the lower surface. This gives the first equation (18.19). We get the second by exchanging \(x\) and \(y\).
  6. We check the sum through the thickness: \(\int_{-\mathrm{h}/2}^{h/2}\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\,\mathrm{d}z = \tfrac{3Q_{\mathrm{x}}}{2h}\big(h - \tfrac{4}{h^2}\cdot\tfrac{2}{3}\,(\tfrac{h}{2})^3\big) = \tfrac{3Q_{\mathrm{x}}}{2h}\cdot\tfrac{2h}{3} = Q_{\mathrm{x}}\), as definition 18.9 demands.

Source: VK (2.16) (PDF p. 28) gives the same parabola through the thickness, written there with \(\nabla^2 w\), and notes that \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}\) vary by a parabolic law. The proof is ours, with the same step as theorem 5.39 for the beam.

The lemma has two more assumptions. No body forces in the directions \(x\) and \(y\): the weight of a horizontal plate acts in the direction \(z\) and does not enter the equation of step 2, while for an inclined plate the component of the weight in the plane of the plate would add a term to step 2. Both surfaces free of tangential load: the pressure of water or snow acts perpendicular to the surface, while with friction on the surface \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\) would not be zero there, the constant \(c\) of step 5 would be different and the parabola shifted.

Let us try an example (figure 18.22).

Take a square plate \(a = 500\) mm, \(h = 10\) mm, simply supported on all edges, under \(q = 10\) kPa (section 18.5). At its centre \(S\) it has \(|M_{\mathrm{x}}| = 119{,}72\) N m/m and at the surfaces the normal stress \(6\,|M_{\mathrm{x}}|/h^2 = 7{,}183\) MPa. The transverse force at the middle \(B\) of an edge is \(Q_{\mathrm{x}} = 0{,}3376\,q\,a = 1688\) N/m (series, SymPy), so by the lemma there \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = 1{,}5 \cdot 1688/0{,}01 = 0{,}253\) MPa. We drew both stresses at the same scale over the same thickness \(h\). The shear stress is only a thin band along the vertical line, the ratio is \(0{,}035\).

Both stresses are proportional to the load, so the ratio is \(\tfrac{1{,}5 \cdot 0{,}3376}{6 \cdot 0{,}04789}\,h/a = 1{,}76\,h/a\), where \(0{,}3376\) and \(0{,}04789\) are the coefficients of the two series, \(Q_{\mathrm{x}} = 0{,}3376\,q\,a\) and \(|M_{\mathrm{x}}| = 0{,}04789\,q\,a^2\) (NumPy). The corresponding strains are the normal \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/E = 3{,}67 \cdot 10^{-5}\) and the shear \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}/G = 3{,}36 \cdot 10^{-6}\), which the model neglects. The shear strain is therefore \(9\,\%\) of the normal one. More important is the energy, which carries the square of the stress: the density of shear energy \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}^2/(2G)\) against the density of normal energy \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}^2/(2E)\) is only \((1{,}76\,h/a)^2 \cdot E/G = (1{,}76\,h/a)^2 \cdot 2(1 + \nu) = 3{,}2 \cdot 10^{-3}\). This is an estimate by the largest densities, which lie at different points, the centre and the middle of an edge, so it does not give the total energy. The total effect on the deflection is computed in section 18.8: at \(a/h = 50\) the deflection computed with the shear strain is only \(1{,}0021\) times the Kirchhoff one. Since the ratio of the densities grows with the square of \(h/a\), at \(a/h = 5\) it would be a hundred times larger, about \(0{,}3\), and the Kirchhoff model is no longer good: there the deflection with the shear strain is \(1{,}2073\) times larger.

Let us sum up. A piece of plate needs the transverse forces \(Q_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(Q_{\mathrm{y}}\) because the moment pairs cannot carry the load (lemma 18.10). From the equilibrium of a piece and the divergence theorem in the plane follows \(D\,\nabla^4 w = q\) (theorem 18.12), and from the equilibrium equation at a point the parabolic shear stress (lemma 18.13). The same equation also follows from a wrong energy, so the equation alone does not determine the edge conditions we need for a solution. That is the work of the next section.
