Chapter 25 Hertz contact and rolling bearings

25.2 The foundation in vibration and in the finite element method

In section 25.1 we laid a beam on a foundation that has its own spring under every point: a deflection \(v\) produces a force \(k\,v\) per unit length, and neighbouring points do not know each other. There we computed rest. This section answers two questions the book has promised. The first (chapter 13): how does the foundation change the modes of vibration of a beam, their shapes and frequencies? The second (chapter 12): where do we put the foundation in a finite element model, so that for a beam on a foundation the same holds as for a beam without it, and what happens if we put it in by a shortcut?

The model stays the one of section 25.1. The foundation is Winkler's (definition 25.1): a force \(k\,v\) per unit length, every point independent, springs pull as well as push. For vibration we add one more idealization and name it: the foundation has no mass, only the beam has inertia. COOK (PDF p. 301) remarks for a beam on a Winkler foundation that in dynamics the foundation also contributes mass. We do not include this mass, because the Winkler model does not say how much of it there is. The beam is steel, section \(20 \times 40\) mm, \(EI = 20\,906{,}7\) N m\(^2\), \(\rho A = 6{,}24\) kg/m (chapter 5), the foundation has \(k = 1{,}0 \cdot 10^6\) N/m\(^2\) as in section 25.1. The letter \(k\) has several meanings in the book. In this section it is always the foundation modulus. We write the mode index \(j\), although theorem 13.35 writes it \(k\).

25.2.1 Modes of a beam on a foundation

We start with what we know: a simply supported beam of length \(L = 2\) m has, by theorem 13.35, the modes \(\sin(j\pi x/L)\) and frequencies \(\omega_{\mathrm{j}0} = (j\pi/L)^2\sqrt{EI/(\rho A)}\). The question: what happens to them when the beam lies on a foundation?

Let us draw figure 25.5, the left part first. The foundation of the first mode consists of the lower horizontal line, the ground, and springs fixed to it, which carry the beam from below. The first mode is one half-wave of a sine above the grey rest position of the beam, with deflection zero at both supports. The springs follow the shape: under the middle they stretch most, near the supports least. Under the second mode the springs on one half stretch and on the other compress, since the deflection there has the opposite sign, and the middle of the beam is at rest. The third mode has two zeros inside the beam, so its springs are stretched, compressed and stretched again in turn. In all three cases the force of the spring at every point is proportional to the deflection at that point. This will decide everything.

Before proving it, let us say what we look for. The beam vibrates when the deflection at every point is \(w(x, t) = \varphi(x)\cos\omega t\), and such a pair \((\omega, \varphi)\) is a mode of vibration (definition 13.6) if it satisfies the equation of motion and the support conditions. The equation of motion of a beam on a foundation must first be derived, since theorem 13.28 does not contain the foundation. That is the first step of the proof.

Theorem 25.7 Modes of a simply supported beam on a foundation (načini prosto podprtega nosilca na podlagi)

Let a uniform beam with constant \(EI\), \(\rho A\) and length \(L\) be simply supported at both ends (definition 13.30), let it lie on a Winkler foundation of modulus \(k > 0\) (definition 25.1) without mass, and let no other load act on it. Then:

  1. The equation of motion is
    \[\rho A\,w_{\mathrm{tt}} + EI\,w_{\mathrm{xxxx}} + k\,w = 0 \quad \text{on } (0, L) , \tag{25.6}\]
    and the support conditions are \(w = w_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0\) at \(x = 0\) and \(x = L\), as for a beam without the foundation.
  2. A pair \((\omega, \varphi)\) with \(\omega > 0\) is a mode of vibration exactly when \(\varphi\) is a nonzero multiple of \(\sin(j\pi x/L)\) with an integer \(j \ge 1\) and
    \[\omega_{\mathrm{j}}^2 = \omega_{\mathrm{j}0}^2 + \frac{k}{\rho A} , \qquad \omega_{\mathrm{j}0} = \frac{(j\pi)^2}{L^2}\sqrt{\frac{EI}{\rho A}} . \tag{25.7}\]
  3. For every \(j\), \(\omega_{\mathrm{j}} > \omega_{\mathrm{j}0}\) and \(\omega_{\mathrm{j}} > \sqrt{k/(\rho A)}\). The difference \(\omega_{\mathrm{j}} - \omega_{\mathrm{j}0} = \dfrac{k/(\rho A)}{\omega_{\mathrm{j}} + \omega_{\mathrm{j}0}}\) is smaller than \(\dfrac{k}{2\rho A\,\omega_{\mathrm{j}0}}\) and tends to zero as \(j \to \infty\).
Proof

We must show that the foundation adds to the equation one term without derivatives, that this term only shifts the frequency, and that the shapes stay sines.

  1. By section 25.1 the foundation has energy \(\tfrac12\int_0^L k\,w^2\,\mathrm{d}x\), that is the energy \(\tfrac12 k\,w^2\) of a spring per unit length, and this energy contains no derivatives. The action is the action (13.30) decreased by \(\int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2}\tfrac12\int_0^L k\,w^2\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\). At \(w + \alpha\eta\) the derivative of this term with respect to \(\alpha\) at \(0\) is \(-\int\!\int k\,w\,\eta\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\), so \(B_1\) of the proof of theorem 13.28 gains this term, and the function \(R\) of step 5 of that proof becomes \(R = -\rho A\,w_{\mathrm{tt}} - EI\,w_{\mathrm{xxxx}} - k\,w + q\) with \(q = 0\). The term \(k\,w\,\eta\) has no boundary contributions, so the natural conditions and the support conditions stay unchanged. The rest of the proof (the fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations) is as for theorem 13.28 and gives equation (25.6). This is claim 1.
  2. Take a mode \((\omega, \varphi)\), \(w = \varphi(x)\cos\omega t\). As in step 2 of the proof of theorem 13.31, \(\big(EI\,\varphi'''' + k\,\varphi - \rho A\,\omega^2\varphi\big)\cos\omega t = 0\), so at \(t = 0\)
    \[EI\,\varphi'''' = (\rho A\,\omega^2 - k)\,\varphi \quad \text{on } [0, L] , \qquad \varphi(0) = \varphi''(0) = \varphi(L) = \varphi''(L) = 0 .\]
  3. Multiply the equation by \(\varphi\) and integrate. Integrating by parts twice (corollary 3.41) gives \(\int_0^L\varphi\varphi''''\,\mathrm{d}x = \big[\varphi\varphi''' - \varphi'\varphi''\big]_0^L + \int_0^L(\varphi'')^2\,\mathrm{d}x\), and the bracket is zero because \(\varphi\) and \(\varphi''\) vanish at both ends. Hence \(EI\int_0^L(\varphi'')^2\,\mathrm{d}x = (\rho A\,\omega^2 - k)\int_0^L\varphi^2\,\mathrm{d}x\).
  4. The integral \(\int\varphi^2\) is positive, since \(\varphi^2\) is continuous, nonnegative and not everywhere zero (lemma 6.10, item 3). If \(\int(\varphi'')^2\) were \(0\), then \(\varphi''\) would be zero everywhere (the same lemma), \(\varphi\) would be linear with \(\varphi(0) = \varphi(L) = 0\), so \(\varphi = 0\), which it is not. So the left side of step 3 is positive and \(\rho A\,\omega^2 - k > 0\). Let \(\Omega = \sqrt{\omega^2 - k/(\rho A)} > 0\).
  5. The equation of step 2 is now \(\varphi'''' = \beta^4\varphi\) with \(\beta^4 = \rho A\,\Omega^2/EI\), and the simply supported beam without a foundation has the same conditions. By theorem 13.31, \((\Omega, \varphi)\) is a mode of the beam without the foundation, so by theorem 13.35 \(\varphi\) is a multiple of \(\sin(j\pi x/L)\) and \(\Omega = \omega_{\mathrm{j}0}\). From the definition of \(\Omega\) it follows that \(\omega^2 = \omega_{\mathrm{j}0}^2 + k/(\rho A)\).
  6. Conversely: for \(\varphi = \sin(j\pi x/L)\) we have \(\varphi'''' = (j\pi/L)^4\varphi\), so \(EI\,\varphi'''' + k\,\varphi = \big(EI\,(j\pi/L)^4 + k\big)\varphi = \rho A\,(\omega_{\mathrm{j}0}^2 + k/(\rho A))\varphi = \rho A\,\omega_{\mathrm{j}}^2\varphi\), since \(EI\,(j\pi/L)^4 = \rho A\,\omega_{\mathrm{j}0}^2\) by equation (13.39). The support conditions hold since \(\varphi'' = -(j\pi/L)^2\varphi\). With step 2 read backwards, \(w = \varphi\cos\omega_{\mathrm{j}} t\) solves equation (25.6). This is claim 2.
  7. Claim 3: \(\omega_{\mathrm{j}}^2\) is a sum of two positive numbers, so it is larger than each of them. The difference of squares \(\omega_{\mathrm{j}}^2 - \omega_{\mathrm{j}0}^2 = k/(\rho A)\) is \((\omega_{\mathrm{j}} - \omega_{\mathrm{j}0})(\omega_{\mathrm{j}} + \omega_{\mathrm{j}0})\), which gives the formula, and since \(\omega_{\mathrm{j}} + \omega_{\mathrm{j}0} > 2\omega_{\mathrm{j}0}\) the difference is smaller than \(k/(2\rho A\,\omega_{\mathrm{j}0})\). Since \(\omega_{\mathrm{j}0} = j^2\omega_{10}\) grows beyond every bound, this estimate tends to zero.

Source: COOK (PDF p. 301): the foundation adds a term \(\tfrac12 k\,w^2\) to the energy, the foundation matrix has the form of the mass matrix. CK example 13.2 (PDF pp. 411 to 413) for the modes of a beam without a foundation. The proof in steps is not from the sources, it combines theorem 13.28, theorem 13.31 and theorem 13.35.

The proof showed three things. The foundation adds to the equation a term \(k\,w\) without derivatives (step 1). For a sine this term is proportional to the sine itself, so the shape stays (step 6). So only the square of the frequency changes, by the constant number \(k/(\rho A)\) (step 5). The last is the reason the foundation does not change the shapes: the spring under a point pulls back in proportion to the deflection at that point, and for a sine the bending force of the beam per unit length, \(EI\,\varphi''''\), is proportional to the deflection too.

Let us look at the numbers on the right part of the figure. The vertical axis is the circular frequency \(\omega\) and the dashed line on it is \(\sqrt{k/(\rho A)} = 400{,}32\) 1/s, the lowest frequency the foundation allows (claim 3). For each mode there are two horizontal lines: the grey one is the frequency without the foundation, the orange one with it, the arrow between them is the shift. For the first mode the frequency jumps from \(142{,}82\) to \(425{,}03\) 1/s, that is \(2{,}98\) times, for the second from \(571{,}28\) to \(697{,}58\) 1/s (\(1{,}22\) times), for the third from \(1285{,}38\) to \(1346{,}28\) 1/s (\(1{,}047\) times) and for the fourth from \(2285{,}13\) to \(2319{,}93\) 1/s (\(1{,}015\) times). The numbers are written for the first two. By claim 3 the shift is less than \(k/(2\rho A\,\omega_{\mathrm{j}0})\), and it falls because the beam has greater own stiffness at higher modes: at \(j = 4\), \(\omega_{40}^2 = 5{,}22 \cdot 10^6\) 1/s\(^2\) and \(k/(\rho A) = 1{,}60 \cdot 10^5\) 1/s\(^2\), so the foundation adds only \(3\) % of the square. For the first mode, \(\omega_{10}^2 = 2{,}04 \cdot 10^4\) 1/s\(^2\) and the foundation adds almost eight times this (\(7{,}9\) times).

A foundation that changes along the length. The theorem is not as general as it looks. If the foundation is not uniform, for example if \(k = 0\) under the left half of the beam and \(k > 0\) under the right, the sine is no longer a mode. For \(\varphi = \sin(\pi x/L)\) we would have \(EI\,\varphi'''' + k(x)\varphi = \big(EI\,(\pi/L)^4 + k(x)\big)\varphi\) and the multiplier would be different at \(x = L/4\) than at \(x = 3L/4\), where \(\varphi \ne 0\). The equation \(= \rho A\,\omega^2\varphi\) with a single \(\omega\) cannot hold. The proof used a constant \(k\) in step 6: a constant multiplier is what the sine preserves.

A beam without supports. The same procedure holds for a free beam lying only on the foundation. The rigid translation \(\varphi = 1\) and the rigid rotation \(\varphi = x - L/2\) have frequency zero without the foundation (\(\varphi'''' = 0\)). With it, each has \(\omega^2 = k/(\rho A)\), that is \(\omega = 400{,}32\) 1/s or \(63{,}7\) Hz: the beam bounces on the springs. This is the dashed line in figure 25.5. In the finite element model (the next part) it shows as the two lowest natural frequencies of a free beam \(L = 4\) m: \(400{,}32\) and \(400{,}32\) 1/s, and the third is \(408{,}42\) 1/s (num-02.out, item 4). The third is the first bending mode: the free beam without the foundation has \(\omega = (4{,}7300/L)^2\sqrt{EI/(\rho A)} = 80{,}9\) 1/s (figure 13.50) and \(\sqrt{80{,}9^2 + 400{,}32^2} = 408{,}4\) 1/s.

25.2.2 The foundation in the finite element model

In chapter 12 we divided the beam into elements. Each element is an interval \([x_{\mathrm{a}}, x_{\mathrm{b}}]\) of length \(h\), has four unknowns \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = (v_1, \varphi_1, v_2, \varphi_2)^\T\) and a deflection \(v_{\mathrm{h}} = \mat H_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) from Hermite functions (definition 12.3). The bending energy gives the stiffness matrix \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) (lemma 12.4), the kinetic energy the consistent mass matrix \(\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\) (lemma 12.43). What does the energy of the foundation give?

Let us draw figure 25.6, the left part first. The ground is below the element and springs are fixed to it, one for every point of the element, all with the same stiffness \(k\) per unit length, as in section 25.1. The dashed line is the rest position of the upper end. The displacement \(v_1\) is the downward displacement of node 1 without rotation, node 2 stays at rest. The blue deflection is then \(v_{\mathrm{h}} = v_1H_1\) and the length \(h\) is the length of the element between the nodes \(x_{\mathrm{a}}\) and \(x_{\mathrm{b}}\). The constant \(k\) is the same under all points. The springs are compressed differently: under node 1 by \(v_1\), under node 2 by nothing.

A spring per unit length has energy \(\tfrac12 k\,v^2\), so the energy of the foundation under the element is an integral over a finite piece \([x_{\mathrm{a}}, x_{\mathrm{b}}]\), and not a sum over small parts. By step 1 of the proof of the next lemma it is a quadratic form in \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\). Before computing it, let us look at the shortcut we see on the right panel: we replace the foundation by two nodal springs, each of stiffness \(kh/2\) (label). The total stiffness is the same, \(kh\), which is the same trick as the lumped mass of a beam, where the mass \(\rho A h\) was divided in halves at the ends (definition 12.44). On the right are the same ground, the same rest position, the same element of length \(h\), the same deflection and the same displacement, but the springs are only at the ends. COOK (PDF p. 301) calls such discrete springs "awkward and error-prone", because their stiffnesses depend on the spacing of the nodes.

Lemma 25.8 Foundation matrix of a beam element (matrika podlage elementa nosilca)

Let \([x_{\mathrm{a}}, x_{\mathrm{b}}]\) be a finite element of a beam of length \(h\) (definition 12.3) on a Winkler foundation of modulus \(k\). Then:

  1. The energy of the foundation under the element is \(U_{\mathrm{p},\mathrm{e}} = \tfrac12\int_{\mathrm{x}_\mathrm{a}}^{x_{\mathrm{b}}}k\,v_{\mathrm{h}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac12\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,\mat k_{\mathrm{p},\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) with
    \[\mat k_{\mathrm{p},\mathrm{e}} = k\int_{\mathrm{x}_\mathrm{a}}^{x_{\mathrm{b}}}\mat H_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat H_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{k h}{420}\begin{pmatrix} 156 & 22h & 54 & -13h \\ 22h & 4h^2 & 13h & -3h^2 \\ 54 & 13h & 156 & -22h \\ -13h & -3h^2 & -22h & 4h^2 \end{pmatrix} . \tag{25.8}\]
  2. \(\mat k_{\mathrm{p},\mathrm{e}} = \dfrac{k}{\rho A}\,\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\), where \(\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\) is the consistent mass matrix (12.47).
  3. The matrix \(\mat k_{\mathrm{p},\mathrm{e}}\) is symmetric and positive definite. A displacement of the element as a whole, \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = (1, 0, 1, 0)^\T\), has \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{p},\mathrm{e}}\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = k h\), and a rotation about the middle, \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = (-h/2, 1, h/2, 1)^\T\), has \(k h^3/12\).
  4. For a beam with \(n\) elements and selection matrices \(\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}\) (theorem 12.9), \(\mat K_{\mathrm{p}} = \sum_{\mathrm{e}}\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{p},\mathrm{e}}\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}\) is the matrix for which \(\vek d^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{p}}\vek d = \int_0^L k\,v_{\mathrm{h}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) at \(v_{\mathrm{h}} = \sum_{\mathrm{I}} d_{\mathrm{I}} H_{\mathrm{I}}\), and \(\mat K_{\mathrm{p}} = \dfrac{k}{\rho A}\,\mat M\) with \(\mat M\) from equation (12.50). For the energy form \(B(w, z) = \int_0^L EI\,w''z''\,\mathrm{d}x + \int_0^L k\,w\,z\,\mathrm{d}x\) we have \((\mat K + \mat K_{\mathrm{p}})_{\mathrm{IJ}} = B(H_{\mathrm{J}}, H_{\mathrm{I}})\).
Proof

We must show that the foundation energy is a quadratic form with this matrix, that the matrix is indeed the mass matrix times a constant, that it has these properties and that it assembles like the others.

  1. Since \(v_{\mathrm{h}} = \mat H_{\mathrm{e}}\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\), \(v_{\mathrm{h}}^2 = \vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat H_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat H_{\mathrm{e}}\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) (lemma 1.101). The column \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) does not depend on \(x\), so by linearity of the integral (lemma 6.10) \(\tfrac12\int k\,v_{\mathrm{h}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac12\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\big(k\int\mat H_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat H_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mathrm{d}x\big)\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\). This is the first expression of claim 1.
  2. The entries \(k\int H_{\mathrm{i}}H_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{d}x\) are the same integrals that SymPy computed in step 4 of the proof of lemma 12.43, with \(k\) instead of \(\rho A\): for example \(k\int H_1^2\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac{13}{35}\,kh = \tfrac{156}{420}\,kh\). All ten different entries are in matrix (25.8) and SymPy shows \(\mat k_{\mathrm{p},\mathrm{e}} - (k/\rho A)\,\mat m_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat 0\) (num-02.py, item 1). This is claim 2.
  3. Symmetry and positive definiteness: by claim 2, \(\mat k_{\mathrm{p},\mathrm{e}}\) is a positive multiple of \(\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\), which has both properties by lemma 12.43. For \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = (1, 0, 1, 0)^\T\) we get \(\tfrac{kh}{420}(156 + 54 + 54 + 156) = kh\) and for \((-h/2, 1, h/2, 1)^\T\), by step 7 of lemma 12.43, \(\tfrac{k}{\rho A}\cdot\rho A h^3/12 = kh^3/12\).
  4. As in step 1 of the proof of theorem 12.9, \(\vek d^\T\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{p},\mathrm{e}}\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}\vek d = \vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{p},\mathrm{e}}\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = \int_{\mathrm{x}_{\mathrm{e}-1}}^{x_{\mathrm{e}}}k\,v_{\mathrm{h}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) and the sum over \(e\) gives \(\int_0^L k\,v_{\mathrm{h}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) by lemma 6.10 (item 1). Since \(\mat M = \sum_{\mathrm{e}}\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}\) and \(\mat k_{\mathrm{p},\mathrm{e}} = (k/\rho A)\,\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\) for every \(e\), \(\mat K_{\mathrm{p}} = (k/\rho A)\,\mat M\).
  5. For \(B\): the entry \((I, J)\) of \(\mat K_{\mathrm{p}}\) is \(\int_0^L k\,H_{\mathrm{J}} H_{\mathrm{I}}\,\mathrm{d}x\) (step 3 of the proof of theorem 12.9 with \(k\,H_{\mathrm{J}} H_{\mathrm{I}}\) in place of \(EI\,H_{\mathrm{J}}''H_{\mathrm{I}}''\)), and \(\mat K\) is by item 2 of that theorem \(\int EI\,H_{\mathrm{J}}''H_{\mathrm{I}}''\,\mathrm{d}x\). The sum is \(B(H_{\mathrm{J}}, H_{\mathrm{I}})\).

Source: COOK §8.8 (PDF p. 301): "Matrix [\(k_\psi\)] has the same form as the mass matrix [m] used in dynamics" for a beam on a Winkler foundation, and the energy \(\tfrac12 k w^2\). Steps 1 to 5 are not from the source, they follow lemma 12.43.

The lemma says something we do not expect: the foundation matrix is the mass matrix multiplied by a constant. The reason is in steps 1 and 2: both the kinetic energy \(\tfrac12\int\rho A\,\dot v_{\mathrm{h}}^2\) and the foundation energy \(\tfrac12\int k\,v_{\mathrm{h}}^2\) are integrals of the square of the field without derivatives, so they have the same quadratic forms. In the bending energy the integrand is the square of the second derivative and the matrix is different.

Let us test it on one element with \(h = 1\) m and \(k = 10^6\) N/m\(^2\). Figure 25.7 shows what energy the foundation gives in different states of the element.

On the left, the axes show the position \(x\) from \(x_{\mathrm{a}}\) to \(x_{\mathrm{b}}\) and vertically the square of the deflection. The blue curve is \(v_{\mathrm{h}}^2\) at \(v_1 = 1\): at node 1 it is \(1\), at node 2 zero, between them it falls as \(H_1^2\). The orange line is the same as two nodal springs see it: the deflection is \(1\) at node 1 and \(0\) at node 2, and what happens between is not asked. The areas under them, which are exactly the entries \(\tfrac{13}{35}h\) and \(\tfrac12 h\), are different. On the right (axes, blue curve) the element is only rotated at node 1: the deflection is zero at both nodes, so the nodal model sees nothing, but in reality the springs in between are stretched and the area is \(h^3/105\).

The nodal model agrees with the chosen matrix only for a displacement of the element as a whole (second row), in all other states it gives a different energy, and in both directions: more for unequal displacements, zero for a rotation. This will be decisive for the bound in the next part.

The lemma holds for the foundation and also immediately gives a discrete version of theorem 25.7.

Corollary 25.9 Frequencies of the model with a foundation (frekvence modela s podlago)

Let a simply supported beam be made of \(n\) Hermite elements with the consistent mass matrix \(\mat M\), let \(\mat K\) be the bending stiffness matrix of the free unknowns (theorem 12.9) and \(\mat K_{\mathrm{p}}\) the foundation matrix (lemma 25.8), all with the displacements of nodes 0 and \(n\) removed. Then the pairs \((\mat K + \mat K_{\mathrm{p}},\ \mat M)\) and \((\mat K,\ \mat M)\) have the same eigenvectors and eigenvalues \(\lambda^h_{\mathrm{r}} + k/(\rho A)\) and \(\lambda^h_{\mathrm{r}}\) (definition 10.7), where \(\lambda^h_{\mathrm{r}} = (\omega^h_{\mathrm{r}0})^2\). So \((\omega^h_{\mathrm{r}})^2 = (\omega^h_{\mathrm{r}0})^2 + k/(\rho A)\) and

\[\omega^h_{\mathrm{r}} \ge \omega_{\mathrm{r}} = \sqrt{\omega_{\mathrm{r}0}^2 + \frac{k}{\rho A}}\]

for every \(r = 1, \dots, N\), where \(N\) is the number of free unknowns and \(\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\) the natural frequency of theorem 25.7.

Proof

We must show that the foundation matrix is a multiple of the mass matrix, then use corollary 13.56.

  1. By item 4 of lemma 25.8, \(\mat K_{\mathrm{p}} = (k/\rho A)\,\mat M\) on all unknowns. Removing the same row and the same column keeps the relation \(\mat K_{\mathrm{p}} = (k/\rho A)\,\mat M\) on the free unknowns too.
  2. \((\mat K + \mat K_{\mathrm{p}})\vek d = \lambda\mat M\vek d\) is equivalent to \(\mat K\vek d = (\lambda - k/\rho A)\,\mat M\vek d\) by step 1, so \(\vek d\) is an eigenvector of the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\) with value \(\lambda - k/\rho A\), and conversely. The eigenvectors are the same, the eigenvalues differ by \(k/\rho A\).
  3. By corollary 13.56, \(\lambda^h_{\mathrm{r}} \ge \lambda_{\mathrm{r}0}\) for \(r = 1, \dots, N\) for a simply supported beam. We add \(k/\rho A\) to both sides: \((\omega^h_{\mathrm{r}})^2 \ge \omega_{\mathrm{r}0}^2 + k/\rho A = \omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2\) by equation (25.7).

Source: The proof in steps is not from the sources, it follows lemma 25.8 and corollary 13.56.

At \(n = 2\), \(\sqrt{143{,}38^2 + 400{,}32^2} = 425{,}22\) and \(\sqrt{634{,}08^2 + 400{,}32^2} = 749{,}87\), exactly what the corollary says. All consistent frequencies are above the exact ones, as they must be. The error grows with the order of the mode: at \(n = 2\) it is \(0{,}045\) % for the first and \(7{,}5\) % for the second mode, since a mesh of two elements describes the second half-wave shape badly. At \(n = 8\) the third error is \(0{,}12\) %.

Nodal springs give something else, and not always higher. At \(n = 2\) and nodal springs the frequencies are \(417{,}98\), \(634{,}08\) and \(1637{,}65\) 1/s. The first and the second are below the exact \(425{,}03\) and \(697{,}58\), which the corollary excludes for the consistent model. The second, \(634{,}08\) 1/s, is exactly the frequency of the model without the foundation: in the second mode the middle node has deflection zero, so the nodal spring does not feel it and the foundation does nothing. The consistent foundation raises the same frequency of the model to \(749{,}87\) 1/s (corollary 25.9), and the exact frequency of the beam on the foundation is \(697{,}58\) 1/s (theorem 25.7), \(63{,}5\) 1/s above the nodal model. At \(n = 4\) and \(8\) all three are above the exact ones. So at a coarse mesh the model with nodal springs can have a frequency below the true one, which the consistent one cannot. The reason is in the table of figure 25.7: the nodal model has another energy of the foundation, larger for some fields and smaller for others than the true one, so it is neither an upper nor a lower bound.

25.2.3 The deflection under a force in the finite element model is always too small

The free beam of section 25.1, of length \(L = 4\) m or \(L = 1\) m, on a foundation with \(k = 10^6\) N/m\(^2\), loaded in the middle by a force \(P = 10\) kN, has a deflection under the force of \(9{,}3142\) mm and \(11{,}3713\) mm (corollary 25.6, \(\beta L = 7{,}438\) and \(1{,}860\)). The model with the consistent foundation matrix gives at \(n = 2, 4, 8, 16\) elements (num-02.out, item 2):

\(L\) \(n = 2\) \(n = 4\) \(n = 8\) \(n = 16\) exact
4 m \(7{,}1744\) \(8{,}9281\) \(9{,}2855\) \(9{,}3123\) \(9{,}3142\)
1 m \(11{,}3290\) \(11{,}3687\) \(11{,}3712\) \(11{,}3713\) \(11{,}3713\)

(all in mm). At \(L = 4\) m the values are always below the exact one, the error is \(23\) %, \(4{,}1\) %, \(0{,}31\) % and \(0{,}02\) %. At \(L = 1\) m the last value looks equal to the exact one, but only to four decimals. With a more accurate computation (num-02.out, item 5) the exact value is \(11{,}371348\) mm and \(n = 8, 16, 32\) give \(11{,}371184\), \(11{,}371338\) and \(11{,}371348\) mm, so still below or equal up to rounding, and the difference falls by a factor of \(16\) when \(n\) doubles (\(1{,}6 \cdot 10^{-4}\), \(1{,}0 \cdot 10^{-5}\), \(6{,}4 \cdot 10^{-7}\) mm). The factor \(16\) is measured, not proved.

Let us draw figure 25.8. On the right part is the axis of the number of elements \(n = 2, 4, 8, 16\) and vertically the deflection. The dashed line is the exact value \(9{,}3142\) mm. The blue line connects the values of the consistent model: it rises from below and approaches the dashed one. We add the orange line of the model with nodal springs, which at \(L = 4\) m is also below the dashed line and still lower, but fits it. On the left part is the same for \(L = 1\) m (axes, the exact value, the blue line): the blue rises from below again, but the orange starts above the dashed line, at \(11{,}9944\) mm, and falls: \(11{,}5234\), \(11{,}4089\), \(11{,}3807\) mm. So at \(L = 1\) m the nodal model is above the exact value for all four meshes.

Why is the blue line always below the dashed one? The model allows only fields that are piecewise cubic polynomials, which is a subspace of all admissible fields. A restriction to fewer fields stiffens the beam, a stiffer beam bends less under the same force, so the deflection under the force is too small. This is the idea of theorem 6.34, and the corollary below proves it.

Corollary 25.10 The deflection under the force is a lower bound for a consistent foundation (poves pod silo je pri konsistentni podlagi spodnja meja)

Let a free beam with constant \(EI\) lie on a Winkler foundation with constant \(k > 0\) and be loaded by point forces \(P_1, \dots, P_{\mathrm{m}}\) at points \(x_1, \dots, x_{\mathrm{m}}\) that are all nodes of a mesh of \(n\) Hermite elements, forces and deflection measured in the same direction (definition 25.1). Let \(v\) be the exact deflection and \(v_{\mathrm{h}}\) the deflection given by the model with the consistent foundation matrix (lemma 25.8), \((\mat K + \mat K_{\mathrm{p}})\vek d = \vek f\). Then

\[\sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^m P_{\mathrm{i}}\,v_{\mathrm{h}}(x_{\mathrm{i}}) \le \sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^m P_{\mathrm{i}}\,v(x_{\mathrm{i}}) , \tag{25.9}\]

that is, the work of the forces on the model is not larger than on the exact field. For one force, \(P\,v_{\mathrm{h}}(x_1) \le P\,v(x_1)\), so \(|v_{\mathrm{h}}(x_1)| \le |v(x_1)|\).

Proof

We must show that the model is the Ritz solution for the energy form \(B\) and that the exact solution is in the weak form with the same form, then use theorem 6.34.

  1. The form \(B(w, z) = \int_0^L EI\,w''z''\,\mathrm{d}x + \int_0^L k\,w\,z\,\mathrm{d}x\) is symmetric and bilinear. It is positive: \(B(w, w) \ge \int k\,w^2\,\mathrm{d}x > 0\) for continuous \(w \ne 0\) (lemma 6.10, item 3), since \(k > 0\), also for a free beam without supports. The load \(\ell(w) = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} P_{\mathrm{i}}\,w(x_{\mathrm{i}})\) is linear and does not depend on the deformation.
  2. The exact deflection \(v\) solves \(EI\,v'''' + k\,v = 0\) on every piece between neighbouring forces and ends, \(v\), \(v'\) and \(v''\) are continuous, the free ends have \(v'' = v''' = 0\), and at the point \(x_{\mathrm{i}}\), \(EI\,\big(v'''(x_{\mathrm{i}}^+) - v'''(x_{\mathrm{i}}^-)\big) = P_{\mathrm{i}}\) (lemma 5.43, \(V = -EI\,v'''\), \(V\) jumps by \(-P_{\mathrm{i}}\)). For any admissible \(w\) (continuous \(w\) and \(w'\)), on every piece by lemma 6.13 \(\int EI\,v''''w = \big[EI\,v'''w - EI\,v''w'\big] + \int EI\,v''w''\).
  3. We add over the pieces. The terms with \(v''w'\) cancel at interior points (continuity) and are zero at the ends. The terms \(EI\,v'''w\) give at every \(x_{\mathrm{i}}\) the sum \(-EI\,[v''']\,w(x_{\mathrm{i}}) = -P_{\mathrm{i}}\,w(x_{\mathrm{i}})\), and are zero at the ends. So \(0 = \int(EI\,v'''' + k\,v)\,w\,\mathrm{d}x = -\ell(w) + B(v, w)\), that is \(B(v, w) = \ell(w)\) for every admissible \(w\): the weak form.
  4. The Hermite functions \(H_{\mathrm{I}}\) are admissible fields (continuous with continuous slope, step 1 of the proof of corollary 13.56). By item 5 of lemma 25.8, \((\mat K + \mat K_{\mathrm{p}})_{\mathrm{IJ}} = B(H_{\mathrm{J}}, H_{\mathrm{I}})\), and \(f_{\mathrm{I}} = \ell(H_{\mathrm{I}})\): the force \(P_{\mathrm{i}}\) is picked up by the function \(H_{\mathrm{I}}\) of node \(x_{\mathrm{i}}\) for displacement (\(H_{\mathrm{I}}(x_{\mathrm{i}}) = 1\), other nodal values \(0\), lemma 12.8), so \(f_{\mathrm{I}} = P_{\mathrm{i}}\) for this \(I\) and zero elsewhere. These are the Ritz equations \(\sum_{\mathrm{J}} B(H_{\mathrm{J}}, H_{\mathrm{I}})\,d_{\mathrm{J}} = \ell(H_{\mathrm{I}})\) for the space \(\{H_{\mathrm{I}}\}\) with \(\psi_0 = 0\) (definition 6.32), so \(v_{\mathrm{h}}\) is the Ritz solution.
  5. By item 2 of theorem 6.34, \(\ell(v_{\mathrm{h}}) \le \ell(v)\), which is equation (25.9). For one force this is \(P\,v_{\mathrm{h}}(x_1) \le P\,v(x_1)\). Both sides are nonnegative: \(P\,v(x_1) = \ell(v) = B(v, v)\) by step 3 with \(w = v\), and \(P\,v_{\mathrm{h}}(x_1) = \ell(v_{\mathrm{h}}) = \sum_{\mathrm{I}} d_{\mathrm{I}}\,\ell(H_{\mathrm{I}}) = \sum_{\mathrm{I}, \mathrm{J}} d_{\mathrm{I}}\,B(H_{\mathrm{J}}, H_{\mathrm{I}})\,d_{\mathrm{J}} = B(v_{\mathrm{h}}, v_{\mathrm{h}})\) by the Ritz equations of step 4. So \(v(x_1)\) and \(v_{\mathrm{h}}(x_1)\) have the sign of the force \(P \ne 0\) or are zero, and the inequality gives \(|v_{\mathrm{h}}(x_1)| \le |v(x_1)|\).

Source: Theorem 6.34 (BATHE (4.89) to (4.91), PDF p. 258, COOK PDF p. 164: "either exact or too stiff"). COOK (PDF p. 302): "convergence occurs with mesh refinement, but a rather fine mesh may be needed to determine bending moments accurately". The proof in steps is not from the sources.

The proof showed that with lemma 25.8 the model is a Ritz solution, so the theorem on energy holds. The bound is for the work of the forces, not for the deflection at every point separately. For two forces, \(P_1 = +10\) kN in the middle and \(P_2 = -10\) kN (upward) at a quarter of the free beam \(L = 4\) m, the work on the exact field is \(169{,}90\) N m, on the model with \(n = 4\) elements \(165{,}55\) and with \(n = 8\) \(169{,}50\) N m, all below the exact (num-02.out, item 7). The individual deflection is also below the exact one in absolute value for these numbers (\(8{,}09\) against \(8{,}31\) mm and \(8{,}46\) against \(8{,}68\) mm at \(n = 4\)), but the theorem does not require this and does not prove it.

Where the bound does not hold: nodal springs. With nodal springs the foundation matrix is not the matrix of the form \(B\) (the energy is different, table of figure 25.7), the model is not a Ritz one and the theorem does not hold. The proof would stop at step 4. The result confirms it: at \(L = 1\) m the deflection is \(11{,}9944\) mm at \(n = 2\), \(5{,}5\) % above the exact \(11{,}3713\) mm, and stays above the exact one for all four meshes (figure 25.8, left side). At \(L = 4\) m it is also below the exact (\(4{,}96\), \(8{,}19\), \(9{,}24\), \(9{,}31\) mm), but this is a coincidence, not a consequence: the theorem says nothing about it. The reason the proof fails is in the table of figure 25.7: for a rotation without displacement the nodal model does not feel the foundation, and for unequal displacements it feels it more strongly, \(2{,}059\) times for opposite displacements, so there is no comparison in either direction.

The mesh must be fine enough for the true deflection. For a beam on a foundation the distance from the force to the largest lift is \(\pi/\beta = 1{,}69\) m, half the wavelength \(2\pi/\beta\) (section 25.1, \(\beta = 1{,}8596\) 1/m, minimum of the deflection at \(\beta x = \pi\)). With \(L = 4\) m and \(n = 8\), \(h = 0{,}5\) m, that is \(\beta h = 0{,}93\), and the error is \(0{,}31\) %, and at \(L = 1\) m and \(n = 2\) (\(h = 0{,}5\) m) \(0{,}37\) %. At \(h = 1\) m (\(n = 4\), \(L = 4\) m), \(\beta h = 1{,}86\) and the error is \(4{,}1\) %. These are two measured cases, not a rule.

This section gave two results we need further on. The first: the foundation in the finite element model is a matrix obtained from the same energy as the others and it is the mass matrix multiplied by \(k/(\rho A)\), so the vibration on a foundation in the model is the same vibration as without it, shifted in the square of the frequency. The second: the shortcut with nodal springs gives, for a uniform foundation, at some meshes frequencies below the true ones and a deflection above the true one, which the consistent model does not do. The bearing in section 25.9 acts at a point, not along the length, so a spring at a node is there the right model and not a shortcut.
