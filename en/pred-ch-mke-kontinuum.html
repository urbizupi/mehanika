Chapter 15 Continuum finite elements

Chapter 15Lecture: Continuum finite elements

1 The triangle: three numbers are the whole field

We have a plate of any shape and we look for the temperature in it. The temperature is one number at every point, and there are infinitely many points. How do we describe such a field with finitely many numbers, and do it so that the pieces do not come apart?

Let us draw the outline of the plate. Now we cut it with straight cuts into triangles, as we would cut a cake. Where do the triangles meet? At their corners, which we call nodes. Let us take a single triangle, enlarge it and number its nodes \(1\), \(2\), \(3\), counterclockwise.

The claim of this part of the lecture is a single one. On a triangle the field is a flat sheet through the three values at the nodes. So it has one slope over the whole triangle, and two neighbours agree on their common edge by themselves, because there only the two ends of the edge decide the field. The unknowns of the whole plate are therefore only the numbers at the nodes.

The claim has three words that ask "why?": flat sheet, one slope, and neighbours agreeing. Let us take them in turn.

1.1 Why a flat sheet and why exactly three numbers?

What is the simplest field that changes in both directions of the plane?

A constant field does not change. The next one is a flat sheet, \(u = \alpha + \beta x + \gamma y\), which has three numbers, \(\alpha\), \(\beta\) and \(\gamma\). Let us draw the triangle obliquely, as if it lay on a table. It has three nodes, \(1\), \(2\), \(3\), one for each number. Over node \(1\) we stand a stick of height \(1\), over the other two nothing, and across the three tips we lay a flat card. That is the function \(L_1\). We do the same for \(L_2\), with height \(1\) over node \(2\), and for \(L_3\).

Why does exactly one card go through three tips? Try it at home. Lay a piece of cardboard on the tips of three pencils that do not stand in a row, and the cardboard stays put, it does not tip over and does not rock. Two tips do not hold it, it still turns about the line through them. Three tips in a row do not hold it either, since they are again only one line. This is the bottom of the explanation, a statement anyone can check: through three points not on one line goes exactly one plane.

Now we build the field with the values \(d_1\), \(d_2\), \(d_3\) at the nodes. We stretch the card \(L_1\) upward by the factor \(d_1\), \(L_2\) by \(d_2\) and \(L_3\) by \(d_3\), and add them:

\[u_{\mathrm{h}} = L_1\,d_1 + L_2\,d_2 + L_3\,d_3 .\]

What is this sum over node \(1\)? There \(L_1 = 1\) and the other two are zero, so \(u_{\mathrm{h}} = d_1\). A sum of flat sheets is again a flat sheet. So three numbers already are the whole field on the triangle. In the book: 15.1.3, equation (15.5).

And if we take \(d = 1\) for all three? We get the horizontal sheet at height \(1\). The functions \(L_{\mathrm{i}}\) add up to \(1\) at every point. Why, the next answer tells.

1.2 What is \(L_{\mathrm{i}}\) and why is it 1 at its own node and 0 at the other two?

We set up the sticks by guessing. Where do we get a function that is \(1\) at one corner and \(0\) at the other two, for a triangle of any shape? Let us try shares of area.

Let us draw the triangle with corners \((0, 0)\), \((40, 0)\) and \((10, 30)\) mm, whose area is \(600\) mm². In it we choose the point \(P\) at \((20, 10)\) mm. We join the point to all three corners, and the triangle falls apart into three smaller ones. The first lies opposite corner \(1\) and has the area \(150\) mm². The second, opposite corner \(2\), has \(250\) mm², and the third \(200\) mm².

What share of the whole is each piece? The first \(150/600 = 0.25\), the second \(250/600 = 0.4167\) and the third \(200/600 = 0.3333\). These are the values of \(L_1\), \(L_2\) and \(L_3\) at the point \(P\). The function \(L_{\mathrm{i}}\) is a share of area, \(L_{\mathrm{i}} = A_{\mathrm{i}}/A\), so we call it an area coordinate (ploskovna koordinata).

Now let us move the point \(P\) to corner \(1\). What happens to the pieces? The piece opposite corner \(1\) grows into the whole triangle, its share is \(1\). The other two are squeezed into a segment and have no area, their share is \(0\). That is exactly what we asked of the sticks in the previous figure.

Why is the share flat inside, without bumps? Let us look at the right part of the figure. The piece \(A_1\) has the base, edge \(2\)–\(3\), and the height \(s\), the distance of the point \(P\) from that edge. The whole triangle has the same base and the height \(h\). The area is half the base times the height, so \(L_1 = s/h\), and the distance from a line changes evenly with position. So the share is a flat sheet.

And why is the sum always \(1\)? Because the three pieces together always make the whole triangle. Shares of a whole add up to \(1\). This too is the bottom of the explanation, nothing more than counting. In the book: 15.1.2, equation (15.3).

1.3 Why is the slope the same all over the triangle and what does it mean?

Let us step onto a roof of flat sheet metal. Does the steepness under our feet depend on where we step? It does not. A flat sheet, like the card \(L_1\), has one single slope. The slope in the direction \(x\) is \(\beta\), in the direction \(y\) it is \(\gamma\), and those are two numbers for the whole triangle. The gradient is constant on the element. In the book: 15.1.5, equation (15.6).

And if the field is not temperatures but displacements?

Let us draw the same triangle, grey dashed, as it is before loading. The load moves every node. The horizontal part of the movement \(u_{\mathrm{i}}\) and the vertical part \(v_{\mathrm{i}}\) we draw as distances with bars at the ends. These are six numbers, the only data the element has. We join the new positions of the nodes and get the triangle after loading, in orange.

What happens to all the points in between? Each component of the displacement is a flat sheet, \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = L_1 u_1 + L_2 u_2 + L_3 u_3\), and the same for \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\). For displacements, though, the slopes are the strains: \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial x\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial y\) and the shear from the mixed slopes. One slope means one strain for the whole triangle, and with it one stress. That is why we call it the constant strain triangle (trikotnik s stalno deformacijo, CST). What does that look like? Every edge stays straight, and so does every straight line we might draw into the triangle. In the book: 15.4.1, equations (15.17) and (15.22).

1.4 Why do two neighbours agree on their common edge?

Now we have a card of its own on every triangle. Do two neighbouring cards meet on their common edge, or does a gap stay between them?

Let us draw two triangles, \(T\) and \(T'\), which share an edge. Their nodes are \(P_{\mathrm{a}}\) and \(P_{\mathrm{b}}\) on the common edge, \(P_{\mathrm{c}}\) of \(T\) and \(P_{\mathrm{c}'}\) of \(T'\). Over each node we stand a stick as tall as the value of the field there: \(d_{\mathrm{a}}\), \(d_{\mathrm{b}}\), \(d_{\mathrm{c}}\) and \(d_{\mathrm{c}'}\). Across the three tips of the triangle \(T\) we lay a card, across the three tips of the triangle \(T'\) a second card.

Where do the cards meet? Over the common edge. The rim of each card over that edge is the segment between the tips over \(P_{\mathrm{a}}\) and \(P_{\mathrm{b}}\). Through two points goes exactly one straight line, which is the second bottom of the explanation. So both rims are the same segment. There is no gap.

Let us take a point \(P\) on the common edge, the share \(\lambda\) of the way from \(P_{\mathrm{a}}\) toward \(P_{\mathrm{b}}\). On both cards it has the same height, \((1 - \lambda)\,d_{\mathrm{a}} + \lambda\,d_{\mathrm{b}}\). The idea of the proof is this: on the edge \(L_{\mathrm{a}}\) is a straight run with the values \(1\) and \(0\) at the ends, that is \(1 - \lambda\), and \(L_{\mathrm{b}} = \lambda\). In the book: 15.1.4, lemma 15.7.

What about the third node? Does \(d_{\mathrm{c}}\) not act on the rim? Let us raise the tip over \(P_{\mathrm{c}}\) by \(\Delta d_{\mathrm{c}}\). The card of \(T\) turns, like a cardboard when we lift one pencil, and it turns exactly about the segment over the common edge, which stays in place. On that edge \(L_{\mathrm{c}} = 0\), so \(d_{\mathrm{c}}\) has no weight there.

Does this always hold? No, and a counterexample shows which condition we used without saying.

Let us draw a figure with the edge \(AB\), \(40\) mm long, and the node \(M\) at its middle. Below \(AB\) lies one triangle, which does not have \(M\) as a corner. Above it lie two triangles, for which \(M\) is a corner. We give the node \(M\) the value \(1\), all the others \(0\), and draw the field along \(AB\). The two upper triangles give a tent: \(0\) at \(A\), \(1\) at \(M\), \(0\) at \(B\). The lower triangle knows only \(A\) and \(B\), so its field is zero along the whole edge. The difference between them is \(0.5\) at \(x = 10\) mm and \(1\) at \(M\). If these were displacements, the plate would open up here. Such an \(M\) is called a hanging node (viseče vozlišče). The condition is therefore: every common edge runs from node to node.

1.5 Why may the field have a kink at an edge?

May a roof have a ridge? In the figure of the two cards the slope changes suddenly at the common rim, there is no derivative there. Does this spoil the computation of the energy?

Let us take a bar cut at \(x = 20\) mm into two blocks, which we move apart for the drawing. Above it we draw an imagined displacement that has a kink at \(x = 20\) mm but no jump. At the contact the blocks pull each other with equal and opposite forces, by Newton's third law. Since the field is the same on both sides of the contact, the work of these two forces cancels to zero. The energy is then the sum of the energies of the blocks, and the energy needs only finite slopes, not smooth ones. A kink has a finite slope on each side.

Now we move the second block so that the field jumps by \(10\) mm. The work of the forces at the contact no longer cancels, the stress times the jump remains. A jump would mean an infinite slope. So a kink is allowed, a jump is not. In the book: 15.2.3, theorem 15.15.

1.6 Back up

Now let us build everything from the bottom up. Three tips fix a flat card, so three numbers are the whole field on a triangle (\(L_1\), \(L_2\), \(L_3\)). A flat card has one slope, so every triangle has one strain (the edges stay straight). Two tips fix the rim, so the cards of neighbours meet without a gap (the common rim). The whole plate is one roof of flat facets.

Let us return to the first figure. Over every triangle of the mesh lies one flat facet, and the unknowns are the heights at the nodes. What then is the stiffness matrix of one element? Only the energy of one flat facet. Since nothing varies inside the triangle, there is no integral to compute: for displacements \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}} = t\,A\,\mat B^\T \mat C \mat B\), thickness times area times the constant integrand. In the book: equation (15.24), for temperature with the computed numbers equation (15.15).

1.7 Four views of the same thing

Geometric, for a few minutes: the cards with their common ridge. This view adds that all three claims are seen in one picture: a flat sheet, one slope and a joint without a gap.

By hand: cardboard on three pencils. Lift one pencil and the cardboard turns about the opposite edge, like \(L_1\), \(L_2\) and \(L_3\). This view adds the feel of why the third node has no weight on the edge.

By numbers: the point \(P\) and its three shares of area. This view adds that the weights are computed from the coordinates of the corners alone, without a picture.

From the bar: in chapter 7 the element was a segment, the field a straight line through two values, the strain \(\varepsilon = u'\) constant. The triangle is the same idea with one more direction. And on every edge of a triangle lives exactly such a bar element, a straight line through two values.

1.8 Assumptions and what they cost

The first assumption: the field is flat inside an element, so the strain in it is constant. Why is it sensible? Let us see how much the true strain changes over one cell.

Let us draw the axis \(x\) along the cantilever, from the clamp at \(0\) to the free end at \(1000\) mm, and upward the strain \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) at the top edge. The bending moment falls linearly from the clamp toward the end, so the strain falls linearly too, from \(\varepsilon_0\) to zero. Now let us cut out a cell of length \(100\) mm at the clamp. Over it the strain falls by \(\tfrac{100}{1000}\,\varepsilon_0\), that is by \(10\) %. A cell of length \(25\) mm has a change of only \(\tfrac{25}{1000}\,\varepsilon_0\), that is \(2.5\) %. With every halving of the cell the deviation from a constant value halves. Every smooth field looks flat from close enough. When does the assumption not hold? When the strain varies strongly inside one element, for instance over the depth of a beam, where the strain is \(\kappa y\), zero at the middle and largest at the edge.

What does it cost? Let us draw the cantilever with a mesh of \(10 \times 2\) cells, each cut into two triangles, and on the left the clamping. On the free end acts the force \(F = 500\) N. The cantilever bends. The exact deflection of the end is \(-7.9719\) mm. The mesh of triangles sags by only \(-0.914\) mm, that is \(0.115\) of the exact value. In the figure the displacements of both are magnified \(20\) times. Why so little, although the mesh is decent? This question opens the third part of the lecture. In the book: 15.4.7.

The second assumption: every common edge runs from node to node. Why do we need it? Only then do the same two values decide the edge from both sides. When is it broken and what does it cost? At a hanging node, where the jump is up to \(1\): the field is no longer one function, and the sum of the energies of the elements is no longer the energy of the body.

The third assumption: the edges are straight. A curved boundary of the plate is replaced by a polygon, look at the outline and the mesh. Why is it allowed? The narrow slivers between a straight edge and the arc are smaller for smaller triangles. What does it cost? A geometric error that falls as the mesh is refined. The cure, elements with curved edges, comes in the second part of the lecture.

2 One square for every quadrilateral

The triangle of the first part can do only one constant strain. We want an element that can do more. But every quadrilateral has its own shape. Do we have to derive the shape functions and compute the integrals again for every shape? No. Today we compute all quadrilaterals on one single square.

First we draw a square with side \(2\). We number the nodes counter-clockwise, from \(1\) at the bottom left to \(4\) at the top left. Through the centre we draw the axes \(\xi\) and \(\eta\), the natural coordinates (naravne koordinate). So the sides of the square are always \(\xi = \pm 1\) and \(\eta = \pm 1\). On the square we draw a grid, as we would draw it with a marker on a sheet of rubber: three blue lines at constant \(\xi\) and three orange ones at constant \(\eta\), at \(-\tfrac12\), \(0\) and \(\tfrac12\).

Now we take the rubber and pin its four corners to the four nodes of the real element. That is the arrow \(\Phi\). The rubber stretches into the quadrilateral with nodes \((0, 0)\), \((40, 0)\), \((36, 30)\) and \((4, 24)\) mm. The grid stretches with it: the lines stay straight, but they are no longer perpendicular and no longer evenly spaced. The centre of the square lands at the point \((20, 13.5)\) mm. At every corner and at the centre we write one more number: \(240\), \(300\), \(246\), \(186\) and at the centre \(243\) mm². What this number means we will see in a few minutes.

The claim of this part: four functions \(N_{\mathrm{i}}\) on the square do two things at once. They put a point of the square at its place in the quadrilateral, and they give it its value of the field. So all the computing runs on the square, and the number \(J\) says how much area of the quadrilateral belongs to a small piece of the square.

This claim uses four ideas we have not explained yet. What are the functions \(N_{\mathrm{i}}\)? Why the same functions for the position and for the field? What is \(J\)? And how do we compute an integral on the square? Let us go in order.

2.1 Why four functions, and what are they?

The triangle had three nodes and three functions \(L_{\mathrm{i}}\). The square has four nodes. What functions do we give them?

Let us draw the square with four nodes. Each node gets one function, a shape function (oblikovna funkcija). We ask of it the same as for the triangle: at its own node it has the value \(1\), at the other three \(0\). For node \(1\) in the corner \((-1, -1)\) we build it from two straight lines. The first is \((1 - \xi)/2\), which is \(1\) on the left side and \(0\) on the right. The second is \((1 - \eta)/2\), which is \(1\) at the bottom and \(0\) at the top. The product is \(1\) only in corner \(1\) and zero on both opposite sides. In general

\[N_{\mathrm{i}}(\xi, \eta) = \tfrac14\,(1 + \xi_{\mathrm{i}}\,\xi)\,(1 + \eta_{\mathrm{i}}\,\eta) .\]

Let us draw where \(N_1\) has the same value. These are the level lines at \(0.9\) near node \(1\), then \(0.7\), \(0.5\), \(0.3\) and \(0.1\) towards the opposite corner. Why are they curved? Because the product of two straight lines at a constant value is a hyperbola.

What happens along one side? On the bottom side of the square \(\eta = -1\), so \(N_3\) and \(N_4\) are zero there, and \(N_1\) and \(N_2\) are straight lines from \(1\) to \(0\). So the field along a side is a straight line, fixed by its two ends alone. This is the same ground as for the triangle: two points fix a line. That is why two neighbouring elements agree on their common side, since they have the same two nodes there.

In the book: 15.5.1, (15.26).

2.2 Why "iso", the same functions for the position and for the field?

Look again at the rubber with its grid. Where is the point of the quadrilateral that sits at \((\xi, \eta)\) in the square? The weights \(N_{\mathrm{i}}\) average the positions of the four nodes:

\[\vek x = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} N_{\mathrm{i}}(\xi, \eta)\,\vek p_{\mathrm{i}} .\]

Let us try it at the centre. There all four weights are \(\tfrac14\), so the centre lands at the average of the nodes:

\[x = \tfrac14\,(0 + 40 + 36 + 4) = 20\ \text{mm} , \qquad y = \tfrac14\,(0 + 0 + 30 + 24) = 13.5\ \text{mm} .\]

And what is the displacement at that point? The same weights average the displacements of the four nodes:

\[\vek u = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} N_{\mathrm{i}}(\xi, \eta)\,\vek u_{\mathrm{i}} .\]

The same functions, the same parameters, the same nodes for the position and for the field. This is exactly what the name isoparametric (izoparametričen) says. The field rides on the grid: the value of the field travels together with the point that the arrow \(\Phi\) carries into the quadrilateral.

What does this sameness buy us? Suppose the nodes have the displacements of a linear field, \(\vek u_{\mathrm{i}} = \vek a_0 + \mat G\,\vek p_{\mathrm{i}}\). Put them into the sum. The part \(\vek a_0\) is multiplied by \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} N_{\mathrm{i}} = 1\). The part \(\mat G\,\vek p_{\mathrm{i}}\) gives \(\mat G \sum_{\mathrm{i}} N_{\mathrm{i}} \vek p_{\mathrm{i}}\), and by the first equation that is just \(\mat G\,\vek x\). So the element returns exactly \(\vek u = \vek a_0 + \mat G\,\vek x\) at every point of the quadrilateral, whatever its shape. Here we see why the functions are the same: because the position is written with the same \(N_{\mathrm{i}}\), the linear term of the field comes out by itself. So rigid motion and constant strain are always inside the element.

In the book: 15.5.2, (15.27), (15.28), 15.36.

2.3 What is \(J\) and why do we need it?

How much area of the quadrilateral belongs to a small piece of the square? We must know, because the stiffness matrix is an integral over the quadrilateral, but we want to integrate over the square.

Again we draw the square. In its middle we place a small cell with sides \(\Delta\xi\) and \(\Delta\eta\), both \(0.5\). Its area on the square is \(0.5 \cdot 0.5 = 0.25\). The map \(\Phi\) carries it into the quadrilateral. There it becomes the orange cell. It is no longer a square, it is almost a parallelogram.

How do we measure its area? The bottom side is the vector \(\Phi_\xi\,\Delta\xi\): how far the point moves in the quadrilateral when it moves by \(\Delta\xi\) in the \(\xi\) direction in the square. The left side is the vector \(\Phi_\eta\,\Delta\eta\). The area of a parallelogram is base times height, which is the cross product of these two vectors. The cross product of the derivatives \(\Phi_\xi \times \Phi_\eta\) has a name, the Jacobian determinant (Jacobijeva determinanta) \(J\). So the area of the orange cell is \(J\,\Delta\xi\,\Delta\eta\), at the centre

\[J\,\Delta\xi\,\Delta\eta = 243 \cdot 0.25 = 60.75\ \text{mm}^2 .\]

The number \(J = 243\) mm² is exactly the one we wrote at the centre in the first figure. So \(J\) says how many mm² of the real element we get per unit area of the square.

Is \(J\) the same everywhere? Let us draw a cell near corner \(4\) of the same size. Its image is visibly smaller, there \(J = 205\) mm². Then a cell near corner \(2\), and its image is larger, \(J = 281\) mm². Both numbers come from the formula for \(J\) a little further down. Why do they differ? At every corner \(J\) is a quarter of the area of the parallelogram on the two sides that meet there. At corner \(2\) these two sides are long, at corner \(4\) shorter. The rubber is stretched more near corner \(2\) than near corner \(4\). So \(J\) changes over the element, and we must carry it along in every integral:

\[\int_{\text{element}} f\,\mathrm{d}A = \int_{\text{square}} f\big(\Phi(\xi, \eta)\big)\,J(\xi, \eta)\,\mathrm{d}A .\]

One detail. For the quadrilateral \(J\) is an affine function, \(J = 243 + 30\,\xi - 27\,\eta\) mm². So the area of the image of a cell is exactly \(J\) at the centre of the cell times the area of the cell, with no approximation. For elements with curved sides this is only an approximation, the better the smaller the cell.

In the book: 15.32, (15.29).

2.4 When does the map fail?

Everything so far assumed that \(J\) is positive everywhere. What if it is not? Take the same quadrilateral and pull node \(3\) inwards.

We start with the same square. The arrow \(\Phi\) now carries it into the quadrilateral with nodes \((0, 0)\), \((40, 0)\), \((14, 8)\) and \((4, 24)\) mm. Node \(3\) is pulled inwards, and the interior angle at it is now larger than \(180\) degrees. Whoever draws this shape sees nothing wrong.

But the rubber cannot stretch into such a shape without folding. The fold is the orange curve in the quadrilateral. On the square it comes from a line that cuts off a triangle at corner \(3\). There \(J < 0\). Take two different points \(P\) and \(Q\) in the square, one in that triangle and one outside. Both land on the same point \((16, 8.96)\) mm of the quadrilateral. The rubber lies in two layers there. At the corners we write the values \(J_{\mathrm{i}}\): \(240\), \(80\), \(-84\) and \(76\) mm². The third is negative.

What does this do to an integral? A piece of the square with negative \(J\) counts its area with a minus sign. The integral subtracts material. Let us compute both integrals:

\[\int_{\text{square}} J\,\mathrm{d}A = 312\ \text{mm}^2 , \qquad \int_{\text{square}} |J|\,\mathrm{d}A = 342.12\ \text{mm}^2 .\]

Which one is right? The first is exactly the area of the drawn shape, but only because the positive and negative shares cancel. The second is the area of nothing we could draw. The change of variables does not hold here, because the map is not one to one.

When are we safe? The theorem says: if \(J\) is positive at all four corners, it is positive everywhere. The idea of the proof in one sentence: \(J\) is affine, so it equals the average of its four corner values with the weights \(N_{\mathrm{i}}\), which are nowhere negative, and an average of positive numbers is positive. In the book: 15.5.3, 15.33, 15.34.

So the assumption is \(J_{\mathrm{i}} > 0\) at all corners. Why it holds for an ordinary mesh: \(J_{\mathrm{i}}\) is a quarter of the cross product of the two sides at the corner, and it is positive when the edge turns left at the corner. Four left turns mean a convex quadrilateral with its nodes numbered counter-clockwise. When it does not hold: an interior angle of \(180\) degrees or more, or nodes numbered the wrong way round. What it does to the result: the area gets a negative share. At the four points where we will evaluate the integrand in the next step, \(J\) is \(171.5\), \(79.2\), \(-15.5\) and \(76.9\) mm². The third lies in the triangle with \(J < 0\) and would enter the integral with a negative weight.

2.5 How do we compute the integral?

How do we compute an integral that we cannot do by hand?

Why can we not? The strain is made of derivatives with respect to \(x\) and \(y\), but we know the functions \(N_{\mathrm{i}}\) as functions of \(\xi\) and \(\eta\). Going over needs the inverse Jacobian matrix, and in it we divide by \(J\). So the integrand of the stiffness matrix is a fraction, not a polynomial. We do what we do with a ruler when we measure the area under a curve: take a few samples, multiply each by its width and add them up. This is the Gauss rule (Gaussovo pravilo).

We draw three squares. On the first there is one single point, at the centre, with the weight \(4\), the area of the square. On the second there are four points at \(\xi, \eta = \pm 0.5774\), each with the weight \(1\). On the third there are nine points. Below we also draw a triangle with its corners and the points of the rules for it: the centroid, the midpoints of the sides and three inner points. Today we stay with the square. Why exactly \(\pm 0.5774\), that is \(\pm 1/\sqrt3\)? These are the zeros of the polynomial \(\xi^2 - \tfrac13\), which is orthogonal to all polynomials of lower degree. So the rule gives zero for its multiples, which is also their integral, and two points are exact up to degree \(3\). In general: \(q\) points in each direction are exact up to degree \(2q - 1\). In the book: 15.42.

The stiffness matrix becomes a sum of four terms, one per point:

\[\mat k_{\mathrm{e}} = \sum_{\mathrm{p}=1}^{4} W_{\mathrm{p}}\,t\,J_{\mathrm{p}}\,\mat B_{\mathrm{p}}^\T\,\mat C\,\mat B_{\mathrm{p}} .\]

In every term there is \(J_{\mathrm{p}}\), the value of \(J\) at the point \(p\), the very one we drew on the cell.

The assumption: the \(2 \times 2\) rule is exact. Why it holds for a rectangle: there \(J\) is constant, the derivatives of \(N_{\mathrm{i}}\) are linear in one variable, and the integrand is of degree at most \(2\) in each variable, which is less than \(3\). So the \(2 \times 2\) rule is exact, and we call it full integration (polna integracija). When it does not hold: on a distorted quadrilateral the integrand is a fraction and the rule is only an approximation. What it does to the result: on the quadrilateral of the book the error fell by a factor of about \(270\) for every added point. This was observed in the numbers, not proved.

In the book: 15.5.4, (15.31), 15.6, (15.35), (15.37), 15.44.

2.6 Why not fewer points?

One point would be four times cheaper. What do we lose?

We draw the square dashed, as it is before the motion. We move the nodes: \(1\) and \(3\) to the right, \(2\) and \(4\) to the left, each by \(a = 0.3\) of half a side. The bottom side gets shorter, the top side longer. The upper half of the element stretches in the \(x\) direction and the lower half shortens, since \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = a\,\eta\).

And the middle? Both midlines stay in place and keep their length. Where they cross, at the centre, is the only point of the \(1 \times 1\) rule. There all strains are zero. So the element with one point sees nothing: the deformation is real, but the energy is zero. This is a spurious mode (lažni način), called the hourglass mode (peščena ura). The four points of the \(2 \times 2\) rule lie off the midlines, where \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = a\,\eta\) is not zero. They notice this mode and charge energy for it.

The assumption of reduced integration (reducirana integracija) \(1 \times 1\), with one single point. Why we would want it: four times less work. When it is dangerous: whenever nothing holds the spurious mode back. What it does to the result: for a beam made of such elements and clamped at one end, the computed displacement can be larger than the length of the beam, even though the strains at the centres look good.

In the book: (15.38), 15.46.

2.7 Back up

Now we build the claim again, from the ground. Rubber pinned to four nodes carries its grid along, and the same four functions carry the position and the field. The small cell says how much area each piece carries: \(J\). Positive \(J\) at the four corners guarantees that the rubber never folds. Four samples at the Gauss points turn the integral into a sum of four terms. One point is too few.

Back to the first figure. The square, the arrow and the numbers \(J\): this is the whole element. The square, the push into the quadrilateral and the area factor.

2.8 Perspectives

Rubber in a few minutes. We pin the corners of the rubber to the nodes and the grid stretches. This view adds that everything we know about the square the rubber carries into the quadrilateral.

Local area. The small cell and its image show that \(J\) is the ratio of two areas at one place. This view adds where \(J\) in the integral comes from.

Numbers. Let us add the four values at the corners:

\[240 + 300 + 246 + 186 = 972\ \text{mm}^2 = 4 \cdot 243\ \text{mm}^2 .\]

This is exactly the area of the quadrilateral, and it is four times the value at the centre. Why? The square has the area \(4\), and the average of the affine function \(J\) over the square is its value at the centre. For a rectangle \(40 \times 30\) mm \(J\) would be \(300\) mm² everywhere, a quarter of \(1200\) mm².

One dimension. One side of the square is the interval from \(-1\) to \(1\), of length \(2\). For a bar element of length \(h\) we stretched the same interval into that element, and the factor was \(h/2\). The plane \(J\) is this same factor, only it measures area instead of length. In the book: 15.5.

One more sentence on eight nodes. If we add to the corners a node at the midpoint of every side, we get the Q8 element, and a side through three points can curve along the edge of the body. In the book: 15.5.7. The same recipe with a cube instead of a square gives the solid elements. In the book: 15.10.

3 Where an element fails: parasitic shear, the patch test and locking

We start with three numbers.

On the horizontal axis we mark what part of the true displacement a computation gives. The true displacement is the line at \(1\), for the cantilever it is the tip deflection \(-7.9719\) mm. The cantilever made of triangles gives \(0.115\) of the true deflection, only \(-0.914\) mm. The same cantilever made of quadrilaterals gives \(0.292\). A tube of an almost incompressible material, such as rubber, gives \(0.1765\) of the true displacement. The mesh is correct, the computation has no error. Who ate the displacement? The answer is one picture.

Let us draw two equal grey rectangles, two cells of a mesh, \(2a\) wide and \(2b\) high. We bend the left cell the way a ruler bends when we flex it between our fingers. The horizontal lines curve, the vertical ones tilt and stay perpendicular to the horizontal ones. That is true bending. We give the right cell exactly the same displacements at its four corners. What does the quadrilateral do? Its horizontal lines stay straight, the two vertical sides tilt as on the left. Straight lines and a tilted side can no longer be perpendicular, so the right angle at the corner changes by \(\gamma = \kappa a\). We call this parasitic shear (parazitni strig): a shear that the true field does not have at all.

The claim of this part in one sentence: an element is too stiff wherever it cannot make the shape the body wants, it builds from the true nodal displacements a field with a strain the true field does not have, it pays energy for that strain, and so the mesh prefers to stay almost at rest.

3.1 Why does a wrong strain mean too small a displacement?

The element pays energy for a strain that is not there. Why would that make the displacement too small and not too large?

We start with the whole cantilever. Let us draw it cut into \(10 \times 2\) cells, each along its diagonal into two triangles. On the left the wall holds it, at the right end acts the force \(F = 500\) N. The true bending is the blue axis, while the mesh bends only this much, both drawn magnified \(20\) times. This is the \(0.115\) of the first figure.

The mesh picks the field with the least potential energy among all the fields it can draw. That is the Ritz property, proved in chapter 6. If every bent shape the mesh can make costs more energy than the true one, the mesh bends less under the same force. In the book: 15.2.4.

Why does a more expensive shape mean a smaller displacement? Here we are at the ground. From the ceiling we hang a soft spring and next to it a stiffer one, and on both the same weight \(F\). The soft one stretches by \(\delta\), the stiff one by the smaller \(\delta'\). The stiff spring asks more energy for the same stretch, so it stretches less under the same weight. A wrong strain turns the mesh into such a stiffer spring.

3.2 What exactly is the triangle missing?

What does a triangle do when we give its corners exactly the true displacements of the bent cell?

We take a single cell next to the wall, \(100 \times 20\) mm. True bending curves the top and bottom edges, the material stretches at the top and shortens at the bottom, the strain is \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \kappa y\), there is no shear. Now we take the triangle with corners \(1\), \(2\) and \(3\) and give it the true displacements at its corners. Node \(1\) stays put. Node \(2\) drops by \(v_2 = -0.1196\) mm, node \(3\) hardly at all. The horizontal displacement is zero at all three corners, so the triangle has no stretch at all, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0\). Side \(1\)-\(3\) stays vertical, side \(1\)-\(2\) tilts, and the right angle at node \(1\) opens by \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} = v_2/a = -1.196 \cdot 10^{-3}\).

So the triangle has traded bending for shear. What does that cost?

We mark the energy on an axis in units of the true energy. The true cell stores \(298.9\) N mm, that is the unit. The cell made of two triangles stores \(2626\) N mm, that is \(8.8\) units. Of this, about \(2160\) N mm is shear, more than four fifths. The cell behaves as if it were almost nine times stiffer. In the book: 15.4.7.

3.3 Why is the quadrilateral better and still too stiff?

The quadrilateral gives \(0.292\) instead of \(0.115\). What can it do that the triangle cannot, and what does it still lack? Let us go back to the picture of the two cells. The quadrilateral has the term \(\xi\eta\) in its displacement, so it shows \(u = \kappa x y\) exactly: the top edge stretches, the bottom one shortens, as in true bending. The triangle cannot do that. But the true vertical displacement also has the terms \(x^2\) and \(y^2\), and the quadrilateral lacks these two. Without them its edges cannot curve as on the left.

We do the derivation right on the picture, in three steps. First: all four corners of the quadrilateral have the same vertical displacement, since they all have the same \(x^2\) and \(y^2\). A bilinear field with four equal values is constant, so the cell only moves rigidly in \(y\) and the horizontal lines stay straight. Second: only two strains are left, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \kappa y\) and the shear \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \kappa x\), largest on the side \(x = a\). Third: we integrate the energy density over the cell and divide by the true energy:

\[\frac{U_{\mathrm{h}}}{U} = \frac{1}{1 - \nu^2} + \frac{a^2}{2\,(1 + \nu)\,b^2} .\]

The first part, \(1.099\), comes from the quadrilateral not being able to contract sideways. The second part is the parasitic shear, and it grows with the square of the side ratio \(a/b\): \(0.385\) at \(a/b = 1\), \(2.404\) at \(2.5\), as drawn, and \(9.615\) at \(5\). A cantilever cell \(100 \times 20\) mm has \(a/b = 5\). For it the shear part is the long orange bar, in total \(10.7\) times too much energy. In the book: 15.7.3, lemma 15.50.

3.4 How do we fix it, and how do we know the fix has not broken the element?

How do we teach the quadrilateral to curve its edges, and how do we then check that we have not spoiled it? The fix suggests itself: we give the element the two missing shapes, \(1 - \xi^2\) and \(1 - \eta^2\), which can curve the edges. That is the element Q6 with incompatible modes (nekompatibilni načini), equation (15.41). On a cell in bending its energy ratio is exactly \(1\), at \(a/b = 1\), \(2.5\) and \(5\). Both shapes are zero at the corners, but not along the edges. Are we allowed to do that? The patch test (preizkus z zaplato) gives the answer.

Let us draw a rectangle \(240 \times 120\) mm and cut it into five deliberately distorted quadrilaterals. We number the nodes: four on the boundary and four inside. A patch is like a piece of cloth that covers a small part of a surface. At the boundary nodes we prescribe the displacements of the field \(u = 10^{-3}(x + y/2)\), \(v = 10^{-3}(x/2 + y)\), which has the same strains, \(10^{-3}\), everywhere. There are no forces. Where do the inner nodes go? If the element can do constant strain, exactly onto the true field. Q4 can: the orange mesh lies on the true field with an error of \(2.7 \cdot 10^{-16}\), which is the computer's rounding.

Why is constant strain exactly the right test? In the first part of the lecture we saw that the strain changes less over a shorter cell of the cantilever, four times less over a cell four times shorter. On ever smaller cells every smooth field is almost constant. An element that cannot even do constant strain, such as the patch has, therefore converges to the wrong answer. Careful, the test is a gate, not a guarantee. It guarantees convergence only for stable elements, which have no false modes without energy, as COOK writes. Q4 with a single Gauss point passes the patch, with an error of \(5.6 \cdot 10^{-16}\), but it has the false hourglass mode from the previous part. And Q4 with the full rule passes the test and is still only \(0.292\) of the truth in bending.

Now the same patch with the Q6 element. The true field is blue. Each Q6 element curves its edges in its own way. Let us look at the neighbours \(e_1\) and \(e_2\) and their common edge \(2\)-\(6\). They match at the nodes, but not in between: the difference is a parabola with its peak at \(13.4\) µm, while the largest displacement in the patch is \(300\) µm. A gap opens between the two elements, and Q6 fails the test on distorted quadrilaterals. The fix QM6 takes the Jacobian matrix from the element centre in the extra shapes and passes the test. On the cantilever it gives \(0.998\) of the true deflection with \(60\) unknowns, while Q4 with \(720\) unknowns still gives only \(0.868\). In the book: 15.7.1, 15.7.2 (theorem 15.48), 15.7.4 (theorem 15.55).

3.5 Why does an almost incompressible material lock?

A different story, the same phenomenon. Why does a mesh that is good to half a percent for steel lose more than four fifths of the displacement for rubber?

Let us draw a quarter of the tube with a \(4 \times 8\) mesh, inner radius \(a = 40\) mm and outer radius \(b = 60\) mm. Supports on both axes replace the other three quarters. From inside the pressure \(p = 30\) MPa pushes. We know the displacement of the inner edge from Lamé's solution. Now we keep the modulus \(E\) and only raise Poisson's ratio towards \(\tfrac12\), where the material does not change its volume. What does the mesh do?

Let us draw the axes: horizontally Poisson's ratio, vertically the displacement ratio, one means exact. Q4 with the full rule gives the ratio \(0.9945\) at \(\nu = 0.3\), \(0.9872\) at \(0.45\), \(0.9521\) at \(0.49\), \(0.6802\) at \(0.499\) and only \(0.1765\) at \(0.4999\). Meanwhile the true displacement of the inner edge has changed by a mere \(2\) %, from \(16.873\) to \(16.531\) µm. The mesh locks (zaklepanje, locking), the way a door locks. Why?

The energy density has two parts, \(w = \tfrac12 K\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}^2 + G\sum e_{\mathrm{ij}}^2\): the first pays for a change of volume, the second for a change of shape. As \(\nu\) goes to \(\tfrac12\), \(K\) goes to infinity while \(G\) stays bounded. The ratio \(K/G\) is \(2.17\) at the left end of the axis, at \(\nu = 0.3\), and \(4999.7\) at the right end, at \(0.4999\). Here we are at the ground again. Let us take a rubber stopper and squeeze it. The stopper gets lower and bulges sideways: it changes its shape easily and its volume hardly at all. The true field of the tube escapes the price by hardly changing its volume, the divergence is \(1.27 \cdot 10^{-4}\) at \(0.3\) and \(7.35 \cdot 10^{-8}\) at \(0.4999\). The element must do the same, and at every Gauss point. If it cannot, it pays \(K\) times the square of the divergence, and the solid orange line drops. Can it?

Let us look at one element. We bend an element, \(2a\) wide and \(2b\) high, with the field \(u = \kappa x y\): the top edge stretches, the bottom one shortens, we get a trapezoid. Since the vertical displacement is again constant, the element cannot contract sideways where it stretches lengthwise, so the volumetric strain is \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} = \kappa y\). It is zero only on the midline. At the four Gauss points it is \(\pm 0.58\,\kappa b\), plus at the top, minus at the bottom. Each of these four points pays \(K\) times the square. The only way for the element to pay nothing is not to bend. Only at the centre would the price be zero. In the book: 15.8, 15.8.1, 15.8.2, 15.8.3 (theorem 15.57), 15.8.4.

3.6 Why does one point less help?

The mesh is locked, we take away three of its four Gauss points, and it unlocks. How can less computing give a better result? Let us count. We draw a large piece of mesh, in it one element with nodes at its corners. Neighbours share nodes, so each element gets one new node with two unknowns, \(u\) and \(v\). With the full rule the element has four Gauss points, at each the constraint "no volume change here". Two unknowns against four constraints: nothing is left free. With a single point there is one constraint, two against one, and half of the freedom remains. At the ground is counting: more independent conditions than unknowns leave only zero.

For the tube we count exactly. The \(4 \times 8\) mesh has \(80\) free unknowns. The full rule gives \(128\) constraints, \(72\) of them independent, \(8\) free fields remain. One point gives \(32\) constraints, \(48\) fields remain. That is what selective integration (izbirna integracija) does: the volume part with one point, the shape part with the full rule, so that the hourglass from the previous part does not appear. The result is the orange dashed line, which stays at \(0.9955\) at \(\nu = 0.4999\). The same holds for the eight-node element Q8: with the full \(3 \times 3\) rule it drops more slowly, to \(0.8908\), with the \(2 \times 2\) rule it does not drop at all. The idea of the proof for a rectangular mesh in one sentence: zero divergence at four points of a rectangle forces the field to be affine on each element, and the clamped edges then force it to zero. In the book: 15.8.5 (theorem 15.58), 15.8.6.

3.7 Back to the top

Let us build it back up. The mesh picks the field with the least energy. If the element adds to the true shape a strain the true field does not have, the shear at the corner in bending or the volume change at the Gauss points in rubber, that shape becomes too expensive and the displacement too small. The cure is always the same: give the element the missing shape (Q6, QM6, Q8) or stop asking for the impossible at too many points (selective integration). Every cure must pass through the gate, the patch. Counting predicts the outcome before we compute. In the first picture let us replace the word shear by the words volume change, and the same picture explains locking. The same thing happened with the hanging rod and the Timoshenko beam. In the book: 15.8.8.

3.8 Four perspectives

By eye, the few-minute explanation. Let us take a ruler and bend it between our fingers. Its edges curve, and the cross lines on it stay perpendicular to the edges. Now let us take a deck of cards and push its top sideways. The cards stay straight, the sides of the deck tilt, and the right angle changes by the angle \(\gamma\). A thing with straight edges can follow bending only by sliding like a deck of cards. The quadrilateral is a deck of cards: straight horizontal lines against curved ones, and the slide is the parasitic shear.

By numbers: the ladder \(0.115\), \(0.292\), \(0.998\) for the cantilever and \(0.1765\) against \(0.9955\) for the tube. This view adds how much each cure is worth.

By energy: \(8.8\) units for the cell of triangles and \(10.7\) for the Q4 cell, \(1.099\) plus \(9.615\). This view adds the cause: almost all of the excess is shear.

By counting: \(2 : 4\) against \(2 : 1\). This view adds a prediction without computing.

Historically, only what the book reports after COOK: the patch test was conceived by Irons, and it answers the question whether the sequence of solutions on ever finer meshes converges to the true solution.

3.9 Assumptions

Pure bending as the test field, \(u = \kappa x y\), \(v = -\kappa(x^2 + \nu y^2)/2\) on the cell. Why it is right: it is the exact solution without shear, so any shear in the element is entirely false. When not: a cantilever with an end force also has true shear, so the formula does not predict the number \(0.292\). Effect: the formula shows the cause and the size of the error, the number from the mesh is what counts.

Plane stress, steel with \(E = 196\) GPa and \(\nu = 0.3\), small strains, in the bending part. When this changes: for the tube below, where plane strain prevents stretching into the thickness. The effect of Poisson's ratio is the first part of the formula, \(1.099\), so \(9.9\) % too much stiffness.

Plane strain in the tube. Why it is essential: in plane stress the material escapes into the thickness, the plate thins by \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = -1.2242 \cdot 10^{-4}\), the bulk modulus stays finite and the mesh does not lock, Q4 with the full rule gives the ratio \(0.9940\) at \(\nu = 0.4999\). Locking is therefore a matter of plane strain, axisymmetry and three dimensions. In the book: 15.8.7.

Counting in a large mesh, one node per element, \(2\) unknowns. Why it is right: neighbours share nodes. When not: small meshes and boundary conditions change the count, the exact dimensions are in a table in the book (Q4 with the full rule, \(0\) free fields at \(n = 2\), \(4\) and \(8\)). Effect: counting is a prediction, the theorem is the proof for rectangles.
