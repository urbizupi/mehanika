The whole chapter on one page (loads more slowly)

In the first three chapters we gathered everything we need to compute a body that we load. Chapter 1 gave the strains from the displacements of the points, chapter 2 the forces inside a body, the stresses and the conditions of equilibrium, and chapter 3 the relation between stresses and strains for a given material. For the hanging bar these three parts took us from the weight to the displacement of every point. From the equilibrium of the part of the bar below a cross section we got the axial force, from it the stress, with Young's modulus the strain, and with the integral the displacement. The route went through every cross section separately.

For a structure made of several parts such a route is long. Take a bracket fixed to a wall by two bars and loaded with a weight at its end. From equilibrium we would get the forces in the bars, from the forces the elongations of the bars, and then we would have to find by geometry where the end of the bracket moves so that both bars stretch or shorten by exactly these amounts at the same time. This chapter shows another route. It does not follow the forces from part to part, it counts work. Forces that load a body slowly do work. An elastic material stores this work as energy and gives it back on unloading, as a stretched spring gives it back. If we can express the energy through the displacements, the equality of work and energy gives us a displacement without geometry.

The same idea also gives a new form of equilibrium. So far we have demanded that the sum of the forces on every small piece of a body is zero. We can also demand something else: that for every imagined small displacement that the supports allow, the work of the external forces equals the work of the stresses inside the body. We will see that the two demands are equivalent. The second has the advantage that it can also be met only approximately, with displacements of simple shapes. On this rest the approximate methods of chapter 6 and the finite element method (metoda končnih elementov), with which computer programs compute structures (chapter 7).

The route is as follows. In the first section we say how work is counted when a force does not point in the direction of motion or changes during the motion, and we compute the energy stored by a bar, by the hanging bar and by structures of bars joined by pins. For a body that is not a bar we must be able to add up quantities over the whole volume and over the surface, so a mathematical section on the integral over a body follows. Then we write down the new form of equilibrium, first for a rigid body, then for a bar and a structure of bars, and then for a body of any shape. From it we get that among all shapes that the supports allow, a body takes the one at which the stored energy, decreased by the products of the loads with the displacements of their points of application, is smallest, and that a force is the derivative of the stored energy with respect to a displacement. Then we exchange the roles of forces and displacements. Imagined forces in equilibrium give the displacement of a chosen point, and the energy written with forces gives the forces in a structure in which equilibrium alone does not determine them. At the end we show that the effects of loads add up, and we prove that the displacement at one point due to a force at a second point equals the displacement at the second point due to the same force at the first.
