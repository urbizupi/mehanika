Chapter 15 Continuum finite elements

15.3 Heat conduction in the plane with triangles

In sections 15.1 and 15.2 we cut a plate into triangles and wrote the field on each one as \(T_{\mathrm{h}} = T_1 L_1 + T_2 L_2 + T_3 L_3\), with three nodal values \(T_{\mathrm{i}}\) and area coordinates \(L_{\mathrm{i}}\) (definition 15.3). We know that such a field is continuous across common edges (lemma 15.7), that its gradient is constant on each triangle (lemma 15.8), and that in elasticity the theorems of the Ritz method from chapter 6 hold for such families (corollary 15.16). What remains is the question of how to get equations from the nodal values. For the bar we did this in chapter 7 with an element matrix and assembly. Here we repeat the same path on a plane temperature problem, because each node then has only one unknown, the temperature. For displacements it will have two, and the element matrices will be twice as large, but the idea will be the same (next section).

We set the problem up as follows. The plate has constant thickness \(t\) (in metres), its two faces are thermally insulated, and the temperature does not vary through the thickness, so \(T\) is a function of two coordinates \((x, y)\) (a plane model. With insulated faces and with a source and boundary conditions that do not vary through the thickness, it is exact. If the faces exchange heat with a coefficient \(h_{\mathrm{f}}\), the temperature varies through the thickness by the fraction \(h_{\mathrm{f}}\,t/(4k)\) of the difference between face and surroundings, for example for \(t = 10\) mm, \(h_{\mathrm{f}} = 25\) W/(m² K) and \(k = 50\) W/(m K) by \(0{,}125\) %). The material is isotropic with constant conductivity \(k\) (Fourier's law in chapter 14), and the heat flux is \(\vek q = -k\,\nabla T\). Constant \(k\) is an assumption: it holds as long as the temperature differences in the plate are so small that the conductivity of the material does not change noticeably between the coldest and the hottest place. Because of it the matrix below will not depend on the temperatures and the equations will be linear. Where \(k\) changes with temperature, the matrix would depend on the solution, and we do not treat that here. The problem is steady, so the temperature does not change with time. This holds when a few units \(a^2/\kappa\) have passed since the last change of the boundary conditions and the sources (Fourier number, chapter 14: at \(\mathrm{Fo} = 1\) the first mode of the bar keeps only \(5{,}2\cdot 10^{-5}\) of its initial height), and it removes all memory of the initial state from the problem, so there is no time in the functional. By the weak form of chapter 14 (the functional is also COOK (12.2-1), PDF p. 473) the temperature of a plate with boundary \(\Gamma\) (lemma 14.52 under the conditions of lemma 14.51: the plate is connected, \(h > 0\) on \(\Gamma_{\mathrm{h}}\), and \(\Gamma_{\mathrm{T}}\) or \(\Gamma_{\mathrm{h}}\) has positive length) makes the functional

\[\Pi(T) = \tfrac12 \int_\Omega k\,|\nabla T|^2\,t\,\mathrm{d}A + \tfrac12 \int_{\Gamma_\mathrm{h}} h\,T^2\,t\,\mathrm{d}s - \int_\Omega Q\,T\,t\,\mathrm{d}A - \int_{\Gamma_\mathrm{h}} h\,T_\infty\,T\,t\,\mathrm{d}s - \int_{\Gamma_\mathrm{q}} \bar q\,T\,t\,\mathrm{d}s , \tag{15.14}\]

a minimum among the temperatures that take the prescribed values on the part \(\Gamma_{\mathrm{T}}\) of the boundary. Here \(Q\) is the power of a heat source per unit volume (W/m³), \(\Gamma_{\mathrm{h}}\) is the part of the boundary where the plate exchanges heat with surroundings at temperature \(T_\infty\) by Newton's law with heat transfer coefficient \(h\), and \(\bar q\) is the heat flux per unit area of the boundary \(\Gamma_{\mathrm{q}}\) flowing into the plate (W/m²). The integral over the planar set \(\Omega\) is an integral over a set with area (definition 4.30), the boundary integrals are integrals over segments and polygonal lines, which we will evaluate piece by piece.

What is new in this section is only how we get a matrix from a triangle and how we treat thermal boundary conditions. Assembly and the reduced system are from chapter 7. Some letters meet letters of other chapters here. \(L_{\mathrm{i}}\) is an area coordinate and not a length, \(k\) is the conductivity (chapter 14), and the element stiffness matrix always carries its subscript, \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\). \(\mat B\) is here a gradient matrix (without arguments), while the bilinear form \(B(\cdot, \cdot)\) of chapter 6 always has two arguments. \(t\) is the plate thickness (the time \(t\) of chapter 14 does not occur in the steady problem), \(A\) is the area of a triangle and not the cross-section of the bar of chapter 7, and \(h\) is the heat transfer coefficient and not an element length.

15.3.1 The matrix of a triangle

First one element. If we insert \(T_{\mathrm{h}}\) in the first term of the functional (15.14), we get a sum of integrals over the triangles. For a field that is smooth only piecewise, the first term is by definition this sum (like the energy in equation (15.12)), and for a smooth field it equals the integral over \(\Omega\) (corollary 4.68, item 1, theorem 15.11). The integrand is the square of the gradient, which is constant on a triangle, so the integral will be the value times the area. We therefore have to write the gradient with the nodal coordinates. This is the first thing we draw: the triangle with nodes \(1\), \(2\), \(3\) at the points \((0, 0)\), \((40, 0)\) and \((10, 30)\) mm, which will be our example. From node \(1\) we drop the height onto the opposite side, and likewise from nodes \(2\) and \(3\). The area coordinate \(L_{\mathrm{i}}\) is zero on the opposite side and one at node \(i\), so it grows uniformly along the height. Its gradient therefore points along the height towards node \(i\) and has length one over the height. From node \(1\) the height is \(h_1 = 28{,}3\) mm, so \(|\nabla L_1| = 1/28{,}3 = 0{,}0354\) per mm. It is drawn as the arrow \(\nabla L_1\) from node \(1\), continuing the height outwards, and likewise \(\nabla L_2\) and \(\nabla L_3\).

From this description we read the gradient with the vertex coordinates. Let \(b_1 = y_2 - y_3\), \(b_2 = y_3 - y_1\), \(b_3 = y_1 - y_2\) and \(c_1 = x_3 - x_2\), \(c_2 = x_1 - x_3\), \(c_3 = x_2 - x_1\). For nodes numbered counterclockwise, \(\nabla L_{\mathrm{i}} = (b_{\mathrm{i}}, c_{\mathrm{i}})/(2A)\). Before we prove this, let us ask what an element matrix is. As for the bar (lemma 7.5), it is the matrix that turns the nodal temperatures \(\vek T_{\mathrm{e}} = (T_1, T_2, T_3)^\T\) into nodal heats \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek T_{\mathrm{e}}\), and the energy of the element is \(\tfrac12\,\vek T_{\mathrm{e}}^\T \mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek T_{\mathrm{e}}\). We call it the conductivity matrix (prevodnostna matrika), because it relates temperatures and heats the way the stiffness matrix relates displacements and forces (COOK, PDF p. 474, table of names: the conductivity matrix corresponds to the conventional stiffness matrix). The heat at the nodes is shown by the figure with the acute triangle.

Lemma 15.17 Conductivity matrix of the triangle (prevodnostna matrika trikotnika)

Let a linear triangle (definition 15.5) have vertices \(P_{\mathrm{i}} = (x_{\mathrm{i}}, y_{\mathrm{i}})\) numbered counterclockwise, area \(A > 0\), thickness \(t > 0\) and conductivity \(k > 0\), all constant. Let \(T_{\mathrm{h}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} T_{\mathrm{i}} L_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(\vek T_{\mathrm{e}} = (T_1, T_2, T_3)^\T\). Let \(b_{\mathrm{i}}\), \(c_{\mathrm{i}}\) be as above, indices cyclic. Then:

  1. \(\nabla L_{\mathrm{i}} = (b_{\mathrm{i}}, c_{\mathrm{i}})/(2A)\) and \(\nabla T_{\mathrm{h}} = \mat B\,\vek T_{\mathrm{e}}\) with the matrix \(\mat B = \frac{1}{2A}\begin{pmatrix} b_1 & b_2 & b_3 \\ c_1 & c_2 & c_3 \end{pmatrix}\). In the figure these are the gradients on the triangle.
  2. The matrix \((\mat k_{\mathrm{e}})_{\mathrm{ij}} = \int_{\text{triangle}} k\,\nabla L_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot\nabla L_{\mathrm{j}}\,t\,\mathrm{d}A\) is \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}} = k\,t\,A\,\mat B^\T \mat B\), that is, \((\mat k_{\mathrm{e}})_{\mathrm{ij}} = \dfrac{k\,t}{4A}\,(b_{\mathrm{i}} b_{\mathrm{j}} + c_{\mathrm{i}} c_{\mathrm{j}})\), and \(\vek T_{\mathrm{e}}^\T \mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek T_{\mathrm{e}} = \int k\,|\nabla T_{\mathrm{h}}|^2\,t\,\mathrm{d}A\). The integral is over the triangle.
  3. \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) is symmetric, \(\vek T_{\mathrm{e}}^\T \mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek T_{\mathrm{e}} \ge 0\), and \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek T_{\mathrm{e}} = \vek 0\) exactly for \(\vek T_{\mathrm{e}} = c\,(1, 1, 1)^\T\). The quadratic form measures the gradient of the field on the triangle.
  4. The sum of each row of \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) is zero. The gradients of the three area coordinates add up to zero.
  5. Let \(\theta_{\mathrm{k}}\) be the angle of the triangle at vertex \(P_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(i, j, k\) three different numbers. Then \((\mat k_{\mathrm{e}})_{\mathrm{ij}} = -\tfrac12\,k\,t\,\cot\theta_{\mathrm{k}}\). For a triangle with all angles at most \(90^\circ\) all off-diagonal entries are \(\le 0\). The angle \(\theta_{\mathrm{k}}\) is an angle of the triangle.
Proof

We have to show the formula for the gradient and its length from the height, that the integral of a constant integrand is the value times the area, and then the properties of the matrix.

  1. By lemma 15.4, item 1, \(L_{\mathrm{i}} = (a_{\mathrm{i}} + b_{\mathrm{i}} x + c_{\mathrm{i}} y)/(2A)\), so \(\nabla L_{\mathrm{i}} = (b_{\mathrm{i}}, c_{\mathrm{i}})/(2A)\). By lemma 15.8, item 1, the field \(T_{\mathrm{h}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} T_{\mathrm{i}} L_{\mathrm{i}}\) has gradient \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} T_{\mathrm{i}} \nabla L_{\mathrm{i}} = \mat B\,\vek T_{\mathrm{e}}\). This is item 1. In the figure the gradient is an arrow in the triangle.
  2. The height tells how long this gradient is. By lemma 15.4, item 2, \(L_{\mathrm{i}}\) is \(0\) at both other vertices, hence on the whole opposite side (an affine function that is zero at two points is zero on the line through them). The derivative along the side is therefore zero and the gradient is perpendicular to it. Let \(\ell_{\mathrm{i}}\) be the length of the opposite side, from \(P_{\mathrm{j}}\) to \(P_{\mathrm{k}}\) (\(j, k\) cyclic after \(i\)). The vector \((b_{\mathrm{i}}, c_{\mathrm{i}}) = (y_{\mathrm{j}} - y_{\mathrm{k}},\ x_{\mathrm{k}} - x_{\mathrm{j}})\) has the same length as the side vector \((x_{\mathrm{k}} - x_{\mathrm{j}},\ y_{\mathrm{k}} - y_{\mathrm{j}})\), because it has the same two components in swapped order and with one sign changed. The area is \(A = \tfrac12\,\ell_{\mathrm{i}}\,h_{\mathrm{i}}\), where \(h_{\mathrm{i}}\) is the height from \(P_{\mathrm{i}}\) to this side. Hence \(|\nabla L_{\mathrm{i}}| = \ell_{\mathrm{i}}/(2A) = 1/h_{\mathrm{i}}\). The gradient points towards \(P_{\mathrm{i}}\), because \(L_{\mathrm{i}}\) grows from \(0\) to \(1\) from the side to \(P_{\mathrm{i}}\).
  3. The integrand \(k\,\nabla L_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot\nabla L_{\mathrm{j}}\,t\) is constant on the triangle, so its integral is the value times the area of the triangle (definition 4.30, the sum of value times cell area for a constant function is the value times the sum of the cell areas). The first part of item 2 is \(k\,t\,A\,(\mat B^\T \mat B)_{\mathrm{ij}}\), which by the rule for the matrix product (definition 1.99) is \(\frac{k\,t\,A}{4A^2}\,(b_{\mathrm{i}} b_{\mathrm{j}} + c_{\mathrm{i}} c_{\mathrm{j}})\). Likewise \(\vek T_{\mathrm{e}}^\T \mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek T_{\mathrm{e}} = k\,t\,A\,|\mat B\,\vek T_{\mathrm{e}}|^2 = \int k\,|\nabla T_{\mathrm{h}}|^2\,t\,\mathrm{d}A\). The constancy holds on the whole triangle.
  4. Symmetry follows from the form \(b_{\mathrm{i}} b_{\mathrm{j}} + c_{\mathrm{i}} c_{\mathrm{j}}\). The quadratic form is \(k\,t\,A\,|\mat B\,\vek T_{\mathrm{e}}|^2 \ge 0\) and is zero exactly when \(\mat B\,\vek T_{\mathrm{e}} = \vek 0\), that is, when \(\nabla T_{\mathrm{h}} = \vek 0\). An affine field with zero gradient is constant, so all three nodal values are equal. If \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek T_{\mathrm{e}} = \vek 0\), then \(\vek T_{\mathrm{e}}^\T \mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek T_{\mathrm{e}} = 0\) too, so \(\vek T_{\mathrm{e}} = c\,(1, 1, 1)^\T\). Conversely, a constant field has zero gradient, so \(\mat B\,(1, 1, 1)^\T = \vek 0\) and \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,(1, 1, 1)^\T = \vek 0\). Item 3 is proved. A field with zero gradient has all node values equal.
  5. By lemma 15.4, \(L_1 + L_2 + L_3 = 1\), so \(\nabla L_1 + \nabla L_2 + \nabla L_3 = \vek 0\) (the three arrows in the figure add up to zero) and \(\mat B\,(1, 1, 1)^\T = \vek 0\), hence \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,(1, 1, 1)^\T = \vek 0\). This is item 4.
  6. The gradients \(\nabla L_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(\nabla L_{\mathrm{j}}\) are perpendicular to the sides opposite \(P_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(P_{\mathrm{j}}\) and point into the interior. The sides meet at \(P_{\mathrm{k}}\) at angle \(\theta_{\mathrm{k}}\), so the angle between the inward perpendiculars is \(180^\circ - \theta_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(\nabla L_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot\nabla L_{\mathrm{j}} = -\cos\theta_{\mathrm{k}}/(h_{\mathrm{i}} h_{\mathrm{j}})\). The height from \(P_{\mathrm{i}}\) to the line \(P_{\mathrm{k}} P_{\mathrm{j}}\) is \(h_{\mathrm{i}} = |P_{\mathrm{i}} P_{\mathrm{k}}|\,\sin\theta_{\mathrm{k}}\), likewise \(h_{\mathrm{j}} = |P_{\mathrm{j}} P_{\mathrm{k}}|\,\sin\theta_{\mathrm{k}}\), and the area is \(A = \tfrac12\,|P_{\mathrm{i}} P_{\mathrm{k}}|\,|P_{\mathrm{j}} P_{\mathrm{k}}|\,\sin\theta_{\mathrm{k}}\). So \((\mat k_{\mathrm{e}})_{\mathrm{ij}} = k\,t\,A\,\nabla L_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot\nabla L_{\mathrm{j}} = -\tfrac12\,k\,t\,\cos\theta_{\mathrm{k}}/\sin\theta_{\mathrm{k}}\). For \(\theta_{\mathrm{k}} \le 90^\circ\) the cotangent is nonnegative.

Source: The formula of item 2 is COOK (12.2-4), \([\mathbf{k}] = \int [\mathbf{B}]^\T[\boldsymbol\kappa][\mathbf{B}]\,\mathrm{d}V\) (PDF p. 473). The coefficients \(b_{\mathrm{i}} = y_{\mathrm{j}} - y_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(c_{\mathrm{i}} = x_{\mathrm{k}} - x_{\mathrm{j}}\) are in the shape functions of the triangle \(N_{\mathrm{i}} = (a_{\mathrm{i}} + b_{\mathrm{i}} x + c_{\mathrm{i}} y)/(2A)\), ONATE1 (4.32b) and (4.33) (PDF p. 158). Items 3 and 4 are proved after the pattern of lemma 7.6. Item 5 is not from the sources.

Let us test the lemma on the triangle of the figure. Take \(k\,t = 1\) W/K (so the entries of the matrix are in W/K). The area is \(A = 600\) mm². The numbers \(b_{\mathrm{i}}\) are \((-30, 30, 0)\) mm, the numbers \(c_{\mathrm{i}}\) are \((-30, -10, 40)\) mm, and the entry \((1,1)\) is \((900 + 900)/2400 = 0{,}75\). The whole matrix (SymPy and NumPy in outlines/ch-mke-kontinuum/num-03.py) is

\[\mat k_{\mathrm{e}} = \begin{pmatrix} 0{,}75 & -0{,}25 & -0{,}5 \\ -0{,}25 & 0{,}41667 & -0{,}16667 \\ -0{,}5 & -0{,}16667 & 0{,}66667 \end{pmatrix}\ \text{W/K} , \tag{15.15}\]

and the row sums are zero. Item 5 of the lemma checks it from the other end. The angles of the triangle are \(71{,}57^\circ\), \(45^\circ\) and \(63{,}43^\circ\), their cotangents \(1/3\), \(1\) and \(1/2\). So \((\mat k_{\mathrm{e}})_{23} = -\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac13 = -0{,}16667\) (the angle at \(P_1\) lies opposite the side \(P_2 P_3\)), \((\mat k_{\mathrm{e}})_{13} = -0{,}5\) (the angle \(45^\circ\) at \(P_2\)) and \((\mat k_{\mathrm{e}})_{12} = -0{,}25\) (the angle at \(P_3\)). All angles are acute and all off-diagonal entries are negative, as item 5 claims. Now the field: for \(T = 20 + 0{,}5\,x - 0{,}2\,y\) the nodal values are \((20, 40, 19)\) K and the lemma gives \(\mat B\,\vek T_{\mathrm{e}} = (0{,}5, -0{,}2)\) K/mm, which is exactly the gradient of the field. This is the right half of the figure: the isotherms are lines perpendicular to the gradient \(\nabla T\), and the flux \(\vek q = -k\,\nabla T\) points from hot to cold, opposite to the gradient.

The matrix has a physical meaning. If the nodal values are \((1, 0, 0)\) K, the triangle needs \(0{,}75\) W supplied at node \(1\) and \(0{,}25\) and \(0{,}5\) W taken out at nodes \(2\) and \(3\): the column \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek T_{\mathrm{e}} = (0{,}75, -0{,}25, -0{,}5)\) W, with sum zero. Heat flows from the hot node \(1\) to the cooler ones, as it must. The negative off-diagonal entries are the path of heat from one node to another.

Item 5 has a condition on the angles. We test it on a triangle where it fails. Take a triangle whose angle at node \(3\) is obtuse: vertices \((0, 0)\), \((40, 0)\), \((20, 5)\) mm, the angle at \(P_3\) is \(151{,}9^\circ\), the cotangent \(-1{,}875\). The same computation gives

\[\mat k_{\mathrm{e}} = \begin{pmatrix} 1{,}0625 & 0{,}9375 & -2 \\ 0{,}9375 & 1{,}0625 & -2 \\ -2 & -2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}\ \text{W/K} , \qquad \mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,(1, 0, 0)^\T = (1{,}0625,\ 0{,}9375,\ -2)^\T\ \text{W} ,\]

the rows again have sum zero, but the entry \((1,2) = +0{,}9375\) is no longer negative. In the figure there is the acute triangle with the heats \(F_{\mathrm{i}}\) that the surroundings supply (arrow into the node) or take away (arrow out of it), and the obtuse triangle with its obtuse angle \(\theta_3\). In the acute triangle the surroundings take heat out of the cold node \(2\), in the obtuse one they supply it, although node \(2\) is colder than node \(1\). The matrix is still symmetric with a nonnegative quadratic form, because items 3 and 4 do not need angles, but item 5 does. What this means for the solution on a whole mesh we do not prove here. In the section on the plate below we will see that the proof of the sign of the error uses exactly the negativity of the off-diagonal entries.

A triangle with area \(A = 0\) (three points on a line) is not covered by the lemma: \(\mat B\) is not defined and the coordinates \(L_{\mathrm{i}}\) do not exist (lemma 15.4 requires a triangle with area). For a very narrow triangle the entries of the matrix are large: by items 4 and 5 of the lemma, \((\mat k_{\mathrm{e}})_{\mathrm{ii}} = \tfrac12\,k\,t\,(\cot\theta_{\mathrm{j}} + \cot\theta_{\mathrm{k}})\), which grows without bound as the angle \(\theta_{\mathrm{j}}\) or \(\theta_{\mathrm{k}}\) tends to \(0\). A small area alone does not cause it, because \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) does not change when the triangle is scaled. The obtuse triangle above already shows it: on the same base of \(40\) mm it has area \(100\) mm² instead of \(600\) mm² and the entry \((3,3)\) equal to \(4\) W/K instead of \(0{,}66667\) W/K, six times larger, because the angle at the base is \(14{,}04^\circ\) with cotangent \(4\) and \((3,3) = \tfrac12(4 + 4)\). Narrow triangles are therefore unfavourable.

15.3.2 Assembly and prescribed temperatures

Now we have one element. Plates of several elements are assembled by theorem 7.13 of chapter 7. Items 1 and 2 of the theorem do not require the element to be a bar: they hold for every structure with elements that have a table of connections and an element matrix. We test them on a plate of two triangles. The rectangle \(60 \times 30\) mm is cut by the diagonal \(1\)-\(3\) into element \(e = 1\) with nodes \((1, 2, 3)\) and element \(e = 2\) with nodes \((1, 3, 4)\). On the right the elements are pulled apart and next to each node the local number is in brackets: the table of connections is \(g_1 = (1, 2, 3)\) and \(g_2 = (1, 3, 4)\) (definition 7.10). Nodes \(1\) and \(3\) belong to both elements.

Both triangles have area \(900\) mm² and differ only in the ordering of the nodes. With lemma 15.17 and \(k\,t = 1\) W/K the matrices \(\mat k_1\) and \(\mat k_2\) (SymPy, outlines/ch-mke-kontinuum/num-03.py) have rows \((0{,}25, -0{,}25, 0)\), \((-0{,}25, 1{,}25, -1)\), \((0, -1, 1)\) and \((1, 0, -1)\), \((0, 0{,}25, -0{,}25)\), \((-1, -0{,}25, 1{,}25)\). By equation (7.12),

\[\mat K = \begin{pmatrix} 1{,}25 & -0{,}25 & 0 & -1 \\ -0{,}25 & 1{,}25 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 & 1{,}25 & -0{,}25 \\ -1 & 0 & -0{,}25 & 1{,}25 \end{pmatrix}\ \text{W/K} . \tag{15.16}\]

The entry \(K_{11} = 0{,}25 + 1 = 1{,}25\) is the sum of the contributions of both elements. The entry \(K_{13} = 0 + 0 = 0\) is zero, although the diagonal joins nodes \(1\) and \(3\) and is a side of both triangles. The angles opposite it, at nodes \(2\) and \(4\), are right angles, so by item 5 of lemma 15.17 the contribution of each element is \(-\tfrac12\,k\,t\,\cot 90^\circ = 0\). Nodes \(2\) and \(4\), on the other hand, have no common element, so \(K_{24} = 0\) already by assembly. The row sums are zero, which holds for each element by lemma 15.17 and therefore for the sum by theorem 7.13.

Row \(I\) of the equation \(\mat K\,\vek T = \vek f\) has by item 1 of theorem 7.13 a physical meaning. The sum \(\sum_{\mathrm{e}} (\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek T_{\mathrm{e}})_{\mathrm{i}}\) is the sum of the heats that the elements around node \(I\) take out of it (at node \(1\) these are both elements, at node \(2\) only element \(1\), and by the lemma this is the heat that must be supplied to the element to hold its nodal temperatures), and \(f_{\mathrm{I}}\) is the heat supplied to the node by the surroundings. So the equation says that in the steady state all the heat supplied to a node is carried away through the elements. At an interior node of a plate without a source \(f_{\mathrm{I}} = 0\) and the sum of the heats taken out is zero.

The energy of the field \(T_{\mathrm{h}}\) over the whole plate is by item 2 of theorem 7.13 \(\tfrac12\,\vek T^\T \mat K\,\vek T = \sum_{\mathrm{e}} \tfrac12\,\vek T_{\mathrm{e}}^\T \mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek T_{\mathrm{e}}\), and each term is by lemma 15.17 item 2 the first term of the functional (15.14) for that element. On the fields \(T_{\mathrm{h}}\) the functional is therefore a quadratic function of the nodal values, \(\tfrac12\,\vek T^\T \mat K\,\vek T - \vek f^\T \vek T\), where \(\mat K\) also receives the convection terms of the sides on \(\Gamma_{\mathrm{h}}\) (lemma 15.19, item 2), and \(\vek f\) collects the terms that are linear in \(\vek T\) (the boundary terms are computed below, in the subsection on heat flux and convection on the boundary). The Ritz solution (definition 6.32) is the field \(T_{\mathrm{h}}\) with the smallest value of the functional among the fields with the prescribed nodal values. As in step 4 of the proof of theorem 6.33, it is the solution of the system \(\mat K\,\vek T = \vek f\) in the rows of the free nodes (theorem 7.18), if the matrix of the free nodes is positive definite (lemma 6.30). Corollary 15.16 on the energy and the best approximation was proved for elasticity. For temperature we do not need it in this section and do not claim it. When this system has a solution is the question we have to settle. The sum runs over both triangles.

Lemma 15.18 Unique solution of the discrete heat problem (enolična rešitev toplotnega problema)

Let a mesh of linear triangles be assembled by theorem 7.13 with the matrices of lemma 15.17, and let it be connected: for any two nodes there is a sequence of nodes in which consecutive nodes lie in a common element. At at least one node the temperature is prescribed. Then the matrix \(\mat K_{\mathrm{ff}}\) of the free nodes is positive definite and the system \(\mat K_{\mathrm{ff}}\,\vek T_{\mathrm{f}} = \vek f_{\mathrm{f}} - \mat K_{\mathrm{fp}}\,\vek T_{\mathrm{p}}\) has exactly one solution. An example of connectedness are the separated elements, which share nodes \(1\) and \(3\).

Proof

We have to show that the condition of theorem 7.18 holds, namely that \(\vek T^\T \mat K\,\vek T = 0\) with zero prescribed values implies \(\vek T = \vek 0\). The hypotheses of corollary 7.14 are met by every element matrix by item 3 of lemma 15.17.

  1. By item 2 of theorem 7.13, \(\vek T^\T \mat K\,\vek T = \sum_{\mathrm{e}} \vek T_{\mathrm{e}}^\T \mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek T_{\mathrm{e}}\). Each term is by item 3 of lemma 15.17 nonnegative, so the sum is zero exactly when every term is zero. The sum runs over the elements.
  2. The term of an element is zero exactly for \(\vek T_{\mathrm{e}} = c_{\mathrm{e}}\,(1, 1, 1)^\T\) (the same item). All three nodes of the element therefore have the same value. This holds for every element.
  3. In the sequence of nodes from the hypothesis consecutive nodes lie in a common element, so by step 2 their values are equal. So all nodal values are equal. Nodes \(1\) and \(3\) are common to both elements, so the chain passes from one element to the other.
  4. At at least one node the value is prescribed and zero, so this common value is zero and \(\vek T = \vek 0\). By theorem 7.18 \(\mat K_{\mathrm{ff}}\) is positive definite and the solution is unique. The value zero spreads over the mesh to all nodes.

Source: The proof is not from the sources. COOK PDF pp. 474 to 475 treats prescribed nodal temperatures as in displacements: a prescribed temperature is like a prescribed displacement, and heat flux and temperature cannot be prescribed at the same node at once.

We need both hypotheses of the lemma, and each has its near miss. If the temperature is prescribed nowhere, \(\mat K\) has by lemma 15.17 the vector \((1, \dots, 1)\) in its kernel: the matrix in equation (15.16) has zero row sums, so \(\mat K\,(1, 1, 1, 1)^\T = \vek 0\). The system \(\mat K\,\vek T = \vek f\) is then solvable only if \(f_1 + f_2 + f_3 + f_4 = 0\). Indeed, add all four equations: each \(T_{\mathrm{J}}\) is multiplied on the left by the sum of column \(J\), which by symmetry equals the sum of row \(J\), that is zero. The total heat supplied must be zero, as for a body that is held steady without warming up. If a solution exists, it is not the only one, since we may add \(c\,(1, 1, 1, 1)^\T\) to it. If, however, the mesh is not connected, the hypothesis of the lemma is violated. Take two triangles without a common node, as the pulled apart elements on the right of the figure would be if each got three nodes of its own, and prescribe the temperature at only one node of the first. There are five free nodes, two in the first triangle and three in the second, and the vector \((0, 0, 1, 1, 1)\) is in the kernel of the reduced matrix: the second triangle may take any constant temperature. So the solution is not unique (if it exists at all).

15.3.3 Heat flux and convection on the boundary

So far the matrix and the right-hand side came only from the interiors of the elements. The boundary terms of the functional (15.14) are integrals over the boundary, and we compute them over the sides of the triangles that lie on the boundary. Take a side between nodes \(i\) and \(j\), of length \(\ell\), and the triangle with this side, with a heat flux \(\bar q\) through the side (the arrows end on the boundary because the flux flows in). On the side the field \(T_{\mathrm{h}}\) has values only from nodes \(i\) and \(j\), which is lemma 15.7. By lemma 15.4 the third coordinate is zero on this side and \(L_{\mathrm{i}} + L_{\mathrm{j}} = 1\). Along the side, measured from node \(i\) by \(s\) from \(0\) to \(\ell\), we therefore have \(L_{\mathrm{i}} = 1 - s/\ell\) and \(L_{\mathrm{j}} = s/\ell\), as the middle picture shows. The integrals of these two functions over the side are elementary, and from them it follows where the heat goes.

Lemma 15.19 Edge heat load and convection on a triangle side (robna toplota in konvekcija na stranici trikotnika)

Let the side between nodes \(i\) and \(j\) of a triangle have length \(\ell\) and thickness \(t\). For constant \(\bar q\) (\(\mathrm{W/m^2}\)), \(h\) (\(\mathrm{W/(m^2\,K)}\)) and \(T_\infty\), if the side is part of the boundary \(\Gamma_{\mathrm{q}}\) or \(\Gamma_{\mathrm{h}}\) respectively: The side is the edge of the triangle.

  1. The contribution of the side to the right-hand side has the same value \(f_{\mathrm{i}} = f_{\mathrm{j}} = \tfrac12\,\bar q\,t\,\ell\) at nodes \(i\) and \(j\), and zero at the third node. Both nodes carry the equal loads.
  2. The contribution to the matrix is \(\dfrac{h\,t\,\ell}{6}\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\) at nodes \(i\), \(j\), and the contribution to the right-hand side is \(f_{\mathrm{i}} = f_{\mathrm{j}} = \tfrac12\,h\,T_\infty\,t\,\ell\). The entries come from products of the coordinates \(L_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(L_{\mathrm{j}}\).
Proof

We have to show that the boundary terms of the functional (15.14) on the side give these formulas.

  1. The term \(-\int \bar q\,T_{\mathrm{h}}\,t\,\mathrm{d}s\) with \(T_{\mathrm{h}} = \sum_{\mathrm{m}} T_{\mathrm{m}} L_{\mathrm{m}}\) is \(-\sum_{\mathrm{m}} T_{\mathrm{m}} \int \bar q\,L_{\mathrm{m}}\,t\,\mathrm{d}s\). The functional has the form \(\tfrac12\,\vek T^\T \mat K\,\vek T - \vek f^\T \vek T\) (as in theorem 6.33), so \(\int \bar q\,L_{\mathrm{m}}\,t\,\mathrm{d}s\) is the contribution to \(f_{\mathrm{m}}\). As above, \(L_{\mathrm{k}} = 0\) on the side, so \(f_{\mathrm{k}} = 0\) for the third node. The flux enters through the edge.
  2. The integral of \(L_{\mathrm{i}}\) over the side is the area of the triangle under the line \(L_{\mathrm{i}}\) with base \(\ell\) and height \(1\), and likewise under the line \(L_{\mathrm{j}}\): \(\int_0^\ell (1 - s/\ell)\,\mathrm{d}s = \ell/2\) and \(\int_0^\ell (s/\ell)\,\mathrm{d}s = \ell/2\). Hence \(f_{\mathrm{i}} = f_{\mathrm{j}} = \bar q\,t\,\ell/2\).
  3. The term \(\tfrac12\int h\,T_{\mathrm{h}}^2\,t\,\mathrm{d}s\) is \(\tfrac12\sum_{\mathrm{m},\mathrm{n}} T_{\mathrm{m}} T_{\mathrm{n}} \int h\,L_{\mathrm{m}} L_{\mathrm{n}}\,t\,\mathrm{d}s\). The integrals are \(\int_0^\ell (1 - s/\ell)^2\,\mathrm{d}s = \ell/3\), \(\int_0^\ell (s/\ell)^2\,\mathrm{d}s = \ell/3\) and \(\int_0^\ell (1 - s/\ell)(s/\ell)\,\mathrm{d}s = \ell/6\), which is \(\frac{\ell}{6}\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\) after multiplying by \(h\,t\). The integrands are built from squares and the product of the coordinates along the side.
  4. The term \(-\int h\,T_\infty\,T_{\mathrm{h}}\,t\,\mathrm{d}s\) gives, as in steps 1 and 2, the value \(\tfrac12\,h\,T_\infty\,t\,\ell\) at nodes \(i\), \(j\). The result is the equal loads.

Source: COOK (12.2-9) (PDF p. 475) for side \(3\)-\(4\) of a quadrilateral with \(J = L_{34}/2\) gives \(\frac{h\,\tau\,L_{34}}{6}\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\) (his \(\tau\) is our \(t\)), COOK (12.2-10) (PDF p. 476) the right-hand side. For the triangle the proof in this form is ours.

Lemma 15.18 also holds with the convection matrices for \(h \ge 0\), because \(\tfrac{h\,t\,\ell}{6}\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\) is symmetric with a nonnegative quadratic form (eigenvalues \(h\,t\,\ell/6\) and \(h\,t\,\ell/2\)). Instead of a prescribed temperature, a side on \(\Gamma_{\mathrm{h}}\) with \(h > 0\) suffices: on a constant field \(c\) it gives \(c^2\,h\,t\,\ell > 0\).

Numbers. Side \(\ell = 40\) mm, thickness \(t = 10\) mm, \(\bar q = 2000\) W/m². Each of the two nodes receives \(f = \tfrac12\cdot 2000\cdot 0{,}01\cdot 0{,}04 = 0{,}4\) W, together \(0{,}8\) W, which is all the heat \(\bar q\,t\,\ell\) through the side. This is the right picture: two equal loads. Half and half is the rule that follows from \(\int L_{\mathrm{m}} = \ell/2\) and not because it would be "fair". With \(h = 25\) W/(m² K), \(h\,t\,\ell/6 = 1{,}667\cdot 10^{-3}\) W/K, so the entry \((i,i)\) contributes \(3{,}333\cdot 10^{-3}\) W/K and the entry \((i,j)\) contributes \(1{,}667\cdot 10^{-3}\) W/K (numerical quadrature with \(3\) points gives the same, num-03.out). The convection matrix also has an off-diagonal entry on the boundary, because the temperature that the two neighbouring nodes share along the side couples the heat exchange of the two nodes.

The advantage of using lemma 15.19 is therefore that a boundary condition has no special rules of its own: a side that lies on the boundary we treat as a one-dimensional piece with two nodes (COOK PDF p. 476 calls it a surface element). A prescribed temperature, however, is not a load but a support: the node is prescribed, and we treat it as a prescribed displacement in theorem 7.18.

15.3.4 Example: a square plate

Now we have everything for a computation. Take the plate of chapter 14: a square of side \(a\) (in the computation \(a = 1\)), temperature on the top edge \(T = \sin(\pi x/a)\), zero on the other three edges, no source. The exact solution is \(u(x, y) = \sin(\pi x/a)\,\sinh(\pi y/a)/\sinh\pi\) and at the centre \(u = 0{,}19927\). The exact solution is smooth on the whole closed square (closed form), so it is not singular at the corners. We make the mesh of \(n \times n\) squares of side \(\ell_{\mathrm{n}} = a/n\), cut each by a diagonal into two triangles, and on the top edge prescribe the value of the sine at the nodes, on the other edges zero. We watch the centre node. In the right picture the same meshes are without diagonals: the squares are Q4 elements (a quadrilateral element with four nodes, introduced in the section on isoparametric elements, for now just a name for another family).

The T3 and Q4 curves show two things: at each halving the error decreases by a factor \(3{,}6\), \(3{,}9\), \(4{,}0\) for T3 and \(4{,}8\), \(4{,}2\), \(4{,}0\) for Q4, that is about \(4 = 2^2\), which is a law \(\ell_{\mathrm{n}}^2\) (an observation from the numbers, not proved here), and T3 is always above the exact value, at \(n = 2\) by \(0{,}0507\), and Q4 below it, at \(n = 2\) by \(0{,}0743\). Lemma 15.20 Sign of the error for the plate (znak napake pri plošči)

Let the plate be a square of side \(a = 1\), \(k\,t = 1\) W/K, without a source, \(\ell_{\mathrm{n}} = 1/n\) the side of a square, a mesh of \(n \times n\) squares with diagonals in any directions (T3), nodes on the boundary having the values of the exact solution \(u\). Let \(e_{\mathrm{I}} = T_{\mathrm{I}} - u(x_{\mathrm{I}}, y_{\mathrm{I}})\) be the error at node \(I\). The mesh is \(n \times n\) squares of side \(\ell_{\mathrm{n}}\).

  1. The row of the matrix \(\mat K\) at an interior node \(I\) is \(4\,T_{\mathrm{I}} - \sum_{\text{4 neighbours along the axes}} T_{\mathrm{J}}\), whatever the direction of the diagonals. In the figure these are the four neighbours of the centre node.
  2. For every \(n \ge 2\), \((\mat K\,\vek u)_{\mathrm{I}} = -4\,u_{\mathrm{I}} \sum_{\mathrm{m} \ge 1} \frac{(\pi \ell_{\mathrm{n}})^{4m}}{(4m)!} < 0\) and \(e_{\mathrm{I}} \ge 0\) at all nodes. For T3 the curve is above the exact value.
  3. Let the row of the matrix of a Q4 mesh at an interior node be \(\tfrac13\,(8\,T_{\mathrm{I}} - \sum_{\text{8 neighbours}} T_{\mathrm{J}})\). This is a hypothesis of this item: it is derived only in the section on isoparametric elements. Then for \(n = 2, 4, 8, 16\) the row applied to \(u\) equals \(u_{\mathrm{I}}\,(8 - 2\cos z - 2\cosh z - 4\cos z\,\cosh z)/3\) with \(z = \pi \ell_{\mathrm{n}}\), that is \(0{,}99388,\ 0{,}063336,\ 0{,}0039633,\ 0{,}00024772\) times \(u_{\mathrm{I}}\), so it is positive, and \(e_{\mathrm{I}} \le 0\) at all nodes. The node has eight neighbours, and the Q4 curve is below the exact value.
Proof

We have to show that the row applied to the exact solution gives a residual with a sign, and that the sign of the error follows from it. The steps for T3 use only lemma 15.17 and the property of the exact solution \(u = \sin(\pi x)\,\sinh(\pi y)/\sinh\pi\), and the step for Q4 also the hypothesis of item 3.

  1. A T3 triangle is a right triangle with equal legs \(\ell_{\mathrm{n}}\). By item 5 of lemma 15.17 the entry between the nodes along a leg (\(45^\circ\) opposite) is \(-\tfrac12\) W/K, along the hypotenuse (the right angle opposite, \(\cot 90^\circ = 0\)) it is \(0\). Each side of a square belongs to two triangles (one in each of the neighbouring squares), so the entry between neighbours along an axis is \(-1\). In the figure these are the four orange segments from the centre node to its four neighbours. The diagonal entry is by item 4 of the same lemma \(4\), because the row sum is zero. The entry between the nodes on a diagonal is \(0\), and the choice of diagonals changes nothing. Item 1.
  2. By the addition formulas, \(u(x + \ell_{\mathrm{n}}, y) + u(x - \ell_{\mathrm{n}}, y) = 2\cos(\pi \ell_{\mathrm{n}})\,u(x, y)\) and \(u(x, y + \ell_{\mathrm{n}}) + u(x, y - \ell_{\mathrm{n}}) = 2\cosh(\pi \ell_{\mathrm{n}})\,u(x, y)\). The row applied to \(\vek u\) is therefore \(u_{\mathrm{I}}\,(4 - 2\cos z - 2\cosh z)\) with \(z = \pi \ell_{\mathrm{n}}\). From the series \(\cos z + \cosh z = 2\sum_{\mathrm{m} \ge 0} z^{4m}/(4m)!\) we subtract \(2\) (the term \(m = 0\)), which gives the form of item 2, negative for \(u_{\mathrm{I}} > 0\). At an interior node \(0 < x_{\mathrm{I}} < 1\) and \(y_{\mathrm{I}} > 0\), so \(u_{\mathrm{I}} > 0\). The points \(x \pm \ell_{\mathrm{n}}\) and \(y \pm \ell_{\mathrm{n}}\) are the four neighbours at distance \(\ell_{\mathrm{n}}\).
  3. Let \(I\) be an interior node. The plate has no source and no prescribed fluxes, so \((\mat K\,\vek T)_{\mathrm{I}} = 0\). Hence \((\mat K\,\vek e)_{\mathrm{I}} = -(\mat K\,\vek u)_{\mathrm{I}}\), which is positive: \(4\,e_{\mathrm{I}} > \sum_{\text{4 neighbours}} e_{\mathrm{J}}\), the error at the node is larger than the mean of the errors of its four neighbours.
  4. Let the smallest value of the error be negative. The error on the boundary is zero, so the smallest value lies at an interior node \(I\), and all neighbours have \(e_{\mathrm{J}} \ge e_{\mathrm{I}}\), so \(\sum_{\mathrm{J}} e_{\mathrm{J}} \ge 4\,e_{\mathrm{I}}\), which contradicts step 3. So \(\min e \ge 0\). This is item 2. The smallest error lies at a node and all neighbours have a larger one, while at the boundary it is zero.
  5. For Q4 likewise, only that the node has eight neighbours on the square around it: the sum over the four diagonal neighbours is by the addition formulas \(4\cos z\,\cosh z\,u_{\mathrm{I}}\) (a product of two sums), the sum over the four axis neighbours is \((2\cos z + 2\cosh z)\,u_{\mathrm{I}}\), so the row on \(\vek u\) is as in item 3. Its sign is positive, so \(8\,e_{\mathrm{I}} < \sum_{\text{8 neighbours}} e_{\mathrm{J}}\). If the largest value of the error were positive, it would lie at an interior node and all neighbours would have \(e_{\mathrm{J}} \le e_{\mathrm{I}}\), a contradiction. So \(\max e \le 0\).

Source: The proof is not from the sources.

The proof showed the cause of the signs: the two rows are residuals with opposite signs, of equal size to leading order. From the series for \(\cos\) and \(\cosh\), \(4 - 2\cos z - 2\cosh z = -z^4/6 - \dots\), and the Q4 row is \(+z^4/6 - \dots\), with the values \(-2{,}4772\cdot 10^{-4}\) and \(+2{,}4772\cdot 10^{-4}\) at \(n = 16\) (both times \(u_{\mathrm{I}}\)). The proof gives the sign of the error, and that for this problem at every node Q4 is below the exact value and T3 above it. That the error is \(\propto \ell_{\mathrm{n}}^2\) and that the sum of the two errors is \(\propto \ell_{\mathrm{n}}^4\) we do not prove here: the table shows \(4\) and \(16\) at halving, and a proof would need a bound on the inverse of the matrix and is beyond this section.

The mean of the T3 and Q4 values is at \(n = 16\) below the exact value by \(2{,}7\cdot 10^{-6}\), while each separately is off by \(9\cdot 10^{-4}\). This is a property of this problem and not a rule for other problems or meshes. In the figure T3 and Q4 lie almost equally far from the exact value, each on its own side.

15.3.5 The tetrahedron in one paragraph

Instead of a plate we cut a body into tetrahedra with four nodes and write the field on each with volume coordinates \(L_1, \dots, L_4\), which we will introduce in the last section of the chapter. The idea is the same. The gradient of each is constant, points perpendicular towards node \(i\) with magnitude one over the height, the element matrix is \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}} = k\,V\,\mat B^\T \mat B\) with \(\mat B\) of three rows and four columns, and \(V\) is the volume. Items 2 to 4 of lemma 15.17 are proved in the same way, including the zero row sum. Item 5 we do not claim for the tetrahedron. The details, including an example with numbers, are in the last section of the chapter.
