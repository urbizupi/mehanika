Chapter 2 Stress and equilibrium

2.9 Turned axes

At the end of section 2.6 three questions were left open. The last one, how the second condition of equilibrium relates \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\), was answered by section 2.8: the two components are equal. Now we take up the first one, how the stress components change when we turn the axes. The second, on which face through the point the normal stress is largest, will wait for section 2.11. The stress components at a point \(P\) are the components of three traction vectors on the faces through \(P\) that are perpendicular to the axes of the coordinate system. If we turn the axes, these are three other faces, other traction vectors act on them, and we split each of them along other axes. How do we compute the nine numbers in the new axes from the nine numbers in the old ones?

The question is not only one of computation. In the bar of section 2.4 only a normal stress acted on the cross section, but on an oblique cut there was also a shear stress, and for steel it is the shear stress that decides when yielding begins. In the pure shear of section 2.6.6 only shear stresses acted on the faces of the cube, but on the diagonal faces only tension and compression. In a plate reinforced with fibres we want the stress on faces along the fibres and across them, not on faces along the edges of the plate (CM Example 4.3.1, PDF p. 186). We place the axes where the computation is easiest, but we need the stress on faces that the material and the structure choose.

The goal of this section is the rule that gives the components in one rectangular Cartesian coordinate system from the components in another. We will derive it first for vectors, then for stress and for strain. Stress and strain will turn out to have the same rule, and this very rule will say exactly what the word means with which books name such quantities. Along the way we will write Cauchy's formula with matrices and show how the shear strain measures the change of a right angle, which in chapter 1 we only announced.

What do we assume? Both coordinate systems are rectangular Cartesian, with the same unit of length, and their origins are at the same point. The last is no restriction. The components of a vector are differences of coordinates ((1.8)), and if we move the origin without turning the axes, the coordinates of all points change by the same three numbers, while their differences stay. We assume nothing about the body or the material. What we derive for stress holds at every point where Cauchy's formula holds, that is, at every interior point of a body in equilibrium at which the average forces per unit area approach the traction vector (theorem 2.24). From section 2.3 we will use the fact that the length of a vector and the scalar product of two vectors are the same numbers in every rectangular Cartesian coordinate system with the same unit. The symmetry of the stress components (theorem 2.36) we will need in one place only, and we will say where. For strain we assume, as in section 1.17, a continuously differentiable displacement field (definition 1.90) and small partial derivatives of the displacements.

2.9.1 The rotation matrix

In section 1.5.5 we turned the axes by a quarter turn about the axis \(z\) and found that the flight of the fly, which had the components \((1, 2, -2)\) in the old axes, has the components \((2, -1, -2)\) in the new axes. We got there by thinking about how many steps along which new axis make up the flight. For an arbitrary turn such thinking is no longer simple, so we need a rule.

From history

The question of how to get the coordinates of a point in one rectangular system from its coordinates in another system with the same origin is older than matrices. MUIR counts it among the problems of coordinate geometry (MUIR, PDF p. 433). As the first paper on his list of papers about it he gives Euler's book Introductio in analysin infinitorum of 1748, more precisely its appendix on surfaces (MUIR, PDF p. 433). The list also contains papers on the rotation of a body, among them Lagrange's of 1773 (MUIR, PDF p. 434). In a rotating body the axes attached to it turn relative to the axes at rest, and this is exactly our problem. Expressions in the coefficients of equations, which we shall meet in section 2.10, appeared in attempts to solve this problem only comparatively late. For a change of coordinates does not reduce to mere elimination of unknowns from a system of linear equations (MUIR, PDF p. 433).

Let us return to the search for the rule that gives, from the components of a vector in the old axes, the components in the turned axes. We name the new axes \(\bar x\), \(\bar y\) and \(\bar z\) and their unit vectors \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{z}}\), as in section 1.5.5. The new axes, too, are the axes of a rectangular Cartesian coordinate system: they are mutually perpendicular, and lengths on them are measured with the same unit as on the old ones. So \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{z}}\) are three unit vectors that are pairwise perpendicular. We can write each of them with components in the old system, like any other vector. For the quarter turn they are

\[\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{x}} = (0, 1, 0) , \qquad \bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{y}} = (-1, 0, 0) , \qquad \bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{z}} = (0, 0, 1) .\]

For a turn about the axis \(z\) by another angle, let us take \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{x}} = (c, s, 0)\), where \(c\) and \(s\) are numbers with \(c^2 + s^2 = 1\), so that the vector is a unit vector. For \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{y}}\) we take \((-s, c, 0)\). This vector is a unit vector too, because \(s^2 + c^2 = 1\), and it is perpendicular to \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{x}}\), because \(c\,(-s) + s\,c + 0 \cdot 0 = 0\). The axis \(z\) stays as it is, \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{z}} = (0, 0, 1)\), and this vector is perpendicular to both previous ones, because their component \(z\) is zero. With \(c = 0\) and \(s = 1\) we get the quarter turn from above. We have met both vectors before. In section 2.4, \(\vek n = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta, 0)\) was the unit normal of the oblique cut of the bar and \(\vek d = (-\sin\theta, \cos\theta, 0)\) the direction in the cut. So the numbers \(c\) and \(s\) are the cosine and the sine of the angle \(\theta\) by which the new axis \(x\) is turned from the old one toward the axis \(y\) (figure 2.48). We will not need the angle itself, the numbers \(c\) and \(s\) are enough. In the examples we take \(c = 0{,}6\) and \(s = 0{,}8\), for which \(c^2 + s^2 = 0{,}36 + 0{,}64 = 1\). This corresponds to the angle \(\theta = 53{,}13^\circ\).

We collect the nine components of the new unit vectors in a matrix, one vector in each row.

Definition 2.37 Rotation matrix (matrika zasuka)

Let two rectangular Cartesian coordinate systems with the same unit of length be given, an old system with the unit vectors of the axes \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) and a new system with the unit vectors of the axes \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{z}}\). The rotation matrix (matrika zasuka) is the \(3 \times 3\) matrix \(\mat Q\) whose row \(k\) holds the components of the vector \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{k}}\) in the old system:

\[Q_{\mathrm{kj}} = \bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{j}} , \tag{2.38}\]

where the indices \(k\) and \(j\) run over \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\). The numbers \(Q_{\mathrm{kj}}\) are the direction cosines (smerni kosinusi) of the new axes with respect to the old ones.

Source: CM (2.2.71), (2.2.72) (PDF pp. 58-59), where the matrix is written \([L]\) with the entries \(\ell_{\mathrm{ij}}\), SADD (1.4.1) (PDF p. 16), where it is written \(Q_{\mathrm{ij}}\).

What do these numbers mean? The entry \(Q_{\mathrm{kj}}\) is the component \(j\) of the vector \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{k}}\) in the old system. By section 2.3 the component of a vector along the old axis \(j\) is its projection onto \(\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\), so \(Q_{\mathrm{kj}} = \bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\), as (2.38) says. For the angle \(\varphi\) between nonzero vectors \(\vek a\) and \(\vek b\), \(\cos\varphi = \vek a\cdot\vek b/(|\vek a|\,|\vek b|)\) (KREYSZIG §9.2 (4), PDF p. 388, section 2.3). The vectors \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\) are unit vectors, so their scalar product is the cosine of the angle between the new axis \(k\) and the old axis \(j\). Hence the name direction cosines. For the turn about the axis \(z\),

\[\mat Q = \begin{bmatrix} c & s & 0 \\ -s & c & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} , \qquad \text{and for } c = 0{,}6 \text{ and } s = 0{,}8 \qquad \mat Q = \begin{bmatrix} 0{,}6 & 0{,}8 & 0 \\ -0{,}8 & 0{,}6 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} .\]

The entry \(Q_{\mathrm{xx}} = c = \cos\theta\) is the cosine of the angle \(\theta\) between the axes \(\bar x\) and \(x\). The entry \(Q_{\mathrm{xy}} = s = \sin\theta\) is the cosine of the angle \(90^\circ - \theta\) between the axes \(\bar x\) and \(y\), since \(\cos(90^\circ - \theta) = \sin\theta\) (school trigonometry, as in section 2.4). CM writes the same matrix for plates with fibres turned by the angle \(\theta\) (CM (4.3.5), PDF p. 187).

Why the name rotation? We usually get the new system by turning the old one. The definition, however, also covers a new system in which the opposite positive direction is chosen on some axis, as in item 2 of the proof of Cauchy's formula (theorem 2.24). With the axis \(x\) reversed, for example, \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{x}} = (-1, 0, 0)\). Such a system is not only turned but also mirrored. Everything we derive holds in both cases. CM keeps the name rotation (zasuk) for the case without mirroring (CM §2.3.7, PDF p. 68), and in section 2.10 we will see how the two cases are told apart by a single number.

How do we get the components of a vector in the new axes? The component of a vector along an axis is its projection onto the unit vector of that axis. So the new component \(\bar a_{\mathrm{x}}\) is the scalar product \(\vek a\cdot\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{x}}\). The scalar product, however, has the same value in every rectangular Cartesian system, so we may compute it with the components in the old system, which we know. This is the whole idea of the next lemma.

Lemma 2.38 Components of a vector in turned axes (komponente vektorja v zasukanih oseh)

Let two rectangular Cartesian coordinate systems with the same unit of length be given, and let \(\mat Q\) be the rotation matrix from the old one to the new one. Let a vector \(\vek a\) have the components \(a_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(a_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(a_{\mathrm{z}}\) in the old system and \(\bar a_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\bar a_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(\bar a_{\mathrm{z}}\) in the new one. Then:

  1. \(\bar a_{\mathrm{k}} = \vek a\cdot\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{k}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} Q_{\mathrm{kj}}\,a_{\mathrm{j}}\) for \(k = x, y, z\), in short
    \[\bar{\vek a} = \mat Q\,\vek a , \tag{2.39}\]
  2. \(a_{\mathrm{j}} = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} Q_{\mathrm{kj}}\,\bar a_{\mathrm{k}}\) for \(j = x, y, z\), in short \(\vek a = \mat Q^\T\,\bar{\vek a}\).

Here \(\vek a\) and \(\bar{\vek a}\) mean the columns of components of the same vector in the old and in the new system, and the sums run over \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\).

Proof

We must show that every new component is the sum of item 1 and every old component the sum of item 2. Both times we compute the same scalar product in two systems.

  1. In the new system the unit vector \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{x}}\) has the components \((1, 0, 0)\) (definition 1.20, applied in the new system). By (2.6), computed in the new system, \(\vek a\cdot\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{x}} = \bar a_{\mathrm{x}} \cdot 1 + \bar a_{\mathrm{y}} \cdot 0 + \bar a_{\mathrm{z}} \cdot 0 = \bar a_{\mathrm{x}}\). Likewise \(\vek a\cdot\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{y}} = \bar a_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\vek a\cdot\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{z}} = \bar a_{\mathrm{z}}\).
  2. The scalar product has the same value in every rectangular Cartesian system with the same unit, because the formula \(\vek a\cdot\vek b = \tfrac12\left(|\vek a|^2 + |\vek b|^2 - |\vek b - \vek a|^2\right)\) of section 2.3 expresses it by lengths only. So we may also compute the number \(\vek a\cdot\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{k}}\) with the components in the old system.
  3. In the old system \(\vek a = (a_{\mathrm{x}}, a_{\mathrm{y}}, a_{\mathrm{z}})\), and by definition 2.37 of the rotation matrix \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{k}} = (Q_{\mathrm{kx}}, Q_{\mathrm{ky}}, Q_{\mathrm{kz}})\). By (2.6), \(\vek a\cdot\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{k}} = Q_{\mathrm{kx}}\,a_{\mathrm{x}} + Q_{\mathrm{ky}}\,a_{\mathrm{y}} + Q_{\mathrm{kz}}\,a_{\mathrm{z}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} Q_{\mathrm{kj}}\,a_{\mathrm{j}}\). Together with step 1 this is item 1.
  4. By (1.38) the three equations for \(k = x, y, z\) are the three components of the product \(\mat Q\,\vek a\) (definition 1.96). This is (2.39).
  5. For item 2 we swap the roles of the systems. By steps 1 and 2, applied to the vector \(\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\), the old unit vector \(\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\) has in the new system the components \(\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\cdot\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\cdot\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\cdot\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{z}}\). The scalar product is symmetric (lemma 2.12, item 1), so these are the numbers \(Q_{\mathrm{xj}}\), \(Q_{\mathrm{yj}}\), \(Q_{\mathrm{zj}}\), the column \(j\) of the matrix \(\mat Q\).
  6. As in steps 1 to 3, only with the systems swapped, \(a_{\mathrm{j}} = \vek a\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\), and when we compute this scalar product with the components in the new system we get \(a_{\mathrm{j}} = \bar a_{\mathrm{x}}\,Q_{\mathrm{xj}} + \bar a_{\mathrm{y}}\,Q_{\mathrm{yj}} + \bar a_{\mathrm{z}}\,Q_{\mathrm{zj}} = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} Q_{\mathrm{kj}}\,\bar a_{\mathrm{k}}\).
  7. By definition 1.100 of the transposed matrix, the number \(Q_{\mathrm{kj}}\) is the entry in row \(j\) and column \(k\) of the matrix \(\mat Q^\T\), so by (1.38) the sum of step 6 is the component \(j\) of the product \(\mat Q^\T\bar{\vek a}\).

Source: CM (2.2.70) (PDF p. 58), SADD (1.4.6), (1.4.7) (PDF p. 17). The sources derive the rule from writing the unit vectors of one system with the unit vectors of the other. The proof with the scalar product is not from the sources.

The proof showed that every new component is the projection onto a new unit vector, computed with the old components. Let us test the lemma on the flight of the fly \(\vek a = (1, 2, -2)\). For the quarter turn, by (1.38),

\[\mat Q\,\vek a = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 2 + 0 \cdot (-2) \\ (-1) \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot 2 + 0 \cdot (-2) \\ 0 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot 2 + 1 \cdot (-2) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix} ,\]

which is what we found in section 1.5.5 by thinking. For the turn with \(c = 0{,}6\) and \(s = 0{,}8\),

\[\bar a_{\mathrm{x}} = 0{,}6 \cdot 1 + 0{,}8 \cdot 2 = 2{,}2 , \qquad \bar a_{\mathrm{y}} = -0{,}8 \cdot 1 + 0{,}6 \cdot 2 = 0{,}4 , \qquad \bar a_{\mathrm{z}} = -2 ,\]

so in the new axes the flight has the components \((\tfrac{11}{5}, \tfrac25, -2)\) (figure 2.49). Its length is \(\sqrt{2{,}2^2 + 0{,}4^2 + 2^2} = \sqrt{4{,}84 + 0{,}16 + 4} = \sqrt 9 = 3\) m, the same as in the old axes, since the arrow is the same. Item 2 gives back the old components: \(a_{\mathrm{x}} = 0{,}6 \cdot 2{,}2 - 0{,}8 \cdot 0{,}4 = 1{,}32 - 0{,}32 = 1\) and \(a_{\mathrm{y}} = 0{,}8 \cdot 2{,}2 + 0{,}6 \cdot 0{,}4 = 1{,}76 + 0{,}24 = 2\).

The lemma holds only between rectangular Cartesian systems, where a component is the projection onto the unit vector of the axis. For the oblique axes of section 1.5.1 the components are not projections, and the rule is different (CM §2.2.4, PDF pp. 48-52).

The rule for the way back has the transposed matrix. When we translate components into the new system and then back, we must get the original ones, so we expect \(\mat Q^\T\) to be the inverse matrix of \(\mat Q\). Matrices with this property have a name.

Definition 2.39 Orthogonal matrix (ortogonalna matrika)

A square matrix \(\mat Q\) is orthogonal (ortogonalna) if

\[\mat Q^\T\mat Q = \mat Q\,\mat Q^\T = \mat I . \tag{2.40}\]

Source: CM (2.3.15) (PDF p. 68), SADD (1.4.9), (1.4.10) (PDF p. 17).

A square matrix is a matrix with as many rows as columns, and the definition holds for every size. By definition 1.107 of the inverse matrix, equation (2.40) says that the transposed matrix is the inverse, \(\mat Q^{-1} = \mat Q^\T\). The name says what the equation means for the rows. The entry in row \(k\) and column \(l\) of the product \(\mat Q\,\mat Q^\T\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} Q_{\mathrm{kj}}\,Q_{\mathrm{lj}}\), the sum of the products of corresponding entries of the rows \(k\) and \(l\), and the equation requires this sum to be \(1\) for \(k = l\) and \(0\) for \(k \neq l\). So the rows are pairwise perpendicular in the sense of definition 2.11, orthogonal, and each has length \(1\). The equation \(\mat Q^\T\mat Q = \mat I\) says the same for the columns.

From history

The names orthogonal transformation (ortogonalna preobrazba) and orthogonal substitution (ortogonalna zamenjava) for the passage to new variables with such a matrix go back to 1852, both in the papers of Sylvester (MUIR, PDF p. 842).

The rotation matrix \(\mat Q\) of this section is orthogonal, as the following lemma says.

Lemma 2.40 Orthogonality of the rotation matrix (ortogonalnost matrike zasuka)

The rotation matrix \(\mat Q\) is orthogonal. Its rows are the components of the new unit vectors of the axes in the old system, and its columns are the components of the old unit vectors of the axes in the new system.

Proof

We must show both equalities in (2.40). We write every entry of the product as the scalar product of two unit vectors.

  1. By definitions 1.98 and 1.100 the entry \((k, l)\) of the product \(\mat Q\,\mat Q^\T\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} Q_{\mathrm{kj}}\,(\mat Q^\T)_{\mathrm{jl}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} Q_{\mathrm{kj}}\,Q_{\mathrm{lj}}\).
  2. By definition 2.37 the rows \(k\) and \(l\) are the components of the vectors \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{l}}\) in the old system, so by (2.6) the sum of step 1 equals \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{l}}\).
  3. The new unit vectors are unit vectors and pairwise perpendicular. So \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{k}} = |\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{k}}|^2 = 1\) (lemma 2.12, item 4) and \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{l}} = 0\) for \(k \neq l\) (definition 2.11). These are exactly the entries of the identity matrix (definition 1.105), so \(\mat Q\,\mat Q^\T = \mat I\).
  4. By step 5 of the proof of lemma 2.38 on the components of a vector in turned axes, the column \(j\) of the matrix \(\mat Q\) is the triple of components of the vector \(\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\) in the new system.
  5. The entry \((i, j)\) of the product \(\mat Q^\T\mat Q\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} Q_{\mathrm{ki}}\,Q_{\mathrm{kj}}\), the sum of the products of corresponding entries of the columns \(i\) and \(j\). By step 4 and by (2.6), computed in the new system, this is \(\vek e_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\).
  6. The old unit vectors are unit vectors and pairwise perpendicular as well, so, as in step 3, \(\mat Q^\T\mat Q = \mat I\).

Source: SADD (1.4.9), (1.4.10) (PDF p. 17), CM (2.5.20) (PDF p. 87). SADD derives the equalities from the two rules for components. The proof with the scalar products of rows and columns is not from the sources.

Let us test it for \(c = 0{,}6\) and \(s = 0{,}8\). The sum of the squares of the entries of the first row is \(0{,}36 + 0{,}64 + 0 = 1\), and the sum of the products of corresponding entries of the first and the second row is \(0{,}6 \cdot (-0{,}8) + 0{,}8 \cdot 0{,}6 + 0 \cdot 0 = 0\). The lemma also tells why the rule for the way back has the transposed matrix: item 2 of lemma 2.38 is \(\vek a = \mat Q^\T\bar{\vek a} = \mat Q^{-1}\bar{\vek a}\), the rule that undoes the rule of item 1.

The converse holds too. If \(\mat Q\) is any orthogonal \(3 \times 3\) matrix, then by what we just said its rows are three pairwise perpendicular unit vectors. Drawn from the origin, they determine three perpendicular axes with the same unit of length, that is, a new rectangular Cartesian coordinate system, and \(\mat Q\) is its rotation matrix.

2.9.2 Cauchy's formula with matrices

At the end of section 2.6.5 we wrote the nine stress components in a table in which each row is one traction vector. In section 2.8.6 we already read this table as a matrix (definition 1.93) and saw that it is symmetric. Now we use it to write Cauchy's formula as the product of a matrix and a vector.

Definition 2.41 Stress matrix (matrika napetosti)

The stress matrix (matrika napetosti) \(\ten\sigma\) at a point \(P\) is the matrix of the stress components at \(P\) in a chosen rectangular Cartesian coordinate system, in which row \(i\) holds the components of the traction vector \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{i}})\):

\[\ten\sigma = \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} & \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} & \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} \\ \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}} & \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} & \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} \\ \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}} & \sigma_{\mathrm{zy}} & \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} \end{bmatrix} . \tag{2.41}\]

Source: SADD (3.2.3) (PDF p. 65), BOWER §2.2.3 (PDF pp. 70-71). CM (4.2.11) (PDF p. 183) writes the transposed matrix.

The stress matrix is a record of the stress at a point in a chosen coordinate system, just as the triple of components is a record of a vector. When we turn the axes, the matrix changes, but the stress at the point stays the same. How the matrix changes we will derive in section 2.9.3. First let us write Cauchy's formula.

  1. By (2.24) the component \(x\) of the traction vector \(\vek t(\vek n)\) is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\,n_{\mathrm{y}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}\,n_{\mathrm{z}}\). The numbers \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}\) are the column \(x\) of the matrix \(\ten\sigma\).
  2. By definition 1.100 of the transposed matrix, the column \(x\) of the matrix \(\ten\sigma\) is the row \(x\) of the matrix \(\ten\sigma^\T\). So by (1.38) the sum of step 1 is the component \(x\) of the product \(\ten\sigma^\T\vek n\).
  3. The same holds for the components \(y\) and \(z\), so
    \[\vek t(\vek n) = \ten\sigma^\T\vek n , \qquad \begin{bmatrix} t_{\mathrm{x}} \\ t_{\mathrm{y}} \\ t_{\mathrm{z}} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} & \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}} & \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}} \\ \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} & \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} & \sigma_{\mathrm{zy}} \\ \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} & \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} & \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} n_{\mathrm{x}} \\ n_{\mathrm{y}} \\ n_{\mathrm{z}} \end{bmatrix} .\]
  4. By theorem 2.36 on the symmetry of stress, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zy}}\). So the stress matrix is symmetric (definition 1.102), \(\ten\sigma^\T = \ten\sigma\), and
    \[\vek t(\vek n) = \ten\sigma\,\vek n . \tag{2.42}\]

We used the symmetry only in step 4. The formula \(\vek t(\vek n) = \ten\sigma^\T\vek n\) holds without it too. CM writes \(\vek t = \ten\sigma\cdot\hat{\vek n}\) and in matrix form \(\{t\} = [\sigma]\{n\}\) (CM (4.2.10), (4.2.11), PDF p. 183), because its stress matrix is our matrix \(\ten\sigma^\T\). BOWER, who writes the indices as we do, writes \(\vek t(\vek n) = \vek n\cdot\ten\sigma\) and warns that because of the symmetry there is no difference between the two conventions (BOWER, PDF pp. 70-71). The difference becomes important for measures of stress that are not symmetric. We will meet them in section 2.13.

From the matrix form we also get the normal and the shear stress on a face with the unit normal \(\vek n\). By definition 2.18 the normal stress is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}} = \vek t\cdot\vek n\). The component \(i\) of the vector \(\ten\sigma\vek n\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,n_{\mathrm{j}}\), so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \big(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,n_{\mathrm{j}}\big)\,n_{\mathrm{i}}\), and exactly this double sum is the product of the row \(\vek n^\T\), the matrix \(\ten\sigma\) and the column \(\vek n\) ((1.41)). By item 3 of lemma 2.14 on splitting a vector, the shear stress is given by \(\tau^2 = |\vek t|^2 - \sigma_{\mathrm{n}}^2\). Together:

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{n}} = \vek n^\T\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} n_{\mathrm{i}}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,n_{\mathrm{j}} , \qquad \tau^2 = |\ten\sigma\,\vek n|^2 - \sigma_{\mathrm{n}}^2 . \tag{2.43}\]

The double sum has nine terms. The terms \(n_{\mathrm{x}}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,n_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(n_{\mathrm{y}}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}}\) are equal because of the symmetry, and the same holds for the other two pairs, so written out

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{n}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\,n_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\,n_{\mathrm{z}}^2 + 2\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}} n_{\mathrm{y}} + 2\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}} n_{\mathrm{z}} + 2\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}\,n_{\mathrm{y}} n_{\mathrm{z}} .\]

This is how CM (4.2.17) writes the normal stress and CM (4.2.18) the shear stress (PDF p. 184).

The formula for \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}}\) has the same form as the strain of a fibre in the direction \(\vek n\) in (1.53), only with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\) instead of \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\). The reason is that both quantities are built in the same way: a matrix maps the direction \(\vek n\) to a vector, and we project this vector back onto the direction \(\vek n\). For stress this vector is the force per unit area \(\ten\sigma\vek n\). For strain it is \(\mat H\vek n\), approximately the difference of the displacements of the ends of a short fibre in the direction \(\vek n\), divided by its length (section 1.17). Its projection onto the direction of the fibre is \(\vek n^\T\mat H\vek n = \vek n^\T\ten\varepsilon\,\vek n\), because in the double sum the terms \(n_{\mathrm{i}}\,H_{\mathrm{ij}}\,n_{\mathrm{j}}\) and \(n_{\mathrm{j}}\,H_{\mathrm{ji}}\,n_{\mathrm{i}}\) combine into \(2\,n_{\mathrm{i}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\,n_{\mathrm{j}}\).

Let us test the formulas on the pure shear of section 2.6.6. The cube is loaded only by forces along its faces. The axis \(x\) points to the right, the axis \(z\) up, we pull the top face to the right, the bottom one to the left, the right one up and the left one down, all with the same force per unit area \(\tau_0 = 50\) MPa. The only nonzero stress components are \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}} = \tau_0\). Take the face with the normal \(\vek n = (\tfrac35, 0, \tfrac45)\), which is perpendicular to neither diagonal of the cube (figure 2.50). By (2.42),

\[\vek t(\vek n) = \ten\sigma\,\vek n = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & \tau_0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ \tau_0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 3/5 \\ 0 \\ 4/5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \tfrac45\,\tau_0 \\ 0 \\ \tfrac35\,\tau_0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 40 \\ 0 \\ 30 \end{bmatrix} \text{MPa} .\]

By (2.43) the normal stress is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}} = 2\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}} n_{\mathrm{z}} = 2 \cdot 50 \cdot \tfrac35 \cdot \tfrac45 = 48\) MPa, which is also \(\vek t\cdot\vek n = 40 \cdot \tfrac35 + 30 \cdot \tfrac45 = 24 + 24 = 48\) MPa. The traction vector has the size \(|\vek t| = \sqrt{40^2 + 30^2} = 50\) MPa, and the shear stress is \(\tau = \sqrt{50^2 - 48^2} = \sqrt{196} = 14\) MPa. The shear part is \(\vek t - \sigma_{\mathrm{n}}\vek n = 14\ \text{MPa} \cdot (\tfrac45, 0, -\tfrac35)\), and it is indeed perpendicular to \(\vek n\), since \(\tfrac45 \cdot \tfrac35 + 0 \cdot 0 - \tfrac35 \cdot \tfrac45 = 0\). On the face perpendicular to the diagonal \(AC\), with the normal \(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, 0, 1)\), the normal stress was \(50\) MPa and there was no shear stress (section 2.6.6). The normal of our face is close to that normal, so the normal stress is close to \(50\) MPa and the shear stress is small.

2.9.3 Stress in turned axes

Now we can answer the first question from the beginning of the section. The component \(\bar\sigma_{\mathrm{kl}}\) in the new axes is the component \(l\) of the traction vector on the face perpendicular to the new axis \(k\). We know this vector from Cauchy's formula in the old axes, and its component along the new axis \(l\) we get by lemma 2.38 as the projection onto \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{l}}\). The indices \(i\), \(j\), \(k\) and \(l\) run over \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\). The first two count the old axes, the other two the new ones.

Theorem 2.42 Transformation of stress components (transformacija komponent napetosti)

Let \(P\) be a point of a body at which Cauchy's formula holds (theorem 2.24), \(\ten\sigma\) the stress matrix at \(P\) in the old rectangular Cartesian coordinate system, \(\bar{\ten\sigma}\) the stress matrix at \(P\) in the new system with the same unit of length, and \(\mat Q\) the rotation matrix from the old system to the new one. Then

\[\bar\sigma_{\mathrm{kl}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} Q_{\mathrm{ki}}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,Q_{\mathrm{lj}} , \qquad \bar{\ten\sigma} = \mat Q\,\ten\sigma\,\mat Q^\T . \tag{2.44}\]
Proof

We must show that the component \(l\) of the traction vector on the face with the normal \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{k}}\), written in the new system, equals the double sum.

  1. By definition 2.25 of the stress components, applied in the new system, \(\bar\sigma_{\mathrm{kl}}\) is the component \(l\) of the vector \(\vek t(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{k}})\) in the new system. By item 1 of lemma 2.38 on the components of a vector in turned axes this is \(\bar\sigma_{\mathrm{kl}} = \vek t(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{k}})\cdot\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{l}}\).
  2. The vector \(\vek t(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{k}})\) is determined by the force on a patch and its area, not by the choice of axes (definition 2.20), so we may compute it in the old system. Cauchy's formula holds in every rectangular Cartesian coordinate system, so also in the old one. We apply it to the normal \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{k}}\), which has the components \(Q_{\mathrm{kx}}\), \(Q_{\mathrm{ky}}\), \(Q_{\mathrm{kz}}\) in the old system (definition 2.37). By (2.22), \(\vek t(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{k}}) = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} Q_{\mathrm{ki}}\,\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{i}})\).
  3. We substitute into step 1 and by items 2 and 3 of the properties of the scalar product (lemma 2.12) take out the numbers: \(\bar\sigma_{\mathrm{kl}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} Q_{\mathrm{ki}}\,\big(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{i}})\cdot\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{l}}\big)\).
  4. We compute the scalar product in the brackets with the components in the old system. By (2.23), \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{i}}) = (\sigma_{\mathrm{ix}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{iy}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{iz}})\), by the definition of the rotation matrix \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{l}} = (Q_{\mathrm{lx}}, Q_{\mathrm{ly}}, Q_{\mathrm{lz}})\), and by (2.6) \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{i}})\cdot\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{l}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,Q_{\mathrm{lj}}\).
  5. We substitute step 4 into step 3 and get \(\bar\sigma_{\mathrm{kl}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} Q_{\mathrm{ki}}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,Q_{\mathrm{lj}}\).
  6. By definitions 1.100 and 1.98 the inner sum \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,Q_{\mathrm{lj}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,(\mat Q^\T)_{\mathrm{jl}}\) is the entry in row \(i\) and column \(l\) of the product \(\ten\sigma\,\mat Q^\T\). The outer sum \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} Q_{\mathrm{ki}}\,(\ten\sigma\,\mat Q^\T)_{\mathrm{il}}\) is the entry in row \(k\) and column \(l\) of the product \(\mat Q\,(\ten\sigma\,\mat Q^\T)\). We do not write the brackets, because the matrix product is associative (item 2 of lemma 1.99 on the properties of the matrix product).

Source: CM (4.3.3) (PDF p. 186), SADD (3.3.1) (PDF p. 68). CM has the transposed stress matrix, but since \((\mat Q\,\ten\sigma^\T\mat Q^\T)^\T = \mat Q\,\ten\sigma\,\mat Q^\T\), the rule has the same form in both conventions. The proof from Cauchy's formula is not from the sources.

The theorem says that for the components in the new axes we need no new reasoning about forces. The nine old components and the rotation matrix are enough, and every new component is a sum of nine products. The theorem needs no approximation and no property of the material, so it holds wherever Cauchy's formula holds. It also matters what the proof did not use: the symmetry of the stress components. So the rule also holds for a matrix that is not symmetric. When \(\ten\sigma\) is symmetric, \(\bar{\ten\sigma}\) is symmetric too. By lemma 1.101 on the transposed product, \(\bar{\ten\sigma}^\T = (\mat Q^\T)^\T\,\ten\sigma^\T\mat Q^\T = \mat Q\,\ten\sigma\,\mat Q^\T = \bar{\ten\sigma}\). This is as it must be, since the second condition of equilibrium, and with it the theorem on the symmetry of stress, holds in every right-handed coordinate system. For a mirrored system, for which we did not prove the theorem, this very computation gives the symmetry. The way back we get by multiplying both sides of (2.44) by \(\mat Q^\T\) from the left and by \(\mat Q\) from the right. Since \(\mat Q^\T\mat Q = \mat I\) by (2.40), what remains is \(\ten\sigma = \mat Q^\T\bar{\ten\sigma}\,\mat Q\).

Most often we turn the axes about one of them. For the turn about the axis \(z\), the row \(x\) of the matrix \(\mat Q\) has the entries \(c\), \(s\), \(0\), and the row \(y\) the entries \(-s\), \(c\), \(0\). Let us compute \(\bar\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) by (2.44).

  1. For \(k = l = x\), \(\bar\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} Q_{\mathrm{xi}}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,Q_{\mathrm{xj}}\). Since \(Q_{\mathrm{xz}} = 0\), only the terms in which \(i\) and \(j\) are \(x\) or \(y\) differ from zero:
    \[\bar\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = Q_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,Q_{\mathrm{xx}} + Q_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,Q_{\mathrm{xy}} + Q_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\,Q_{\mathrm{xx}} + Q_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\,Q_{\mathrm{xy}} .\]
  2. We substitute \(Q_{\mathrm{xx}} = c\) and \(Q_{\mathrm{xy}} = s\): \(\bar\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = c^2\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + cs\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} + sc\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}} + s^2\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\).
  3. By the theorem on the symmetry of stress, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\), so the two middle terms combine into \(2cs\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\).

In the same way we get the other components. We checked all of them with SymPy:

\[\begin{aligned} \bar\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} &= c^2\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + s^2\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} + 2cs\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} , \\ \bar\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} &= s^2\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + c^2\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} - 2cs\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} , \\ \bar\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} &= -cs\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + cs\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} + (c^2 - s^2)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} , \\ \bar\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} &= c\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} + s\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} , \qquad \bar\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = -s\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} + c\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} , \qquad \bar\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} . \end{aligned} \tag{2.45}\]

With \(c = \cos\theta\) and \(s = \sin\theta\) the first three lines are the formulas SADD (3.3.4) (PDF p. 68), and all of them are also written in CM (4.3.7) (PDF p. 187), where \(2cs\) and \(c^2 - s^2\) are expressed with the double angle.

Let us test the rule on the bar of section 2.2. This is a steel bar with a diameter of 10 mm, pulled at both ends by a force of 10 kN, with the axis \(x\) along the bar, the axis \(y\) up and the axis \(z\) toward us. Far from the ends the only nonzero stress component is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma = 127{,}32\) MPa (section 2.6.6). We turn the axes about the axis \(z\) with \(c = 0{,}6\) and \(s = 0{,}8\), so with \(c^2 = 0{,}36\), \(s^2 = 0{,}64\) and \(cs = 0{,}48\). By (2.45),

\[\begin{aligned} \bar\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} &= 0{,}36\,\sigma = 45{,}84\ \text{MPa} , \\ \bar\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} &= 0{,}64\,\sigma = 81{,}49\ \text{MPa} , \\ \bar\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} &= -0{,}48\,\sigma = -61{,}12\ \text{MPa} , \end{aligned}\]

and all other components are zero (figure 2.51). Does this agree with section 2.4? The face with the normal \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{x}} = (\tfrac35, \tfrac45, 0)\) is the oblique cut with \(\cos\theta = 0{,}6\). By (2.13) the normal stress on it is \(\sigma\cos^2\theta = 0{,}36\,\sigma\), and by (2.14) the shear stress is \(\sigma\sin\theta\cos\theta = 0{,}48\,\sigma\). There we also computed that the shear part equals \(-\sigma\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\vek d\), where \(\vek d = (-\sin\theta, \cos\theta, 0)\) is exactly \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{y}}\). So its component along the axis \(\bar y\) is \(-0{,}48\,\sigma\), which is \(\bar\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\). Besides the size of the shear stress the rule also gives its direction: the shear part points opposite to the axis \(\bar y\).

The numbers hold one more pattern: \(\bar\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \bar\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0{,}36\,\sigma + 0{,}64\,\sigma = \sigma\). The normal stresses on two perpendicular faces add up to \(\sigma\), as we saw in the table of section 2.4 at \(30^\circ\) and \(60^\circ\). The first two lines of (2.45) tell why. When we add them, the terms \(2cs\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) and \(-2cs\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) cancel, and what remains is \((c^2 + s^2)\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}) = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\). So in a turn about the axis \(z\) the sum \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) does not change. That in every turn the sum of all three diagonal components does not change we will show in section 2.11.

The second example is the pure shear of section 2.9.2: the cube is loaded only by forces along its faces, of size \(\tau_0\) per unit area, and the only nonzero stress components are \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}} = \tau_0\). In section 2.6.6 we found with Cauchy's formula, face by face, that there is no shear on the faces perpendicular to the diagonals of the cube. Now we place the axes along the diagonals and compute all nine components at once. We look at the cube from the front as in figure 2.28, with the corners \(A\) bottom left, \(B\) bottom right, \(C\) top right and \(D\) top left. Let the new axis \(\bar x\) point along the diagonal from \(A\) toward \(C\), the new axis \(\bar z\) along the diagonal from \(B\) toward \(D\), and let the axis \(\bar y\) stay the axis \(y\):

\[\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{x}} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\,(1, 0, 1) , \qquad \bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{y}} = (0, 1, 0) , \qquad \bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{z}} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\,(-1, 0, 1) .\]

All three are unit vectors, for example \(|\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{x}}|^2 = \tfrac12\,(1 + 0 + 1) = 1\), and pairwise perpendicular, for example \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{x}}\cdot\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{z}} = \tfrac12\,(-1 + 0 + 1) = 0\). This is a turn about the axis \(y\) by an eighth of a full turn. We compute the product of three matrices in two steps.

  1. By definition 1.98 the entry \((i, l)\) of the product \(\ten\sigma\,\mat Q^\T\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,Q_{\mathrm{lj}}\), that is, the scalar product of the row \(i\) of the matrix \(\ten\sigma\) with the row \(l\) of the matrix \(\mat Q\), the vector \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{l}}\). The row \(x\) of the matrix \(\ten\sigma\) has the entries \(0\), \(0\), \(\tau_0\), and its scalar products with \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{z}}\) are \(\tau_0/\sqrt2\), \(0\), \(\tau_0/\sqrt2\). The row \(y\) is zero. The row \(z\) has the entries \(\tau_0\), \(0\), \(0\), and its scalar products are \(\tau_0/\sqrt2\), \(0\), \(-\tau_0/\sqrt2\). So
    \[\ten\sigma\,\mat Q^\T = \frac{\tau_0}{\sqrt2} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix} .\]
  2. The entry \((k, l)\) of the product \(\mat Q\,(\ten\sigma\,\mat Q^\T)\) is the sum of the products of corresponding entries of the row \(k\) of the matrix \(\mat Q\) and the column \(l\) of the matrix above. The row \(x\) of the matrix \(\mat Q\) has the entries \(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\), \(0\), \(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\). With the column \(x\) of the matrix above, with the entries \(\tfrac{\tau_0}{\sqrt2}\), \(0\), \(\tfrac{\tau_0}{\sqrt2}\), it gives \(\bar\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \tfrac{\tau_0}{2}\,(1 + 1) = \tau_0\), and with the column \(z\), with the entries \(\tfrac{\tau_0}{\sqrt2}\), \(0\), \(-\tfrac{\tau_0}{\sqrt2}\), it gives \(\bar\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \tfrac{\tau_0}{2}\,(1 - 1) = 0\). The row \(z\) of the matrix \(\mat Q\) has the entries \(-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\), \(0\), \(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\) and gives with the column \(x\) the number \(\bar\sigma_{\mathrm{zx}} = \tfrac{\tau_0}{2}\,(-1 + 1) = 0\) and with the column \(z\) the number \(\bar\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = \tfrac{\tau_0}{2}\,(-1 - 1) = -\tau_0\). The row \(y\) of the matrix \(\mat Q\) has the entries \(0\), \(1\), \(0\) and picks out the row \(y\) of the matrix above, which is zero. So
    \[\bar{\ten\sigma} = \begin{bmatrix} \tau_0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -\tau_0 \end{bmatrix} .\]

In the axes along the diagonals the same stress has no shear component at all. On the faces perpendicular to the diagonal \(AC\) the tension \(\tau_0\) acts, and on the faces perpendicular to the diagonal \(BD\) the compression \(\tau_0\) (figure 2.52). This is what we found in section 2.6.6 with Cauchy's formula for each face separately, now in one computation for all faces perpendicular to the new axes. For \(\tau_0 = 50\) MPa, \(\bar\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 50\) MPa and \(\bar\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = -50\) MPa. The sum of the diagonal components is zero before the turn and after it.

2.9.4 Strain in turned axes

Do the strain components also change by the same rule? In section 1.17 we got the components of the small strain tensor from the partial derivatives of the displacement field, \(\ten\varepsilon = \tfrac12\,(\mat H + \mat H^\T)\) ((1.51)), where \(\mat H\) is the displacement gradient at the point \(P\) with the entries \(H_{\mathrm{ij}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}\) (definition 1.109). Here \(x_{\mathrm{j}}\) means the coordinate \(x\), \(y\) or \(z\). In the new axes the displacement has the components \(\bar u_{\mathrm{k}}\), which by lemma 2.38 we get from the old ones, and the points have the new coordinates \(\bar x_{\mathrm{l}}\). The partial derivatives of the new components with respect to the new coordinates make up the matrix \(\bar{\mat H}\), and from it we get \(\bar{\ten\varepsilon} = \tfrac12\,(\bar{\mat H} + \bar{\mat H}^\T)\). So first we must find out how the displacement gradient changes.

The idea is this. The partial derivative with respect to the new coordinate \(\bar x_{\mathrm{l}}\) is the limit of a quotient in which the point moves by a short step along the new axis \(l\). In the old axes this step is oblique, and the change of the displacement over a short step in any direction is given by theorem 1.88 on the change of a field over a short step. So we need its hypotheses.

Lemma 2.43 Displacement gradient in turned axes (gradient pomikov v zasukanih oseh)

Let the components \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(u_{\mathrm{z}}\) of the displacement field in the old system satisfy the hypotheses of theorem 1.88 on the change of a field over a short step at the point \(P\): they are defined at all points whose distance from \(P\) is less than some number \(r > 0\), their partial derivatives exist at these points and are continuous at \(P\). Let \(\mat H\) be the displacement gradient at \(P\) in the old system, \(\mat Q\) the rotation matrix from the old system to the new one, \(\bar u_{\mathrm{k}}\) the components of the displacement and \(\bar x_{\mathrm{l}}\) the coordinates in the new system. Then the partial derivatives \(\bar H_{\mathrm{kl}} = \partial\bar u_{\mathrm{k}}/\partial\bar x_{\mathrm{l}}\) at \(P\) exist and

\[\bar{\mat H} = \mat Q\,\mat H\,\mat Q^\T . \tag{2.46}\]
Proof

We must show that the limit that defines \(\partial\bar u_{\mathrm{k}}/\partial\bar x_{\mathrm{l}}\) at \(P\) exists and equals the entry \((k, l)\) of the matrix \(\mat Q\,\mat H\,\mat Q^\T\).

  1. By definition 1.25 of the partial derivative, applied in the new system, \(\partial\bar u_{\mathrm{k}}/\partial\bar x_{\mathrm{l}}\) at \(P\) is the limit of the quotient \(\big(\bar u_{\mathrm{k}}(P_{\mathrm{t}}) - \bar u_{\mathrm{k}}(P)\big)/t\) as \(t\) approaches zero. The point \(P_{\mathrm{t}}\) has the new coordinate \(\bar x_{\mathrm{l}}\) larger by \(t\) than \(P\) and the other two new coordinates equal. So the vector from \(P\) to \(P_{\mathrm{t}}\) has in the new system the component \(l\) equal to \(t\) and the other two zero ((1.8)), and by lemma 1.21 \(P_{\mathrm{t}} = P + t\,\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{l}}\).
  2. By item 1 of lemma 2.38, applied to the displacement vector at the points \(P_{\mathrm{t}}\) and \(P\), at every point \(\bar u_{\mathrm{k}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} Q_{\mathrm{ki}}\,u_{\mathrm{i}}\). So the numerator of the quotient is \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} Q_{\mathrm{ki}}\,\big(u_{\mathrm{i}}(P + t\,\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{l}}) - u_{\mathrm{i}}(P)\big)\).
  3. The step \(t\,\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{l}}\) has in the old system the components \(t\,Q_{\mathrm{lx}}\), \(t\,Q_{\mathrm{ly}}\), \(t\,Q_{\mathrm{lz}}\) (definition 2.37) and the length \(|t|\), because \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{l}}\) is a unit vector. By theorem 1.88 on the change of a field over a short step, for every component \(u_{\mathrm{i}}\) and for \(0 < |t| < r\),
    \[u_{\mathrm{i}}(P + t\,\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{l}}) - u_{\mathrm{i}}(P) = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} H_{\mathrm{ij}}\,t\,Q_{\mathrm{lj}} + R_{\mathrm{i}}(t) ,\]
    and for every \(\eta > 0\) there is a \(\delta > 0\) such that \(|R_{\mathrm{i}}(t)| \le \eta\,|t|\) for every \(t\) with \(0 < |t| < \delta\). For all three components at once we take the smallest of the three bands \(\delta\).
  4. We substitute step 3 into step 2 and divide by \(t\). The quotient is
    \[\frac{\bar u_{\mathrm{k}}(P_{\mathrm{t}}) - \bar u_{\mathrm{k}}(P)}{t} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} Q_{\mathrm{ki}}\,H_{\mathrm{ij}}\,Q_{\mathrm{lj}} + \sum_{\mathrm{i}} Q_{\mathrm{ki}}\,\frac{R_{\mathrm{i}}(t)}{t} .\]
    The first double sum does not depend on \(t\). We call it \(d\).
  5. We write the definition of the limit 1.5 for this quotient and the number \(d\). We choose an error \(\eta' > 0\) and take \(\eta = \eta'/4\) in step 3. Every entry \(Q_{\mathrm{ki}}\) is a component of the unit vector \(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{k}}\), so \(|Q_{\mathrm{ki}}| \le 1\), as in step 3 of the proof of theorem 2.24. By the triangle inequality for numbers (lemma 1.6, item 1), for \(0 < |t| < \delta\)
    \[\Big|\sum_{\mathrm{i}} Q_{\mathrm{ki}}\,\frac{R_{\mathrm{i}}(t)}{t}\Big| \le \sum_{\mathrm{i}} |Q_{\mathrm{ki}}|\,\frac{|R_{\mathrm{i}}(t)|}{|t|} \le 3\,\eta = \tfrac34\,\eta' < \eta' .\]
    So the difference between the quotient and the number \(d\) is less than \(\eta'\), as the definition of the limit requires.
  6. So the limit exists and equals \(d = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} Q_{\mathrm{ki}}\,H_{\mathrm{ij}}\,Q_{\mathrm{lj}}\). As in step 6 of the proof of theorem 2.42 on the transformation of stress components, this is the entry \((k, l)\) of the matrix \(\mat Q\,\mat H\,\mat Q^\T\).

Source: CM (2.5.21) (PDF p. 87) gives the same rule for components in general, BOWER Appendix B.1.1 (PDF p. 756) takes the gradient as the first example of a quantity with such a rule. The proof with the theorem on the change of a field is not from the sources.

From the gradient we get the strains at once.

Lemma 2.44 Transformation of strain components (transformacija komponent deformacije)

Under the hypotheses of lemma 2.43 the matrix of the small strain tensor at the point \(P\) in the new system is

\[\bar{\ten\varepsilon} = \mat Q\,\ten\varepsilon\,\mat Q^\T . \tag{2.47}\]
Proof

We must show that the rule for the gradient gives the same rule for its symmetric part.

  1. By (1.51), written in the new system, \(\bar{\ten\varepsilon} = \tfrac12\,(\bar{\mat H} + \bar{\mat H}^\T)\).
  2. By lemma 2.43, \(\bar{\mat H} = \mat Q\,\mat H\,\mat Q^\T\). By lemma 1.101 on the transposed product, used twice, and since \((\mat Q^\T)^\T = \mat Q\), \(\bar{\mat H}^\T = \mat Q\,\mat H^\T\mat Q^\T\).
  3. We substitute into step 1: \(\bar{\ten\varepsilon} = \tfrac12\,(\mat Q\,\mat H\,\mat Q^\T + \mat Q\,\mat H^\T\mat Q^\T)\).
  4. The matrix product is distributive and a number may be taken out (item 3 of lemma 1.99 on the properties of the matrix product), so we may take \(\mat Q\) out on the left and \(\mat Q^\T\) on the right: \(\bar{\ten\varepsilon} = \mat Q\,\tfrac12\,(\mat H + \mat H^\T)\,\mat Q^\T = \mat Q\,\ten\varepsilon\,\mat Q^\T\).

Source: SADD (2.3.1) (PDF p. 47), CM (3.4.29) (PDF p. 135).

The rule is exact for the matrix defined by (1.51). This matrix, however, describes the deformation well only when the partial derivatives of the displacements are small (section 1.17). The same four steps with a minus instead of a plus give \(\bar{\ten\omega} = \mat Q\,\ten\omega\,\mat Q^\T\) for the small rotation tensor \(\ten\omega = \tfrac12\,(\mat H - \mat H^\T)\) (definition 1.116). The Green-Lagrange strain tensor \(\mat E = \tfrac12\,(\mat F^\T\mat F - \mat I)\) ((1.47)) also changes by the same rule, without the assumption of small derivatives. Since \(\mat I = \mat Q\,\mat Q^\T\), the deformation gradient in the new system is \(\bar{\mat F} = \mat I + \bar{\mat H} = \mat Q\,(\mat I + \mat H)\,\mat Q^\T = \mat Q\,\mat F\,\mat Q^\T\), so \(\bar{\mat F}^\T\bar{\mat F} = \mat Q\,\mat F^\T\mat Q^\T\mat Q\,\mat F\,\mat Q^\T = \mat Q\,\mat F^\T\mat F\,\mat Q^\T\) and \(\bar{\mat E} = \mat Q\,\mat E\,\mat Q^\T\) (CM (3.4.29), PDF p. 135).

Let us test the lemma on the sheared cube of section 1.6.2. A steel cube with the edge \(h = 10\) mm is glued to a table by its bottom face, and its top face was pushed by \(s = 10\) µm in the direction of the axis \(x\). The origin is at the bottom front left corner \(A\), the axis \(z\) points up, and the displacement field is \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = 0{,}001\,z\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\). The only nonzero partial derivative is \(H_{\mathrm{xz}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial z = 0{,}001\), so by (1.51) the only nonzero strain components are \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zx}} = 0{,}0005\). The matrix \(\ten\varepsilon\) has the same form as the stress matrix of pure shear, only with the number \(0{,}0005\) instead of \(\tau_0\). So in the axes along the diagonals \(AC\) and \(BD\) of the front face, by the same computation,

\[\bar{\ten\varepsilon} = \begin{bmatrix} 0{,}0005 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -0{,}0005 \end{bmatrix} .\]

The number \(\bar\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0{,}0005\) is the strain of the fibre along the diagonal \(AC\), and \(\bar\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = -0{,}0005\) the strain of the fibre along the diagonal \(BD\). In the table of section 1.6.2 we computed exactly, from the lengths of the fibres, \(+5{,}001 \cdot 10^{-4}\) and \(-4{,}999 \cdot 10^{-4}\). The difference comes from the squares of the derivatives, which the small strain tensor leaves out. In the diagonal axes there are no shear components. The same deformation that is shear in the axes \(x\) and \(z\) is stretching in one direction and shortening in the other in the axes along the diagonals (figure 2.53), as section 1.1 announced.

We can also check the lemma directly, without matrices. A point has the new coordinates \(\bar x\), \(\bar y\), \(\bar z\), and its old coordinates are the components of the vector from the origin \(A\) to the point, so by item 2 of lemma 2.38 \(z = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\,(\bar x + \bar z)\). By item 1 of the same lemma the new components of the displacement are

\[\begin{aligned} \bar u_{\mathrm{x}} &= \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\,(u_{\mathrm{x}} + u_{\mathrm{z}}) = 0{,}0005\,(\bar x + \bar z) , \\ \bar u_{\mathrm{y}} &= 0 , \\ \bar u_{\mathrm{z}} &= \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\,(-u_{\mathrm{x}} + u_{\mathrm{z}}) = -0{,}0005\,(\bar x + \bar z) . \end{aligned}\]

The partial derivatives with respect to the new coordinates are \(\partial\bar u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial\bar x = \partial\bar u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial\bar z = 0{,}0005\) and \(\partial\bar u_{\mathrm{z}}/\partial\bar x = \partial\bar u_{\mathrm{z}}/\partial\bar z = -0{,}0005\), and all others are zero. These are the entries of the matrix \(\mat Q\,\mat H\,\mat Q^\T\), as the lemma requires (we checked with SymPy). The symmetric part of this matrix is \(\bar{\ten\varepsilon}\) above. The antisymmetric part has only the entries \(\bar\omega_{\mathrm{xz}} = 0{,}0005\) and \(\bar\omega_{\mathrm{zx}} = -0{,}0005\) and describes a turn without a change of shape (definition 1.116): besides stretching and shortening, the diagonals also turn a little.

What about the strain of a single fibre? By (1.53) the strain of a short fibre in the direction of the unit vector \(\vek n\) is approximately \(\vek n^\T\ten\varepsilon\,\vek n\). In the new system the same direction has the components \(\bar{\vek n} = \mat Q\,\vek n\), and by lemma 1.101 and by (2.40)

\[\bar{\vek n}^\T\bar{\ten\varepsilon}\,\bar{\vek n} = \vek n^\T\mat Q^\T\,\mat Q\,\ten\varepsilon\,\mat Q^\T\,\mat Q\,\vek n = \vek n^\T\ten\varepsilon\,\vek n .\]

In both systems we get the same number, as must be, since the strain of a fibre is the ratio of two lengths, and lengths do not depend on the axes. The same holds for the normal stress on a given face, \(\bar{\vek n}^\T\bar{\ten\sigma}\,\bar{\vek n} = \vek n^\T\ten\sigma\,\vek n\), which section 2.3 announced with the words that the stresses computed with the scalar product do not depend on how we place the axes. The converse holds too. The strains of the fibres in all directions determine all six components. The component \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) is given by the fibre along the axis \(x\), and the component \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\), by section 1.17.4, by the fibre along the diagonal between the axes \(x\) and \(y\) together with the fibres along the two axes: the diagonal stretches by \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\) more than the average of those two fibres. Since the strains of the fibres are the same in both systems, the components in the new system are determined, and the rule (2.47) is the only one that makes this possible.

2.9.5 The change of a right angle

Definition 1.3 says that the shear strain of two perpendicular directions describes how the right angle between them changes. In section 1.17 we got the component \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \tfrac12\,(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial y + \partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial x)\) from partial derivatives, and we put off its geometric meaning to this chapter, where we compute angles from components. The angle between two vectors comes from the scalar product: for the angle \(\varphi\) between nonzero vectors \(\vek a\) and \(\vek b\), \(\cos\varphi = \vek a\cdot\vek b/(|\vek a|\,|\vek b|)\) (section 2.3, KREYSZIG §9.2 (4), PDF p. 388).

The model is this. The point \(P\) lies in a body whose displacement field satisfies the hypotheses of theorem 1.88 on the change of a field over a short step. From \(P\) we draw two short fibres of the same length \(h\), in the directions of the unit vectors \(\vek m\) and \(\vek n\), which are perpendicular, \(\vek m\cdot\vek n = 0\). The vectors of the fibres before loading are \(h\,\vek m\) and \(h\,\vek n\). After loading, by section 1.17, the vectors of the fibres are \(h\,\mat F\vek m\) and \(h\,\mat F\vek n\), where \(\mat F = \mat I + \mat H\) is the deformation gradient ((1.45)), each up to a remainder that is small compared with \(h\). Let the fibres be so short that we neglect the remainders. How large is the angle \(\varphi\) between the fibres after loading (figure 2.54)?

  1. By the formula for the cosine, \(\cos\varphi = (h\,\mat F\vek m)\cdot(h\,\mat F\vek n)/\big(|h\,\mat F\vek m|\,|h\,\mat F\vek n|\big)\). By item 3 of the properties of the scalar product the numerator is \(h^2\,(\mat F\vek m)\cdot(\mat F\vek n)\). By (1.10) the length of the multiple is \(|h\,\mat F\vek m| = \sqrt{h^2\,|\mat F\vek m|^2} = h\,|\mat F\vek m|\), because \(h > 0\), and likewise for \(\vek n\). The factor \(h^2\) cancels:
    \[\cos\varphi = \frac{(\mat F\vek m)\cdot(\mat F\vek n)}{|\mat F\vek m|\,|\mat F\vek n|} .\]
  2. From \(\mat F = \mat I + \mat H\) and \(\mat I\vek m = \vek m\) (definition 1.105), \(\mat F\vek m = \vek m + \mat H\vek m\), and likewise \(\mat F\vek n = \vek n + \mat H\vek n\).
  3. By item 2 of the properties of the scalar product we expand: \((\vek m + \mat H\vek m)\cdot(\vek n + \mat H\vek n) = \vek m\cdot\vek n + \vek m\cdot(\mat H\vek n) + (\mat H\vek m)\cdot\vek n + (\mat H\vek m)\cdot(\mat H\vek n)\).
  4. The first term is zero, because the directions are perpendicular.
  5. The second term is \(\vek m\cdot(\mat H\vek n) = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} m_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} H_{\mathrm{ij}}\,n_{\mathrm{j}}\), and the third is \((\mat H\vek m)\cdot\vek n = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \big(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} H_{\mathrm{ij}}\,m_{\mathrm{j}}\big)\,n_{\mathrm{i}}\). In the third we swap the names of the indices \(i\) and \(j\), which only count, and get \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} m_{\mathrm{i}}\,H_{\mathrm{ji}}\,n_{\mathrm{j}}\). The sum of the two is \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} m_{\mathrm{i}}\,(H_{\mathrm{ij}} + H_{\mathrm{ji}})\,n_{\mathrm{j}} = 2 \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} m_{\mathrm{i}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\,n_{\mathrm{j}} = 2\,\vek m^\T\ten\varepsilon\,\vek n\), because by (1.51) \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} = \tfrac12\,(H_{\mathrm{ij}} + H_{\mathrm{ji}})\).
  6. Up to here the computation is exact: \((\mat F\vek m)\cdot(\mat F\vek n) = 2\,\vek m^\T\ten\varepsilon\,\vek n + (\mat H\vek m)\cdot(\mat H\vek n)\).
  7. Now we use that the derivatives are small, in size below \(0{,}001\). The components of the unit vectors \(\vek m\) and \(\vek n\) are in size at most \(1\). The term \(2\,\vek m^\T\ten\varepsilon\,\vek n\) is a sum of derivatives multiplied by components, and the last term \((\mat H\vek m)\cdot(\mat H\vek n)\) a sum of products of two derivatives multiplied by components. The denominator is almost \(1\). The square of the length is \(|\mat F\vek m|^2 = 1 + 2\,\vek m\cdot(\mat H\vek m) + |\mat H\vek m|^2\), so \(|\mat F\vek m|\) differs from \(1\) by about \(\vek m\cdot(\mat H\vek m)\), a sum of derivatives multiplied by components, and likewise \(|\mat F\vek n|\). The numerator is itself a sum of terms with derivatives, so when we replace the denominator by \(1\), the fraction changes only by terms with products of two derivatives. If we leave out all terms with products of two derivatives, what remains is
    \[\cos\varphi \approx 2\,\vek m^\T\ten\varepsilon\,\vek n .\]
    We may do this only when \(2\,\vek m^\T\ten\varepsilon\,\vek n\) is not itself as small as these products. What this means in numbers, step 8 shows for a pair of axes.
  8. For \(\vek m = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(\vek n = \vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\), only the term with \(m_{\mathrm{x}} = 1\) and \(n_{\mathrm{y}} = 1\) in the double sum differs from zero, so \(\vek m^\T\ten\varepsilon\,\vek n = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\) and \(\cos\varphi \approx 2\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\). For this pair we estimate the error in numbers. By (1.38) the vector \(\mat H\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) has the components \(H_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(H_{\mathrm{yx}}\), \(H_{\mathrm{zx}}\), and the vector \(\mat H\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) the components \(H_{\mathrm{xy}}\), \(H_{\mathrm{yy}}\), \(H_{\mathrm{zy}}\). The last term we left out is therefore \(H_{\mathrm{xx}}H_{\mathrm{xy}} + H_{\mathrm{yx}}H_{\mathrm{yy}} + H_{\mathrm{zx}}H_{\mathrm{zy}}\), a sum of three products of two derivatives, and it is in size below \(3 \cdot 10^{-6}\). Since \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\cdot(\mat H\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = H_{\mathrm{xx}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), by the square of the length in step 7 \(|\mat F\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}|\) differs from \(1\) by about \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), and \(|\mat F\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}|\) by about \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\), and both strains are in size below \(0{,}001\). When we replace the denominator by \(1\), the fraction therefore changes by at most about two thousandths of its value. When \(2\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\) is about \(0{,}001\), both errors are only a few millionths, a few hundred times less than \(2\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\). When \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\) itself is only a few millionths, however, the term we left out can be larger than \(2\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\), and the formula can even give the wrong sign.

The angle \(\varphi\) is close to a right angle. We call the decrease of the right angle \(\gamma = 90^\circ - \varphi\). From school trigonometry, \(\cos(90^\circ - \gamma) = \sin\gamma\), as in section 2.4, so

\[\sin\gamma \approx 2\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} . \tag{2.48}\]

So twice the shear strain is the sine of the angle by which the right angle between the fibres along the axes \(x\) and \(y\) decreases. The sign tells the direction: for a positive \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\), \(\cos\varphi > 0\) and the angle decreases, for a negative one it increases. SADD describes the sign of the shear strain in the same way (PDF p. 43). The same derivation with \(\vek m = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(\vek n = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) gives \(\sin\gamma \approx 2\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}\), and with the other pairs of axes the other two shear components.

CM and SADD say the same even more briefly: \(2\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\) is simply the decrease of the right angle, measured in radians (CM (3.5.8), (3.5.9), PDF p. 142, SADD (2.2.3), PDF p. 43). CM arrives at this from a formula with the sine like our (2.48), by taking \(\sin\gamma \approx \gamma\) for small deformations (CM, PDF p. 142). The radian (radian) is a unit of angle that we have not defined yet, and we do not need it here, since the formula with the sine says the same: a larger \(2\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\) means a larger decrease of the right angle. This is why books call the number \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 2\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\) the engineering shear strain (inženirska strižna deformacija) (CM, PDF p. 141, SADD, PDF p. 43), and our component \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\) is half of it. Without the assumption of small derivatives, the computation up to step 6 is exact also with the Green-Lagrange strain tensor. Since \(\mat F^\T\mat F = \mat I + 2\,\mat E\) ((1.47)), \((\mat F\vek m)\cdot(\mat F\vek n) = \vek m^\T\mat F^\T\mat F\,\vek n = 2\,\vek m^\T\mat E\,\vek n\) and \(\cos\varphi = 2\,\vek m^\T\mat E\,\vek n/(|\mat F\vek m|\,|\mat F\vek n|)\) (CM (3.5.7), (3.5.8), PDF p. 142).

Let us test it on the sheared cube, at the corner \(A\) between the edges \(AB\) along the axis \(x\) and \(AD\) along the axis \(z\) (figure 2.54, right). Here \(2\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = 0{,}001\). Exactly, from the vectors of the edges of section 1.6.2, \((10\,000, 0, 0)\) and \((10, 0, 10\,000)\) in micrometres, we get

\[\cos\varphi = \frac{10\,000 \cdot 10 + 0 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot 10\,000}{10\,000 \cdot 10\,000{,}005} = 0{,}0009999995 .\]

The approximation \(2\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = 0{,}001\) from formula (2.48) differs from the exact value by only \(5 \cdot 10^{-10}\). For the cube the displacement field is linear, so there is no remainder in the derivation at all. The last term of step 6 is zero too, because the only nonzero derivative is \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial z = 0{,}001\), and so \(\mat H\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = \vek 0\). The difference comes only from the denominator, which we replaced by \(1\): \(|\mat F\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}| = \sqrt{1 + 0{,}001^2} = 1{,}0000005\), and \(0{,}001/1{,}0000005 = 0{,}0009999995\). The right angle decreased by \(\gamma = 0{,}0573^\circ\) (computed with NumPy). Between the diagonals \(AC\) and \(BD\), however, \(\bar\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = 0\) in the diagonal axes, and the formula predicts that the right angle between them stays. Exactly, the vectors of the diagonals after loading are \((10\,010, 0, 10\,000)\) and \((-9\,990, 0, 10\,000)\), their scalar product is \(10\,010 \cdot (-9\,990) + 10\,000 \cdot 10\,000 = -99\,999\,900 + 100\,000\,000 = 100\), since \(10\,010 \cdot 9\,990 = (10\,000 + 10)(10\,000 - 10) = 10\,000^2 - 10^2\), and the cosine of the angle between them is \(5 \cdot 10^{-7}\), two thousand times less than between the edges. The angle between the diagonals changes only by a term with the square of the shift.

Formula (2.48) holds as long as the partial derivatives of the displacements are small, also those that describe a turn, and as long as the shear strain is not as small as the squares of the derivatives. For large strains or large turns we use the exact formula with \(\mat E\) and with the lengths \(|\mat F\vek m|\) and \(|\mat F\vek n|\).

2.9.6 Tensors

Let us collect what we have derived. The components of a vector change in the passage to a new system by the rule \(\bar{\vek a} = \mat Q\,\vek a\). The matrices of the stress, of the small strain tensor, of the displacement gradient and of the small rotation tensor change by the rule \(\bar{\mat A} = \mat Q\,\mat A\,\mat Q^\T\). Lengths, scalar products, the normal stress on a given face and the strain of a given fibre do not change. In section 1.5.5 we wrote that a vector in mechanics is a quantity whose components change in a definite way when the axes are turned, while the arrow stays the same, and then we could not say in what way. Now we know. The same can be said for quantities with nine components.

From history

In his lectures on quaternions, printed in Dublin in 1853, Hamilton wrote every quaternion \(q\) as a product of two factors, \(q = Tq \times Uq\) (CAJNOT2, PDF p. 163). He called the first, \(Tq\), the tensor (tenzor), and it was only a number that stretches a unit vector to the right length (CAJNOT2, PDF p. 163). The word then had no connection with a directed quantity. According to TIMO, the notions of tensor and tensor-triad (tenzorska trojica) were first introduced into the theory of elasticity by Woldemar Voigt, who as professor at Göttingen specially studied the elastic properties of crystals (TIMO, PDF p. 284 and 286). LOVE writes that any strain is equivalent to three simple extensions along three perpendicular directions without shear, which we shall meet in section 2.11. It was proposed to call a quantity that is equivalent to its components in the same way as a simple extension a tensor. A quantity that is equivalent to its components in the same way as a strain should be called a tensor-triad (LOVE, PDF p. 61). Today's meaning differs from Hamilton's, since a tensor is itself a directed quantity of a general type, which in special cases is an ordinary vector (CAJNOT2, PDF p. 163).

Let us return to the quantities with nine components that change under a rotation of the axes by the rule \(\bar{\mat A} = \mat Q\,\mat A\,\mat Q^\T\).

Definition 2.45 Second-order tensor (tenzor drugega reda)

In mechanics a second-order tensor (tenzor drugega reda) is a quantity that in every rectangular Cartesian coordinate system is described by nine numbers, its components \(A_{\mathrm{ij}}\), collected in the matrix \(\mat A\), and whose components change in the passage to another such system with the same unit of length by the rule

\[\bar{\mat A} = \mat Q\,\mat A\,\mat Q^\T , \qquad \bar A_{\mathrm{kl}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} Q_{\mathrm{ki}}\,A_{\mathrm{ij}}\,Q_{\mathrm{lj}} , \tag{2.49}\]

where \(\mat Q\) is the rotation matrix from the old system to the new one. A quantity with three components that change by the rule \(\bar{\vek a} = \mat Q\,\vek a\) is a first-order tensor (tenzor prvega reda), and a quantity with a single component that does not change is a zero-order tensor (tenzor ničtega reda).

Source: SADD (1.5.1) (PDF p. 18), CM (2.5.21) (PDF p. 87).

So a first-order tensor is a vector in the sense of mechanics, and a zero-order tensor is a scalar, for example the length of a fibre or the normal stress on a given face. By theorem 2.42 the stress at a point is a second-order tensor, and this is why books call it the stress tensor (napetostni tenzor), which in section 2.6.5 we only mentioned. By lemmas 2.43 and 2.44 the displacement gradient and the small strain tensor are second-order tensors too, and by the same computation so are the small rotation tensor and the Green-Lagrange strain tensor. So stress and strain are quantities of the same kind, although the first measures forces and the second a change of shape.

Just as a vector is not the triple of its components, a tensor is not the matrix of its components. The stress at a point is one, but its matrix depends on how we place the axes. SADD says it in words: the components of the stress tensor change with the choice of coordinates, but the tensor itself, which describes the state of stress at the point, does not (SADD, PDF pp. 18-19). Since the rule (2.49) gives the components in every other system from the components in one, it is enough to know a tensor in a single system.

May we prescribe the components of a tensor in each system as we like? No. Suppose someone claimed that the stress at some point is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 127{,}32\) MPa in every coordinate system, with all other components zero. The bar of section 2.9.3 shows that this cannot be true: in the axes turned with \(c = 0{,}6\) and \(s = 0{,}8\) the components of the same stress are \(45{,}84\) MPa, \(81{,}49\) MPa and \(-61{,}12\) MPa. By contrast, the matrix \(-p\,\mat I\) of the fluid at rest of section 2.6.6 has the same components in every system, since by (2.40) \(\mat Q\,(-p\,\mat I)\,\mat Q^\T = -p\,\mat Q\,\mat Q^\T = -p\,\mat I\). This is the fact that the pressure in a fluid at rest is the same in all directions, written with the rule. CM states that every second-order tensor that has the same components in all systems is a multiple of the matrix \(\mat I\) (CM, PDF p. 87).

Our definition is a special form of a more general concept, and let us say in what way. First, it holds only for rectangular Cartesian coordinate systems, so books call such tensors Cartesian tensors (kartezični tenzorji) (SADD §1.5, PDF p. 18). The word tensor without an adjective generally means a concept in which the coordinates are not necessarily rectangular Cartesian, for example cylindrical, and the components change by other rules (SADD, PDF p. 19). Second, mathematics defines a tensor without coordinates, as a linear map whose input is a vector and whose output is a vector (BOWER Appendix B.1.1, PDF p. 756). Linear means that it maps the sum of two vectors to the sum of their images and a multiple of a vector to the same multiple of its image, as holds for the product with a matrix by lemma 1.97. The stress tensor maps the unit normal \(\vek n\) to the traction vector \(\vek t(\vek n) = \ten\sigma\vek n\), and the displacement gradient maps a short step \(\vek h\) to the difference of displacements \(\mat H\vek h\), both at the same point. The matrix is the record of such a map in a chosen system, and the rule (2.49) tells how the record of the same map changes when we change the system. Third, there are also tensors of higher orders, with components with more indices and with a rule in which one factor \(Q\) appears for each index (SADD (1.5.1), PDF p. 18). In chapter 3 we will meet a quantity with four indices that relates stress to strain.

From history

The new development of electrodynamics and the theory of relativity demanded a new calculus with directional quantities, the calculus of tensors, as used by the general notion of the previous paragraph (CAJNOT2, PDF p. 163). In 1901 it was developed chiefly by G. Ricci and T. Levi-Civita, and later by Albert Einstein and others (CAJNOT2, PDF p. 164).

In pure shear we found axes in which all shear components of the stress are zero, and for the sheared cube axes in which all shear components of the strain are zero. In the axes of pure shear the normal stress was \(\tau_0\) on one face and \(-\tau_0\) on another. Do such axes exist for every stress? And is \(\tau_0\) the largest normal stress on all faces through the point? This is the second question from the beginning of the section. On a face perpendicular to such an axis the traction vector is parallel to the normal, \(\ten\sigma\vek n = \lambda\,\vek n\) for some number \(\lambda\), which we can write as \((\ten\sigma - \lambda\,\mat I)\,\vek n = \vek 0\). For the unknown direction \(\vek n\) these are three equations, and whether they have any solution \(\vek n \neq \vek 0\) is decided by a single number that we compute from the matrix \(\ten\sigma - \lambda\,\mat I\). How we compute this number and what its properties are is the subject of section 2.10.
